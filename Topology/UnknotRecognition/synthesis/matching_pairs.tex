\section{Two-row matching consequences before generic enumeration}
\label{sec:matching-pairs}

The sign preprocessor of Section~\ref{sec:matching-support} inspects individual
RREF rows. A mixed-sign row may become useful after combination with another
row. The actual-source pilot finds fifty additional reductions, including
two sectors whose raw matching nullity is four. This continuation adds an
exact two-row implication search before the expensive generic-Q phase.
The independent source-equation checker and certificate schema are unchanged.

\subsection{Find every one-sided consequence in a two-row span}

Let $F$ be the already authenticated zero coordinates, and let $a,b$ be
fixed rational matching consequences. For signs $s_a,s_b\in\{-1,1\}$,
consider
\[
              w=s_a a+\lambda s_b b,\qquad\lambda\ge0.
\]
For each $j\notin F$, the inequality $w_j\ge0$ either supplies a rational
lower or upper bound on $\lambda$, supplies no bound, or makes the interval
empty. Intersecting these constraints and $[0,\infty)$ takes $O(k)$
rational operations. A finite nonempty interval is sampled at its midpoint;
an unbounded interval at its lower endpoint plus one. A singleton is
sampled at its sole point. Only a strictly positive active coefficient
produces a step. Every such coordinate is forced zero by the same
nonnegative-sum argument as before.

\begin{proposition}[Complete pair-span detection, sufficient global closure]
For any fixed rational row family and authenticated zero set, the four
signed interval tests detect a nonzero nonnegative active consequence in
any two-row span whenever one exists. Repeated pair steps interleaved with
single-row sign propagation preserve the full feasible matching cone and
terminate after at most $k$ successful steps. For at most $k$ original rows,
the closure costs $O(k^4)$ scalar inspections, with polynomial bit cost.
It need not find every forced coordinate in the entire row space.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Any combination with a nonzero first coefficient can be positively rescaled
into one of the stated signed forms. A combination with only one nonzero
coefficient is handled by sign propagation; row order and signs also cover
this case. The active nonnegativity inequalities describe a closed interval.
If it has positive length, each active coefficient is a nonnegative affine
function throughout that interval. Such a function is zero at an interior
point only if it is identically zero. Thus the midpoint or the selected
interior point finds a positive coefficient whenever one is possible.
The singleton case is checked directly.

Every step uses an actual matching consequence and previously established
zeros, so induction authenticates all newly forced coordinates. Each step
adds at least one coordinate. A pair sweep has $O(k^2)$ pairs and four
$O(k)$ interval tests per pair; at most $k+1$ sweeps occur. The intervening
single-row sweeps have the same polynomial overall bound. Rows are never
replaced by generated combinations: each interval uses the original fixed
rational rows. Bound endpoints are ratios of their coefficients and sampling
adds only constant-factor digit growth. This avoids iterative coefficient
blow-up. Source provenance has the existing polynomial rational-elimination
cost. Reinstating proved-zero coordinates gives the same full cone
isomorphism as in Section~\ref{sec:matching-support}.
\end{proof}

For example, the preceding mixed rows $(1,0,-1,2)$ and $(0,1,2,-1)$ now
force all four coordinates. But the three rows
\[
 (1,0,0,-2,1,1),\quad(0,1,0,1,-2,1),\quad(0,0,1,1,1,-2)
\]
produce no single or pair step. Their sum $(1,1,1,0,0,0)$ forces the first
three coordinates zero, while $(0,0,0,1,1,1)$ remains feasible. Indeed each
pair's active pivot columns require its two coefficients nonnegative;
the two corresponding free columns require their ratio to be both at least
two and at most one half. This counterexample concerns general row systems,
not a claimed geometric realization. It prevents treating the pair fixed
point as a complete maximal-support LP theorem.

\subsection{Keep source authority and complete discovery intact}

The cheap phase uses the existing kernel's canonical RREF equations and
first performs the preceding single-row closure. When a pair creates new
zeros, single-row propagation resumes before another pair sweep. Source
provenance is still compiled only when a reduction exists. The actual source
RREF must equal the recovered kernel equations, and replaying the same
closure must give the same forced set.

Each step records a rational combination of one or two RREF rows. Their
source-equation coefficient vectors are combined exactly, coalesced and
serialized as the ordinary sorted source-row combination. No new schema or
pair claim is entrusted to the checker. It reconstructs each combination
from the original matching equations, requires every triangle term to cancel,
and checks its nonnegative active coefficients and exact newly forced set.
The checker imports neither the pair search nor its interval calculations.

The native automatic standard branch continues to run preprocessing only
above matching nullity three. A successful reduction recompiles the original
source with its retained allowed types, then uses the existing adaptive
low-dimensional discovery or generic fallback. A completed negative
certificate retains the original support and restores omitted coordinates
as zeros. Removing its support annotation still permits full original-sector
replay. Candidate caps and cancellation remain in force; an unfinished
query is inconclusive.

If the retained matching nullity is $r$, the same conditional geometry bound
now uses the stronger polynomial preprocessor:
\[
 \operatorname{poly}(T+k+B)+\operatorname{poly}(T+B)(1+k')^{O(r)}.
\]
The quartic sign scan, source recompilation and provenance are charged;
component queries and their proofs remain separate. No logarithmic bound
on $r$ for arbitrary knot inputs or complete small family of sectors has
been proved. More polynomial reductions can remove expensive raw-dimensional
work on actual sources without supplying the missing global theorem.
