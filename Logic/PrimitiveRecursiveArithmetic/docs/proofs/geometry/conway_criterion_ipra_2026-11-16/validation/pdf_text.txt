                                                        Abstract
         The Conway criterion is a local symmetry test implying that a topological disk tiles the
     plane periodically by translations and half-turns. Its usual proofs describe matching strips or
     double the tile into a generalized parallelogram or hexagon. Those pictures correctly identify the
     construction, but a completely formal proof must still rule out a gap or overlap at the vertices
     where several boundary arcs meet.
         This report gives a finite, constructive proof for rational piecewise-linear disks, the natural
     arithmetic presentation of polygonal and polyform instances. If the counterclockwise boundary
     is split into six paths a, b, c, d, e, f , with a translating to the reverse of d and the other four paths
     invariant under their endpoint half-turns, define
                  t = E − A = D − B,          u = E + F − B − C,           He (x) = E + F − x.
     The explicit family
                              T + mt + nu,         He (T ) + mt + nu     (m, n ∈ Z)
     is shown to be a tiling. The proof projects the two orbit representatives to the period torus
     R2 /(Zt + Zu). Their oriented boundaries cancel. After a finite common subdivision they form a
     nonnegative two-cycle, hence have a constant coverage multiplicity. An exact open-path shoelace
     calculation gives
                                        det(t, u) = 2 area(T ) > 0,
     so the two representatives have exactly the area of one torus fundamental cell and the con-
     stant multiplicity is one. This simultaneously proves coverage, disjointness of interiors, and
     nondegeneracy of the period lattice.
        Every ingredient is finite: signed rational arithmetic, lists of vertices, a certified triangulation,
     segment clipping, a dual spanning tree, and bounded sums. We formulate the resulting number-
     coded theorem as a quantifier-free universal schema of intuitionistic primitive recursive arithmetic
     (iPRA), audit the proof for constructive principles, and give a dependency-ordered route to a
     Lean theorem of type
                                 ([] : List(Fm PTm 0)) ⊢ [PRAAx] φConway .
     The packet also contains exact Python reference code, a nonconvex worked example, figures,
     and an uncompiled Lean blueprint. The mathematical theorem is proved here; a kernel-checked
     Prf PRAAx object remains a clearly delimited formalization task.

Deliverable status. The report proves the finite PL theorem without excluded middle, choice,
compactness, or a classical covering-space argument. The executable companion validates the
algebraic and torus certificates of the worked example using exact rational arithmetic. The Lean
files contain no admitted facts, but they are a blueprint rather than a completed formal proof.

1     Statement, representation, and logical scope
1.1     The ordinary geometric criterion
Let T ⊆ R2 be a closed topological disk. Choose six consecutive boundary points
                                                A, B, C, D, E, F
in counterclockwise order; adjacent chosen points are allowed to coincide, so an intervening boundary
part may be empty. Write
                    a = AB,      b = BC,      c = CD,    d = DE,   e = EF,   f = FA
for the corresponding oriented boundary paths. Conway’s condition is:
    (i) a translation takes A to E, B to D, and the path a to the path ED = rev(d);
 (ii) each of b, c, e, f is invariant, as an unoriented path, under the half-turn interchanging its
      endpoints;
 (iii) at least three of A, . . . , F are distinct.
The conclusion is that congruent copies of T , obtained using translations and half-turns, tile the
plane periodically. This is the labelled formulation emphasized by Schattschneider [1]; it removes
the ambiguity in saying merely that two boundary parts are “parallel.”
    The strip proof is geometrically persuasive: translate copies along a, d to form a strip, rotate a
second strip around a midpoint of e or f , and repeat. A modern exposition explicitly observes that
this description is still vague at the vertices and cites simple connectivity and covering spaces as a
repair [2, Remark 3.4.4]. The purpose of this report is to make that repair finite and arithmetical.

1.2     Why an arithmetic presentation is needed
First-order iPRA has natural numbers as objects. It does not directly quantify over arbitrary subsets
of R2 , arbitrary continuous maps, or an uncountable family of tile copies. A theorem in iPRA must
therefore specify:
    • how a tile and its boundary are number-coded;
    • which finite certificate says that the code is a disk;
    • which primitive-recursive functions produce the proposed tiling certificate; and
    • which bounded, decidable predicate verifies that certificate.
    The exact theorem proved here uses a rational piecewise-linear disk. After multiplying all
coordinates by a common denominator, one may equivalently use integer coordinates. A disk
certificate consists of a finite list of positively oriented triangles with exact edge-incidence data.
The union of the triangles is the tile, their interiors are pairwise disjoint, every interior edge occurs
twice with opposite orientations, and the remaining oriented edges form one simple boundary cycle.
The six boundary paths are finite vertex lists along that cycle. A geometrically empty path is
represented by a singleton list.
    This covers polygons, polyominoes, polyiamonds, polyhexes, and every exact finite straight-
line model normally used in applications of the criterion. It is also the correct finite kernel for
extensions to effectively named curved boundaries: an extension must supply enough modulus and
approximation data to reduce the relevant claim to compatible finite PL certificates. We do not
silently identify that stronger represented-analysis theorem with the finite theorem proved below.

Definition 1.1 (Normalized PL Conway datum). A normalized PL Conway datum is a positively
oriented rational PL disk T , a certified triangulation of T , and six nonempty vertex lists a, b, c, d, e, f
whose concatenation is the oriented boundary cycle, satisfying

                              τt (a) = rev(d),                         t = E − A,                        (1)
                         HB+C (b) = rev(b),                                                              (2)
                         HC+D (c) = rev(c),                                                              (3)
                         HE+F (e) = rev(e),                                                              (4)
                         HF +A (f ) = rev(f ).                                                           (5)

Here τw (x) = x + w, while Hs (x) = s − x is the half-turn whose doubled center is s. Equality of paths
means equality after deleting consecutive duplicate vertices and redundant collinear subdivision
vertices.
     Using doubled centers avoids division by two. Every displayed equation is an equality of finite
lists of rational points, hence is decidable by cross multiplication.

1.3    The explicit theorem
Set
                         t = E − A,        u = E + F − B − C,         R = HE+F .                         (6)
For (m, n) ∈ Z2 , define two tile orbits
                           0
                          Tm,n = T + mt + nu,           1
                                                       Tm,n = R(T ) + mt + nu.                           (7)

Theorem 1.2 (Finite PL Conway criterion). For every normalized PL Conway datum:
 (a) det(t, u) = 2 area(T ) > 0, so t, u generate a rank-two lattice;
 (b) the family in equation (7) covers R2 ;
 (c) distinct members have disjoint interiors; and
 (d) every boundary pairing is a translation or a half-turn generated by the four specified centrosym-
     metries.
Consequently T admits a periodic monohedral tiling using only translations and half-turns. A lattice
fundamental cell contains exactly two tile copies by area.
   The proof occupies sections 2 to 6. It is constructive in a stronger sense than mere intuitionistic
acceptability: from the finite datum it computes the periods, the two orbit representatives, a torus
subdivision, and every bounded patch.

2     Finite affine and path algebra
Everything needed from Euclidean geometry reduces to polynomial identities in two coordinates
and recursion over finite lists.

2.1    Points, translations, and half-turns
For points or vectors x = (x1 , x2 ) and y = (y1 , y2 ), write

                                        det(x, y) = x1 y2 − x2 y1 .

For a vector w, let τw (x) = x + w. For a doubled center s, let

                                              Hs (x) = s − x.

Thus Hs is rotation by 180◦ about s/2, but no midpoint division occurs in the formal language.

Lemma 2.1 (Half-turn algebra). For all doubled centers p, q and all points x,

                                       Hp (Hp (x)) = x,                                                  (9)
                                       Hq (Hp (x)) = τq−p (x),                                          (10)
                                        Hp (τw (x)) = τ−w (Hp (x)).                                     (11)

Proof. Expand the definitions:

           p − (p − x) = x,       q − (p − x) = x + (q − p),              p − (x + w) = (p − x) − w.

These are ring identities over the integers. Their iPRA translations are handled by the integer-
difference layer and ipra_ring.

    For an oriented finite path P = (p0 , . . . , pr ), transformations act pointwise. Its reverse is

                                          rev(P ) = (pr , . . . , p0 ).

As an oriented one-chain we also write −P for rev(P ). Concatenation is denoted by P ∗ Q when
the last vertex of P is the first vertex of Q.

2.2     Doubled centers forced by endpoints
Introduce the doubled centers of the four centrosymmetric paths:

                    p = B + C,        q = C + D,       r = E + F,         s = F + A.             (12)

The translated-pair condition gives

                                      E = A + t,       D = B + t.                                (13)

Hence
                                       q = p + t,      r = s + t.                                (14)
The second period in equation (6) is simply

                                              u = r − p.                                         (15)

Combining equations (10), (14) and (15) yields

          Hr Hp = τu ,           Hr Hq = τu−t ,             Hr Hr = id,           Hr Hs = τt .   (16)

These four identities are the whole group-theoretic content of the construction.

2.3     Oriented path identities
The Conway hypotheses say

                     Hp (b) = −b,     Hq (c) = −c,   Hr (e) = −e,    Hs (f ) = −f.

Apply Hr and use equation (16).
Lemma 2.2 (Pairing with the second orbit). The half-turned tile R(T ) = Hr (T ) has boundary
paths satisfying

                                          Hr (b) = −τu (b),                                      (17)
                                          Hr (c) = −τu−t (c),                                    (18)
                                          Hr (e) = −e,                                           (19)
                                          Hr (f ) = −τt (f ).                                    (20)

Moreover the translated-pair relation τt (a) = −d remains a translated-pair relation after applying
Hr .
Proof. For b, for example,

                               τu (b) = Hr Hp (b) = Hr (−b) = −Hr (b),

which is equation (17). The other three calculations are identical, using the corresponding equations
in equation (16). Finally, applying Hr to τt (a) = −d and using equation (11) gives

                                        τ−t (Hr (a)) = −Hr (d).

Thus the two paths of the turned tile pair under the lattice period t, with the direction reversed as
required.

   No angle, length, trigonometric function, or real-number limit appears. The local matching
equations are list equalities and affine polynomial identities.

3.2      Why pairwise edge matching is not yet a proof
Theorem 3.1 rules out an exposed open boundary arc, but by itself it does not prove that the plane
is covered exactly once. Around a marked endpoint, paired arcs could conceivably wind so as to
leave a gap, or several tiles could overlap. A picture suggests the correct cyclic order, but a formal
proof must derive it.
Conway criterion in iPRA                                                                        9/32


   One classical repair constructs a surface from abstract tile copies and invokes covering-space
theory. That route is valid, but it imports more topology than iPRA needs. We instead quotient the
explicit placement by the period lattice. Only two tiles remain. Their sum is an oriented two-cycle
on a finite torus subdivision. The cycle condition forces a globally constant integer multiplicity,
and an area identity computes that integer as one. Thus the same calculation settles all vertices
simultaneously.

3.3    The group generated by the permitted half-turns
The construction also explains why only the designated half-turns are needed. The half-turns about
doubled centers p, q, r, s generate the translations

                                      t = q − p = r − s,            u = r − p,

by equation (10). Conversely every turned tile in equation (7) is obtained by R = Hr followed by a
lattice translation. Therefore all placements lie in the group generated by the original boundary
half-turns. This is the wallpaper-group p2 mechanism described geometrically in [1], written here as
exact integer-vector algebra.


4     The determinant–area identity
The decisive numerical invariant is obtained from an open-path version of the shoelace sum. Keeping
paths open makes the contribution of a translation visible.

4.1    The open-path shoelace functional
For a finite path P = (p0 , . . . , pk ), define

                                           J (P ) =         det(pi , pi+1 ).                     (21)
                                                      X

                                                      i<k

If P is closed, J (P ) is twice its oriented polygonal area. More generally:

Lemma 4.1 (Elementary J -laws). Let P run from X to Y . Then

                                    J (P ∗ Q) = J (P ) + J (Q),                                  (22)
                                     J (rev P ) = −J (P ),                                       (23)
                                      J (τw P ) = J (P ) + det(w, Y − X),                        (24)
                                      J (Hs P ) = J (P ) + det(s, X − Y ).                       (25)

Proof. The first two statements are finite-sum rearrangements. For translation, each edge contributes

                         det(pi + w, pi+1 + w) = det(pi , pi+1 ) + det(w, pi+1 − pi ).

The added terms telescope to det(w, Y − X). For equation (25), first negate every point; this
preserves each determinant, changes the endpoints to −X, −Y , and then translate by s. Applying
equation (24) gives
                            J (Hs P ) = J (P ) + det(s, (−Y ) − (−X)).
All proofs are inductions over the path list and polynomial normalization.

Lemma 4.2 (Centrally symmetric path). If P runs from X to Y and

                                        HX+Y (P ) = rev(P ),

then
                                        J (P ) = det(X, Y ).                                       (26)

Proof. By equations (23) and (25),

                               −J (P ) = J (P ) + det(X + Y, X − Y ).

Bilinearity and antisymmetry give

                                det(X + Y, X − Y ) = −2 det(X, Y ).

Therefore 2J (P ) = 2 det(X, Y ), and cancellation of the positive numeral 2 yields equation (26). In
the object theory one can avoid a primitive division operation: this is integer cancellation in the
difference ring, already supported by the ProveIt arithmetic layer.

4.2    The translated pair
The relation τt (a) = rev(d), with a : A → B, gives by equations (23) and (24)

                                  J (a) + J (d) = − det(t, B − A).                                 (27)

The four central paths give

         J (b) = det(B, C),   J (c) = det(C, D),      J (e) = det(E, F ),   J (f ) = det(F, A).    (28)

Theorem 4.3 (Period–area identity). For every normalized PL Conway datum,

                                     det(t, u) = 2 area(T ) > 0.                                   (29)

Proof. Add equations (27) and (28). Since D = B + t and E = A + t,

                       J (∂T ) = − det(t, B − A) + det(B, C) + det(C, B + t)
                                   + det(A + t, F ) + det(F, A)
                              = det(t, A + F − B − C).

The terms det(B, C) + det(C, B) and det(A, F ) + det(F, A) cancel. Because

                              u=E+F −B−C =t+A+F −B−C

and det(t, t) = 0, the last expression is det(t, u).
    It remains to identify J (∂T ) with twice the area. Sum the oriented shoelace values of the
certified triangles. Every interior edge appears once in each direction, so its determinant terms
cancel. The uncancelled edges are exactly the oriented boundary cycle. Thus

                               J (∂T ) = 2         area(∆) = 2 area(T ).

                                             ∆∈D

Every triangle in the disk certificate has nonnegative oriented area, and at least one has positive
area; hence 2 area(T ) > 0. This proves equation (29).

Corollary 4.4 (Nondegenerate period lattice). The vectors t, u are linearly independent, the lattice
L = Zt + Zu has a positively oriented fundamental parallelogram, and its area is exactly twice the
tile area.
Proof. In two dimensions, det(t, u) > 0 is precisely positive linear independence. The covolume of
L is det(t, u), which equals 2 area(T ) by theorem 4.3.

    This identity is stronger than a post hoc area check. It derives the second period’s nondegeneracy
directly from the Conway boundary equations, even when some marked points coincide or some
paths are empty.


5     The finite torus-cycle argument
We now close the vertex gap. The key move is to quotient by the lattice whose existence was just
proved.

Theorem 5.3 (Multiplicity-one torus partition). The projected closed sets π(T ) and π(R(T )),
counted with all lattice lifts implicit in the projection, cover the torus exactly once away from their

Proof. The coefficient of every open two-simplex of K is k = 1, so each such simplex is contained in
exactly one tile interior. If two tile interiors met, their intersection would be open in R2 and would
contain an open subdivision face, contradicting coefficient one.
   The union of the finitely many projected closed triangle pieces is closed. Every point on the
subdivision skeleton is a limit of an adjacent open face. The tile covering that face is closed and
therefore contains the limit point. Hence the skeleton is covered as well. Boundaries may coincide,
as permitted in a tiling.

5.5   An exact checker-friendly variant
The executable companion uses an equivalent certificate that avoids constructing the full face graph
for its example. It triangulates the tile, changes to lattice coordinates, clips all relevant translated
triangles to the unit square, and obtains finitely many closed convex rational polygons. It verifies:

 (1) every piece lies in the unit square;

 (2) every pairwise intersection has area zero; and

 (3) the sum of piece areas is exactly one.

For rational polygons, these facts imply a partition: a nonempty gap in a finite closed polygonal
union contains a rational open triangle of positive area, while a positive-area overlap is detected by
a pairwise intersection. The full iPRA development may use either this convex-piece certificate or
the face-multiplicity certificate. The latter mirrors the conceptual proof; the former can be shorter
computationally.


6     Proof of the Conway criterion
We can now assemble the preceding lemmas.

Proof of theorem 1.2. The period–area identity theorem 4.3 gives

                                      det(t, u) = 2 area(T ) > 0,

   Project the two representatives T and R(T ) to T = R2 /L. By theorem 5.1, their sum is a
boundaryless nonnegative two-chain. By the finite common-subdivision and constant-multiplicity
argument of section 5, this chain has constant multiplicity k. Exact area comparison and positivity

partition of the torus by the two orbit representatives.

    Take the full inverse image under the quotient map π : R2 → T. The inverse image of π(T ) is
exactly the collection T + mt + nu, and the inverse image of π(R(T )) is exactly R(T ) + mt + nu,
for (m, n) ∈ Z2 . Since the torus is covered, the plane is covered. Since torus interiors are disjoint,
the interiors of distinct lifted tiles are disjoint. This proves parts (b) and (c).
    Finally, theorem 3.1 identifies every neighboring pair by the translated edge equation or one of
the four designated half-turn equations. The periods themselves are compositions of those half-turns
by equation (16), so every placement uses only translations and half-turns generated by the Conway
data. This proves part (d) and the periodic-tiling conclusion.

6.1    Degenerate marked points and empty arcs
The proof does not require the six marked points to be pairwise distinct. If, say, B = C, then b
may be the singleton path (B); its half-turn equation is trivial and J (b) = 0 = det(B, B). All affine
and chain identities remain valid. Likewise A = B makes a empty and forces D = E through the
translated-pair condition.
    The usual requirement that at least three marked points be distinct prevents vacuous labellings in
the topological statement. In the certified PL theorem, positive disk area already forces det(t, u) > 0,
hence enough geometric nondegeneracy for the construction. Keeping the conventional requirement
in ConwayOK makes the arithmetic statement match the established criterion.

6.2    Why the proof is stronger than a drawing
There are three logically separate tasks:
  (i) construct candidate placements;
 (ii) show every open boundary arc has a matching neighbor; and
 (iii) prove global coverage and nonoverlap, including at vertices.
The strip argument directly addresses the first two. The torus cycle and area computation address
the third without a case analysis of the possible coincidences among A, . . . , F . In particular, no
angle-sum calculation is required. All vertex configurations are absorbed into the single equality
k = 1.

7     Encoding the proof in intuitionistic PRA
This section turns the geometric proof into an arithmetic program and proof obligation. The
point is not merely that all objects are finite. We must identify total primitive-recursive functions,
quantifier-free invariants, and induction parameters.

7.1   The ambient theory
The ProveIt development uses iPRA in its ordinary first-order form: one function symbol for every
primitive-recursive function, defining equations, successor axioms, equality, intuitionistic natural
deduction, and induction for quantifier-free formulas. Its checked Lean embedding represents
derivations by
                                         Prf PRAAx Γ A,
written in repository notation as Γ ⊢ [PRAAx]A. The existing IPRADerivations library supplies
arithmetic, signed differences, order, finite sums, pairing, bounded search, and determinant infras-
tructure. The intended endpoint is not a meta-level Lean theorem about natural numbers but an
actual term of type
                             ([] : List(Fm PTm 0)) ⊢ [PRAAx]φConway .
Only after that term exists should the checked bridge pra_iff_native transport it to the native
scalar calculus.

7.2   Number codes
One convenient total coding is as follows.

Signed integers.     Represent z ∈ Z by a pair (z + , z − ) ∈ N2 , denoting z + − z − . Equality is

                                        z + + w− = w+ + z − .

The existing ZT layer already implements this difference-ring view and turns polynomial identities
in the quotient ring into iPRA derivations.

Rationals. Represent a rational by (n, d), where n is a signed integer and d ≥ 1. Reduction to
lowest terms is unnecessary. Equality and order use cross multiplication. Addition and multiplication
are primitive recursive, and every division introduced by segment intersection is represented by a
numerator/positive-denominator pair.

Finite lists. Use the checked pairing function and its projections recursively. A list code stores its
length and a nested payload, or equivalently uses a tagged nil/cons representation. Lookup, map,
reverse, concatenation, bounded fold, and normalization are all primitive recursive. The defining
equations are proved by quantifier-free induction on length.
Conway criterion in iPRA                                                                         16/32


Geometry. A point is a pair of rationals. A path is a list of points. A triangle is a triple of points.
A disk certificate contains:

 (1) a triangle list;

 (2) a list of oriented triangle edges;

 (3) an involutive pairing of interior edges;

 (4) the remaining boundary cycle; and

 (5) six index intervals selecting a, b, c, d, e, f .

All malformed decodings return fixed defaults. Correctness predicates guard the meaningful branch.

7.5   Internal proof decomposition
The mathematical proof translates to the following object-theory blocks.

Block A: path algebra. Induction over the path code proves concatenation, reversal, translation,
and half-turn equations for PathJ. Every induction formula is an equation of primitive-recursive
terms.

Block B: central paths and the area identity. Use the path equations and integer-ring
normalization to derive J (P ) = det(X, Y ) from the central-symmetry list equality. Sum the six
contributions. A second induction over the triangle list cancels paired interior edges and proves
boundary shoelace equals the sum of triangle double areas.

Block C: torus boundary. Map the six path equations into the quotient-coordinate represen-
tation. “Equal modulo the lattice” is an explicit integer-pair witness, not a quotient type in the
object language. The four identities in theorem 2.2 make the boundary fold evaluate to zero.

Block D: constant multiplicity. The refinement certificate gives, for each oriented edge, its two
incident faces. A bounded fold computes the boundary coefficient and proves adjacent counts equal.
Induction over the supplied dual-tree enumeration proves every face count equals the root count.

Block E: multiplicity one. A finite area sum gives

                                        k · det(t, u) = det(t, u).

The positive determinant witness obtained from the disk certificate permits cancellation. No integral-
domain axiom for arbitrary encoded rings is needed; this is ordinary natural/integer arithmetic.
Conway criterion in iPRA                                                                          18/32


7.6   No universal evaluator is required
The theorem quantifies over data for fixed primitive-recursive decoders and checkers. It does
not quantify over codes of arbitrary primitive-recursive programs and then appeal to a universal
evaluation function. This distinction matters because full universal evaluation is not primitive
recursive. Every function symbol used in equation (35) is fixed once and for all by explicit recursion.

7.7   Uniformity and extraction
The proof is uniform in the input code. Under Curry–Howard reading, a proof of equation (35)
justifies the concrete extraction already displayed in equations (6) and (7). Given valid data, the
witnessing tiling is not found by search:

                    t = E − A,       u = E + F − B − C,         R(x) = E + F − x.

Search is used only for finite normalization, clipping, and traversal certificates. The central geometric
construction is closed-form.

Do not use real quotients in the object theory. The host layer may describe R2 /L, but the
arithmetic implementation should use rational lattice coordinates in a unit square with identified
side tags. Congruence modulo L is witnessed by integer index pairs.

Do not hide planarity in an oracle. The common refinement must be either produced by
an explicit clipping/arrangement constructor or supplied as a certificate whose existence is then
constructively generated. A predicate named isTiling with no verified algorithm would merely
rename the theorem.

tree. Every parent and traversal order is numerical data.

is validation only. The final theorem must inhabit Prf PRAAx.

Do not overstate the curved-boundary scope. The first formal target should be the rational
PL theorem. A represented-continuum extension needs explicit approximation and separation
moduli and should be a separate module.

10     Constructive and foundational audit
A proof can be finite in appearance while still relying on hidden classical principles. This section

10.1    Principles used
 (1) Primitive recursion on finite lists. Path sums, triangle sums, normalization, clipping, and
     graph traversal are bounded folds or recursions.

 (2) Quantifier-free induction. This proves loop invariants, telescoping sums, interior-edge
     cancellation, and propagation along a finite tree.

 (3) Decidable integer and rational comparisons. Signs of determinants, segment intersections,
     and sort order are reduced to natural-number comparisons.

 (4) Finite bounded search. Used to select the least available ear, intersection, parent, or
     unvisited face. Failure returns a default certificate and is excluded by the correctness premise.

 (5) Intuitionistic logic with explicit witnesses. The period vectors, half-turn, triangulation
     data, lattice shifts, and spanning tree are all computed or supplied as finite data.

 (6) Polynomial equality and cancellation. Determinant identities live in the signed-difference
     ring; positivity permits the final cancellation in natural arithmetic.

10.2    Principles not used
 (1) No law of excluded middle for arbitrary propositions. Every case split is on a Boolean
     equality or order test for finite codes.

 (2) No choice. Finite selections use least bounded search, yielding explicit indices.

 (3) No compactness or infinite branch argument. Periodicity reduces the global problem to
     one finite torus cell.
Conway criterion in iPRA                                                                          26/32


 (4) No completeness of the real numbers. All coordinates and intersections are rational
     pairs; all equalities are exact.

 (5) No Jordan curve theorem. The input disk property is a finite triangulation certificate.
     The conclusion is obtained from a torus subdivision, not from separation by an arbitrary
     curve.

 (6) No covering-space theorem or fundamental-group computation. The role of the torus
     fundamental class is replaced by theorem 5.2, a dual-tree induction.

 (7) No measure theory. “Area” is a finite determinant sum. Additivity follows from oriented
     edge cancellation.

 (8) No universal primitive-recursive evaluator. Every checker and constructor is a fixed
     symbol.

10.3    Where positivity enters
The cycle argument alone gives C = k[T] for some k ∈ N. Positivity enters twice:

 (a) the disk triangulation orients every input triangle positively, so the face coefficients are natural
     coverage counts rather than signed cancellation artifacts;

 (b) det(t, u) = 2 area(T ) > 0 permits cancellation in equation (34).

Both facts are witnessed by finite determinant inequalities. There is no appeal to an abstract
positivity cone.

10.4    Why the torus is safe in iPRA
The notation R2 /L is explanatory. The formal arithmetic proof need never construct an infinite
quotient set. It uses:

   • the unit rational square in lattice coordinates;

   • side tags recording the identifications α = 0 ∼ 1 and β = 0 ∼ 1;

   • finitely many clipped rational pieces;

   • a finite edge/face incidence table; and

   • integer witnesses for lattice congruence.

Thus every use of “torus” expands to a finite data structure and decidable relations.

10.5    Faithfulness to the traditional theorem
The local hypotheses are exactly the labelled Conway criterion, specialized only in representation:
each boundary part is a rational PL path and the tile comes with a rational triangulation. The
output is stronger than mere existence because it gives the periods and placements explicitly. What
is not formalized here is a universal theorem over arbitrary topological embeddings with no finite
presentation. Such a statement is not literally a first-order arithmetic sentence until a representation
of continuous objects is chosen.
Conway criterion in iPRA                                                                      27/32


    For an effectively presented curved tile, a separate theorem could assume primitive-recursive
names, continuity and inverse-separation moduli, plus finite certificates that the relevant boundary
symmetries hold. The finite torus multiplicity argument should then be recovered by sufficiently
fine stable PL approximations, but proving that stability is additional represented-topology work.

11     Further research questions
Q1. Smallest object-theory proof. What is the smallest complete Prf PRAAx derivation of the
    PL criterion when measured by normalized Lean term size or by the concise proof-checker
    metrics used in ConciseIPRA? The torus coefficient lemma may admit a very small generic
    library theorem.

Q2. Avoiding a full arrangement. Can the formal proof use only pairwise convex clipping, area
    additivity, and a finite separation lemma, thereby eliminating explicit face enumeration and
    dual spanning trees? Which route produces the simpler proof object?

Q3. A general periodic-cycle principle. Formalize a reusable theorem: a finite family of
    positively oriented rational PL disks whose projected boundaries cancel on a lattice torus and
    whose total area equals the lattice covolume forms a tiling. Conway’s criterion would then be a
    short boundary-cancellation corollary. The same principle may cover generalized parallelogons
    and other wallpaper-group constructions.

Q4. Other wallpaper groups. Replace half-turns by rotations of orders 3, 4, 6 or by glide
    reflections. Which local boundary equations imply a finite orbifold cycle of multiplicity one?
    A uniform “orbifold chain certificate” could subsume several classical tiling criteria.

Q5. Represented curved boundaries. Starting from the repository’s constructive Jordan
    framework, identify minimal primitive-recursive moduli ensuring that a curved Conway datum
    has stable finite PL torus certificates. Can one avoid a full constructive Schoenflies theorem?

Q6. Automatic criterion discovery. Given a rational polygonal tile, use bounded search to
    find six boundary breakpoints and normalized path correspondences satisfying ConwayOK. The
    search is decidable for a fixed finite subdivision. What is its complexity, and can symmetry
    hashing make it practical for large polyforms?
 Conway criterion in iPRA                                                                      28/32


 Q7. Certificate minimization. The worked example’s cutter returns 58 convex pieces. Find
     a canonical minimal torus subdivision or a proof-producing simplifier that merges adjacent
     pieces while preserving exact boundary tags.

 Q8. Reverse characterization. Schattschneider discusses Conway tiles as those admitting
     suitable p2-ishedral tilings. What finite arithmetic hypotheses on a periodic half-turn tiling
     suffice to reconstruct the six paths and prove the converse in iPRA?

 Q9. Degenerate and self-contacting fundamental domains. The theorem assumes a disk with
     simple boundary. Can the chain method classify when a non-simple generalized parallelogon
     still yields a legitimate tile after cancellation of retraced arcs?

Q10. Formal proof extraction to graphics. Generate SVG or TikZ tiling patches directly
     from the checked torus certificate, so that the illustration is a verified rendering of the same
     number-coded data used in the theorem.

B      Primitive-recursive torus constructor
The following pseudocode spells out one total implementation. Every loop has an explicit finite
bound.
BuildTorus(code c):
  if not DiskOK(c) or not ConwayOK(c):
      return EmptyCertificate

 t := E(c) - A(c)
 u := E(c) + F(c) - B(c) - C(c)
 delta := det(t,u)
 if delta <= 0: return EmptyCertificate

 triangles := DecodeCertifiedTriangles(c)
 turned := map (x |-> E(c)+F(c)-x) triangles
 pieces := []

 for triangle q in triangles ++ turned:
     q’ := map LatticeCoordinates(t,u,delta) q
     I := all integer horizontal shifts whose box meets [0,1]^2
     J := all integer vertical shifts whose box meets [0,1]^2
     for i in I:
       for j in J:
         p := ClipConvex(q’ + (i,j), UnitSquare)
         if TwiceArea(p) > 0: append p to pieces

 intersections := all pairwise segment intersections in pieces
 split every edge at its sorted intersection list
 graph := build directed half-edge graph with torus side tags
 faces := bounded face walk from every unused half-edge
 trianglesK := triangulate each rational face
 dualTree := least-search spanning tree of face adjacency
 counts := coverage count of each triangleK by original pieces

 return Certificate(pieces, graph, faces, trianglesK, dualTree, counts)
Conway criterion in iPRA                                                                      30/32


    The verifier checks:

 (1) all decoded rational denominators are positive;

 (2) all segment splits and cyclic face walks are exact;

 (3) every directed half-edge has the prescribed reverse, including side identifications;

 (4) every small triangle is positively oriented;

 (5) the dual tree visits every face exactly once;

 (6) the boundary coefficient of every edge is zero;

 (7) adjacent face counts agree; and

 (8) the common count is one by the determinant-area identity.

   A simpler certificate can omit graph, faces, and counts, retaining the clipped convex pieces
and checking pairwise zero-area intersections plus total area one. The report’s Python reference
uses this variant. For a first object-theory implementation, the dual-tree form has the advantage
that the logical core is just equality propagation on a finite graph.
