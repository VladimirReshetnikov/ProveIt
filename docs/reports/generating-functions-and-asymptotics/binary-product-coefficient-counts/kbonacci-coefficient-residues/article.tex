\begin{abstract}
For the $k$-bonacci numbers initialized by $k$ ones, consider the product
$P_n(x)=\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}(1+x^{F_{i+k}^{(k)}})$, with $k\ge2$.
We give a self-contained finite-state argument that, for every modulus $m$
and residue $a$, the number of coefficients of $P_n$ congruent to $a$
has a rational ordinary generating function in $n$. The construction also
proves rationality for the number of coefficients equal to any prescribed
positive integer. Its key ingredients are a unique binary normal form
avoiding $1^k0$, a factorization of each representation fiber into disjoint
carry blocks, and an exact counter with only $k+1$ coordinates. Modulo two,
a uniform automaton with $4k+2$ states gives
\[
 \sum_{n\ge0}\#\{j:[x^j]P_n(x)\text{ is odd}\}\,z^n
 =\frac{1+2z^k}{1-2z+2z^k-2z^{k+1}}.
\]
We then prove that same odd-coefficient identity a \emph{second} time, by a
completely disjoint elementary route: replacing selected plus signs by minus
signs according to a periodic coherent signing makes every coefficient of the
signed product lie in $\{-1,0,1\}$, parity becomes a squared norm, and an exact
overlap identity closes the recurrence. The second proof shares no lemma with
the first, and it holds for every quasifibonacci weight sequence with
superincreasing seeds, not only for Stanley's initialization.
These arguments provide proposed proofs of Conjectures 6.2 and 6.3 in
Stanley's arXiv version 3. We explicitly handle internal zero coefficients,
prove two \emph{different} exponential sparsity statements with different
denominators, derive exponential asymptotics twice (analytically and by
Perron--Frobenius), and include two independent exact computational suites
and finite certificates for two modulo-three identities.
None of the general theorems depends on a finite numerical fit.
\end{abstract}

\section{The problem and the main results}
\label{sec:intro}

An exponentially large polynomial can contain substantial finite-state
structure. The issue is to expose that structure without confusing
representations of an exponent with distinct exponents, or confusing the
number of representations modulo $m$ with the number of exponents having
that residue. Both distinctions matter in the problem considered here.

A second, quite different mechanism is also available for the modulus two:
a suitable periodic signing of the factors makes the product \emph{flat},
and parity then becomes a squared norm. This manuscript carries both
mechanisms, because they prove the same identity from disjoint hypotheses
and each reaches conclusions the other cannot.

\subsection{The documented target}

Stanley's paper \emph{Theorems and Conjectures on Some Rational Generating
Functions} asks whether coefficient-residue counts for generalized Fibonacci
products always have rational generating functions. It also proposes a
particularly small formula for the odd-coefficient count
\cite[Conjectures 6.2 and 6.3, pp.~23--24]{Stanley}. Our numbering of those
conjectures refers specifically to arXiv:2101.02131v3. The article subsequently
appeared in the \emph{European Journal of Combinatorics} in 2024
\cite{StanleyJournal}.

The relevant background includes Stanley's factorization of equal-weight
pairs \cite[Lemma 5.2]{Stanley} and the symbolic dynamic programming methods
of Ekhad and Zeilberger \cite{EZ}. The finite-state argument below instead
organizes one canonical representative per exponent and counts its fiber.
We prove the required canonical-pair factorization directly, so that central
argument has no dependence on an omitted proof from the literature.

For the second proof there is also important prior work to acknowledge.
Zhao proved flatness of finite signed Fibonacci products when $k=2$
\cite{Zhao}. Diao introduced the quasifibonacci seed conditions used in
Section~\ref{sec:signed} and proved flatness of the infinite all-minus
product for even recurrence order \cite[Definition~1.1 and
Theorem~1.1]{Diao}. The argument here uses a varying, periodic signing, works
for both even and odd orders, concerns every finite product, and gives its
exact support count. These distinctions matter: simply replacing every plus
sign by a minus sign does \emph{not} work for odd $k$
(Section~\ref{sec:allminus}).

\label{sec:provenance}

This article is the merger of two previously separate research packages that
independently attacked the same conjecture page.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.6em]

\subsection{Definitions and conventions}

Fix an integer $k\ge2$. Define
\begin{equation}
 F_1^{(k)}=\cdots=F_k^{(k)}=1,
 \qquad F_{i+1}^{(k)}=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}F_{i-j}^{(k)}\quad(i\ge k).
 \label{eq:F}
\end{equation}
Set $w_i=F_{i+k}^{(k)}$ for $i\ge0$. Thus the first weights are
\[
\begin{array}{c|rrrrrrrr}
k=2&1&2&3&5&8&13&21&34\\
k=3&1&3&5&9&17&31&57&105\\
k=4&1&4&7&13&25&49&94&181.
\end{array}
\]
In particular, the $k=3$ initialization is \emph{not} the alternative
$1,2,4,7,\ldots$ sometimes used for tribonacci numeration.

Write
\begin{equation}
 P_n(x)=\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}(1+x^{w_i})
       =\sum_{j=0}^{D_n}c_n(j)x^j,
 \qquad D_n=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}w_i,
 \label{eq:product}
\end{equation}
with $P_0=1$ and $D_0=0$. All the $c_n(j)$ are nonnegative integers.
For $m\ge2$ and $0\le a<m$, distinguish
\begin{align}
 h_{m,a}^{(k)}(n)
   &=\#\{j:0\le j\le D_n,\ c_n(j)>0,\ c_n(j)\equiv a\pmod m\},
   \label{eq:hpositive}\\
 \mathfrak h_{m,a}^{(k)}(n)
   &=\#\{j:0\le j\le D_n,\ c_n(j)\equiv a\pmod m\}.
   \label{eq:hall}
\end{align}
Their ordinary generating functions are denoted $H_{m,a}^{(k)}(z)$ and
$\cH_{m,a}^{(k)}(z)$, respectively. The first convention counts nonzero
terms; the second counts every coefficient position through the degree.
They differ only at residue zero. Proving both avoids any dependence on
how the word ``coefficients'' is interpreted. Coefficients at exponents
beyond the degree are never counted. This is a finite-product problem:
$n$ is the number of factors, not an upper bound on the exponent being
counted.

We abbreviate the odd count by
\begin{equation}
 a_k(n)=h_{2,1}^{(k)}(n)=\mathfrak h_{2,1}^{(k)}(n),
 \qquad a_k(0)=1,
 \label{eq:ak}
\end{equation}
the two conventions agreeing at residue one because a missing position has
coefficient zero, which is even.

For a word $u=u_0\cdots u_{n-1}\in\{0,1\}^n$, define
\begin{equation}
 \val(u)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}u_iw_i.
 \label{eq:value}
\end{equation}
Throughout the article, words are written and read in \emph{increasing
weight order}: the leftmost digit has weight $w_0$. Expansion of
\eqref{eq:product} shows that $c_n(j)$ is exactly the number of length-$n$
words with value $j$.

\subsection{The main results}

\begin{theorem}[Residue rationality; Conjecture 6.2]
\label{thm:mainrat}
For all $k\ge2$, $m\ge2$, and $0\le a<m$, both
$H_{m,a}^{(k)}(z)$ and $\cH_{m,a}^{(k)}(z)$ belong to $\Q(z)$.
The nonzero-term function has an explicitly constructible integer
linear representation with at most $(k+1)m^{k+1}$ states.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[The odd-coefficient formula; Conjecture 6.3]
\label{thm:mainodd}
For every $k\ge2$,
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\displaystyle
 H_{2,1}^{(k)}(z)=\cH_{2,1}^{(k)}(z)=\sum_{n\ge0}a_k(n)z^n
 =\frac{1+2z^k}{1-2z+2z^k-2z^{k+1}}.}
 \label{eq:mainodd}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Prescribed exact multiplicity]
\label{thm:mainexact}
Fix $k\ge2$ and $q\ge1$. Then
\[
 \sum_{n\ge0}\#\{j:c_n(j)=q\}\,z^n
\]
is rational. A finite-state construction uses at most
$(k+1)(q+2)^{k+1}$ states.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Seed independence and signed flatness]
\label{thm:seedmain}
Let $w_0,w_1,\ldots$ be any \emph{quasifibonacci sequence of order $k$}
in the sense of Definition~\ref{def:weights}: positive integers with
superincreasing seeds $w_i>\sum_{j<i}w_j$ for $0\le i\le k-1$, continued by
the $k$-term recurrence. Let $\eps_0,\eps_1,\ldots$ be any coherent signing
(Definition~\ref{def:sign}). Then every coefficient of the signed product
$\prod_{i<n}(1+\eps_ix^{w_i})$ lies in $\{-1,0,1\}$; the parity of
$\coef{m}{\prod_{i<n}(1+x^{w_i})}$ is the square of the corresponding signed
coefficient; and the number of odd coefficients is independent of the seeds
and of the signing and again equals $a_k(n)$ in \eqref{eq:mainodd}.
\end{theorem}

Theorems~\ref{thm:mainrat}--\ref{thm:mainexact} are proved by the
finite-state route of Sections~\ref{sec:normal}--\ref{sec:eliminate}.
Theorem~\ref{thm:seedmain} is proved by the signed route of
Section~\ref{sec:signed}, which \emph{re-proves} Theorem~\ref{thm:mainodd}
as the special case of Stanley's seeds (Lemma~\ref{lem:originalseeds}).
The two proofs of \eqref{eq:mainodd} are logically independent; see
Section~\ref{sec:twice}.

\subsection{What this article deliberately keeps twice}
\label{sec:twice}

Merging two disjoint solutions of one problem is only worthwhile if the
duplication is preserved where it carries information. The following
thirteen items are kept in duplicate on purpose. Each is flagged in the
text with the marker \textbf{[Kept twice: $\langle$item$\rangle$]}.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.9em,label=\textbf{D\arabic*.}]

\section{Unique normal forms for represented exponents}
\label{sec:normal}

The weight sequence satisfies
\begin{equation}
 w_0=1,\qquad
 w_i=(k-1)2^{i-1}+1\ (1\le i<k),\qquad
 w_i=\sum_{j=1}^k w_{i-j}\ (i\ge k).
 \label{eq:weights}
\end{equation}
The middle identity follows by induction from \eqref{eq:F}; it records the
exceptional initial segment that must not be replaced by a generic
$k$-bonacci initialization. Section~\ref{sec:signed} works with a much larger
class of initial segments, but the normal form of the present section is
tied to \eqref{eq:weights}.

\begin{definition}
A binary word is \emph{canonical} if it avoids the consecutive substring
$1^k0$. Let $\cL_k$ be the language of canonical words, including the empty
word. A normalization step is the replacement
\begin{equation}
 1^k0\longrightarrow 0^k1
 \label{eq:rewrite}
\end{equation}
inside a fixed-length word.
\end{definition}

Every normalization step preserves value, by the recurrence for $w_i$.
It strictly increases the ordinary binary integer $\sum_i u_i2^i$:
a step starting at position $r$ increases that integer by $2^r$.
There are only finitely many words of a fixed length. Therefore every
sequence of normalization steps terminates, and its terminal word is
canonical. Uniqueness requires a separate argument.

\begin{lemma}[A lower-weight bound]
\label{lem:lower}
If a word $u$ of length $i$ has no $k$ consecutive ones, then
$\val(u)<w_i$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For $i=0$ the assertion is $0<1$. For $1\le i<k$, the sum of all the lower
weights is less than $w_i$; indeed \eqref{eq:weights} gives
\begin{equation}
 w_i-\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}w_j=k-i>0.
 \label{eq:superincreasing}
\end{equation}
Proceed by induction for $i\ge k$. Let $r$ be the run of ones at the
high-weight end of $u$. Then $0\le r\le k-1$. The next digit is zero,
and the remaining word has length $i-r-1$. The induction hypothesis gives
\[
 \val(u)<w_{i-1}+\cdots+w_{i-r}+w_{i-r-1}\le w_i.
\]
Here the first sum is empty if $r=0$; the final inequality holds because
these are at most the $k$ most recent positive weights in the recurrence.
\end{proof}

Equation~\eqref{eq:superincreasing} is exactly the statement that Stanley's
own seeds are superincreasing; it is reused in
Lemma~\ref{lem:originalseeds} to make Theorem~\ref{thm:seedmain} imply
Theorem~\ref{thm:mainodd}.

\begin{lemma}[Canonical injectivity]
\label{lem:injective}
Two distinct canonical words of the same length have distinct values.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
First observe that every portion of a canonical word strictly below a
zero digit contains no $k$ consecutive ones. Otherwise a run of at least
$k$ ones would eventually meet a zero, producing the forbidden pattern.

Now take two distinct canonical words and let $i$ be their highest
position of disagreement. Suppose the first has a one there and the
second a zero. Their higher contributions agree. By the observation and
Lemma~\ref{lem:lower}, the entire lower contribution of the second word is
less than $w_i$. The first word contributes $w_i$ at position $i$ and a
nonnegative amount below it. Its value is therefore strictly larger.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[A regular cross-section]
\label{prop:normal}
Every fiber of $\val:\{0,1\}^n\to\N$ contains exactly one canonical word.
Thus normalization terminates at the same word regardless of the order
of the steps. Moreover,
\begin{equation}
 \cL_k=(0\mid10\mid\cdots\mid1^{k-1}0)^*1^*.
 \label{eq:language}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Termination supplies a canonical word in every fiber, and
Lemma~\ref{lem:injective} gives uniqueness. In a canonical word, the run
of ones before each zero has length less than $k$; the final run of ones
is unrestricted. Cutting immediately after each zero gives exactly
\eqref{eq:language}, with unique cuts.
\end{proof}

This avoids a potentially invalid shortcut: termination by itself does
not establish uniqueness of a rewriting system. Here uniqueness is a
consequence of an explicit numerical separation argument.

\begin{corollary}[Support size]
\label{cor:support}
Let $s_k(n)=\#\{j:c_n(j)>0\}$. Then
\begin{equation}
 S_k(z):=\sum_{n\ge0}s_k(n)z^n
 =\frac{1}{(1-z)(1-z-z^2-\cdots-z^k)}
 =\frac1{1-2z+z^{k+1}}.
 \label{eq:support}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Canonical words are in bijection with represented exponents. In
\eqref{eq:language}, a block ending in zero has generating function
$z+\cdots+z^k$, and the final run of ones has generating function
$1/(1-z)$. Unique decomposition gives the first fraction; multiplication
of its denominator gives the second.
\end{proof}

The support generating function \eqref{eq:support} is available only on this
route; the signed argument of Section~\ref{sec:signed} has no access to it.
That is precisely why the two sparsity statements of
Section~\ref{sec:asymptotic} are different theorems.

\section{The structure of a canonical representation fiber}
\label{sec:fibers}

Counting canonical words counts exponents, but does not yet reveal their
coefficients. We need to count \emph{all} words with the value of a given
canonical word. The next factorization turns that task into a small
weighted automaton.

For $j\ge0$ and $c_1,\ldots,c_j\in\{0,1\}$, call
\begin{equation}
 \begin{pmatrix}
 0^k(c_1 0^{k-1})\cdots(c_j0^{k-1})1\\
 1^k(c_1 1^{k-1})\cdots(c_j1^{k-1})0
 \end{pmatrix}
 \label{eq:block}
\end{equation}
a \emph{carry block}. Parentheses indicate concatenation blocks, not
multiplication. When $j=0$, this is simply the pair $(0^k1,1^k0)$.

\begin{theorem}[Canonical-fiber factorization]
\label{thm:factor}
Let $a$ be canonical and let $b$ be any binary word of the same length.
Then $\val(a)=\val(b)$ if and only if the two-row word $(a,b)$ is a
concatenation of diagonal columns $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$ and carry blocks
\eqref{eq:block}. Such a factorization, when it exists, is unique.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Necessity.} Normalize $b$ to $a$, using
Proposition~\ref{prop:normal}. For each integer $i$, let $t_i$ be the
number of normalization steps that start at position $i$. Set $t_i=0$
outside $0\le i\le n-k-1$. Summing the changes at each coordinate gives
\begin{equation}
 a_i=b_i-\sum_{h=0}^{k-1}t_{i-h}+t_{i-k}
 \qquad(0\le i<n).
 \label{eq:rewritecounts}
\end{equation}
All $t_i$ are nonnegative integers. We use only this equation and the
binary bounds on $a_i,b_i$.

If every $t_i$ is zero, the rows agree. Otherwise let $r$ be the least
index with $t_r>0$. At position $r$, equation~\eqref{eq:rewritecounts}
reduces to $a_r=b_r-t_r$. Hence
\[
 t_r=1,\qquad (a_r,b_r)=(0,1).
\]
At each of the next $k-1$ positions, the negative sum already contains
$t_r=1$, while the positive term is zero. Inductively the binary bounds
force
\begin{equation}
 t_{r+1}=\cdots=t_{r+k-1}=0,
 \qquad
 (a_i,b_i)=(0,1)\quad(r<i<r+k).
 \label{eq:forcedrun}
\end{equation}

At the separator $s=r+k$, the equation becomes
\begin{equation}
 a_s=b_s+1-t_s.
 \label{eq:separator}
\end{equation}
We claim $t_s\le1$. If $s$ cannot be the start of a valid step, then
$t_s=0$ by definition. Otherwise position $s+1$ exists, and
$t_{s+1-k}=t_{r+1}=0$ because $k\ge2$. The equation at $s+1$ has a
negative sum containing $t_s$ and no positive contribution. Binary bounds
therefore imply $t_s\le1$.

If $t_s=0$, equation~\eqref{eq:separator} forces
$(a_s,b_s)=(1,0)$, closing a carry block. If $t_s=1$, it forces
$a_s=b_s=c_1$, and the next $k-1$ positions satisfy the same forced pattern
as \eqref{eq:forcedrun}. Repeating the argument at the next separator
produces a chain of starts
\[
 r,\ r+k,\ r+2k,\ \ldots,\ r+jk
\]
with count one at each start and zero at the intervening positions.
It must eventually close, because only finitely many starts are possible.
The resulting two-row segment is exactly \eqref{eq:block}.

After its closing position, this chain makes no further contribution to
\eqref{eq:rewritecounts}: its final start is $k$ positions before the
closure. We can therefore take the next nonzero $t_i$, if any, and repeat
the same argument. Positions between these disjoint blocks are diagonal.
This proves necessity without counting normalization histories.

\emph{Sufficiency.} A diagonal column changes no value. For a carry block
starting at $r$, place $t_i=1$ at $r,r+k,\ldots,r+jk$ and zero elsewhere.
The coordinate differences of the two rows are exactly the changes in
\eqref{eq:rewritecounts}. Each such formal change has weighted sum
\[
 w_{i+k}-(w_i+\cdots+w_{i+k-1})=0.
\]
Thus every carry block preserves value at its actual position, and so
does a concatenation of blocks and diagonal columns.

\emph{Uniqueness.} Parse from the low-weight end. If the current column is
diagonal, it cannot start a carry block, so it is forced to be a diagonal
factor. Otherwise a block starts with $k$ columns $(0,1)$. At each ensuing
separator, $(1,0)$ must close the block, while an equal pair $(c,c)$ must
continue it and is followed by $k-1$ columns $(0,1)$. Every choice is
therefore determined by the two-row word itself.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The block shapes agree with the appropriate orientation of Stanley's
pair generators \cite[Lemma 5.2]{Stanley}. The proof above is specialized
to a canonical upper row and establishes exactly the version needed here.
In particular, it does not assume that a numerical equality of weighted
sums automatically has a unique local carry history.
\end{remark}

\section{An exact counter with only \texorpdfstring{$k+1$}{k+1} coordinates}
\label{sec:counter}

For a fixed canonical upper word, read its digits one at a time. There is
a balanced state, where a completed factorization can end, and excursion
states $1,\ldots,k$. In an excursion, states $1,\ldots,k-1$ record progress
through a required run of $(0,1)$ columns, and state $k$ waits at a
separator. A transition selects the bottom digit as well as the next
state.

Let $p$ count paths in the balanced state, and let $b_i$ count paths in
excursion state $i$. The initial counter is
\begin{equation}
 v_\eps=(p,b_1,\ldots,b_k)=(1,0,\ldots,0).
 \label{eq:initial}
\end{equation}
Reading an upper zero or one acts by the following nonnegative integer
linear maps:
\begin{align}
 Z(p,b_1,\ldots,b_k)&=(p,\ p+b_k,\ b_1,\ldots,b_{k-1}),\label{eq:Z}\\
 O(p,b_1,\ldots,b_k)&=(p+b_k,\ b_k,\ 0,\ldots,0).\label{eq:O}
\end{align}

To verify the first equation, an upper zero permits either a diagonal
$(0,0)$ in the balanced state or a new excursion beginning with $(0,1)$.
An existing state $i<k$ advances to $i+1$ on $(0,1)$. At state $k$, an
upper zero can only be an equal separator $(0,0)$, returning to state 1.
For an upper one, the balanced state permits the diagonal $(1,1)$.
State $k$ either closes the excursion on $(1,0)$ or continues it on
$(1,1)$; no other excursion state permits that upper digit. These are
precisely \eqref{eq:Z}--\eqref{eq:O}.

\begin{proposition}[The output is the coefficient]
\label{prop:counter}
For every canonical word $a\in\{0,1\}^n$, the final first coordinate $p$
obtained from \eqref{eq:initial}--\eqref{eq:O} is $c_n(\val(a))$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
A path that ends balanced specifies a complete factorization of a pair
$(a,b)$ of the form in Theorem~\ref{thm:factor}. That theorem identifies
such pairs with all words $b$ of the same value. Different paths cannot
count the same bottom word twice, because the pair factorization is
unique. Conversely every such bottom word has its factorization and
therefore its path. The first coordinate counts exactly these paths.
\end{proof}

\noindent\textbf{[Kept twice: structural witness.]}
Proposition~\ref{prop:counter} produces the \emph{exact nonnegative integer}
$c_n(j)$. Theorem~\ref{thm:flat} below produces instead a \emph{signed}
value in $\{-1,0,1\}$ whose square is the parity of $c_n(j)$. Neither
statement implies the other: the counter says nothing about signs, and
flatness says nothing about the size of $c_n(j)$. Their consistency is
asserted and checked in Section~\ref{sec:crosscheck}.

\begin{example}
For $k=2$ and $n=5$, the weights are $1,2,3,5,8$. The canonical word
$\bits{00001}$ represents 8. The counters are
\[
 (1,0,0)\xrightarrow{0}(1,1,0)\xrightarrow{0}(1,1,1)
 \xrightarrow{0}(1,2,1)\xrightarrow{0}(1,2,2)
 \xrightarrow{1}(3,2,0).
\]
Thus $[x^8]P_5(x)=3$. The three representations, in the same low-to-high
convention, are $\bits{00001}$, $\bits{00110}$, and $\bits{11010}$.
\end{example}

The coefficient itself can be large; nevertheless its residue is computed
by the same $k+1$ coordinates reduced modulo $m$. No division by a residue,
field structure, or primality assumption is needed.

\section{Rationality for every residue, including the zero correction}
\label{sec:rational}

\subsection{The finite machine}

Fix $m\ge2$. Keep the counter $v$ in $(\Z/m\Z)^{k+1}$ and augment it by a
run state $r\in\{0,1,\ldots,k\}$. The latter is the current trailing run
of upper ones, capped at $k$. The initial state is $(v_\eps,0)$.
On an upper digit, use
\begin{equation}
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
\text{input}&\text{counter update}&\text{run update}\\ \hline
0&Z(v)\pmod m&r\mapsto0\quad\text{provided }r<k,\\
1&O(v)\pmod m&r\mapsto\min(k,r+1).
\end{array}
\label{eq:modmachine}
\end{equation}
The zero transition from $r=k$ is rejected. Thus legal paths read exactly
the canonical words. There are at most $(k+1)m^{k+1}$ nonrejecting states;
only states reachable from the initial state need be retained.

Let $A$ be the integer adjacency matrix of this finite labeled graph:
$A_{uv}$ is the number of input labels taking state $u$ to state $v$.
Even though the counters live modulo $m$, the entries and matrix arithmetic
of $A$ live in $\Z$. Let $\lambda$ select the initial state, and let $f_a$
be the column vector that is one exactly when the first counter is $a$.
Proposition~\ref{prop:counter} gives
\begin{equation}
 h_{m,a}^{(k)}(n)=\lambda A^nf_a,
 \qquad
 H_{m,a}^{(k)}(z)=\lambda(I-zA)^{-1}f_a.
 \label{eq:resolvent}
\end{equation}
Since $\det(I-zA)$ has constant term one, the inverse exists formally and
has rational entries. This proves residue rationality for nonzero terms.
In particular, this construction counts each exponent once, not once for
each of its representations.

\subsection{Internal zero coefficients}

For $k\ge3$, some exponents between zero and the degree are not represented.
For instance, at $k=3,n=2$ the polynomial is
$1+x+x^3+x^4$ and has a zero coefficient at exponent 2. These missing
positions belong to residue zero under the full-position convention.

Put
\begin{equation}
 Q_k(z)=1-z-z^2-\cdots-z^k,
 \qquad B_k(z)=\sum_{j=2}^{k-1}(k-j)z^j,
 \label{eq:QB}
\end{equation}
where $B_2=0$.

\begin{proposition}[An explicit hole generating function]
\label{prop:holes}
The number of zero positions in $0\le j\le D_n$ has generating function
\begin{equation}
 J_k(z):=\sum_{n\ge0}\bigl(D_n+1-s_k(n)\bigr)z^n
 =B_k(z)S_k(z).
 \label{eq:holes}
\end{equation}
Consequently
\begin{equation}
 \cH_{m,a}^{(k)}(z)=H_{m,a}^{(k)}(z)
       +\one_{a=0}\frac{B_k(z)}{1-2z+z^{k+1}}.
 \label{eq:zerocorrection}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The initial values and recurrence in \eqref{eq:weights} give
\[
 W_k(z):=\sum_{i\ge0}w_i z^i
 =\frac{1+\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}(k-j)z^j}{Q_k(z)}.
\]
Since $D_n$ is the sum of the first $n$ weights,
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{n\ge0}(D_n+1)z^n
 =\frac{1+zW_k(z)}{1-z}
 =\frac{1+B_k(z)}{(1-z)Q_k(z)}
 =(1+B_k(z))S_k(z).
 \label{eq:degreegf}
\end{equation}
Subtract \eqref{eq:support}. A missing position contributes only to
residue zero, which gives \eqref{eq:zerocorrection}.
\end{proof}

This finishes the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:mainrat}. The case $m=1$, if
included, is immediate from $S_k$ for nonzero terms and $(1+B_k)S_k$ for
all positions. For $k=2$ there are no holes, and the two conventions agree
for every residue. At $k=3$ the hole function is $z^2S_3(z)$; at $k=4$ it
is $(2z^2+z^3)S_4(z)$.

\section{Exact multiplicities and a general finite-state principle}
\label{sec:exact}

To test a coefficient against a fixed positive integer $q$, it is enough
to distinguish $0,1,\ldots,q$ and ``at least $q+1$.'' This works because
all additions in the counter are nonnegative.

Set $M=q+1$ and define $\sigma_M(t)=\min(M,t)$. For nonnegative integers,
\begin{equation}
 \sigma_M(u+v)=\sigma_M(\sigma_M(u)+\sigma_M(v)).
 \label{eq:saturate}
\end{equation}
Apply $\sigma_M$ coordinatewise after every counter update. Induction using
\eqref{eq:saturate} shows that the resulting counter is the coordinatewise
saturation of the exact counter, not an approximation to it. Thus its
first coordinate equals $q$ exactly when the true coefficient equals $q$.

There are $q+2$ possible values per coordinate, so adjoining the canonical
run state gives at most $(k+1)(q+2)^{k+1}$ states. Mark first coordinate
$q$ as accepting and apply the resolvent construction
\eqref{eq:resolvent}. This proves Theorem~\ref{thm:mainexact}. Fixed finite
sets of positive multiplicities, and fixed positive thresholds, can be
handled by changing the final-state indicator.

\begin{proposition}[Finite-output principle]
\label{prop:principle}
Suppose a regular language contains exactly one representative of each
object being counted. Suppose further that a nonnegative integer weight
of a representative is read from a fixed coordinate of a finite-dimensional
counter updated by fixed nonnegative integer linear maps on each input letter. Then the
length generating function for objects whose weight has a fixed residue
modulo $m$ is rational. The same is true for objects with any fixed exact
weight, or with weight in any fixed finite set.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Take the product of an automaton for the regular language with the finite
set of counter values modulo $m$. For exact weights, saturate above the
largest requested weight. Saturation commutes with addition and with
multiplication by a nonnegative integer, so it commutes with all the
counter updates. In both cases the desired set is recognized by a finite
machine and its length counts have an integer adjacency representation.
\end{proof}

The nonnegativity hypothesis is important for saturation. If a computation
can later subtract large quantities, identifying all large values would
lose information. Modulo-$m$ reduction has no analogous problem for
integer linear maps, but the present exact-multiplicity conclusion does.

The significant problem-specific work is therefore not the final
finite-state rationality argument. It is the construction of a regular
cross-section and a correct finite-dimensional counter for the fibers.
For the present weights these are supplied by
Proposition~\ref{prop:normal} and Theorem~\ref{thm:factor}.

\section{A uniform parity automaton}
\label{sec:parity}

The general residue construction is enough to prove rationality. To obtain
a simple formula for every $k$, we now exploit the special structure
modulo two. Let $e_j$ be the $j$th unit vector in $(\Z/2\Z)^k$.
We describe states by $(p;b;r)$, with $b=(b_1,\ldots,b_k)$ and $r$ the
canonical run state.

\emph{Scope warning.} From here through Section~\ref{sec:eliminate}, the
letters $A_j,B_j,C_j,D,E_j,F,X$ denote automaton states and their
generating series, not the polynomials $B_k(z)$ and $V_k(z)$, the degree
$D_n$, or the constant $C_k$.

The following state families suffice:
\begin{align*}
 A_j&=(1;e_1+\cdots+e_j;0) &&(0\le j\le k),\\
 B_j&=(1;e_j+\cdots+e_k;0) &&(2\le j\le k),\\
 C_j&=(1;0;j) &&(1\le j\le k),\\
 D&=(0;e_1;1),\\
 E_j&=(0;e_j;0) &&(2\le j\le k),\\
 F&=(1;e_1;1).
\end{align*}
For $A_0$ the excursion vector is zero. We merge $(0;e_1;0)$ with $D$:
these two states have first coordinate zero and identical relevant
successors, namely $E_2$ on zero and the zero counter on one. This uses
$k\ge2$. Any zero counter, and any already forbidden input, is merged
into a dead state $X$.

The initial state is $A_0$. Every $A_j,B_j,C_j$, and $F$ is accepting;
$D,E_j$, and $X$ are not. All transitions are specified in
Table~\ref{tab:parity}. There are
\[
 (k+1)+(k-1)+k+1+(k-1)+1+1=4k+2
\]
states, including $X$. No minimality claim is needed.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
State or range & Input 0 & Input 1\\
\midrule
$A_j$, $0\le j<k$ & $A_{j+1}$ & $C_1$\\
$A_k$ & $B_2$ & $D$\\
$B_j$, $2\le j<k$ & $B_{j+1}$ & $D$\\
$B_k$ & $A_0$ & $D$\\
$C_j$, $1\le j<k$ & $A_1$ & $C_{j+1}$\\
$C_k$ & $X$ & $C_k$\\
$D$ & $E_2$ & $X$\\
$E_j$, $2\le j<k$ & $E_{j+1}$ & $X$\\

$X$ & $X$ & $X$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{The parity automaton, valid uniformly for $k\ge2$. Empty index
ranges at $k=2$ are simply omitted; $A_2,C_2,E_2,B_2$ still exist.}
\label{tab:parity}
\end{table}

\begin{proposition}[Correctness of the uniform automaton]
\label{prop:parity}
The accepted length-$n$ words of Table~\ref{tab:parity} are precisely the
canonical words whose represented exponent has an odd coefficient in
$P_n(x)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The stated tuples include the initial counter and are closed under the
updates \eqref{eq:Z}--\eqref{eq:O} modulo two, with the canonical run rule.
For example, if $j<k$, a zero shifts the prefix $e_1+\cdots+e_j$ right and
introduces $e_1$, giving $A_{j+1}$. From $A_k$, the new first excursion
coordinate is $1+1=0$ modulo two, giving $B_2$. Suffixes in the $B$ family
then shift until they disappear and return to $A_0$.

On a one, every $A_j$ with $j<k$ loses its excursion coordinates and goes
to $C_1$. From $A_k$ or a $B_j$, the last excursion coordinate is one, so
$p$ changes from one to zero and the new excursion vector is $e_1$, giving
$D$. From $E_k$, a one changes $p$ from zero to one and gives $F$.
The remaining transitions follow by shifting a single $e_j$, or by
updating the run length of a zero excursion vector. The only forbidden
zero in these nondead families is the transition from $C_k$.

The two tuples merged into $D$ have the same acceptance and the same
successor classes, as noted above. A zero vector stays zero under both
linear maps, so no continuation of it can be accepted. Hence merging it
with $X$ is legitimate even when its original run state differs.
Induction on the input length proves that the table computes the same
first-coordinate parity and canonicality as the general machine.
Proposition~\ref{prop:counter} identifies that parity with the coefficient.
\end{proof}

\section{Symbolic elimination and the first proof of the odd formula}
\label{sec:eliminate}

For each state in Table~\ref{tab:parity}, use the same letter to denote the
generating function of accepted continuations from that state. In this
section, therefore, $A_j$ denotes a series rather than a tuple. An accepting
state has a constant contribution one for the empty continuation; a
nonaccepting state has constant contribution zero. The dead state has
series $X=0$. Write
\[
 H=A_0,\qquad u=z^k,\qquad R_k(z)=1+z+\cdots+z^{k-1}=\frac{1-u}{1-z}.
\]
All equations below are identities of formal power series.

\subsection{The run states and the single-excursion states}

The $C$ chain satisfies $C_k=1+zC_k$ and
$C_i=1+zA_1+zC_{i+1}$ for $i<k$. Back-substitution gives
\begin{equation}
 C_i=\frac1{1-z}
       +\frac{z(1-z^{k-i})}{1-z}A_1
 \qquad(1\le i\le k).
 \label{eq:Cseries}
\end{equation}
In particular,
\begin{equation}
 C_2-C_1=-z^{k-1}A_1.
 \label{eq:Cdiff}
\end{equation}
The transitions from $A_1$ and $F$ give
\[
 A_1=1+zA_2+zC_1,\qquad F=1+zA_2+zC_2.
\]
Subtracting and applying \eqref{eq:Cdiff},
\begin{equation}
 F=(1-u)A_1.
 \label{eq:Fseries}
\end{equation}
The $D,E$ states give $D=zE_2$, $E_i=zE_{i+1}$ for $2\le i<k$, and
$E_k=zD+zF$. Consequently
\[
 D=u(D+F).
\]
Since $1-u$ is invertible as a formal power series,
\begin{equation}
 D=uA_1.
 \label{eq:Dseries}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Closing the two long chains}

The chain beginning with $A_k$ and continuing through
$B_2,\ldots,B_k$ has $k$ accepting states, each with a one-transition to
$D$, and then returns to $H$ on zeros. Thus
\begin{equation}
 A_k=R_k(1+zD)+uH.
 \label{eq:Akseries}
\end{equation}
Similarly the $k$ states $A_0,\ldots,A_{k-1}$ give
\begin{equation}
 H=R_k(1+zC_1)+uA_k.
 \label{eq:Hchain}
\end{equation}
Combining \eqref{eq:Akseries} and \eqref{eq:Hchain}, then canceling
$1-u$, gives
\begin{equation}
 (1+u)(1-z)H=1+u+zC_1+zuD.
 \label{eq:combined}
\end{equation}

The first transition equation is $H=1+zA_1+zC_1$.
The formula for $C_1$ in \eqref{eq:Cseries} then gives a second convenient
identity:
\begin{equation}
 (1-z)H=1+z(1-u)A_1.
 \label{eq:second}
\end{equation}
Substitute $D=uA_1$ and $zC_1=H-1-zA_1$ into
\eqref{eq:combined}. Its right-hand side becomes
\[
 u+H-z(1-u^2)A_1.
\]
Now use \eqref{eq:second}, multiplied by $1+u$, to eliminate $A_1$.
The result is
\[
 \bigl(2(1+u)(1-z)-1\bigr)H=1+2u.
\]
Replacing $u$ by $z^k$ proves
\[
 H=\frac{1+2z^k}{1-2z+2z^k-2z^{k+1}}.
\]
Proposition~\ref{prop:parity} identifies $H$ with $H_{2,1}^{(k)}$ and
completes the \emph{first} proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:mainodd}.

Only factors with constant term one were canceled or inverted in this
argument. In particular, the proof does not require dividing by $z$, and
is valid at the formal-series level including the constant coefficient.
The number of states is linear in $k$, but elimination reduces the final
denominator degree to $k+1$.

\subsection{A recurrence and initial values}
\label{sec:recurrence}

The rational formula yields
\begin{equation}
 a_k(n)=2^n\quad(0\le n\le k),
 \qquad
 a_k(n)=2a_k(n-1)-2a_k(n-k)+2a_k(n-k-1)
 \quad(n\ge k+1).
 \label{eq:oddrec}
\end{equation}
The range $n\ge k+1$ matters: the numerator contains the additional term
$2z^k$. The first nontrivial collision occurs at length $k+1$ and gives
$a_k(k+1)=2^{k+1}-2$.

\section{A second, independent proof: signed cancellation}
\label{sec:signed}

\noindent\textbf{[Kept twice: Conjecture 6.3.]}
This section proves Theorem~\ref{thm:mainodd} again, and proves it in the
greater generality of Theorem~\ref{thm:seedmain}. It uses none of
Sections~\ref{sec:normal}--\ref{sec:eliminate}: no canonical word, no
carry block, no counter, no automaton, and no rational-function ansatz.
What it buys is stated in item D1 of Section~\ref{sec:twice}.

\subsection{Quasifibonacci weights and coherent signings}

\begin{definition}
\label{def:weights}
A \emph{quasifibonacci sequence of order $k$} is a sequence of positive
integers $w_0,w_1,\ldots$ satisfying
\begin{align}
 w_i&>D_i:=\sum_{r=0}^{i-1}w_r &&(0\le i\le k-1),\label{eq:seed}\\
 w_i&=\sum_{r=i-k}^{i-1}w_r &&(i\ge k).\label{eq:recw}
\end{align}
We put $D_0=0$ and $D_n=\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}w_r$.
The term agrees with Diao's definition \cite[Definition~1.1]{Diao}, with
``level'' replaced by ``order.''
\end{definition}

The seed condition is often called \emph{superincreasing}: each seed is
larger than all earlier seeds combined. For instance $(1,3,5)$ and
$(2,7,15)$ are valid order-three seed lists; $(1,2,3)$ is not.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:sign}
A sign sequence $\eps_0,\eps_1,\ldots\in\{-1,1\}$ is \emph{coherent} if
\begin{equation}
 \eps_i=-\prod_{r=i-k}^{i-1}\eps_r\qquad(i\ge k).
 \label{eq:signrec}
\end{equation}
Its first $k$ signs can be chosen arbitrarily. A convenient canonical choice
is
\begin{equation}
 \eps_i=\begin{cases}-1,&i\equiv k\ \ (\mathrm{mod}\ k+1),\\
 1,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}
 \label{eq:canonical}
\end{equation}
Define the signed product by
\[
 \Phi_n(x)=\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}(1+\eps_i x^{w_i})=\sum_{m\in\Z}\varphi_n(m)x^m .
\]
The coefficient function $\varphi_n(m)$ vanishes outside $0\le m\le D_n$.
When every $\eps_i=1$ this is the ordinary product $P_n$ of
\eqref{eq:product}.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Uniform parity and signed flatness]
\label{thm:flat}
For every sequence in Definition~\ref{def:weights}, every coherent signing,
and every $n\ge0$:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item every coefficient of $\Phi_n$ is $-1$, $0$, or $1$;
\item for each $m\in\Z$, the residue modulo two of $\coef{m}{P_n}$ is
$\varphi_n(m)^2$;
\item the number of odd coefficients is independent of the seeds and of the
initial signs, and satisfies
\begin{align}
 a_k(n)&=2^n &&(0\le n\le k),\label{eq:hinit}\\
 a_k(n)&=2a_k(n-1)-2a_k(n-k)+2a_k(n-k-1) &&(n\ge k+1),\label{eq:hrec}
\end{align}
so that $\sum_{n\ge0}a_k(n)z^n=(1+2z^k)/(1-2z+2z^k-2z^{k+1})$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

The proof occupies Sections~\ref{sec:sep}--\ref{sec:norm}. It has two
distinct parts: first flatness, then an exact norm recurrence. The latter
alone would not justify counting odd coefficients without the former.

\subsection{Arithmetic of the exponent sequence}
\label{sec:sep}

\begin{lemma}[Separation]
\label{lem:sep}
The weights are strictly increasing. For every $j\ge0$,
\begin{equation}
 w_{j+1}>D_j,\qquad 2w_j>D_j.
 \label{eq:sep}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The seeds are strictly increasing by \eqref{eq:seed}. At the first
recurrence step, $w_k=D_k>w_{k-1}$. Each subsequent weight is a sum of
$k\ge2$ positive weights including its immediate predecessor, so strict
increase persists.

For $0\le j\le k-2$, the first inequality in \eqref{eq:sep} is the seed
condition at index $j+1$. For $j=k-1$, we have $w_k=D_k>D_{k-1}$. If
$j\ge k$, the recurrence for $w_{j+1}$ includes the two preceding weights,
so induction gives
\[
 w_{j+1}\ge w_j+w_{j-1}>D_{j-1}+w_{j-1}=D_j ,
\]
where $w_j>D_{j-1}$ is the first inequality one index lower. This proves the
first inequality at all indices. For $j=0$ the second inequality is
immediate. For $j\ge1$, combine the first inequality one index lower with
strict increase:
\[
 D_j=D_{j-1}+w_{j-1}<w_j+w_j=2w_j .
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Exact overlap length]
\label{lem:overlap-length}
For $n\ge k+1$, put $j=n-k-1$. Then
\begin{equation}
 D_{n-1}=w_{n-1}+D_j.
 \label{eq:overlaplength}
\end{equation}
Thus the coefficient ranges of $\Phi_{n-1}(x)$ and
$x^{w_{n-1}}\Phi_{n-1}(x)$ overlap exactly in the interval
$[w_{n-1},w_{n-1}+D_j]$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Split $D_{n-1}=w_0+\cdots+w_{n-2}$ after its first $j$ summands. The
remaining $k$ summands are $w_{n-k-1},\ldots,w_{n-2}$, whose sum is
$w_{n-1}$ by \eqref{eq:recw}. The interval assertion follows because
$\deg\Phi_{n-1}=D_{n-1}$ and both endpoint coefficients are nonzero.
Here ``coefficient range'' means the containing interval, not necessarily a
support without internal gaps.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Stanley's seeds are admissible]
\label{lem:originalseeds}
The weights $w_i=F^{(k)}_{i+k}$ of \eqref{eq:weights} form a
quasifibonacci sequence of order $k$: $w_0=1$,
$w_i=(k-1)2^{i-1}+1$ for $1\le i\le k-1$, and
$w_i-D_i=k-i>0$ for $0\le i\le k-1$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
This is \eqref{eq:weights} together with \eqref{eq:superincreasing}.
Directly: the first new term after the $k$ initial ones is $k$, and each
subsequent initial step replaces one of those ones by the newest term, so
the new value is twice its predecessor minus one. Summing
$w_i=(k-1)2^{i-1}+1$ over $1\le r<i$ and adding $w_0=1$ gives
$D_i=(k-1)(2^{i-1}-1)+i$, whence $w_i-D_i=k-i$. The later weights satisfy
\eqref{eq:recw} by definition.
\end{proof}

Consequently Theorem~\ref{thm:flat} applied to these weights re-proves
Theorem~\ref{thm:mainodd}.

\subsection{Coherent signs and reflection}

\begin{lemma}[Periodic description of all coherent signings]
\label{lem:period}
A signing is coherent if and only if it is periodic with period dividing
$k+1$ and every consecutive block of length $k+1$ has product $-1$.
There are exactly $2^k$ such signings.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Equation \eqref{eq:signrec} says that each length-$(k+1)$ window has product
$-1$. Dividing the products for two consecutive windows cancels the $k$
common signs and gives $\eps_{r+k+1}=\eps_r$. Conversely, a periodic sign
block with product $-1$ has that product in every window, hence satisfies
\eqref{eq:signrec}. Its first $k$ signs are free and its final sign is their
negative product. The canonical choice \eqref{eq:canonical} is the block of
$k$ plus signs followed by one minus sign.
\end{proof}

Put $\Pi_n=\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}\eps_i$, including $\Pi_0=1$.

\begin{lemma}[Reflection]
\label{lem:reflection}
For every integer $m$,
\begin{equation}
 \varphi_n(D_n-m)=\Pi_n\,\varphi_n(m).
 \label{eq:reflection}
\end{equation}
If $n\ge k+1$ and $j=n-k-1$, then
\begin{equation}
 \Pi_{n-1}\Pi_j=-\eps_{n-1}.
 \label{eq:Esign}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For an individual factor,
$x^{w_i}(1+\eps_i x^{-w_i})=\eps_i(1+\eps_i x^{w_i})$.
Multiplication gives $x^{D_n}\Phi_n(x^{-1})=\Pi_n\Phi_n(x)$ and proves
\eqref{eq:reflection}, including the zero coefficients outside the range.
Since $\Pi_j^2=1$, the product $\Pi_{n-1}\Pi_j$ consists precisely of the
$k$ signs of indices $j=n-k-1$ through $n-2$. By \eqref{eq:signrec} its
value is $-\eps_{n-1}$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The pointwise cancellation identity}
\label{sec:pointwise}

This subsection contains the decisive calculation. It is an identity over
the integers, before reducing anything modulo two.

\begin{proposition}[Two disjoint tails]
\label{prop:pointwise}
Let $n\ge k+1$, $j=n-k-1$, and $v=w_j$. For $0\le u\le D_j$,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\displaystyle
 \varphi_n(w_{n-1}+u)=\eps_{n-1}\eps_j
       \bigl(\varphi_j(u-v)-\varphi_j(u+v)\bigr).}
 \label{eq:pointwise}
\end{equation}
The two terms on the right cannot both be nonzero.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:sep}, $w_{j+1}>D_j$, so the factors of index $j+1$ or
higher have all their nonconstant terms in degrees larger than $D_j$.
Therefore, on the interval $0\le u\le D_j$,
\begin{equation}
 \varphi_{n-1}(u)=\varphi_j(u)+\eps_j\varphi_j(u-v).
 \label{eq:low}
\end{equation}
There is no convergence or truncation approximation in this equation:
later factors simply cannot contribute to such a low exponent.

Reflect the high end of $\Phi_{n-1}$ using
$D_{n-1}=w_{n-1}+D_j$ from Lemma~\ref{lem:overlap-length} and
\eqref{eq:low}. We obtain
\begin{align}
 \varphi_{n-1}(w_{n-1}+u)
 &=\Pi_{n-1}\varphi_{n-1}(D_{n-1}-w_{n-1}-u)\notag\\
 &=\Pi_{n-1}\varphi_{n-1}(D_j-u)\notag\\
 &=\Pi_{n-1}\bigl(\varphi_j(D_j-u)+\eps_j\varphi_j(D_j-u-v)\bigr)\notag\\
 &=\Pi_{n-1}\Pi_j\bigl(\varphi_j(u)+\eps_j\varphi_j(u+v)\bigr)\notag\\
 &=-\eps_{n-1}\bigl(\varphi_j(u)+\eps_j\varphi_j(u+v)\bigr).
 \label{eq:high}
\end{align}
Now use $\Phi_n=\Phi_{n-1}+\eps_{n-1}x^{w_{n-1}}\Phi_{n-1}$ and add
\eqref{eq:high} to $\eps_{n-1}$ times \eqref{eq:low}. The two copies of
$\varphi_j(u)$ cancel exactly, leaving \eqref{eq:pointwise}.

For the first tail to be nonzero, $u\ge v$ is necessary. For the second,
$u\le D_j-v$ is necessary. These conditions cannot hold simultaneously,
because $2v=2w_j>D_j$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:sep}. More explicitly, the possible
tail intervals are
\[
 [0,D_j-v]\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad[v,D_j],
\]
with an interval understood to be empty if its endpoints are reversed.
They are disjoint.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
At the very first overlap, $n=k+1$ and $j=0$. Both tails are empty:
$\varphi_0(-w_0)=\varphi_0(w_0)=0$. Thus the only overlapping coefficient,
at exponent $w_k=D_k$, cancels. This is an important endpoint check on the
indexing.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Flatness and the parity lift}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:flat}(i)--(ii)]
For $0\le n\le k$, the seed condition ensures that two distinct subsets of
$\{w_0,\ldots,w_{n-1}\}$ cannot have the same sum. Indeed, at their largest
index of disagreement, the larger seed exceeds the sum of all earlier
seeds. Every nonzero coefficient of $\Phi_n$ is therefore a single product
of signs and is $1$ or $-1$.

Proceed by strong induction on $n$. For $n\ge k+1$, outside
$[w_{n-1},D_{n-1}]$ at most one of the two terms $\Phi_{n-1}$ and
$\eps_{n-1}x^{w_{n-1}}\Phi_{n-1}$ contributes. The coefficient there is zero
or, up to sign, an earlier coefficient. Inside the overlap,
Proposition~\ref{prop:pointwise} expresses it as the signed difference of
two coefficients of $\Phi_j$, where $j<n$, with at most one nonzero term.
The induction hypothesis consequently proves flatness everywhere.

Modulo two, $\eps_i\equiv1$, so $P_n\equiv\Phi_n$ in $\F_2[x]$. A flat
integer coefficient is odd exactly when its square is one. Therefore
\begin{equation}
 (\coef{m}{P_n}\bmod2)=\varphi_n(m)^2\in\{0,1\}.
 \label{eq:paritylift}
\end{equation}
This proves both assertions.
\end{proof}

The result can also be read as a signed enumeration statement. Among all
subsets having a specified weight, split them according to the product of
their assigned signs. The sizes of the two classes differ by at most one.
This signing is not necessarily the parity of the subset's cardinality. For
odd $k$, that distinction is exactly what makes the theorem possible.

\begin{corollary}[An infinite-product extension]
\label{cor:infinite}
For any admissible seeds and coherent signing, the formal infinite product
$\prod_{i\ge0}(1+\eps_i x^{w_i})$ has all coefficients in $\{-1,0,1\}$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The weights tend to infinity. For each fixed exponent, all sufficiently
late factors contribute only their constant term, so the coefficient
stabilizes to one of the finite-product coefficients already proved flat.
\end{proof}

For even $k$, choosing all initial signs negative makes every sign negative;
this recovers the setting of Diao's infinite-product theorem \cite{Diao}.
For odd $k$, a periodic coherent signing replaces that all-minus choice.

\subsection{The exact norm recurrence}
\label{sec:norm}

Define
\[
 q_n=\normsq{\Phi_n}:=\sum_{m\in\Z}\varphi_n(m)^2,
 \qquad
 G_n=\sum_{m\in\Z}\varphi_{n-1}(w_{n-1}+m)\,\varphi_{n-1}(m).
\]
All sums here and below are finite. Expanding the square in
$\Phi_n=\Phi_{n-1}+\eps_{n-1}x^{w_{n-1}}\Phi_{n-1}$ gives
\begin{equation}
 q_n=2q_{n-1}+2\eps_{n-1}G_n.
 \label{eq:normrec}
\end{equation}
Flatness and \eqref{eq:paritylift} imply $q_n=a_k(n)$, but we first derive
the norm recurrence directly.

\begin{lemma}[The overlap inner product]
\label{lem:inner}
For $n\ge k+1$ and $j=n-k-1$,
\begin{equation}
 \eps_{n-1}G_n=q_j-q_{j+1}.
 \label{eq:inner}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Only $0\le u\le D_j$ can contribute to $G_n$. Substitute \eqref{eq:low} and
\eqref{eq:high}, abbreviating $v=w_j$ and $e=\eps_j$:
\[
 \eps_{n-1}G_n=-\sum_{u=0}^{D_j}
 \bigl(\varphi_j(u)+e\,\varphi_j(u+v)\bigr)
 \bigl(\varphi_j(u)+e\,\varphi_j(u-v)\bigr).
\]
The product $\varphi_j(u+v)\varphi_j(u-v)$ vanishes for every $u$, by the
same separation $2v>D_j$ used in Proposition~\ref{prop:pointwise}. The two
mixed sums are equal after a change of index. If
\[
 T=\sum_{u\in\Z}\varphi_j(u)\varphi_j(u-v),
\]
then the preceding display becomes $\eps_{n-1}G_n=-q_j-2eT$.
On the other hand, multiplication of $\Phi_j$ by $1+ex^v$ yields
$q_{j+1}=2q_j+2eT$. Eliminating $T$ proves $\eps_{n-1}G_n=q_j-q_{j+1}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:flat}(iii)]
The superincreasing seed block gives $q_n=2^n$ for $0\le n\le k$.
Substituting \eqref{eq:inner} into \eqref{eq:normrec} gives
\[
 q_n=2q_{n-1}-2q_{n-k}+2q_{n-k-1}\qquad(n\ge k+1).
\]
Since $q_n=a_k(n)$, this proves \eqref{eq:hinit}--\eqref{eq:hrec}.
Neither the coefficients of this recurrence nor its initial values depend
on the seeds or on the first $k$ signs.

Finally multiply $\sum_n a_k(n)z^n$ by $V_k(z)=1-2z+2z^k-2z^{k+1}$. Its
constant coefficient becomes one, its coefficients of degrees
$1,\ldots,k-1$ vanish, its coefficient of degree $k$ is two, and all later
coefficients vanish by the recurrence. Thus the product is $1+2z^k$.
\end{proof}

By Lemma~\ref{lem:originalseeds}, this is a second and independent proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:mainodd}, and it also proves the seed-independent
Theorem~\ref{thm:seedmain}. The two proofs share no lemma: the finite-state
route never forms a signed product, and the signed route never forms a
canonical word.

\subsection{Examples, and the role of the signs}
\label{sec:allminus}

\paragraph{The first genuinely overlapping order-three example.}
The standard order-three weights are $1,3,5,9,17,31,57,105,193,\ldots$.
The canonical coherent signs repeat $+,+,+,-$. For the first four factors,
\begin{align*}
 \Phi_4(x)&=(1+x)(1+x^3)(1+x^5)(1-x^9)\\
 &=1+x+x^3+x^4+x^5+x^6+x^8\\
 &\quad-x^{10}-x^{12}-x^{13}-x^{14}-x^{15}-x^{17}-x^{18}.
\end{align*}
There are fourteen nonzero coefficients. The ordinary all-plus product has
a coefficient two at $x^9$ and fourteen odd coefficients elsewhere.
The recurrence agrees: $a_3(4)=2\cdot8-2\cdot2+2\cdot1=14$, matching row
$n=4$, column $k=3$ of Table~\ref{tab:odd}.

A later example displays the disjoint-tail mechanism rather than just a
single cancellation. At $n=8$ we have $j=4$, $v=w_4=17$ and $D_4=18$.
The overlap is $105\le m\le123$. Formula \eqref{eq:pointwise} allows
nonzero coefficients there only at $m=105,106,122,123$; each is $-1$.
The fifteen intermediate positions cancel.

\paragraph{Why the naive all-minus choice fails.}
For the same weights,
\[
 [x^9](1-x)(1-x^3)(1-x^5)(1-x^9)=-2 .
\]
The contributions come from the singleton $\{9\}$ and the triple
$\{1,3,5\}$; both have negative cardinality sign. An odd-order recurrence
replaces $k$ selected parts by one part without changing cardinality
parity. The coherent signing instead forces those two contributions to have
opposite signs. This explains why the proof must change the signing, not
merely reuse the all-minus Fibonacci product.

\paragraph{An OEIS connection.}
For $k=2$ the sequence satisfies $a_2(n)=2\,\mathrm{A}104767(n)$ for
$n\ge1$, with the separate value $a_2(0)=1$. The cited OEIS entry records
the corresponding Fibonacci signed-product interpretation \cite{OEIS}.
No new OEIS identifier is assigned or implied for the tables accompanying
this manuscript.

\begin{remark}[The seed hypothesis cannot simply be omitted]
\label{rem:seedhyp}
\textbf{[Kept twice: initialization boundaries.]}
For $k=3$ and seeds $(1,2,3)$, the first three all-plus factors already
have an even coefficient at exponent three and only six odd coefficients,
not $2^3$. Likewise, duplicate order-two seeds $(1,1)$ give $(1+x)^2$, with
two odd coefficients instead of four. Theorem~\ref{thm:seedmain} is not
asserting the same initial values for arbitrary positive seed lists.
This is a different degeneration from the $k=1$ failure discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:notclaimed}, which concerns the order rather than the
seeds.
\end{remark}

\section{A positive recurrence and a finite counting formula}
\label{sec:positive}

The minimal recurrence \eqref{eq:oddrec} has a negative coefficient. A
slightly longer one has only nonnegative coefficients and makes both
positivity and the analytic asymptotics of Section~\ref{sec:analyticroot}
more transparent. Put
\[
 R_k(z)=1+z+\cdots+z^{k-1},\qquad
 L_k(z)=z+z^2+\cdots+z^{k-1}+2z^{2k},\qquad
 N_k(z)=1+2z^k .
\]
Direct multiplication gives
\begin{equation}
 R_k(z)V_k(z)=1-L_k(z).
 \label{eq:factor}
\end{equation}
Indeed $(1-z)R_k=1-z^k$ and $V_k=(1-2z)+2z^k(1-z)$, so
$R_kV_k=R_k-2zR_k+2z^k(1-z^k)=1-z-\cdots-z^{k-1}-2z^{2k}$.

\begin{corollary}[Positive recurrence]
\label{cor:positive}
For every $n\ge2k$,
\begin{equation}
 a_k(n)=\sum_{r=1}^{k-1}a_k(n-r)+2a_k(n-2k).
 \label{eq:positive}
\end{equation}
Equivalently,
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{n\ge0}a_k(n)z^n=\frac{N_k(z)R_k(z)}{1-L_k(z)} .
 \label{eq:positiveGF}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Multiply numerator and denominator of \eqref{eq:mainodd} by $R_k$ and use
\eqref{eq:factor}. The numerator has degree $2k-1$, so extracting
coefficients in degrees at least $2k$ proves \eqref{eq:positive}.
\end{proof}

Formula \eqref{eq:positiveGF} is the generating function of the following
simple objects. Choose a prefix of size $d+ek$, where $0\le d<k$ and
$e\in\{0,1\}$; the prefix has one color if $e=0$ and two colors if $e=1$.
Follow it by an ordered sequence of parts of sizes $1,2,\ldots,k-1,2k$,
where the part $2k$ has two colors and the other parts each have one color.
Total size is counted by $z$.

This is an exact equinumerosity obtained from the generating function. It
is \emph{not} claimed to be an explicit bijection with the odd-coefficient
positions of the original product.

In particular, an entirely finite, nonrecursive expression is
\begin{equation}
 a_k(n)=\sum_{e=0}^1\sum_{d=0}^{k-1}
 \ \sum_{\substack{m_1,\ldots,m_{k-1},b\ge0\\
 \sum_{r=1}^{k-1}r m_r+2kb=n-d-ek}}
 2^{e+b}\frac{(m_1+\cdots+m_{k-1}+b)!}{m_1!\cdots m_{k-1}!\,b!} .
 \label{eq:multinomial}
\end{equation}
An impossible constraint contributes zero. The multinomial coefficient
orders the chosen parts; the power of two supplies their colors and, when
applicable, the prefix color. This proves \eqref{eq:multinomial} directly
from the description above.

\begin{proposition}[Minimal recurrence order]
\label{prop:minimal}
Over $\Q$, the smallest order of an eventual homogeneous
constant-coefficient recurrence for $a_k(n)$ is $k+1$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The denominator $V_k$ has degree $k+1$. It is relatively prime to
$N_k(z)=1+2z^k$, because
\[
 V_k(z)=(1-2z)N_k(z)+2z^{k+1}.
\]
A common root would have to be zero, but $N_k(0)=1$. Thus the fraction in
\eqref{eq:mainodd} is reduced. If a recurrence with denominator polynomial
$Q(z)$ holds eventually, then $Q(z)\sum a_k(n)z^n$ is a polynomial, so the
reduced denominator $V_k$ divides $Q$. Its degree is therefore a lower
bound for the recurrence order, and the recurrence \eqref{eq:oddrec}
attains it.
\end{proof}

\section{Asymptotics: growth twice, and two sparsity statements}
\label{sec:asymptotic}

Let $\beta_k$ be the positive root greater than one of
\begin{equation}
 \beta_k^k=\beta_k^{k-1}+\cdots+\beta_k+1,
 \label{eq:beta}
\end{equation}
equivalently $t_k=1/\beta_k$ is the root in $(0,1)$ of
$Q_k(z)=1-z-\cdots-z^k$; and let $r_k$ be the root in $(0,1)$ of
$V_k(z)=1-2z+2z^k-2z^{k+1}$, with $\rho_k=1/r_k$. By \eqref{eq:factor} and
$R_k>0$ on $(0,1)$, the number $r_k$ is simultaneously the unique positive
solution of $L_k(r)=1$; the two routes below therefore concern the same
constant.

\subsection{The analytic route to the dominant root}
\label{sec:analyticroot}

\noindent\textbf{[Kept twice: dominant-root asymptotic, proof 1 of 2.]}

\begin{theorem}[Asymptotic count, analytic proof]
\label{thm:asymptotic}
For every fixed $k\ge2$ there is $0<\theta_k<\rho_k$ with
\begin{equation}
 a_k(n)=C_k\rho_k^n+O(\theta_k^n),
 \qquad
 C_k=\frac{N_k(r_k)R_k(r_k)}{r_kL_k'(r_k)}>0 .
 \label{eq:asymptotic}
\end{equation}
Moreover $1<\rho_k<2$, and $\rho_k$ is the unique real root in $(1,2)$ of
\begin{equation}
 \rho^{k+1}-2\rho^k+2\rho-2=0 .
 \label{eq:characteristic}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
$L_k$ is strictly increasing on the positive real axis, with $L_k(0)=0$ and
$L_k(1)=k+1>1$. Thus $r_k\in(0,1)$ exists uniquely and $L_k'(r_k)>0$. Also
\[
 L_k(1/2)=1-2^{1-k}+2^{1-2k}<1,
\]
so $r_k>1/2$ and $\rho_k<2$.

For complex $z$ with $|z|<r_k$ the triangle inequality gives
$|L_k(z)|\le L_k(|z|)<1$. If $|z|=r_k$ and $L_k(z)=1$, equality in the
triangle inequality forces all summands to have the same argument, and
their sum is positive real. The term $z$ occurs with positive coefficient,
so $z$ itself is positive real, whence $z=r_k$. Therefore $r_k$ is the only
zero of $1-L_k$ on its smallest root circle, and it is simple.

By \eqref{eq:positiveGF} the singular part of the generating function at
$r_k$ is $C_k/(1-z/r_k)$ with $C_k$ as displayed. Subtracting it leaves a
rational function analytic in a disk of radius strictly larger than $r_k$;
Cauchy's coefficient bound on a circle inside that disk gives the
exponentially smaller error term. Finally, \eqref{eq:factor} and
$R_k(r_k)>0$ give $V_k(r_k)=0$; substituting $r_k=1/\rho_k$ yields
\eqref{eq:characteristic}, and uniqueness follows from the uniqueness of
the positive root of $1-L_k$ together with positivity of $R_k$ on the
positive axis.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The combinatorial route to the dominant root, and strict sparsity
among nonzero coefficients}
\label{sec:pfroot}

\noindent\textbf{[Kept twice: dominant-root asymptotic, proof 2 of 2; and
sparsity statement 1 of 2.]}

\begin{theorem}[Growth and strict sparsity among nonzero coefficients]
\label{thm:growth}
For fixed $k\ge2$,
\begin{equation}
 1<\rho_k<\beta_k<2,
 \qquad
 a_k(n)=\gamma_k\rho_k^n+O(\theta_k^n)
 \label{eq:growth}
\end{equation}
for some $0<\theta_k<\rho_k$, where
\begin{equation}
 \gamma_k=-\frac{1+2r_k^k}{r_kV_k'(r_k)}>0 .
 \label{eq:gamma}
\end{equation}
In particular $a_k(n)/s_k(n)$ tends to zero exponentially, where $s_k(n)$
is the number of \emph{nonzero} coefficients of $P_n$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
First, $V_k(0)=1$ and $V_k(1)=-1$. On $[0,1]$,
\[
 V_k'(z)=-2+2z^{k-1}\bigl(k-(k+1)z\bigr)<0,
\]
because the maximum of $z^{k-1}(k-(k+1)z)$ is $((k-1)/(k+1))^{k-1}<1$.
Thus $r_k$ exists uniquely and is simple.

To exclude other poles of the same modulus, use the automaton of
Table~\ref{tab:parity} rather than complex analysis. Remove $X$ and $C_k$
from its graph. The remaining states form one strongly connected component:
every state is reachable from $A_0$, and the transition table supplies a
return path to $A_0$. There is an all-zero cycle of length $2k$ through the
$A$ and $B$ families. Replacing its first edge $A_0\to A_1$ with
$A_0\to C_1\to A_1$ gives a cycle of length $2k+1$. The component is
therefore aperiodic. Its spectral radius is strictly greater than one: at a
common multiple of those two lengths there are two different closed walks,
whose arbitrary concatenations already give exponential growth.

The Perron--Frobenius theorem for a finite irreducible aperiodic
nonnegative matrix now gives a simple strictly dominant positive
eigenvalue. The other productive component, the loop at $C_k$, has spectral
radius one. Both the initial state and accepting states meet the main
component, so its leading contribution is nonzero. Hence the rational
series has one simple dominant pole, necessarily $r_k$, and coefficient
extraction gives \eqref{eq:growth}--\eqref{eq:gamma}. Subdominant
polynomial factors are absorbed by choosing $\theta_k$ strictly above the
moduli of all other contributing eigenvalues.

Let $t_k=1/\beta_k$, the unique root in $(0,1)$ of $Q_k$. Its simplicity
and the triangle inequality show that no other root of $Q_k$ has modulus
$t_k$: equality would force the terms $z$ and $z^2$ to have the same
positive argument, hence $z=t_k$. Equation~\eqref{eq:support} therefore
gives $s_k(n)\sim\delta_k\beta_k^n$ with $\delta_k>0$. Also
$1/2<t_k\le1/\beta_2<2/3$ and $1-2t_k+t_k^{k+1}=0$, so
\[
 V_k(t_k)=t_k^k(2-3t_k)>0 .
\]
Since $V_k$ is strictly decreasing, $r_k>t_k$, that is $\rho_k<\beta_k$.
The remaining inequalities are immediate from the locations of $r_k,t_k$.
Taking the ratio of the two asymptotic formulas proves exponential
sparsity among nonzero coefficients.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[The two leading constants agree]
\label{cor:constants}
$C_k=\gamma_k$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Differentiate \eqref{eq:factor}: $R_k'V_k+R_kV_k'=-L_k'$. At $z=r_k$ we
have $V_k(r_k)=0$, so $R_k(r_k)V_k'(r_k)=-L_k'(r_k)$. Substituting into
\eqref{eq:asymptotic} gives
\[
 C_k=\frac{N_k(r_k)R_k(r_k)}{r_kL_k'(r_k)}
   =\frac{N_k(r_k)R_k(r_k)}{-r_kR_k(r_k)V_k'(r_k)}
   =-\frac{N_k(r_k)}{r_kV_k'(r_k)}=\gamma_k .\qedhere
\]
\end{proof}

Both expressions are retained in Tables~\ref{tab:growth80} and
\ref{tab:growth}, each computed from its own defining formula. Agreement of
the two columns is a further arithmetic check on the merged data.

\noindent\textbf{[Kept twice: the inequality $\rho_k<\beta_k$.]}
Theorem~\ref{thm:growth} obtained it from $V_k(t_k)=t_k^k(2-3t_k)>0$;
Section~\ref{sec:densityall} obtains it again from $L_k(1/\beta_k)<1$,
without reference to $V_k$.

\subsection{Zero density in the full coefficient range}
\label{sec:densityall}

\noindent\textbf{[Kept twice: sparsity statement 2 of 2.]}
The following theorem is \emph{not} Theorem~\ref{thm:growth} restated. Its
denominator counts every position from exponent zero to the degree,
including positions whose coefficient is zero. The signed route has no
access to the support generating function \eqref{eq:support} and therefore
cannot prove Theorem~\ref{thm:growth}; conversely the statement below holds
for arbitrary superincreasing seeds, where $s_k(n)$ is not given by
\eqref{eq:support} at all.

\begin{theorem}[Density among all positions]
\label{thm:densityall}
Let $w_0,w_1,\ldots$ be any quasifibonacci sequence of order $k$. Then
$D_n=\Theta(\beta_k^n)$, $\rho_k<\beta_k$, and
\begin{equation}
 \frac{a_k(n)}{D_n+1}=O\!\left((\rho_k/\beta_k)^n\right)\longrightarrow0 .
 \label{eq:density}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The number $\beta_k\in(1,2)$ satisfies $\sum_{i=1}^k\beta_k^{-i}=1$, the
growth equation of the exponent sequence. For every admissible seed list,
$D_n=\Theta(\beta_k^n)$. One sees this without a spectral theorem: choose
positive constants bounding $w_i/\beta_k^i$ above and below for the first
$k$ indices, and propagate the bounds through \eqref{eq:recw}. Summing
preserves the same exponential order.

Put $s=\beta_k^{-1}=t_k$. Since the $k$ terms $s,\ldots,s^k$ sum to one, we
have $s^k<1/2$. Consequently
\[
 L_k(s)=\bigl(s+\cdots+s^{k-1}\bigr)+2s^{2k}=1-s^k+2s^{2k}<1,
\]
so $s<r_k$ and $\rho_k<\beta_k$. Combining with
Theorem~\ref{thm:asymptotic} gives \eqref{eq:density}.
\end{proof}

The denominator of \eqref{eq:density} counts every coefficient position
from zero to the degree, including positions with coefficient zero.
\emph{No assertion about density among nonzero coefficients only is implicit
in \eqref{eq:density}}; that separate statement is
Theorem~\ref{thm:growth}, and it is proved only for Stanley's
initialization, where the support count \eqref{eq:support} is available.

\subsection{Large-order expansion}
\label{sec:largek}

There is also a useful asymptotic as $k\to\infty$, with no counterpart on
the finite-state route:
\begin{equation}
 \rho_k=2-2^{1-k}-(2k-4)\,4^{-k}+O(k^2 8^{-k}).
 \label{eq:largek}
\end{equation}
Here is a justification that keeps the varying parameter under control.
Write $t=2^{-k}$ and $\delta=2-\rho_k$. Equation \eqref{eq:characteristic}
becomes
\begin{equation}
 \delta=2t(1-\delta)(1-\delta/2)^{-k}.
 \label{eq:delta}
\end{equation}
For all sufficiently large $k$, the equivalent function
$f(\delta)=\delta(2-\delta)^k-2+2\delta$ is negative at zero and positive
at $4t$, since $f(4t)=4(1-2t)^k-2+8t\to2$. The uniqueness already proved
places the relevant root in $(0,4t)$. Thus $k\delta\to0$, and Taylor
expansion, uniformly on that interval, gives
\[
 (1-\delta)(1-\delta/2)^{-k}=1+\frac{k-2}{2}\delta+O(k^2\delta^2).
\]
First \eqref{eq:delta} yields $\delta=2t+O(kt^2)$; substituting back gives
$\delta=2t+2(k-2)t^2+O(k^2t^3)$, proving \eqref{eq:largek}. This expansion
is separate from the fixed-$k$, large-$n$ asymptotic of
Theorem~\ref{thm:asymptotic}.

\section{Exact algorithms}
\label{sec:algorithms}

\noindent\textbf{[Kept twice: odd-count algorithm families, and coefficient
oracles.]}
Two families of algorithms are supplied, descending from the two proofs.
Section~\ref{sec:crosscheck} explains why their agreement is informative
while the agreement of two members of the same family is not.

\subsection{Counting without expanding a polynomial}

The recurrence \eqref{eq:oddrec} computes $a_k(0),\ldots,a_k(N)$ in $O(N)$
integer arithmetic operations. A streaming implementation needs only $k+1$
past values; the supplied \code{count_prefix} returns the whole list and so
intentionally uses $O(N)$ integer storage.

For an isolated very large index, let
\[
 \chi_k(X)=X^{k+1}-2X^k+2X-2 .
\]
Compute $X^N\bmod\chi_k(X)$ by binary powering. If the remainder is
$\sum_{i=0}^k b_iX^i$, then
\begin{equation}
 a_k(N)=\sum_{i=0}^k b_i2^i .
 \label{eq:fast}
\end{equation}
To justify this, let $E$ be the shift operator on the sequence. The proved
recurrence says $\chi_k(E)a=0$ beginning at index zero in shift-operator
form, so polynomial division applied to $E^N$ gives \eqref{eq:fast}.
Naive polynomial multiplication and reduction use $O(k^2\log(N+1))$ ring
operations. The same code works modulo any positive integer; it does not
assume the modulus is prime.

These are arithmetic-operation bounds, not bit-complexity bounds. In exact
arithmetic $a_k(N)$ has $\Theta(N)$ bits for fixed $k$, by
Theorem~\ref{thm:asymptotic}; writing it already takes linear time in its
bit length.

\subsection{A single coefficient's parity in linearly many steps}
\label{sec:oracle}

Proposition~\ref{prop:pointwise} gives an efficient coefficient oracle.
Precompute the weights, partial sums, and signs through index $n-1$.
To evaluate $\varphi_n(m)$, first return zero if $m\notin[0,D_n]$. For
$n>k$:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
\item if $m<w_{n-1}$, replace $(n,m)$ by $(n-1,m)$;
\item if $m>D_{n-1}$, replace $(n,m)$ by $(n-1,m-w_{n-1})$ and multiply the
accumulated sign by $\eps_{n-1}$;
\item otherwise set $j=n-k-1$, $u=m-w_{n-1}$, $v=w_j$. Formula
\eqref{eq:pointwise} gives at most one continuing branch: use
$\varphi_j(u-v)$ if $u\ge v$; use $-\varphi_j(u+v)$ if $u+v\le D_j$;
otherwise return zero. In either continuing case multiply also by
$\eps_{n-1}\eps_j$.
\end{enumerate}
When the index is at most $k$, a greedy subset-sum test on the
superincreasing seeds finishes the computation. At every step the index
strictly decreases; there is no branching tree. Hence the query uses $O(n)$
arithmetic comparisons, additions, and sign changes after $O(n+k)$-entry
preprocessing, including validation of the seed block. The absolute value of
the resulting signed coefficient is the requested parity of the all-plus
coefficient.

For fixed $k$ and seed bit length $b$, the stored exponents have $O(n+b)$
bits. The arithmetic-step description therefore does not assert
constant-time operations on arbitrarily large exponents. Its principal
advantage is avoiding storage proportional to the exponentially large
degree.

\subsection{The exact integer coefficient from the counter}

The counter of Section~\ref{sec:counter} gives a different oracle with a
different output: for Stanley's seeds it returns the exact nonnegative
integer $c_n(j)$, not a sign. Given any binary word, normalize it and run
\eqref{eq:initial}--\eqref{eq:O}. The supplied function is
\code{coefficient_for_canonical}; for example
\code{coefficient_for_canonical((0,0,0,0,1), k=2)} returns $3$, in agreement
with the worked example of Section~\ref{sec:counter}.
Neither oracle subsumes the other: the signed oracle accepts arbitrary
superincreasing seeds and arbitrary initial sign blocks but returns only a
value in $\{-1,0,1\}$; the counter oracle returns the exact multiplicity but
is tied to \eqref{eq:weights}.

\subsection{Direct multiplication as an independent check}

An integer bitset represents a polynomial over $\F_2$ by storing the
coefficient of $x^m$ in bit $m$. Multiplication by $1+x^w$ is exactly
\begin{verbatim}
bits ^= bits << w
\end{verbatim}
and the final odd-coefficient count is \code{bits.bit_count()}. This is an
excellent small-instance oracle, but its memory requirement is proportional
to the polynomial degree; the supplied implementation has an explicit
degree cap to prevent accidental enormous allocations.

The signed sparse and dense checks perform integer polynomial
multiplication, not just multiplication modulo two. They therefore test the
stronger flatness claim and the sign-sensitive overlap identity as well.
The reference expansion \code{expand} of the finite-state package is a third
and separate implementation: it begins with the coefficient vector $[1]$ and
multiplies by each factor $1+x^{w_i}$ using integer additions at offsets
zero and $w_i$, using neither normalization, nor the counter, nor a guessed
recurrence.

\subsection{Complexity of the finite-state machines}

Once a machine with $N$ states has been constructed, advancing its count
vector by one length uses at most $2N$ integer additions, because every
state has at most two labeled successors. Thus the first $L$ coefficient
histograms can be generated in $O(LN)$ integer-arithmetic operations after
graph construction. This is again an arithmetic-operation bound, not a
bit-complexity bound: the counts themselves grow with $L$. Constructing each
successor updates $k+1$ counter coordinates, so a straightforward
breadth-first construction uses $O(kN)$ such coordinate operations plus
dictionary overhead. Very large parameters can still produce a large
machine; the supplied code raises an error when its explicit state limit is
exceeded rather than returning a truncated graph.

\subsection{Why a finite recurrence certificate proves an identity}

\begin{lemma}[Cayley--Hamilton certificate]
\label{lem:certificate}
Suppose $u_n=\lambda A^nf$ for an $N\times N$ matrix $A$ over $\Q$.
Let $Q(z)=1+q_1z+\cdots+q_dz^d$ and let $P(z)$ have degree less than $d$.
To prove $\sum_{n\ge0}u_nz^n=P(z)/Q(z)$, it suffices to check that
$Q(z)\sum u_nz^n$ agrees with $P(z)$ through degree $d-1$, and that its
next $N$ coefficients, in degrees $d,\ldots,d+N-1$, are zero.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For $n\ge0$, define
\[
 r_n=u_{n+d}+q_1u_{n+d-1}+\cdots+q_du_n
 =\lambda A^n\bigl(A^d+q_1A^{d-1}+\cdots+q_dI\bigr)f.
\]
By Cayley--Hamilton, $r_n$ obeys the order-$N$ recurrence supplied by the
characteristic polynomial of $A$. Its first $N$ values being zero forces
all its values to be zero. The initial coefficient check then proves the
formal identity $Q\sum u_nz^n=P$.
\end{proof}

\noindent\textbf{[Kept twice: proof-hygiene mechanisms.]}
Thus the state-space dimension, not an arbitrary choice of a large
experimental cutoff, determines how many checks certify a candidate. The
argument does not require the candidate denominator to be the full
characteristic polynomial, and it permits substantial cancellation.
The complementary hygiene statement for the signed route --- that no
rational generating function is assumed and no unproved word-rewriting
monoid is used --- is Appendix~\ref{app:dependency}.

\subsection{Two explicit modulo-three identities}

For the following formulas the residue is one, so internal zeros are
irrelevant. Define
\begin{align*}
 P_2(z)&=1-2z+4z^2-6z^3+8z^4-10z^5+8z^6-6z^7,\\
 Q_2(z)&=1-4z+8z^2-12z^3+16z^4-20z^5+19z^6-12z^7+4z^8,
\end{align*}
and
\begin{align*}
 P_3(z)&=1-2z+4z^3-6z^4+8z^6-10z^7+8z^9-6z^{10},\\
 Q_3(z)&=1-4z+4z^2+4z^3-12z^4+8z^5+8z^6-20z^7\\
       &\hspace{1.5em}+11z^8+8z^9-12z^{10}+4z^{11}.
\end{align*}
Then the finite certificates establish
\begin{equation}
 H_{3,1}^{(2)}(z)=\frac{P_2(z)}{Q_2(z)},
 \qquad
 H_{3,1}^{(3)}(z)=\frac{P_3(z)}{Q_3(z)}.
 \label{eq:mod3}
\end{equation}
These expressions appear in Stanley's discussion
\cite[p.~24]{Stanley}; their forms are not claimed as discoveries here.
The $k=2$ congruence case is already covered by his earlier theorem.

For $k=2$, the constructed matrix has dimension 35 and $\deg Q_2=8$.
The certificate checks the eight initial numerator coefficients and 35
consecutive zero residuals. For $k=3$, the corresponding numbers are 58,
11, and 58. The JSON files contain every reachable counter state, every
labeled transition, the numerator and denominator arrays in ascending
coefficient order, the exact sequence prefix, and all residuals. Rebuilding
the graph and checking these integers reproduces the all-$n$ proof via
Lemma~\ref{lem:certificate}. This part is a finite computational certificate;
the symbolic proof for arbitrary $k$ and modulus remains separate.

\section{Scope, limitations, and the next mathematical questions}
\label{sec:scope}

\subsection{What the argument establishes}

The finite-state construction addresses the coefficient-residue conjecture
for the entire family of products \eqref{eq:product}, not just several
numerical values of $k$ or prime moduli. It provides a proof mechanism, an
explicit algorithm for the rational function, a closed parity formula, and a
rationality result for fixed exact multiplicities. The normal-form,
fiber-factorization, and counter arguments are all given in full.

The signed construction addresses the parity statement alone, but does so
for every quasifibonacci weight sequence with superincreasing seeds and for
all $2^k$ coherent signings, and yields in addition signed flatness, the
infinite-product corollary, the positive recurrence, the finite multinomial
formula, minimality of the recurrence order, and the large-order expansion
\eqref{eq:largek}.

The distinction between established background and the proposed
contribution is important. General finite-state rationality is a standard
linear-algebra fact. Carry-block shapes have a precedent in Stanley's work.
Flatness of truncated all-minus Fibonacci products at $k=2$ is Zhao's
\cite{Zhao}, and the quasifibonacci seed conditions together with the
even-order infinite all-minus theorem are Diao's \cite{Diao}. The present
routes combine a unique canonical cross-section with an exact fiber counter,
and a periodic coherent signing with a pointwise two-tail cancellation.
The supplied evidence supports those routes; it does not, by itself, certify
that no equivalent proof exists elsewhere.

\subsection{What is not being claimed}
\label{sec:notclaimed}

The argument is not a proof of every conjecture in Stanley's paper.
In particular, it does not automatically cover arbitrary initial
polynomials, arbitrary lists of factor coefficients, products with several
nonconstant monomials per factor, or arbitrary sequences satisfying a
constant-coefficient recurrence. Such changes may destroy either the
regular cross-section or the finite counter used here. No claim is made
that the generic residue state counts in Table~\ref{tab:states} are
minimal, or that every denominator has an unexplained universal form.
No higher-moment conjecture from the same paper is claimed solved.
Coherent signing is proved sufficient for flatness; \emph{all} flat signings
are not classified. No explicit bijection between the colored compositions
of Section~\ref{sec:positive} and odd-coefficient positions is supplied;
their equinumerosity is proved by generating functions only.

\noindent\textbf{[Kept twice: initialization boundaries.]}
The restriction $k\ge2$ is genuine. At $k=1$, all weights equal one and
$P_n(x)=(1+x)^n$; the canonical-injectivity argument no longer applies.
Even the displayed parity formula is already wrong at $n=4$: it would
predict four odd coefficients, whereas $(1+x)^4$ has two. This boundary case
is excluded throughout, as in the nontrivial $k\ge2$ framework of the
source's pair construction. It is a different degeneration from the failure
of non-superincreasing seeds recorded in Remark~\ref{rem:seedhyp}.

For moduli greater than two, changing a factor's sign no longer leaves its
residue unchanged, so the parity lift \eqref{eq:paritylift} is unavailable
in that form. The signed route therefore does \emph{not} prove
Theorem~\ref{thm:mainrat}; that theorem rests entirely on the finite-state
route. Conversely, the finite-state route is nailed to the initialization
\eqref{eq:weights}: its canonical normal form and carry blocks are derived
from exactly that initial segment, and it does not prove
Theorem~\ref{thm:seedmain}.

The manuscript has not been independently refereed or formalized in a
proof assistant. The computational checks are exact but finite; their
role is not to substitute for the general proofs. The special
Cayley--Hamilton certificates are distinguished because a proved finite
dimension bound makes those finite checks logically sufficient.
The numerical constants are high-precision approximations, not certified
interval enclosures.

\subsection{Concrete continuations}

One natural next problem is to classify the reachable modulo-$m$ counter
vectors uniformly in $k$, as was done here for $m=2$. A sufficiently
explicit classification for $m=3$ might explain the relation between the
two displayed fractions in \eqref{eq:mod3} and give a general symbolic
formula. The state-count table suggests structure, but is not offered
as a theorem outside its checked range.

A second question is whether a finite collection of root-of-unity-weighted
products can replace the parity lift at moduli greater than two. Flatness
over $\{-1,0,1\}$ by itself supplies no such result.

A third question is how much of the fiber-counting method survives for
other numeration systems. Proposition~\ref{prop:principle} isolates the
necessary ingredients: a regular set of unique representatives and a
finite-dimensional nonnegative counter. Establishing either ingredient
for a new recurrence is a substantive mathematical problem, rather than
an automatic consequence of the recurrence being linear.

A fourth is which weaker seed conditions preserve the same count. The
signed proof uses the seed condition twice --- to obtain the initial values
$2^n$, and to establish the separation inequalities of Lemma~\ref{lem:sep}
that make the tails disjoint. Changing only the initial values without
checking that separation would not justify reusing the argument.

Finally, a formal proof could focus on the short dependency chain of the
finite-state route: termination and canonical injectivity; the rewrite-count
identity and its block decomposition; the two counter maps; and the
finite-state resolvent. The uniform parity table and its elimination would
then form a separate, comparatively small algebraic verification. The signed
route's chain, displayed in Appendix~\ref{app:dependency}, is shorter still
and uses only elementary arithmetic.

\appendix
\section{A compact proof dependency map for the signed route}
\label{app:dependency}

The logical order is
\[
\begin{gathered}
\text{seed separation and recurrence}\quad+\quad\text{coherent signs}\\
\Downarrow\\
\text{low-degree truncation and signed reflection}\\
\Downarrow\\
\text{pointwise two-tail identity}\\
\Downarrow\\
\text{flatness}\quad+\quad\text{overlap inner-product identity}\\
\Downarrow\\
\text{parity count recurrence and rational generating function}.
\end{gathered}
\]
Concretely: Lemma~\ref{lem:sep} and Lemma~\ref{lem:period} feed
\eqref{eq:low} and Lemma~\ref{lem:reflection}; these give
Proposition~\ref{prop:pointwise}; that gives Theorem~\ref{thm:flat}(i)--(ii)
and Lemma~\ref{lem:inner}; and those give Theorem~\ref{thm:flat}(iii).
Five lemmas and one proposition, all elementary arithmetic.

In particular, neither an assumed rational generating function nor any
unproved description of a word-rewriting monoid is used in this argument.
All boundary cases are covered by the convention that coefficient functions
vanish outside their finite ranges. This is the signed route's counterpart
to Lemma~\ref{lem:certificate}: together they are the two answers this
manuscript gives to the question of whether anything here is numerical
curve fitting.
