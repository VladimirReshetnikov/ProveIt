\begin{abstract}
We study the parity of coefficients of finite products whose exponents satisfy a
$k$-term Fibonacci recurrence. The target is the rational generating function
stated in Stanley's Conjecture~6.3. We prove that identity for every $k\ge2$ and,
more generally, for every quasifibonacci sequence with superincreasing initial
seeds. The key construction changes selected plus signs in the product to minus
signs: a periodic sign block of length $k+1$ with product $-1$ makes every
coefficient belong to $\{-1,0,1\}$. Since signs disappear modulo two, a parity
count becomes a squared-coefficient norm. An exact overlap identity then gives
a recurrence of order $k+1$. We also obtain a positive recurrence, a colored
composition interpretation, minimality of the recurrence order, exponential
asymptotics, and a coefficient-parity algorithm requiring only a single branch
at each step. All principal identities are proved independently of the
computational checks included in the archive.
\end{abstract}

\section{The selected problem and the scope of the result}
\subsection{A documented conjecture}
Fix $k\ge2$. In Stanley's notation, the $k$-bonacci numbers have initial values
\[
F^{(k)}_1=\cdots=F^{(k)}_k=1,
\qquad
F^{(k)}_{r+1}=F^{(k)}_r+\cdots+F^{(k)}_{r-k+1}\quad(r\ge k).
\]
Set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stanley-product}
I_n^{(k)}(x)=\prod_{i=1}^n\bigl(1+x^{F^{(k)}_{i+k-1}}\bigr).
\end{equation}
Let $h_k(n)$ be the number of its coefficients that are odd, with $h_k(0)=1$.
The statement labeled Conjecture~6.3 in arXiv:2101.02131v3 is precisely the
identity on the title page \cite[\S6, p.~24]{Stanley}. The paper was published
in \emph{European Journal of Combinatorics} in 2024. We use the conjecture
numbering of the cited arXiv version throughout.

This is a finite-product problem: $n$ is the number of factors, rather than an
upper bound on the exponent being counted. Zeros inside the polynomial's
coefficient range are harmless because they are not odd. Coefficients beyond
the degree are also not counted.

\subsection{What is proved, and what is not claimed}
The main theorem proves the stated identity uniformly in $k$. The proof does
not infer a recurrence from finitely many terms. Instead, it establishes an
identity for every coefficient in the overlap of two translated products and
then sums it exactly. It also shows that the same formula works for arbitrary
superincreasing seeds, not just the seeds in \eqref{eq:stanley-product}.

There is important prior work to acknowledge. Zhao proved flatness of finite
signed Fibonacci products when $k=2$ \cite{Zhao}. Diao introduced the
quasifibonacci seed conditions used below and proved flatness of the infinite
all-minus product for even recurrence order \cite[Definition~1.1 and
Theorem~1.1]{Diao}. The argument here uses a varying, periodic signing, works
for both even and odd orders, concerns every finite product, and gives its
exact support count. These distinctions matter: simply replacing every plus
sign by a minus sign does \emph{not} work for odd $k$.

The literature check for this report examined the primary sources listed in
the bibliography, including the available Stanley version and related later
work. No later proof of this particular all-$k$ parity formula was located.
That is a bounded literature-search statement, not a certification that no
such proof exists. The mathematical claim made here is the validity of the
proof below; a claim of first publication would require a separate review.

The result does not settle Stanley's general coefficient-residue conjecture
for every modulus, nor the higher-moment identities in the same paper.

\section{A stronger theorem for arbitrary seeds}
\begin{definition}\label[definition]{def:weights}
A \emph{quasifibonacci sequence of order $k$} is a sequence of positive
integers $w_1,w_2,\ldots$ satisfying
\begin{align}
 w_i&>S_{i-1}:=\sum_{r=1}^{i-1}w_r &&(1\le i\le k),\label{eq:seed}\\
 w_n&=\sum_{r=n-k}^{n-1}w_r &&(n\ge k+1).\label{eq:recw}
\end{align}
We put $S_0=0$ and $S_n=\sum_{r=1}^n w_r$.
The term agrees with Diao's definition, with ``level'' replaced by ``order.''
\end{definition}

The seed condition is often called \emph{superincreasing}: each seed is larger
than all earlier seeds combined. For instance, $(1,3,5)$ and $(2,7,15)$ are
valid order-three seeds; $(1,2,3)$ is not.

\begin{definition}\label[definition]{def:sign}
A sign sequence $\eps_1,\eps_2,\ldots\in\{-1,1\}$ is \emph{coherent} if
\begin{equation}\label{eq:signrec}
 \eps_n=-\prod_{r=n-k}^{n-1}\eps_r\qquad(n\ge k+1).
\end{equation}
Its first $k$ signs can be chosen arbitrarily. A convenient canonical choice is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:canonical}
 \eps_i=\begin{cases}-1,&k+1\mid i,\\1,&k+1\nmid i.\end{cases}
\end{equation}
Define the ordinary and signed products by
\[
 I_n(x)=\prod_{i=1}^n(1+x^{w_i}),\qquad
 A_n(x)=\prod_{i=1}^n(1+\eps_i x^{w_i})=\sum_{m\in\Z}a_n(m)x^m.
\]
The coefficient function $a_n(m)$ is zero outside $0\le m\le S_n$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Uniform parity and signed flatness]\label{thm:main}
For every sequence in \cref{def:weights}, every coherent signing, and every
$n\ge0$, the following conclusions hold.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item Every coefficient of $A_n$ is $-1$, $0$, or $1$.
\item For each $m\in\Z$, the residue modulo two of $\coef{m}{I_n}$ is
      $a_n(m)^2$.
\item The number of odd coefficients is independent of the seeds and satisfies
\begin{align}
 h_k(n)&=2^n &&(0\le n\le k),\label{eq:hinit}\\
 h_k(n)&=2h_k(n-1)-2h_k(n-k)+2h_k(n-k-1) &&(n\ge k+1).\label{eq:hrec}
\end{align}
Consequently,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:H}
 H_k(z):=\sum_{n\ge0}h_k(n)z^n
 =\frac{1+2z^k}{1-2z+2z^k-2z^{k+1}}.
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

The next five sections prove the theorem. The proof has two distinct parts:
first flatness, then an exact norm recurrence. The latter alone would not
justify counting odd coefficients without the former.

\section{Arithmetic of the exponent sequence}
\begin{lemma}[Separation]\label[lemma]{lem:sep}
The weights are strictly increasing. For every $j\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sep}
 w_{j+2}>S_j,\qquad 2w_{j+1}>S_j.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The seeds are strictly increasing by \eqref{eq:seed}. At the first recurrence
step, $w_{k+1}=S_k>w_k$. Each subsequent weight is a sum of $k\ge2$ positive
weights including its immediate predecessor, so strict increase persists.

For $0\le j\le k-2$, the first inequality follows directly from the seed
condition. For $j=k-1$, we have $w_{k+1}=S_k>S_{k-1}$. If $j\ge k$, the
recurrence includes the two preceding weights, so induction gives
\[
 w_{j+2}\ge w_{j+1}+w_j>S_{j-1}+w_j=S_j.
\]
This proves the first inequality at all indices. For $j=0$, the second
inequality is immediate. For $j\ge1$, use the first inequality one index
lower together with strict increase:
\[
 S_j=S_{j-1}+w_j<w_{j+1}+w_{j+1}.
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Exact overlap length]\label[lemma]{lem:overlap-length}
For $n\ge k+1$, put $j=n-k-1$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:overlaplength}
 S_{n-1}=w_n+S_j.
\end{equation}
Thus the coefficient ranges of $A_{n-1}(x)$ and
$x^{w_n}A_{n-1}(x)$ overlap exactly in the interval
$[w_n,w_n+S_j]$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Split $S_{n-1}$ after its first $j$ summands. The remaining $k$ summands
have sum $w_n$ by \eqref{eq:recw}. The interval assertion follows because
$\deg A_{n-1}=S_{n-1}$ and both endpoint coefficients are nonzero.
Here ``coefficient range'' means the containing interval, not necessarily a
support without internal gaps.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[The original seeds are admissible]\label[lemma]{lem:originalseeds}
For $w_i=F^{(k)}_{i+k-1}$, the first $k$ weights are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:originalseeds}
 w_1=1,\qquad w_i=(k-1)2^{i-2}+1\quad(2\le i\le k),
\end{equation}
and satisfy $w_i-S_{i-1}=k-i+1>0$ for $2\le i\le k$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The first new term after the $k$ initial ones is $k$. During the subsequent
initial steps, moving the recurrence window replaces one of those initial
ones by the newest term, so the new value is twice its predecessor minus one.
This proves \eqref{eq:originalseeds} by induction. Summing that expression gives
\[
 S_{i-1}=(k-1)(2^{i-2}-1)+(i-1),
\]
which yields the claimed difference. The later weights satisfy
\eqref{eq:recw} by their definition.
\end{proof}

\section{Coherent signs and reflection}
\begin{lemma}[Periodic description of all coherent signings]\label[lemma]{lem:period}
A signing is coherent if and only if it is periodic with period dividing
$k+1$ and every consecutive block of length $k+1$ has product $-1$.
There are $2^k$ such signings.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Equation \eqref{eq:signrec} says that each length-$(k+1)$ window has product
$-1$. Dividing the products for two consecutive windows cancels the $k$
common signs and gives $\eps_{r+k+1}=\eps_r$. Conversely, a periodic sign
block with product $-1$ has that product in every window, and hence satisfies
\eqref{eq:signrec}. Its first $k$ signs are free and its final sign is their
negative product. The canonical choice \eqref{eq:canonical} is the block of
$k$ plus signs followed by one minus sign.
\end{proof}

Put $E_n=\prod_{i=1}^n\eps_i$, including $E_0=1$.

\begin{lemma}[Reflection]\label[lemma]{lem:reflection}
For every integer $m$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reflection}
 a_n(S_n-m)=E_n a_n(m).
\end{equation}
If $n\ge k+1$ and $j=n-k-1$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Esign}
 E_{n-1}E_j=-\eps_n.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For an individual factor,
$x^{w_i}(1+\eps_i x^{-w_i})=\eps_i(1+\eps_i x^{w_i})$.
Multiplication gives $x^{S_n}A_n(x^{-1})=E_nA_n(x)$ and proves
\eqref{eq:reflection}, including the zero coefficients outside the range.
Since $E_j^2=1$, the product $E_{n-1}E_j$ consists precisely of the $k$
signs from $j+1=n-k$ through $n-1$. Its value is $-\eps_n$.
\end{proof}

\section{The pointwise cancellation identity}
This section contains the decisive calculation. It is an identity over the
integers, before reducing anything modulo two.

\begin{proposition}[Two disjoint tails]\label[proposition]{prop:pointwise}
Let $n\ge k+1$, $j=n-k-1$, and $v=w_{j+1}$. For $0\le u\le S_j$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pointwise}
\boxed{\displaystyle
 a_n(w_n+u)=\eps_n\eps_{j+1}
       \bigl(a_j(u-v)-a_j(u+v)\bigr).}
\end{equation}
The two terms on the right cannot both be nonzero.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By \cref{lem:sep}, the factors of index $j+2$ or higher have all their
nonconstant terms in degrees larger than $S_j$. Therefore, on the interval
$0\le u\le S_j$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:low}
 a_{n-1}(u)=a_j(u)+\eps_{j+1}a_j(u-v).
\end{equation}
There is no convergence or truncation approximation in this equation:
later factors simply cannot contribute to such a low exponent.

Reflect the high end of $A_{n-1}$ using
$S_{n-1}=w_n+S_j$ and \eqref{eq:low}. We obtain
\begin{align}
 a_{n-1}(w_n+u)
 &=E_{n-1}a_{n-1}(S_j-u)\notag\\
 &=E_{n-1}\bigl(a_j(S_j-u)
          +\eps_{j+1}a_j(S_j-u-v)\bigr)\notag\\
 &=E_{n-1}E_j\bigl(a_j(u)+\eps_{j+1}a_j(u+v)\bigr)\notag\\
 &=-\eps_n\bigl(a_j(u)+\eps_{j+1}a_j(u+v)\bigr).
 \label{eq:high}
\end{align}
Now use $A_n=A_{n-1}+\eps_nx^{w_n}A_{n-1}$ and add
\eqref{eq:high} to $\eps_n$ times \eqref{eq:low}. The two copies of
$a_j(u)$ cancel exactly, leaving \eqref{eq:pointwise}.

For the first tail to be nonzero, $u\ge v$ is necessary. For the second,
$u\le S_j-v$ is necessary. These conditions cannot hold simultaneously,
because $2v>S_j$. More explicitly, the possible tail intervals are
\[
 [0,S_j-v]\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad[v,S_j],
\]
with an interval understood to be empty if its endpoints are reversed.
They are disjoint.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
At the very first overlap, $n=k+1$ and $j=0$. Both tails are empty:
$a_0(-w_1)=a_0(w_1)=0$. Thus the only overlapping coefficient, at exponent
$w_{k+1}=S_k$, cancels. This is an important endpoint check on the indexing.
\end{remark}

\section{Proof of flatness and the parity lift}
\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{thm:main}(i)--(ii)]
For $0\le n\le k$, the seed condition ensures that two distinct subsets
of $\{w_1,\ldots,w_n\}$ cannot have the same sum. Indeed, at their largest
index of disagreement, the larger seed exceeds the sum of all earlier
seeds. Every nonzero coefficient of $A_n$ is therefore a single product of
signs and is $1$ or $-1$.

Proceed by strong induction on $n$. For $n\ge k+1$, outside
$[w_n,S_{n-1}]$ at most one of the two terms
$A_{n-1}$ and $\eps_nx^{w_n}A_{n-1}$ contributes. The coefficient there
is zero or, up to sign, an earlier coefficient. Inside the overlap,
\cref{prop:pointwise} expresses it as the signed difference of two
coefficients of $A_j$, where $j<n$, with at most one nonzero term.
The induction hypothesis consequently proves flatness everywhere.

Modulo two, $\eps_i=1$, so $I_n\equiv A_n$ in $\F_2[x]$. A flat
integer coefficient is odd exactly when its square is one. Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:paritylift}
 (\coef{m}{I_n}\bmod2)=a_n(m)^2\in\{0,1\}.
\end{equation}
This proves both assertions.
\end{proof}

The result can also be read as a signed enumeration statement. Among all
subsets having a specified weight, split them according to the product of
their assigned signs. The sizes of the two classes differ by at most one.
This signing is not necessarily parity of the subset's cardinality. For
odd $k$, that distinction is exactly what makes the theorem possible.

\begin{corollary}[An infinite-product extension]\label[corollary]{cor:infinite}
For any admissible seeds and coherent signing, the formal infinite product
$\prod_{i\ge1}(1+\eps_i x^{w_i})$ has all coefficients in $\{-1,0,1\}$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The weights tend to infinity. For each fixed exponent, all sufficiently
late factors contribute only their constant term, so the coefficient
stabilizes to one of the finite-product coefficients already proved flat.
\end{proof}

For even $k$, choosing all initial signs negative makes every sign negative;
this recovers the setting of Diao's infinite-product theorem. For odd $k$,
a periodic coherent signing replaces that all-minus choice.

\section{The exact norm recurrence and generating function}
Define
\[
 q_n=\normsq{A_n}:=\sum_{m\in\Z}a_n(m)^2,
 \qquad
 B_n=\sum_{m\in\Z}a_{n-1}(w_n+m)a_{n-1}(m).
\]
All sums here and below are finite. Expanding the square in
$A_n=A_{n-1}+\eps_nx^{w_n}A_{n-1}$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:norm}
 q_n=2q_{n-1}+2\eps_n B_n.
\end{equation}
Flatness and \eqref{eq:paritylift} imply $q_n=h_k(n)$, but we first derive
the norm recurrence directly.

\begin{lemma}[The overlap inner product]\label[lemma]{lem:inner}
For $n\ge k+1$ and $j=n-k-1$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:inner}
 \eps_n B_n=q_j-q_{j+1}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Only $0\le u\le S_j$ can contribute to $B_n$. Substitute
\eqref{eq:low} and \eqref{eq:high}, abbreviating
$v=w_{j+1}$ and $e=\eps_{j+1}$:
\[
 \eps_nB_n=-\sum_{u=0}^{S_j}
 \bigl(a_j(u)+e a_j(u+v)\bigr)
 \bigl(a_j(u)+e a_j(u-v)\bigr).
\]
The product $a_j(u+v)a_j(u-v)$ vanishes for every $u$, by the same
separation $2v>S_j$ used in \cref{prop:pointwise}. The two mixed sums are
equal after a change of index. If
\[
 T=\sum_{u\in\Z}a_j(u)a_j(u-v),
\]
then the preceding display becomes $\eps_nB_n=-q_j-2eT$.
On the other hand, multiplication of $A_j$ by $1+ex^v$ yields
$q_{j+1}=2q_j+2eT$. Eliminating $T$ proves
$\eps_nB_n=q_j-q_{j+1}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{thm:main}(iii)]
The superincreasing seed block gives $q_n=2^n$ for $0\le n\le k$.
Substitution of \eqref{eq:inner} into \eqref{eq:norm} gives
\[
 q_n=2q_{n-1}-2q_{n-k}+2q_{n-k-1}\qquad(n\ge k+1).
\]
Since $q_n=h_k(n)$, this proves \eqref{eq:hinit}--\eqref{eq:hrec}.
Neither the coefficients of this recurrence nor its initial values depend
on the seeds or on the first $k$ signs.

Finally multiply $H_k(z)$ by
$D_k(z)=1-2z+2z^k-2z^{k+1}$. Its constant coefficient becomes one.
Its coefficients of degrees $1,\ldots,k-1$ vanish, its coefficient of
degree $k$ is two, and all later coefficients vanish by the recurrence.
Thus $D_kH_k=1+2z^k$, proving \eqref{eq:H}.
\end{proof}

By \cref{lem:originalseeds}, this is a proof of the statement of Stanley's
Conjecture~6.3. It also proves the seed-independent extension in
\cref{thm:main}.

\section{Examples, checks, and the role of the signs}
\subsection{The first genuinely overlapping order-three example}
The standard order-three weights are
\[
 1,3,5,9,17,31,57,105,193,\ldots.
\]
The canonical coherent signs repeat $+,+,+,-$. For the first four factors,
\begin{align*}
 A_4(x)&=(1+x)(1+x^3)(1+x^5)(1-x^9)\\
 &=1+x+x^3+x^4+x^5+x^6+x^8\\
 &\quad-x^{10}-x^{12}-x^{13}-x^{14}-x^{15}-x^{17}-x^{18}.
\end{align*}
There are fourteen nonzero coefficients. The ordinary all-plus product has
a coefficient two at $x^9$ and fourteen odd coefficients elsewhere.
The recurrence gives the same answer:
$h_3(4)=2\cdot8-2\cdot2+2\cdot1=14$.

A later example displays the disjoint-tail mechanism rather than just a
single cancellation. At $n=8$, we have $j=4$, $v=17$, and $S_j=18$.
The overlap is $105\le m\le123$. Formula \eqref{eq:pointwise} allows
nonzero coefficients there only at $m=105,106,122,123$; each is $-1$.
The fifteen intermediate positions cancel.

\subsection{Why the naive all-minus choice fails}
For the same weights,
\[
 [x^9](1-x)(1-x^3)(1-x^5)(1-x^9)=-2.
\]
The contributions come from the singleton $\{9\}$ and the triple
$\{1,3,5\}$; both have negative cardinality sign. An odd-order recurrence
replaces $k$ selected parts by one part without changing cardinality
parity. The coherent signing instead forces those two contributions to
have opposite signs. This explains why the proof must change the signing,
not merely reuse the all-minus Fibonacci product.

\section{A positive recurrence and a finite counting formula}
The minimal recurrence in \eqref{eq:hrec} has a negative coefficient. A
slightly longer one has only nonnegative coefficients and makes both
positivity and asymptotic analysis more transparent.

Put
\[
 B_k(z)=1+z+\cdots+z^{k-1},\qquad
 L_k(z)=z+z^2+\cdots+z^{k-1}+2z^{2k}.
\]
Direct multiplication gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:factor}
 B_k(z)D_k(z)=1-L_k(z).
\end{equation}
Indeed, $(1-z)B_k=1-z^k$, and the terms of degree $k$ cancel in
$B_k(1-2z)+2z^k(1-z)B_k$.

\begin{corollary}[Positive recurrence]\label[corollary]{cor:positive}
For every $n\ge2k$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:positive}
 h_k(n)=\sum_{r=1}^{k-1}h_k(n-r)+2h_k(n-2k).
\end{equation}
Equivalently,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:positiveGF}
 H_k(z)=\frac{(1+2z^k)B_k(z)}{1-L_k(z)}.
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Multiply numerator and denominator of \eqref{eq:H} by $B_k$ and use
\eqref{eq:factor}. The numerator has degree $2k-1$, so extracting
coefficients in degrees at least $2k$ proves \eqref{eq:positive}.
\end{proof}

Formula \eqref{eq:positiveGF} is the generating function of the following
simple objects. Choose a prefix of size $d+ek$, where $0\le d<k$ and
$e\in\{0,1\}$; the prefix has one color if $e=0$ and two colors if
$e=1$. Follow it by an ordered sequence of parts of sizes
$1,2,\ldots,k-1,2k$, where the part $2k$ has two colors and the other
parts each have one color. Total size is counted by $z$.

This is an exact equinumerosity obtained from the generating function.
It is not claimed to be an explicit bijection with the odd coefficient
positions of the original product.

In particular, an entirely finite, nonrecursive expression is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:multinomial}
 h_k(n)=\sum_{e=0}^1\sum_{d=0}^{k-1}
 \ \sum_{\substack{m_1,\ldots,m_{k-1},b\ge0\\
 \sum_{r=1}^{k-1}r m_r+2kb=n-d-ek}}
 2^{e+b}\frac{(m_1+\cdots+m_{k-1}+b)!}{m_1!\cdots m_{k-1}!b!}.
\end{equation}
An impossible constraint contributes zero. The multinomial coefficient
orders the chosen parts; the power of two supplies their colors and,
when applicable, the prefix color. This proves \eqref{eq:multinomial}
directly from the description above.

\begin{proposition}[Minimal recurrence order]\label[proposition]{prop:minimal}
Over $\Q$, the smallest order of an eventual homogeneous constant-coefficient
recurrence for $h_k(n)$ is $k+1$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The denominator $D_k$ has degree $k+1$. It is relatively prime to
$N_k(z)=1+2z^k$, because
\[
 D_k(z)=(1-2z)N_k(z)+2z^{k+1}.
\]
A common root would have to be zero, but $N_k(0)=1$. Thus the fraction
in \eqref{eq:H} is reduced. If a recurrence with denominator polynomial
$Q(z)$ holds eventually, $Q(z)H_k(z)$ is a polynomial, so the reduced
denominator $D_k$ divides $Q$. Its degree is therefore a lower bound
for the recurrence order. The recurrence already proved attains it.
\end{proof}

\section{Exponential asymptotics and density}
\subsection{The unique dominant root}
Let $r_k$ be the unique positive solution of $L_k(r_k)=1$, and write
$\lambda_k=r_k^{-1}$.

\begin{theorem}[Asymptotic count]\label{thm:asymptotic}
For every fixed $k\ge2$, there is a number $0<\mu_k<\lambda_k$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:asymptotic}
 h_k(n)=C_k\lambda_k^n+O(\mu_k^n),
 \qquad
 C_k=\frac{(1+2r_k^k)B_k(r_k)}{r_kL_k'(r_k)}>0.
\end{equation}
Moreover $1<\lambda_k<2$, and $\lambda_k$ is the unique real root
in $(1,2)$ of
\begin{equation}\label{eq:characteristic}
 \lambda^{k+1}-2\lambda^k+2\lambda-2=0.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
$L_k$ is strictly increasing on the positive real axis, with
$L_k(0)=0$ and $L_k(1)>1$. Thus $r_k\in(0,1)$ exists uniquely and
$L_k'(r_k)>0$. Also
\[
 L_k(1/2)=1-2^{1-k}+2^{1-2k}<1,
\]
so $r_k>1/2$.

For complex $z$ with $|z|<r_k$, the triangle inequality gives
$|L_k(z)|\le L_k(|z|)<1$. If $|z|=r_k$ and $L_k(z)=1$, equality
in the triangle inequality forces all the summands to have the same
argument, and their sum is positive real. The term $z$ occurs with
positive coefficient, so $z$ itself is positive real. Hence $z=r_k$.
This proves that $r_k$ is the only zero of $1-L_k$ on its smallest
root circle, and it is simple.

By \eqref{eq:positiveGF}, the singular part of $H_k$ at $r_k$ is
$C_k/(1-z/r_k)$, with $C_k$ as displayed. Subtracting it leaves a
rational function analytic in a disk of radius strictly larger than
$r_k$. Cauchy's coefficient bound on a circle inside that disk gives
the exponentially smaller error term in \eqref{eq:asymptotic}.
Finally, \eqref{eq:factor} and $B_k(r_k)>0$ give $D_k(r_k)=0$;
substituting $r_k=1/\lambda_k$ yields \eqref{eq:characteristic}.
Uniqueness follows from the uniqueness of the positive root of
$1-L_k$ and the fact that $B_k$ is positive on the positive axis.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Odd coefficients have zero density in the full range}
Let $\rho_k\in(1,2)$ satisfy
$\sum_{i=1}^k\rho_k^{-i}=1$, the growth equation of the exponent sequence.
For every admissible seed list, $S_n=\Theta(\rho_k^n)$. One can see this
without a spectral theorem: choose positive constants bounding
$w_i/\rho_k^i$ above and below for the first $k$ indices, and propagate
the bounds through \eqref{eq:recw}. Summing preserves the same
exponential order.

Put $s=\rho_k^{-1}$. Since the $k$ terms $s,\ldots,s^k$ sum to one,
we have $s^k<1/2$. Consequently
\[
 L_k(s)=1-s^k+2s^{2k}<1,
\]
so $s<r_k$ and $\lambda_k<\rho_k$. It follows that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:density}
 \frac{h_k(n)}{S_n+1}=O\!\left((\lambda_k/\rho_k)^n\right)\longrightarrow0.
\end{equation}
The denominator counts every coefficient position from zero to the degree,
including positions with coefficient zero. No assertion about density
\emph{among nonzero coefficients only} is implicit in \eqref{eq:density}.

\subsection{Large-order expansion}
There is also a useful asymptotic as $k\to\infty$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:largek}
 \lambda_k=2-2^{1-k}-(2k-4)\,4^{-k}+O(k^2 8^{-k}).
\end{equation}
Here is a justification that keeps the varying parameter under control.
Write $t=2^{-k}$ and $\delta=2-\lambda_k$. Equation
\eqref{eq:characteristic} becomes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:delta}
 \delta=2t(1-\delta)(1-\delta/2)^{-k}.
\end{equation}
For all sufficiently large $k$, the equivalent function
$f(\delta)=\delta(2-\delta)^k-2+2\delta$ is negative at zero and
positive at $4t$, since $f(4t)=4(1-2t)^k-2+8t\to2$.
The uniqueness already proved places the relevant root in $(0,4t)$.
Thus $k\delta\to0$, and Taylor expansion, uniformly on that interval,
gives
\[
 (1-\delta)(1-\delta/2)^{-k}
 =1+\frac{k-2}{2}\delta+O(k^2\delta^2).
\]
First \eqref{eq:delta} yields $\delta=2t+O(kt^2)$; substituting this
back gives $\delta=2t+2(k-2)t^2+O(k^2t^3)$, proving
\eqref{eq:largek}. This expansion is separate from the fixed-$k$,
large-$n$ asymptotic in \eqref{eq:asymptotic}.

\section{Exact algorithms}
\subsection{Counting without expanding a polynomial}
The recurrence computes $h_k(0),\ldots,h_k(N)$ in $O(N)$ integer
arithmetic operations after the initial block is available. A streaming
implementation needs only $k+1$ past values. The included
\texttt{count\_prefix} routine returns the whole list, so it intentionally
uses $O(N)$ integer storage instead.

For an isolated very large index, let
\[
 \chi_k(X)=X^{k+1}-2X^k+2X-2.
\]
Compute $X^N\bmod\chi_k(X)$ by binary powering. If the remainder is
$\sum_{i=0}^k b_iX^i$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fast}
 h_k(N)=\sum_{i=0}^k b_i2^i.
\end{equation}
To justify this, let $E$ be the shift operator on the sequence. The proved
recurrence says $\chi_k(E)h=0$ beginning at index zero in the shift-operator
form, so polynomial division applied to $E^N$ gives \eqref{eq:fast}.
Naive polynomial multiplication and reduction use
$O(k^2\log(N+1))$ ring operations. The same code works modulo any
positive integer; it does not assume the modulus is prime.

These are arithmetic-operation bounds, not bit-complexity bounds. In exact
arithmetic, $h_k(N)$ itself has $\Theta(N)$ bits for fixed $k$, by
\cref{thm:asymptotic}. Writing it already takes linear time in its bit length.

\subsection{A single coefficient's parity in linear many steps}
The pointwise proof gives an efficient coefficient oracle. Precompute the
weights, partial sums, and signs through index $n$. To evaluate $a_n(m)$,
first return zero if $m\notin[0,S_n]$. For $n>k$:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
\item If $m<w_n$, replace $(n,m)$ by $(n-1,m)$.
\item If $m>S_{n-1}$, replace $(n,m)$ by $(n-1,m-w_n)$ and multiply the
      accumulated sign by $\eps_n$.
\item Otherwise set $j=n-k-1$, $u=m-w_n$, and $v=w_{j+1}$.
      Formula \eqref{eq:pointwise} gives at most one continuing branch:
      use $a_j(u-v)$ if $u\ge v$; use $-a_j(u+v)$ if
      $u+v\le S_j$; otherwise return zero. In either continuing case
      multiply also by $\eps_n\eps_{j+1}$.
\end{enumerate}
When the index is at most $k$, a greedy subset-sum test on the
superincreasing seeds finishes the computation. At every step the index
strictly decreases; there is no branching tree. Hence the query uses
$O(n)$ arithmetic comparisons, additions, and sign changes after
$O(n+k)$-entry preprocessing (including validation of the seed block). The absolute value of the resulting signed
coefficient is the requested parity of the all-plus coefficient.

For fixed $k$ and seed bit length $b$, the stored exponents have
$O(n+b)$ bits. The arithmetic-step description therefore does not assert
constant-time operations on arbitrarily large exponents. Its principal
advantage is avoiding storage proportional to the exponentially large degree.

\subsection{Direct multiplication as an independent check}
An integer bitset represents a polynomial over $\F_2$ by storing its
coefficient of $x^m$ in bit $m$. Multiplication by $1+x^w$ is exactly
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
bits ^= bits << w
\end{lstlisting}
and the final odd-coefficient count is \texttt{bits.bit\_count()}.
This is an excellent small-instance oracle, but its memory requirement is
proportional to the polynomial degree. The supplied implementation has an
explicit degree cap to prevent accidental enormous allocations.

The signed sparse and dense checks perform integer polynomial multiplication,
not just multiplication modulo two. They therefore test the stronger
flatness claim and the sign-sensitive overlap identity as well.

\section{Further questions and boundaries of the method}
The proof exposes several natural next questions without resolving them.
For moduli greater than two, changing a factor's sign no longer leaves
its residue unchanged, so the parity lift is unavailable in this form.
A finite collection of root-of-unity-weighted products might replace it,
but flatness over $\{-1,0,1\}$ by itself supplies no such result.

The positive composition model provides an enumerative explanation for
$h_k(n)$ after the rational function has been proved. An explicit bijection
between its colored objects and odd coefficient positions would give a
different kind of explanation. None is asserted here.

It is also reasonable to ask which weaker seed conditions preserve the
same count or admit a related rational function. The proof uses the seed
condition twice: to obtain the initial values $2^n$, and to establish the
separation inequalities that make the tails disjoint. Changing only the
initial values without checking that separation would not justify reusing
the argument.

Finally, coherent signs were shown to be sufficient and completely
parameterized within the coherence rule. We did not classify \emph{all}
possible sign sequences whose finite products happen to be flat. Such a
necessity theorem is a separate question.

\section{Conclusion}
The selected conjectural identity follows from a simple structural fact:
with an appropriate periodic signing, the large central overlap cancels,
leaving two tails that are too far apart to interact. This proves flatness,
turns parity into a squared norm, and reduces the norm update to a
seed-independent recurrence.

The resulting theorem covers every recurrence order $k\ge2$, every
admissible seed list, every coherent signing, and every finite number of
factors. Its consequences include an exact rational generating function,
a minimal-order recurrence, an alternative positive recurrence, a
nonrecursive finite formula, precise exponential growth, and practical
exact algorithms. These conclusions are supported by the proofs above;
the attached computations are a reproducible audit rather than a
substitute for proof. Independent mathematical review and a fuller
priority check remain appropriate before presenting the result as a new
publication.

\section{A compact proof dependency map}
The logical order is
\[
\begin{gathered}
\text{seed separation and recurrence}\quad+\quad\text{coherent signs}\\
\Downarrow\\
\text{low-degree truncation and signed reflection}\\
\Downarrow\\
\text{pointwise two-tail identity}\\
\Downarrow\\
\text{flatness}\quad+\quad\text{overlap inner-product identity}\\
\Downarrow\\
\text{parity count recurrence and rational generating function}.
\end{gathered}
\]
In particular, neither an assumed rational generating function nor any
unproved description of a word-rewriting monoid is used in the main
argument. All boundary cases are covered by the convention that coefficient
functions vanish outside their finite ranges.
