\begin{abstract}
We study matrix groups and lattices over the omnific integers $\Oz$ and the
Gaussian omnific integers $\GOz$. Three phenomena are treated, each in its
own part and under its own hypotheses.

\emph{Algebraic groups.} A real algebraic group acquires no nonconstant
points over the ring of surreal normal forms with nonnegative exponents
exactly when it has no nonidentity real unipotent element, equivalently
when its identity component is reductive with compact real derived group.
For a closed subgroup scheme of $\GL_{N}$ over $\Z$ this gives an exact
dichotomy: either every omnific point is an ordinary integral point, or the
constant-term kernel contains an injective copy of the additive class of
purely infinite normal forms. Over the Gaussian omnific integers the
criterion is that the complex identity component be a torus. This answers,
for such group schemes, a question on rigid affine varieties left open in
the omnific Diophantine report.

\emph{Elementary groups.} For $n\ge3$ the kernel of constant-term reduction
$E_n(\Oz)\to\SL_n(\Z)$, and its Gaussian analogue, has no nontrivial
homomorphism to any set-sized group, so $\SL_n(\Z)$, respectively
$\SL_n(\Z[i])$, is the universal set-sized quotient; this strengthens the
universal ring-quotient theorem of the sibling report to arbitrary abstract
group maps. Two independent proofs are given, and a third that rebuilds a
ring from the root maps and so derives the group theorem from the ring
theorem. The kernel is torsion-free and centerless, has no nontrivial
set-sized normal subgroup, and its nonidentity conjugacy classes are proper
classes; the Steinberg groups have the analogous universal quotient; and in
the set-sized rings of supports of size below a regular uncountable
$\kappa$ every nonidentity kernel element has least detecting group of
cardinality exactly $\kappa^{<\kappa}$. In rank two the answer
reverses: $E_2$ is an amalgam, its kernel is a free product of copies of
the purely infinite ideal, and one fixed countable group separates all
elements, while finite groups see nothing of the kernel. Explicit
non-elementary $2\times2$ unipotents become commutators after one
stabilization.

\emph{Lattices.} For finite sums of ordinary positive-semidefinite forms with
successively infinitesimal weights we give exact criteria for shortest
nonzero vectors, shortest unimodular vectors and closest points to every
surreal target. When a shortest vector fails to exist the energy values have
no surreal infimum, their lower bounds are an explicit cut, and one monomial
vector improves every set-sized family at once; usual Gauss and LLL reduced
bases then do not exist.

This report merges four independently written manuscripts; it states which
source proved each result, prints shared results once, and keeps every
source's limitations. It is an AI-assisted draft: it has not been refereed,
and nothing in it has been formalized in Lean.
\end{abstract}

\section{Scope, results, and conventions}\label{ogl:sec:scope}

An omnific integer has an ordinary integer constant coefficient and an
arbitrary, possibly transfinite, purely infinite part whose coefficients are
arbitrary real numbers. This mixture makes linear algebra over $\Oz$ quite
different both from linear algebra over $\Z$ and from linear algebra over the
surreal field. This report studies three linear questions over $\Oz$ and over
the Gaussian omnific integers $\GOz$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\Roman*),leftmargin=*]
\item Which algebraic groups acquire new omnific points
(\cref{ogl:alg:part})?
\item Can the purely infinite directions of an elementary matrix group act on
any ordinary set, when no algebraicity or continuity is imposed on the action
(\cref{ogl:el:part})?
\item Does a positive-definite form on $\Oz^n$ have a shortest vector, and
what replaces it when it does not (\cref{ogl:lat:part})?
\end{enumerate}
The answers reveal three distinct mechanisms: geometric rigidity controlled by
unipotent elements; an abstract representation obstruction controlled by the
supply of surreal exponent scales and a third matrix index; and a competition
between the constant-term retraction and monomial vectors lying on irrational
kernels.

\subsection{Principal results}\label{ogl:sub:results}

Bracketed numbers name the source manuscripts (\cref{ogl:sub:provenance}).
Here $(D,k)$ is $(\Z,\R)$ or $(\Z[i],\C)$, $\Pi_k$ is the class of normal
forms with coefficients in $k$ supported on strictly positive exponents,
$\Bk=k\oplus\Pi_k$, and $\A_{D,k}=D\oplus\Pi_k$, so that $\A_{\Z,\R}=\Oz$ and
$\A_{\Z[i],\C}=\GOz$ (\cref{ogl:sub:notation}).

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\arabic*)},leftmargin=*,itemsep=3pt]
\item \textbf{Real omnific rigidity} \src{06}. For a real algebraic group $G$:
$G(\BR)=G(\R)$ iff $G(\R)$ has no unipotent element other than $1$ iff $G$ has
no subgroup isomorphic to $\Ga$ iff $G^0$ is reductive with $(G^0)^{\der}(\R)$
compact. For a closed subgroup scheme $\cG\subseteq\GL_{N,\Z}$ these are
equivalent to $\cG(\Oz)=\cG(\Z)$; otherwise $(\Pi,+)$ embeds in the kernel of
$\ct:\cG(\Oz)\to\cG(\Z)$ (\cref{ogl:alg:thm:real}).
\item \textbf{Gaussian omnific rigidity} \src{06}. For a complex algebraic group,
$G(\BC)=G(\C)$ iff $G^0$ is a torus; the same holds for $\Z[i]$-models and
$\GOz$ (\cref{ogl:alg:thm:complex}). Thus $\SO_3(\Oz)$ has $24$ elements
while $\SO_3(\GOz)$ contains injective unipotent families. Together, (1) and
(2) answer the question \replabel{odg:q:affine} of the omnific Diophantine
report for closed subgroup schemes of $\GL_N$ (\cref{ogl:alg:rem:affine}).
\item \textbf{Bounded fibers, unipotent kernels, filtrations} \src{06}. Bounded
real fibers over a torus give rigidity (\cref{ogl:alg:thm:fibers}); the
constant-term kernel of a unipotent group is $\exp(\mathfrak u\otimes\Pi_k)$
(\cref{ogl:alg:prop:bch}); $\UT_n(\Pi_k)$ has the exact filtration
$[\Fil_r,\Fil_s]=\Fil_{r+s}$ and nontrivial characters
(\cref{ogl:alg:thm:ut}).
\item \textbf{The elementary kernel for $n\ge3$} \src{06, 07, 11}. Let
$P_n=\ker(\ct:E_n(\A_{D,k})\to\SL_n(D))$. It is generated by the elementary
matrices with purely infinite parameters, it is perfect and nontrivial,
$E_n(\A_{D,k})=P_n\rtimes\SL_n(D)$, and every homomorphism from $P_n$ to a
set-sized group is trivial. Hence composition with $\ct$ identifies the
homomorphisms from $E_n(\A_{D,k})$ and from $\SL_n(D)$ to every set-sized group
(\cref{ogl:el:thm:invisible}). Two independent proofs are printed. The theorem
implies, for $k\in\{\R,\C\}$, the universal ring-quotient theorem
\replabel{osq:thm:universal} (\cref{ogl:el:rem:ring}); conversely a third
proof (11) rebuilds a set-sized ring from the root maps of a group
homomorphism and derives the theorem from the ring theorem
(\cref{ogl:ker:thm:rootkernel}).
\item \textbf{A congruence tower and the boundaries} \src{06, 07}. $P_n$ is not
simple: a proper-class chain of congruence subgroups separates its elements,
though no set-sized subfamily does (\cref{ogl:el:thm:tower}). The theorem fails
in every fixed set-sized Hahn workspace and for finite supports
(\cref{ogl:el:sec:boundaries}); an abstract collision criterion isolates the
size input (\cref{ogl:el:prop:criterion}). On the finite side,
$\SL_n$ of the finite surreals has universal set-sized quotient $\SL_n(k)$
for $n\ge3$ \mergetag{} (\cref{ogl:el:prop:finiteside}).
\item \textbf{Rank two} \src{07}. $E_2(\A_{D,k})\cong\SL_2(D)*_{C_D}B_D$; the kernel
$P_2$ is a free product of copies of $(\Pi_k,+)$ indexed by $\PP^1(\Frac D)$;
homomorphisms into the fixed countable group $\SL_2(\Frac(D)(t))$ separate
every finite subset of $E_2$; neither $E_2$ nor $P_2$ has a universal
set-sized quotient (\cref{ogl:el:thm:amalgam,ogl:el:thm:freeproduct,ogl:el:thm:countable}).
\item \textbf{The detection trichotomy} \src{07}. A nonidentity $g\in E_n$ is
detected by a finite group iff $\ct(g)\ne1$; a nonidentity element of $P_2$
has least detecting cardinal $\aleph_0$; a nonidentity element of $P_n$,
$n\ge3$, has no set-sized detecting target (\cref{ogl:el:thm:trichotomy}).
\item \textbf{Characters and stabilization} \src{07}. $E_2(\Oz)^{\ab}\cong\Z/12\oplus\Pi$
(classical, Cohn; Mirzaii--Torres), while every abelian image of $E_2(\GOz)$
kills $P_2$ (\cref{ogl:el:prop:realab,ogl:el:prop:gaussianab}). A cusp-residue
obstruction shows that $I+\omega^{2a}\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha&1\\-\alpha^2&-\alpha\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$,
$\alpha\in k\setminus\Frac D$, lies in $\SL_2\setminus E_2$; after one
stabilization it is a commutator of two elements of $P_3$
(\cref{ogl:el:thm:nonel,ogl:el:thm:stabilize}).
\item \textbf{Structure of the kernel, Steinberg groups and set-sized
fragments} \src{11}. For $n\ge3$ the kernel $P_n$ is torsion-free (so is the
constant-term kernel of $\GL_n(\A_{D,k})$ for every $n$) and centerless, it
has no nontrivial set-sized normal subgroup, every nonidentity element has a
proper-class conjugacy class, and finite subgroups of $E_n(\A_{D,k})$ inject
under $\ct$
(\cref{ogl:ker:thm:torsionfree,ogl:ker:thm:center,ogl:ker:cor:normalsets,ogl:ker:prop:finitesubgroups}).
The Steinberg group $\St_n(\A_{D,k})$ has universal set-sized quotient
$\St_n(D)$ and a perfect kernel with no set-sized images
(\cref{ogl:ker:thm:steinberg}). With the Bass--Milnor--Serre theorem the
profinite completion of $E_n(\Oz)$ is $\SL_n(\widehat\Z)$
(\cref{ogl:ker:cor:profinite}). In the set-sized ring of normal forms with
fewer than $\kappa$ exponents in $\bigoplus_{\alpha<\kappa}\Q$, $\kappa$
regular uncountable, the kernel has no nontrivial image of cardinality below
$\kappa$ (\cref{ogl:ker:thm:fragmentgroup}); combined with the sibling
report's field bound, each nonidentity kernel element has least detecting
group of cardinality exactly $\kappa^{<\kappa}$ \mergetag{}
(\cref{ogl:ker:prop:exact}).
\item \textbf{Shortest vectors and missing infima} \src{10}. For a separated
positive pencil $\cE=\sum_j\varepsilon_jq_j$ on $\A_{D,k}^n$: a shortest nonzero
vector exists iff the deepest kernel meets $D^n$ nontrivially; otherwise the
nonnegative lower bounds of the energy values are exactly
$\varepsilon_m(\Ofin)_{\ge0}$ and one monomial vector improves every set-sized
family (\cref{ogl:lat:thm:main,ogl:lat:thm:simultaneous}).
\item \textbf{Unimodular vectors and closest points} \src{10}. A rational-span
criterion decides unimodular attainment, and unimodular energies always have a
countable coinitial family (\cref{ogl:lat:thm:unimodular}); closest points
exist for every surreal target iff every prefix kernel is rational, and then
the closest points lie among at most $2^n$ (real) or $4^n$ (Gaussian) ordinary
candidates after removing the target's purely infinite part
(\cref{ogl:lat:thm:closestflag,ogl:lat:thm:alltargets}). The ordinary
backbone of these statements is already in the Hahn--Tate report and is cited
there (\cref{ogl:lat:sub:tate}).
\item \textbf{Reduction obstructions} \src{10}. In the nonattainment case no
Gauss-reduced basis and no $\delta$-LLL basis with ordinary
$\delta\in(1/4,1]$ exists, and a Gaussian variant holds for $\delta\in(1/2,1]$
(\cref{ogl:lat:thm:nolll,ogl:lat:thm:gaussianlll}).
\end{enumerate}

These statements are proved here, but proving a statement here is not a claim
that nobody has previously proved it. Each source names its own most
distinctive contributions and credits its classical inputs
(Appendix~\ref{ogl:app:nonclaims}); no exhaustive historical-priority claim is made.

\subsection{Class size and foundations}\label{ogl:sub:foundations}

The surreal field $\No$, the rings $\Oz$ and $\GOz$, and most groups below are
proper classes. Sources 06 and 07 work in G\"odel--Bernays set theory with
global choice, source 10 in NBG; 07 and 06 note that global choice is never
used, and no proof printed here uses it. Source 11 allows G\"odel--Bernays
class theory with global choice and uses choice only for sets (a successor
cardinal above a target, and the equivalence of reverse well ordering with the
absence of increasing sequences); its set-sized fragments give ordinary ZFC
statements. Each individual surreal, normal-form
support, matrix, tuple and finite word is a set. A homomorphism from a class
group is a class function satisfying the group identities; statements about
all such homomorphisms are read one class function at a time, and no set of
all such maps is formed. Residual properties are universally quantified kernel
statements. A class quotient may be read as a kernel with its universal
property, or through Scott's trick (06).

Every cardinality contradiction is a contradiction about sets: for a target
set $H$ one produces, inside the source, a set of parameters that cannot inject
into $H$. Sources 06 and 07 choose a cardinal $\lambda>|H|$; the Hartogs ordinal
of $H$ serves equally and needs no choice, as in the sibling report
(\replabel{osq:thm:universal}). No cardinal is assigned to a proper class.
Source 07 adds that the class statements concern class homomorphisms existing
in the chosen foundational setting, not externally described maps in a larger
metatheory.

\subsection{Notation and conventions}\label{ogl:sub:notation}

\paragraph{Sign convention.} Exponents are those of Conway normal forms:
supports are reverse well ordered and $\omega^\gamma$ is \emph{infinite} for
$\gamma>0$, even for infinitesimal $\gamma>0$. \emph{Purely infinite} refers to
support: $-\omega$ and $\sqrt2\,\omega$ are purely infinite, $\omega-1$ is
infinite but not purely infinite, and $\omega^{-1}$ is not purely infinite. In
the Hahn convention $t=\omega^{-1}$ all exponent signs change. This agrees with
the omnific reports, whose letter $\Pi$ we use for the purely infinite ideal
(\replabel{found:eq:omnific} in the foundations report).

\paragraph{Two constant maps.} $\ct$ extracts the coefficient of $\omega^0$
from an element of $\Bk$; it is a ring homomorphism there, not on $\No$
(both $\omega$ and $\omega^{-1}$ have constant coefficient $0$). $\st$ is the
standard part of a \emph{finite} surreal or surcomplex number. For example
$\ct(\omega+7)=7$, although $\omega+7$ is not finite. The two are never
interchanged.

\paragraph{Two notions of finite.} ``Finite surreal'' means bounded in modulus
by an ordinary integer, not a normal form with finite support (10): the

\section{Normal forms, constant terms, and support gaps}\label{ogl:sec:background}

This section collects the facts about normal forms used in all three parts.
All four sources prove them (source 11 its units, gap and single-product
facts), and so do the omnific reports
(\replabel{osq:lem:ct}, \replabel{osq:lem:gap}, \replabel{osq:prop:common},
\replabel{odg:prop:units}); they are background, not new results.

\subsection{Normal forms}\label{ogl:sub:normalforms}

Every surreal number has a unique Conway normal form
\begin{equation}\label{ogl:eq:nf}
 f=\sum_{s\in S}a_s\omega^s,\qquad a_s\in\R\setminus\{0\},
\end{equation}
where $S\subseteq\No$ is a set and is reverse well ordered: every nonempty
subset has a greatest element \cite{Conway,Gonshor,Berarducci}. Addition is
coefficientwise; multiplication is Hahn convolution, and the coefficient of a
product at a fixed exponent is a finite sum. The sign of a nonzero normal form is
the sign of its leading coefficient, and $\omega^{s+t}=\omega^s\omega^t$ is
Conway's monomial law, not an analytic exponential. Complex coefficients give
exactly $\SC$, by separating real and imaginary parts; a finite union of
reverse-well-ordered supports is reverse well ordered, so no larger support
convention is needed. For $f\in\SC$, $|f|=(f\bar f)^{1/2}$; the leading
coefficient of $f\bar f$ is the squared modulus of the leading coefficient of
$f$ (07).

The Hahn support lemma is used freely: products are defined, and a series
supported on strictly negative exponents has a formal geometric inverse near
$1$, so that for $\gamma>0$
\begin{equation}\label{ogl:eq:geom}
 (1-\omega^{-\gamma})^{-1}=\sum_{m\ge0}\omega^{-m\gamma}.
\end{equation}
This is an identity of normal forms. It does not assert convergence of a
sequence in the fine topology of $\No$ (06, 07, 10).

\subsection{The rings and the constant term}\label{ogl:sub:rings}

For $k\in\{\R,\C\}$ and a unital subring $D\subseteq k$ put
\begin{equation}\label{ogl:eq:rings}
 \Pi_k=\{f:\supp f\subseteq\No^{>0}\},\qquad
 \Bk=k\oplus\Pi_k,\qquad
 \A_{D,k}=D\oplus\Pi_k,
\end{equation}
with coefficients in $k$. The zero series belongs to $\Pi_k$. Then
$\Oz=\A_{\Z,\R}$ and $\GOz=\A_{\Z[i],\C}$; $\BR=\A_{\R,\R}$ and $\BC=\A_{\C,\C}$
are the nonnegative-support rings.

\begin{lemma}[Constant term, units and discreteness \src{06, 07, 10}]\label{ogl:lem:units}
\leavevmode
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\Pi_k$ is an ideal of $\Bk$ and of $\A_{D,k}$, and
$\ct:\A_{D,k}\to D$ is a surjective ring homomorphism with kernel $\Pi_k$,
split by the inclusion of constants. It commutes with conjugation and with
every ordinary finite matrix.
\item $\ddeg(fg)=\ddeg f+\ddeg g$ for nonzero $f,g\in\Bk$, and
$\Bk^\times=k^\times$, $\A_{D,k}^\times=D^\times$. In particular
$\Oz^\times=\{\pm1\}$ and $\GOz^\times=\{\pm1,\pm i\}$.
\item An element of $\Bk$ with a nonzero positive-exponent part has infinite
modulus. Hence $\Bk$ has no nonzero infinitesimal, its finite elements are
exactly $k$, the finite elements of $\A_{D,k}$ are exactly $D$, every nonzero
element of $\A_{D,k}$ for $D\in\{\Z,\Z[i]\}$ has modulus at least $1$, and
every vector of $\A_{D,k}^n\setminus D^n$ has infinite Euclidean norm.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
All exponents occurring are nonnegative, and a sum of two nonnegative
exponents is zero only when both are zero; so the constant coefficient of a
product is the product of the constant coefficients, and a product with a
factor in $\Pi_k$ has positive support. Additivity, the kernel and the section
are immediate. Commutation with conjugation and ordinary matrices is
coefficientwise.

Hahn multiplication has leading coefficient the product of the leading
coefficients, which gives the degree formula. If $fg=1$ with $f,g\in\Bk$,
both degrees are nonnegative and add to zero, so both are zero; a normal form
with nonnegative support and leading exponent $0$ is constant. Intersecting
with $\A_{D,k}$ and applying the argument to the inverse gives
$\A_{D,k}^\times=D^\times$.

If the positive-exponent part is nonzero, it has a greatest exponent
$\gamma>0$, and the leading term of the absolute value (real case) or of the
squared modulus (complex case) is $|c|\omega^\gamma$ or $|c|^2\omega^{2\gamma}$,
which dominates every ordinary constant; adding a constant does not change
this. Nonzero integers and Gaussian integers have modulus at least one. For
vectors apply this to one nonconstant coordinate.
\end{proof}

Negative exponents are what matter, not size: $\omega+\omega^{-1}$ is infinite
but does not lie in $\BR$, and $\ct$ is not order preserving on $\BR$, since
$\omega-1>0$ while $\ct(\omega-1)=-1$ (06, 10).

\begin{lemma}[Bounded coordinates are constant \src{06}]\label{ogl:lem:bounded}
If $f\in\BR$ and $|f|\le M$ for an ordinary real $M$, then $f\in\R$; hence
$\Oz\cap\R=\Z$. If $z\in\BC$ has real and imaginary parts bounded by ordinary
reals, then $z\in\C$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
This is \cref{ogl:lem:units}(iii), applied coordinatewise.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Gaps below sets of exponents}\label{ogl:sub:gaps}

\begin{lemma}[Gaps \src{06, 07, 10}]\label{ogl:lem:gap}
\leavevmode
\begin{enumerate}
\item Every set of surreal numbers has a strict upper bound and a strict lower
bound in $\No$.
\item For every set $S\subseteq\No^{>0}$ there is $\delta>0$ with $m\delta<s$
for all $s\in S$ and all ordinary $m\ge1$.
\item For every set-indexed family $(h_s)_{s\in S}$ of positive infinite
surreals there is $\gamma>0$ with $\omega^{2\gamma}\ll h_s$ for every $s$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For a set $T$, the cuts $\{T\mid\varnothing\}$ and $\{\varnothing\mid T\}$
give (i). For (ii) apply the set cut $\{0\mid T\}$ to
$T=\{s/m:s\in S,\ m\ge1\}$, and take $\delta=1$ for empty $S$. For (iii) apply
(ii) to the set $\{\ddeg h_s\}$ of positive exponents and take $\gamma=\delta/2$;
the leading exponent of $\omega^{2\gamma}/h_s$ is $2\gamma-\ddeg h_s<0$.
\end{proof}

The full-class hypothesis is substantive (06, 07, 10). The support
$\{1/m:m\ge1\}$ of $\sum_{m\ge1}\omega^{1/m}\in\Pi$ has no positive \emph{real}
lower bound, but it has a positive surreal one, for instance $1/\omega$.
Confusing the two assertions would invalidate the main proofs of Parts II and
III; in a fixed exponent group such as $\R$ they fail
(\cref{ogl:el:sec:boundaries,ogl:lat:sec:scope}).

\begin{proposition}[Single-product divisibility \src{06, 07}]\label{ogl:prop:factor}
For every set-sized family $\mathcal F\subseteq\Pi_k$ there is $\delta>0$ with
$\omega^{-\delta}f\in\Pi_k$ for all $f\in\mathcal F$. In particular every
$c\in\Pi_k$ is a product $c=\omega^\delta(\omega^{-\delta}c)$ of two elements
of $\Pi_k$, and $\Pi_k^r=\Pi_k$ for every ordinary $r\ge1$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The union of the supports is a set of positive exponents; choose $\delta$
strictly below it by \cref{ogl:lem:gap}. Translating supports by $-\delta$
keeps them positive. Induction gives the powers.
\end{proof}

This is \replabel{osq:prop:common}(i),(ii) of the sibling report.
\part{Omnific points of algebraic groups}\label{ogl:alg:part}

This part is source 06's. An algebraic group is affine and of finite type over
a field unless stated otherwise; for a real algebraic group $G$, $G^0$ is its
\emph{algebraic} identity component, not the identity component of the real
Lie group $G(\R)$. The main theorems are \cref{ogl:alg:thm:real} and
\cref{ogl:alg:thm:complex}.

\section{A bounded-fiber rigidity principle}\label{ogl:alg:sec:fibers}

\subsection{Tori and finite schemes}
A torus over $k$ is an algebraic group that becomes a product of copies of
$\Gm$ over an algebraic closure of $k$.

\begin{lemma}[Torus freezing \src{06}]\label{ogl:alg:lem:torus}
For every real torus $T$, $T(\BR)=T(\R)$. For every complex torus $T$,
$T(\BC)=T(\C)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
A complex torus is isomorphic over $\C$ to $\Gm^r$, and its $\BC$-points are
$(\BC^\times)^r=(\C^\times)^r$ by \cref{ogl:lem:units}. For a real torus,
extend scalars from $\BR$ to $\BR\otimes_\R\C=\BC$. After a complex splitting
the resulting point is constant by the complex case; hence the original point
is a constant complex point fixed by the real conjugation descent datum, and
such points are exactly $T(\R)$. This is ordinary Galois descent for points;
$\BR\hookrightarrow\BC$ is injective.
\end{proof}

The complex case is the special case, for the coordinate ring of a torus, of
the remark in the omnific Diophantine report that an affine complex variety
whose coordinate ring is generated by units has only constant $\BC$-points
(subsection ``An algebraic-geometric formulation'' of
\replabel{odg:sec:rigidity}).

\begin{lemma}[Finite schemes do not acquire points \src{06}]\label{ogl:alg:lem:finite}
If $X$ is a finite scheme over $k=\R$ or $\C$, or more generally a
zero-dimensional affine scheme of finite type, then $X(\Bk)=X(k)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Every element of the finite-dimensional coordinate algebra $k[X]$ is algebraic
over $k$, so its image under a $\Bk$-point is algebraic over $k$ in $F$. The
only elements of $\No$ algebraic over $\R$ are real, because $\R$ is real
closed and $\No$ is an ordered field extension; the complex case follows
because $\C$ is algebraically closed. Nilpotents map to $0$. The coordinate
algebra of a zero-dimensional affine scheme of finite type is Artinian and
finite-dimensional, so no reducedness is needed.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Bounded real fibers}

\begin{theorem}[Bounded fibers above a torus \src{06}]\label{ogl:alg:thm:fibers}
Let $X\hookrightarrow\mathbb A_\R^m$ be an affine real scheme of finite type,
$T$ a real torus and $\pi:X\to T$ a morphism. Suppose that for every
$t\in T(\R)$ the real points of the fiber $X_t$ are bounded in the given affine
coordinates. Then $X(\BR)=X(\R)$. No bound uniform in $t$ is required.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $x\in X(\BR)$. By \cref{ogl:alg:lem:torus}, $\pi(x)=t\in T(\R)$. Applying
$\ct$ gives a real point $\ct(x)$ of $X_t$, so that fiber is nonempty; choose
an ordinary $M>0$ bounding all coordinates of $X_t(\R)$. The statement that
every solution of the finitely many polynomial equations of $X_t$ satisfies
$-M\le x_j\le M$ is a first-order formula of ordered fields with real
parameters. Every embedding of real closed fields is elementary (Tarski's
quantifier elimination; see \cite{Marker}). Apply this to $\R\subset K$, where
$K\subset\No$ is the real closure inside $\No$ of the field generated by $\R$
and the coordinates of $x$; it is a set. So the bounds hold for $x$, and each
coordinate of $x$ is real by \cref{ogl:lem:bounded}.
\end{proof}

The only transfer used is transfer between real closed \emph{fields}. No
elementary equivalence of $\Z$ and $\Oz$, and no transfer in a language with an
omnific-integer predicate, is asserted (06). The same transfer proves the
bounded semialgebraic rigidity theorem \replabel{odg:thm:bounded}; for
algebraic sets, the case of the trivial torus of \cref{ogl:alg:thm:fibers}
gives the constant-point form of that theorem, while \replabel{odg:thm:bounded}
covers bounded semialgebraic sets and \cref{ogl:alg:thm:fibers} covers
unbounded algebraic sets with bounded fibers over a torus. Neither contains the
other.

\begin{corollary}[Complex zero-dimensional fibers \src{06}]\label{ogl:alg:cor:quasifinite}
If $X$ is affine of finite type over $\C$ and $\pi:X\to T$ is a morphism to a
complex torus whose fibers over complex points are zero-dimensional, then
$X(\BC)=X(\C)$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Freeze $\pi(x)$ by \cref{ogl:alg:lem:torus}, then apply
\cref{ogl:alg:lem:finite} to that fiber.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Compact-by-torus groups \src{06}]\label{ogl:alg:cor:compacttorus}
Let $G$ be a connected real reductive group whose derived group $H=G^{\der}$
has compact real points. Then $G(\BR)=G(\R)$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The quotient $T=G/H$ is a torus, by the structure theory of connected
reductive groups \cite{MilneAG}. A fiber with a real point $g$ has real points
$gH(\R)$, a compact set, so its coordinates are bounded in any affine
embedding; empty real fibers are bounded too. Apply \cref{ogl:alg:thm:fibers}.
\end{proof}

\status{The bounded-fiber formulation is 06's bridge between normal-form
rigidity and algebraic groups. Its inputs are unit rigidity, classical torus
splitting and real-closed-field transfer; it generalizes the bounded-coordinate
and constant-product arguments of the omnific Diophantine report without
treating them as new.}

\section{Unipotents and the classification}\label{ogl:alg:sec:dichotomy}

\subsection{A polynomial subgroup from one unipotent}

A matrix $u\in\GL_N(k)$ is unipotent if $u-1_N$ is nilpotent; the notion does
not depend on the faithful representation.

\begin{lemma}[Polynomial one-parameter subgroup \src{06}]\label{ogl:alg:lem:exp}
Let $k$ have characteristic zero, $G\subseteq\GL_{N,k}$ an algebraic group
and $1_N\ne u\in G(k)$ unipotent. Then
\[
 N_0=\log u=\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\frac{(-1)^{j+1}}j(u-1_N)^j\ne0,\qquad
 v(t)=\exp(tN_0)=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\frac{t^jN_0^j}{j!}
\]
define a closed immersion $v:\Ga\hookrightarrow G$, with $v(s+t)=v(s)v(t)$ and
$v(-t)=v(t)^{-1}$ over every $k$-algebra.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The exponential and logarithm identities used are identities modulo a fixed
nilpotence power, hence polynomial identities over $\Q$; so $N_0$ is nilpotent,
$\exp N_0=u$ and $N_0\ne0$. For each integer $m\ge0$, $v(m)=u^m\in G(k)$, and
$\det v(t)=1$. Each defining equation of $G$ pulls back to a polynomial in
$k[t]$ vanishing at all nonnegative integers, hence to zero, so $v$ lands in
$G$. If $(N_0)_{pq}\ne0$, the polynomial function
$g\mapsto(\log g)_{pq}/(N_0)_{pq}$ recovers $t$ on the image, so
$k[G]\to k[t]$ is surjective: $v$ is a closed immersion and is injective on
points. The group laws follow from the commuting nilpotent exponential
identities.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Purely infinite parameters \src{06}]\label{ogl:alg:cor:expI}
Let $(D,k)=(\Z,\R)$ or $(\Z[i],\C)$. If the $k$-fiber of a closed subgroup
scheme $\cG\subseteq\GL_{N,D}$ contains a nonidentity unipotent, then the map
of \cref{ogl:alg:lem:exp} restricts to an injective homomorphism
\[
 v:(\Pi_k,+)\hookrightarrow\cG(\A_{D,k}),\qquad \ct(v(a))=1_N .
\]
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
For $a\in\Pi_k$ every positive-degree term of $v(a)$ has entries in $\Pi_k$,
which is closed under multiplication and under multiplication by $k$; so
$v(a)$ and $v(-a)$ have entries in $D+\Pi_k$. The $D$-equations of $\cG$
vanish, since their substitution into $v(t)$ is the zero polynomial. The
constant term is $1_N$, and $\log v(a)=aN_0$ gives injectivity.
\end{proof}

Rationality of the unipotent is unnecessary: irrational coefficients of $N_0$
multiply purely infinite terms and do not affect the integral constant
coefficient.

\subsection{The real structural input}

A connected linear algebraic group in characteristic zero has a unipotent
radical $R_u(G)$ and is reductive when it is trivial; for connected reductive
$G$ the derived group is connected semisimple and $G/G^{\der}$ is a torus; a
nontrivial unipotent group over $\R$ has nontrivial real points, and its
exponential is an isomorphism of schemes from its Lie algebra
\cite[Proposition~14.32]{MilneAG}.

\begin{lemma}[Real semisimple compactness criterion \src{06}]\label{ogl:alg:lem:compact}
A connected semisimple real algebraic group $H$ has no nonidentity real
unipotent element if and only if $H(\R)$ is compact.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
If $H(\R)$ is compact, average an inner product in a faithful representation;
every element is then orthogonal, and an orthogonal unipotent matrix is the
identity because orthogonal matrices are diagonalizable over $\C$.

Conversely, by Cartan's theorem a faithful representation has a basis in which
$H$ is stable under transpose \cite[Theorem~1.16 and Example~1.17(c)]{MilneSV};
so its real Lie algebra $\mathfrak h$ is stable under transpose. If every
element of $\mathfrak h$ is skew-symmetric, the real Lie identity component of
$H(\R)$ is a closed subgroup of the orthogonal group, hence compact, and a real
algebraic group has finitely many real connected components; so $H(\R)$ is
compact. Otherwise there is a nonzero symmetric $S\in\mathfrak h$. As
$\mathfrak h$ is semisimple its center is zero, so $\operatorname{ad}S\ne0$;
for $\langle A,B\rangle=\tr(AB^{\mathsf T})$ the operator $A\mapsto[S,A]$ is
self-adjoint (permute the factors cyclically in the trace), so it has an
eigenvector $Y\ne0$ with real eigenvalue $\lambda\ne0$. Diagonalize $S$
orthogonally; $[S,Y]=\lambda Y$ makes $Y$ map the $s$-eigenspace of $S$ into
the $(s+\lambda)$-eigenspace, so $Y$ is nilpotent. The Lie exponential of $tY$,
here a finite polynomial, lies in $H(\R)$, and at $t=1$ it is a nonidentity
unipotent (the truncated logarithm recovers $Y$).
\end{proof}

The proof uses ordinary real Lie theory only at the Cartan and exponential
steps and no global exponential on $\SC$. One may also obtain the noncompact
direction from real rank and relative root groups \cite[Sections~22--23]{MilneRG}.

\subsection{The two dichotomies}

\begin{theorem}[Omnific rigidity and real unipotents \src{06}]\label{ogl:alg:thm:real}
For a real algebraic group $G$ the following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $G(\BR)=G(\R)$;
\item $G(\R)$ has no unipotent element other than the identity;
\item $G$ has no real algebraic subgroup isomorphic to $\Ga$;
\item $G^0$ is reductive and $(G^0)^{\der}(\R)$ is compact.
\end{enumerate}
Let $\cG\hookrightarrow\GL_{N,\Z}$ be a closed subgroup scheme and $G=\cG_\R$.
The conditions are also equivalent to $\cG(\Oz)=\cG(\Z)$. When they fail there
is an injective homomorphism
\[
 (\Pi,+)\longrightarrow\ker\bigl(\ct:\cG(\Oz)\to\cG(\Z)\bigr),\qquad
 a\longmapsto\exp(aN),
\]
for a fixed nonzero real nilpotent matrix $N$, the exponential being a finite
polynomial; and every ordinary integral point $q$ lies on the injectively
parametrized family $a\mapsto q\exp(aN)$ of omnific points.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Assume (2). The unipotent radical of $G^0$ is trivial, so $G^0$ is reductive;
its derived group has no real unipotents, hence compact real points by
\cref{ogl:alg:lem:compact}; so (4) holds and $G^0(\BR)=G^0(\R)$ by
\cref{ogl:alg:cor:compacttorus}. For $g\in G(\BR)$ put $g_0=\ct(g)\in G(\R)$.
The component group $\pi_0(G)=G/G^0$ is finite, so its $\BR$-points are
constant (\cref{ogl:alg:lem:finite}); $g$ and $g_0$ have the same image there,
hence $g_0^{-1}g\in G^0(\BR)$ is real, and applying $\ct$ shows it is $1$.
Thus (2) implies (1), and (4) implies (1) by the same component argument.

If $G(\R)$ has a nonidentity unipotent, \cref{ogl:alg:lem:exp} (over $\BR$)
gives nonconstant points and a real $\Ga$ subgroup; conversely a $\Ga$
subgroup gives nonconstant points by substituting a purely infinite parameter
into its nonconstant polynomial coordinates, and its real points are
unipotent. So (1), (2), (3) are equivalent, and (2) implies (4) was shown.

For the integral model: in the rigid case an omnific point is a constant
point of $G$, whose matrix entries and inverse-determinant coordinate lie in
$\Oz\cap\R=\Z$ (\cref{ogl:lem:bounded}); so it is an ordinary integral point.
In the nonrigid case \cref{ogl:alg:cor:expI} gives the injection, and left
translation by $q\in\cG(\Z)$ gives the family through $q$.
\end{proof}

Compactness of $G(\R)$ is not the criterion: every real torus, including the
noncompact $\Gm$, is rigid, while a noncompact semisimple group is not. Only the
characteristic-zero fiber is used; smoothness of the integral model at the
primes is not needed. Keeping the model fixed matters: ordinary integral points
depend on the model, whereas the criterion for new omnific points depends only
on the real fiber (06).

\begin{theorem}[The Gaussian dichotomy \src{06}]\label{ogl:alg:thm:complex}
For a complex algebraic group $G$, $G(\BC)=G(\C)$ if and only if $G^0$ is a
torus. For a closed subgroup scheme $\cG\hookrightarrow\GL_{N,\Z[i]}$,
$\cG(\GOz)=\cG(\Z[i])$ if and only if $(\cG_\C)^0$ is a torus. If rigidity fails,
the constant-term kernel contains an injective image of $(\Pi_\C,+)$ under a
finite matrix exponential.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Over an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero, a connected group
with no nontrivial connected unipotent subgroup is a torus
\cite[Corollary~17.25]{MilneAG}; so a connected group that is not a torus has
a nonidentity unipotent. If $G^0$ is a torus, \cref{ogl:alg:lem:torus}
freezes its points and the component argument of \cref{ogl:alg:thm:real}
gives $G(\BC)=G(\C)$. Otherwise \cref{ogl:alg:lem:exp,ogl:alg:cor:expI}
supply injective purely infinite families, also on the integral model;
constant points of $\GOz$ have entries in $\Z[i]$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The rigid-affine-variety question for group schemes \mergetag]\label{ogl:alg:rem:affine}
The omnific Diophantine report asks for which affine varieties $X$ over $\Z$
one has $X(\Oz)=X(\Z)$ (\replabel{odg:q:affine}). For $X$ a closed subgroup
scheme of $\GL_{N,\Z}$, \cref{ogl:alg:thm:real} answers it completely: exactly
when $X_\R$ has no nonidentity real unipotent, equivalently when $X_\R^0$ is
reductive with compact real derived group; in the Gaussian setting,
$X(\GOz)=X(\Z[i])$ exactly when $(X_\C)^0$ is a torus
(\cref{ogl:alg:thm:complex}). \emph{Status} (batch 31): the question remains
open for general affine varieties of dimension at least two. For curves that
report now answers it, after this remark was written. Let $\Bbbk$ be a field of
characteristic zero and call a point nonconstant if its coordinates lie in
that report's Hahn ring over $\Bbbk$ of series with nonnegative support (for a
nonzero exponent group), but not all in $\Bbbk$. A smooth geometrically
integral affine curve over $\Bbbk$ has nonconstant points exactly when it is
the affine line (\replabel{odg:cr:thm:affine}). A geometrically integral
affine curve, singular or not, has them exactly when its normalization is the
affine line (\replabel{odg:sg:thm:main}). Hence an affine model whose real
fiber is geometrically integral with normalization other than
$\mathbb A^1_\R$ satisfies $X(\Oz)=X(\Z)$, and a $\Z[i]$-model whose complex
fiber is geometrically integral with normalization other than
$\mathbb A^1_\C$ satisfies $X(\GOz)=X(\Z[i])$
(\replabel{odg:sg:thm:omnific}). For such a $\Z[i]$-model, nonordinary
$\GOz$-points exist exactly when the complex normalization is
$\mathbb A^1_\C$ and $X(\Z[i])\ne\varnothing$
(\replabel{odg:sg:cor:gaussian}). Over $\Oz$ one case stays open: a real
curve with normalization $\mathbb A^1_\R$ whose integer points all lack a
real preimage under the normalization, such as the isolated real node of
$Y^2=X^2(X-1)$ (\replabel{odg:sg:q:real}). In the terms of that report, a
satisfactory criterion
should separate polynomial parametrization, torus rigidity and genuinely
algebraic branches; for group schemes the first two decide rigidity:
nonconstant points exist exactly when a polynomial unipotent family exists, and
otherwise the identity component is compact-by-torus. In the nonrigid case the
theorem supplies families through every integral point but does not describe
all omnific points. The theorem also contains
several results of that report as special cases: the unipotent subgroup
$(\Pi,+)\hookrightarrow\SO(q,\Oz)$ for indefinite forms in at least three
variables (\replabel{odg:cor:unipotent}; $\SO(q)_\R$ is then isotropic, hence
has real unipotents), the finiteness of $\SO_n(\Oz)$
(\replabel{odg:cor:orthogonal}; $\SO_n$ is compact), and the torus and unit
remark cited after \cref{ogl:alg:lem:torus}. The explicit matrices of those
results remain useful: \cref{ogl:alg:thm:real} gives existence of a family,
while \replabel{odg:cor:unipotent} writes it down.
\end{remark}

\section{Orthogonal examples}\label{ogl:alg:sec:examples}

\subsection{Compact real groups and Gaussian escape}

For the standard positive-definite form, $\OO_n(\Oz)=\OO_n(\Z)$ is the group of
signed permutation matrices, of order $2^nn!$, and $\SO_n(\Oz)$ consists of
those of determinant one (\replabel{odg:cor:orthogonal}, proved there by
sources 01 and 02 of that report and reproved by 06 through the column bound
$\sum_ig_{ij}^2=1$). In particular $|\SO_3(\Oz)|=24$.

Over $\C$ the same group scheme escapes (06). Take
$v=(1,i,0)^{\mathsf T}$, $w=(0,0,1)^{\mathsf T}$ and
\[
 N=vw^{\mathsf T}-wv^{\mathsf T}
 =\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\0&0&i\\-1&-i&0\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad N^{\mathsf T}=-N,\quad N^3=0,\quad N^2\ne0.
\]
The finite exponential is
\begin{equation}\label{ogl:alg:eq:complexexample}
 g(a)=1_3+aN+\frac{a^2}{2}N^2
 =\begin{pmatrix}
 1-a^2/2&-ia^2/2&a\\
 -ia^2/2&1+a^2/2&ia\\
 -a&-ia&1
 \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
For every $a\in\Pi_\C$ it lies in $\SO_3(\GOz)$ ($\exp(aN)^{\mathsf T}=\exp(-aN)$,
and $\det=1$ since $N$ is nilpotent), has constant term $1_3$, and its
$(1,3)$ entry recovers $a$. Neither compactness of the real form nor a
positive-definite defining form prevents new Gaussian points: the equation
$z_1^2+z_2^2+z_3^2=1$ is not a positivity statement over $\C$. A
conjugate-transpose condition, in contrast, defines a real algebraic unitary
group, which must be analyzed as a real group by \cref{ogl:alg:thm:real}, not as
this complex orthogonal group (06).

\subsection{Real, not rational, unipotents}

The form $q=x^2+y^2-3z^2$ is anisotropic over $\Q$, so $\SO(q)(\Q)$ has no
nonidentity unipotent; but $\SO(q)(\Oz)$ has purely infinite unipotent
families. The existence of such a family is a special case of
\replabel{odg:cor:unipotent} (any nondegenerate indefinite integral form in at
least three variables), whose construction already allows irrational real
isotropic vectors; it is not reproved here. Source 06 adds two points that the
earlier corollary leaves implicit.

\emph{No rational unipotent} \src{06}. Anisotropy: a primitive integer solution
of $x^2+y^2=3z^2$ would have $3\mid x,y$ (squares are $0,1$ modulo $3$), hence
$3\mid z$. If $u\ne1$ were a unipotent in $\SO(q)(\Q)$ with $N=\log u$, then
$\exp(tN)$ preserves $q$ for all $t$ (\cref{ogl:alg:lem:exp}), so
$N^{\mathsf T}Q+QN=0$ with $Q=\diag(1,1,-3)$; for $r$ maximal with $N^r\ne0$ and
rational $z$ with $N^rz\ne0$, $B(N^rz,N^rz)=(-1)^rB(z,N^{2r}z)=0$, contradicting
anisotropy.

\emph{An explicit family} \src{06}. With $v=(\sqrt3,0,1)^{\mathsf T}$ and
$w=(0,1,0)^{\mathsf T}$ one has $B(v,v)=B(v,w)=0$, $B(w,w)=1$, and
\[
 N=vw^{\mathsf T}Q-wv^{\mathsf T}Q
 =\begin{pmatrix}0&\sqrt3&0\\-\sqrt3&0&3\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad N^{\mathsf T}Q+QN=0,\quad N^3=0,
\]
\begin{equation}\label{ogl:alg:eq:realexample}
 \exp(aN)=
 \begin{pmatrix}
 1-3a^2/2&\sqrt3a&3\sqrt3a^2/2\\
 -\sqrt3a&1&3a\\
 -\sqrt3a^2/2&a&1+3a^2/2
 \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
For $a\in\Pi$ this lies in $\SO(q)(\Oz)$ with constant term $1_3$, and its
$(3,2)$ entry is $a$; $a=\omega$ gives an explicit nonconstant omnific matrix.
The polynomial subgroup exists over $\R$, not over $\Q$, and that suffices for
omnific integrality. There is no conflict with the rigidity of nonzero binary
norm fibers in the omnific Diophantine report: the ambient group here is
three-dimensional with noncompact real derived group.

\section{Unipotent kernels and unitriangular filtrations}\label{ogl:alg:sec:unipotent}

Let $U$ be a unipotent algebraic group over $k\in\{\R,\C\}$; in a faithful
representation it is conjugate over $k$ to a group of upper unitriangular
matrices, its Lie algebra $\mathfrak u$ consists of nilpotent matrices with a
common nilpotence bound, and exponential and logarithm are mutually inverse
polynomial maps \cite[Proposition~14.32]{MilneAG}.

\begin{proposition}[The constant-term kernel of a unipotent group \src{06}]\label{ogl:alg:prop:bch}
There is a polynomial-coordinate identification
\[
 \ker\bigl(U(\Bk)\to U(k)\bigr)=\exp\bigl(\mathfrak u(k)\otimes_k\Pi_k\bigr),
\]
where the right side means finite combinations of a fixed $k$-basis of
$\mathfrak u$ with coefficients in $\Pi_k$, and multiplication corresponds to
the truncated Baker--Campbell--Hausdorff polynomial. For an integral model with
unipotent $k$-fiber, its constant-term identity fiber is the same group inside
the faithful representation.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Exponential and logarithm are polynomials over $k$, so they commute with
$\ct$. A Lie-algebra point has zero constant term exactly when its coordinates
lie in $\Pi_k$, and then its exponential has constant term the identity. The
Baker--Campbell--Hausdorff expression truncates, and its brackets have
coefficients in $\Pi_k$. For an integral model these points and their inverses
satisfy the defining equations as in \cref{ogl:alg:cor:expI}; the converse
follows by embedding into the $k$-fiber and taking logarithms.
\end{proof}

No convergence of an exponential series at an infinite argument is asserted;
every displayed matrix function is a finite polynomial.

Put $U_n=\UT_n(\Pi_k)$, the upper unitriangular matrices with entries above the
diagonal in $\Pi_k$, and for $r\ge1$
\[
 \Fil_r=\{g\in U_n:g_{ij}=0\text{ whenever }0<j-i<r\},
 \qquad \Fil_1=U_n,\quad \Fil_n=1.
\]

\begin{theorem}[Exact commutator filtration \src{06}]\label{ogl:alg:thm:ut}
For all $r,s\ge1$, $[\Fil_r,\Fil_s]=\Fil_{r+s}$. Consequently the lower central
series is $\gamma_r(U_n)=\Fil_r$, the derived series is $U_n^{(j)}=\Fil_{2^j}$,
and for $n\ge2$ the nilpotency class is $n-1$ and the derived length is
$\lceil\log_2n\rceil$. The first superdiagonal induces
$U_n/[U_n,U_n]\cong(\Pi_k,+)^{n-1}$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Write $\Fil_r=1+J_r$ with $J_r$ the corresponding strictly upper triangular
matrices; $J_rJ_s\subseteq J_{r+s}$ and finite geometric inversion of $1+X$
give $[\Fil_r,\Fil_s]\subseteq\Fil_{r+s}$. Elimination of successive
superdiagonals writes every member of $\Fil_t$ as a finite product of
$e_{ij}(a)$ with $j-i\ge t$ and $a\in\Pi_k$. For a generator $e_{ij}(a)$ of
$\Fil_{r+s}$ with $r+s<n$ choose $h=i+r$, so $h-i\ge r$ and $j-h\ge s$, write
$a=bc$ with $b,c\in\Pi_k$ (\cref{ogl:prop:factor}), and use
$[e_{ih}(b),e_{hj}(c)]=e_{ij}(bc)$. If $r+s\ge n$ both sides are trivial.
Induction gives the two series; $\Fil_t\ne1$ exactly when $t<n$, witnessed by
$e_{1n}(a)$, $a\ne0$. The first superdiagonal is additive, onto
$(\Pi_k)^{n-1}$, with kernel $\Fil_2$.
\end{proof}

For every positive surreal exponent $\rho$, coefficient extraction
$f\mapsto\coeff{\rho}{f}$ is a nonzero additive map $\Pi_k\to k$; composed with a
first-superdiagonal coordinate it is a nontrivial homomorphism $U_n\to(k,+)$
for $n\ge2$. A purely infinite unipotent group can therefore be visible to
small groups. The opposite-root commutators of \cref{ogl:el:part} destroy all
such visibility; the invisibility theorem there is not a statement about all
purely infinite matrix groups (06).

calculus are classical specializations; the exact formulas use the

are not presented as independent discoveries (06).}
\part{Elementary groups and their set-sized images}\label{ogl:el:part}

This part asks how much of an omnific matrix a set-sized group can detect. A
group representation of matrices need not come from a homomorphism of their
coefficient rings, so the question is genuinely stronger than the ring question
answered in the sibling report (\replabel{osq:thm:universal}). The answer
depends sharply on whether a third row and column are available: two rows admit
nonmultiplicative coefficient specializations, and three rows provide the
commutator relation that recovers multiplication, which the supply of surreal
scales turns into a cardinality obstruction (07). The part follows source 07,
with source 06's normality, splitting, tower and boundary results inserted.
Source 11's third proof of the main theorem is in \cref{ogl:el:sec:high}; its

\section{Setting}\label{ogl:el:sec:setting}

For a commutative unital ring $A$, $E_n(A)$ is the subgroup of $\SL_n(A)$
generated by the elementary matrices $e_{ij}(a)=1_n+aE_{ij}$, $a\in A$,
$i\ne j$. Generation always means finite words. \emph{We never identify
$E_n(A)$ with $\SL_n(A)$ for an omnific ring}; for $n=2$ they differ
(\cref{ogl:el:thm:nonel}). Direct multiplication of matrix units gives, for
distinct $i,h,j$,
\begin{equation}\label{ogl:el:eq:steinberg}
 e_{ij}(a)e_{ij}(b)=e_{ij}(a+b),\qquad
 [e_{ih}(a),e_{hj}(b)]=e_{ij}(ab),
\end{equation}
finite polynomial identities over any commutative ring \cite[Chapter~III, Section~1]{Weibel}.

Throughout, $k\in\{\R,\C\}$, $D\subseteq k$ is a unital subring (for most
statements $D=\Z$ or $\Z[i]$), $A=\A_{D,k}$, and
\begin{equation}\label{ogl:el:eq:kernel}
 P_n=\ker\bigl(\ct:E_n(A)\to E_n(D)\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $\ct$ is applied entrywise; the inclusion of constant matrices is a
section. For $D=\Z,\Z[i]$ Euclidean row reduction gives $E_n(D)=\SL_n(D)$ (the
entries of the first column of a determinant-one matrix generate the unit
ideal; reduce it to a unit times $e_1$, remove the unit by an elementary
diagonal block, and induct; over $\Z[i]$ use the Gaussian norm). These are
finite ordinary matrix arguments; no Euclidean algorithm in $\Oz$ is assumed
(06, 07).

\begin{lemma}[The split relative kernel \src{07, 11}]\label{ogl:el:lem:normalgen}
For every $n\ge2$, $P_n$ is the normal closure in $E_n(A)$ of the matrices
$e_{ij}(x)$, $x\in\Pi_k$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Let $N'$ be that normal closure; $N'\subseteq P_n$. Modulo $N'$,
$e_{ij}(d+x)=e_{ij}(d)e_{ij}(x)$ is congruent to the constant $e_{ij}(d)$, so
every word is congruent to a matrix of $E_n(D)$; if the word lies in $P_n$, its
constant representative is the identity. So $P_n=N'$. Only the split inclusion
of $D$ is used, not a relative exactness theorem.
\end{proof}

Source 11 proves the same lemma for every split unital ring $D\oplus I$ with
ideal $I$ (its Lemma~5.1), by the same finite-word replacement, and stresses
that it uses neither normality of the root-generated subgroup, nor a
presentation of $E_n$, nor $E_n=\SL_n$.

For $n\ge3$ the normal closure is not needed: the root subgroups already
generate a normal subgroup (06).

\begin{proposition}[An abstract idempotent-ideal lemma \src{06}]\label{ogl:el:prop:normal}
Let $A$ be a commutative unital ring, $n\ge3$, and $I$ an ideal in which every
element is a product of two elements of $I$. Then
$P=\langle e_{ij}(a):a\in I,\ i\ne j\rangle$ is perfect and normal in $E_n(A)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For a generator $e_{ij}(a)$ choose a third index $h$ and $a=bc$ with
$b,c\in I$; \eqref{ogl:el:eq:steinberg} writes it as a commutator of two
elements of $P$, so $P=[P,P]$. Conjugating a generator by $z=e_{kl}(r)$,
$r\in A$, gives, except in the opposite-root case $(k,l)=(j,i)$, either the
generator or a product of root elements with parameters $a$ and $\pm ra$, all
in $I$. For the opposite root write $e_{ij}(a)=[e_{ih}(b),e_{hj}(c)]$ and use
\begin{align}
 e_{ji}(r)e_{ih}(b)e_{ji}(-r)&=e_{jh}(rb)e_{ih}(b),\label{ogl:el:eq:conjone}\\
 e_{ji}(r)e_{hj}(c)e_{ji}(-r)&=e_{hi}(-cr)e_{hj}(c);\label{ogl:el:eq:conjtwo}
\end{align}
both conjugated factors lie in $P$, hence so does their commutator.
\end{proof}

A version with $I^2=I$ and finite sums of products follows by splitting
$e_{ij}(a)$ along \eqref{ogl:el:eq:steinberg}; the single-product hypothesis
holds for $\Pi_k$ by \cref{ogl:prop:factor}. The perfectness half, in this
$I^2=I$ form and for any group generated by root maps, is also source 11's
(\cref{ogl:ker:cor:transfer}(iii)).

\begin{proposition}[Exact split kernel \src{06}]\label{ogl:el:prop:split}
For $n\ge3$ and every unital subring $D\subseteq k$,
\[
 E_n(A)=P_n\rtimes E_n(D),\qquad
 P_n=\langle e_{ij}(a):a\in\Pi_k,\ i\ne j\rangle .
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $P'$ be the subgroup on the right; it is normal by
\cref{ogl:prop:factor,ogl:el:prop:normal}. Writing $e_{ij}(d+b)=e_{ij}(b)e_{ij}(d)$
and moving purely infinite factors to the left using normality writes every
$g\in E_n(A)$ as $g=pq$ with $p\in P'$, $q\in E_n(D)$; applying $\ct$ gives
$\ct(g)=q$. Hence $P_n=P'$, $P'\cap E_n(D)=1$, and the constant inclusion is a
section.
\end{proof}

\section{Two collision mechanisms}\label{ogl:el:sec:collision}

A set-sized target identifies two of any sufficiently large set of root
elements. To turn such a collision into the death of an arbitrary root element
one needs a divisor: a purely infinite quotient of the prescribed parameter by
the difference of the two colliding parameters. The two sources find the
divisor in different ways, and both ways are already lemmas of the sibling
report, which is where the sources found them.

\subsection{Division by a difference of nearby monomials}

\begin{lemma}[Absorption \src{06}]\label{ogl:el:lem:absorb}
Fix $0\ne c\in\Pi_k$. There is $\varepsilon>0$ such that for all
$0<\beta<\alpha<\varepsilon$,
\begin{equation}\label{ogl:el:eq:absorption}
 \frac{c}{\omega^\alpha-\omega^\beta}
 =\sum_{m\ge0}c\,\omega^{-\alpha-m(\alpha-\beta)}\in\Pi_k .
\end{equation}
For every ordinal $\Lambda$ the interval $(0,\varepsilon)$ contains $\Lambda$
distinct exponents, for instance $\alpha_\xi=\varepsilon/(2+\xi)$, $\xi<\Lambda$,
with surreal arithmetic on the embedded ordinals.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Choose $\delta>0$ strictly below $\supp c$ (\cref{ogl:lem:gap}) and put
$\varepsilon=\delta/(4\omega)$ in the exponent field, so that $m\varepsilon<\delta$
for every ordinary $m\ge1$. With $\gamma=\alpha-\beta>0$,
$(\omega^\alpha-\omega^\beta)^{-1}=\omega^{-\alpha}(1-\omega^{-\gamma})^{-1}
=\sum_{m\ge0}\omega^{-\alpha-m\gamma}$ by \eqref{ogl:eq:geom}. Every exponent of
the product with $c$ has the form $s-\alpha-m\gamma$ with $s\in\supp c$, and
$s-\alpha-m\gamma>\delta-(m+1)\varepsilon>0$; cancellation cannot create a
nonpositive exponent. The $\alpha_\xi$ are distinct and lie in $(0,\varepsilon)$.
\end{proof}

This is the subordinate division lemma \replabel{osq:lem:division} of the
sibling report (sources 09, 10, 11 there), with the admissible scales
$(0,\varepsilon)$ inside its class $U_{\No}(\supp c)$; source 06 cites it as the
geometric collision construction of the quotient report's base manuscript,
adapted to produce an arbitrary prescribed root parameter.

\begin{remark}[No series is mapped term by term \src{06}]\label{ogl:el:rem:nomapseries}
If $d=\omega^\alpha-\omega^\beta$ and $b$ is the whole series in
\eqref{ogl:el:eq:absorption}, the only identity used is $db=c$. A target
homomorphism is applied to a finite matrix commutator involving $b$, never to
the summands. For the truncations $b_M$ one has
\begin{equation}\label{ogl:el:eq:remainder}
 d\,b_M=c\bigl(1-\omega^{-(M+1)(\alpha-\beta)}\bigr),
\end{equation}
with nonzero remainder; replacing $b$ by a truncation would not prove the
theorem (compare \replabel{osq:rem:nolimit}).
\end{remark}

\subsection{Rescaling a large set field}

\begin{lemma}[Exponent scaling \src{07}]\label{ogl:el:lem:scale}
For every $h\in\No^{>0}$,
$\Phi_h\bigl(\sum_sa_s\omega^s\bigr)=\sum_sa_s\omega^{hs}$ is an ordered field
automorphism of $\No$ fixing $\R$, with inverse $\Phi_{1/h}$; it extends
coefficientwise to $\SC$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Multiplication by $h$ is a strictly increasing additive bijection of the
exponent group; it preserves reverse well ordering, set size, exponent sums and
the finite convolution formulas, and the leading coefficient.
\end{proof}

This is the dilation $\mathsf S_h$ of the surcomplex automorphism report
(\replabel{saut:eq:dilation}, with $t=\omega^{-1}$) and of
\replabel{odg:def:ex:dilation}. No compatibility with the surreal exponential,
simplicity order or birthday is asserted (07).

\begin{lemma}[Set-field compression \src{07}]\label{ogl:el:lem:compress}
For every $\delta>0$ and every infinite set cardinal $\lambda$ there is a set
subfield $L\subset\No$ with $|L|\ge\lambda$ and $\supp a\subset(-\delta,\delta)$
for every $a\in L$; in particular $(a-b)^{-1}$ has the same support bound for
distinct $a,b\in L$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Let $L_0$ be the subfield generated by $\lambda$ distinct surreals (for
instance embedded ordinals); it is a set with at least $\lambda$ elements. The
union $T$ of the supports of its members is a set; choose $\beta>0$ with
$|s|<\beta$ for $s\in T$ (\cref{ogl:lem:gap}), put $h=\delta/\beta$ and
$L=\Phi_h(L_0)$. Then $|hs|<\delta$ for every exponent of every member of $L$,
and $(a-b)^{-1}\in L$.
\end{proof}

The quantifiers matter: $L$ is chosen after $\delta$ and $\lambda$, and the
supports of the whole field, not only of its generators, are compressed (07).

\begin{lemma}[A commutator-ready family \src{07}]\label{ogl:el:lem:ready}
Let $0\ne x\in\Pi_k$ and $H$ a set. There are $\delta>0$ and a set subfield
$L\subset\No$ with $|L|>|H|$ such that, with $\tau=\omega^{2\delta}$,
\[
 \tau a\in\Pi_k\quad(a\in L),\qquad
 \frac{x}{\tau(a-b)}\in\Pi_k\quad(a,b\in L,\ a\ne b).
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Choose $\delta>0$ with $4\delta<s$ for all $s\in\supp x$ and apply
\cref{ogl:el:lem:compress} with a cardinal larger than $|H|$. Every exponent
of $\tau a$ lies in $(\delta,3\delta)$, and every exponent of
$x\omega^{-2\delta}(a-b)^{-1}$ exceeds $4\delta-2\delta-\delta=\delta$. The
auxiliary field may stay real when $x$ has complex coefficients.
\end{proof}

This is the scaled-field collision lemma \replabel{osq:lem:collision} of the
sibling report (source 06 there, with 03) in the form needed for root
parameters: there $m=X^{g/2}$ and $mL\subseteq\Pi_K$ give
$(mx-my)\cdot m/(x-y)=m^2$; here $\tau a$ plays the role of $mx$ and the
prescribed $x$ that of $m^2$. Source 07 credits the scaled-field collision to
the quotient report's base manuscript.

\section{Three or more rows: no set-sized images}\label{ogl:el:sec:high}

\begin{theorem}[The purely infinite elementary kernel is invisible to sets \src{06, 07, 11}]\label{ogl:el:thm:invisible}
Let $k\in\{\R,\C\}$, $D\subseteq k$ a unital subring, $A=\A_{D,k}$ and $n\ge3$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item $P_n=\langle e_{ij}(a):a\in\Pi_k,\ i\ne j\rangle$, and
$E_n(A)=P_n\rtimes E_n(D)$.
\item $P_n$ is perfect and nontrivial.
\item Every class homomorphism from $P_n$ to a set-sized group is trivial.
\item For every set-sized group $H$, composition with $\ct$ is a bijection
$\Hom(E_n(D),H)\to\Hom(E_n(A),H)$. Thus $E_n(D)$ is the universal set-sized
group quotient of $E_n(A)$; it is $\SL_n(D)$ for $D\in\{\Z,\Z[i]\}$, so for
$E_n(\Oz)$ and $E_n(\GOz)$, and $\SL_n(k)$ for $D=k$.
\end{enumerate}
No continuity, preservation of infinite sums, definability or algebraicity of a
homomorphism is assumed.
\end{theorem}

Assertion (iii) is stronger than the factorization (iv): a homomorphism defined
only on the kernel need not extend to $E_n(A)$, and all three proofs below treat
such maps directly (06, 07, 11). Source 06 states (i)--(iv) for $D\in\{\Z,\Z[i]\}$ and, in its
corollary on other constant rings, for every set-sized unital $D\subseteq k$;
source 07 states them for $D\in\{\Z,\Z[i]\}$, and its proof uses $D$ only
through the split inclusion of \cref{ogl:el:lem:normalgen}, so it applies
verbatim to every unital $D\subseteq k$. Source 11 states them for
$D\in\{\Z,\Z[i]\}$ (its Theorem~A, proved as its Theorem~5.2 with Lemma~5.3),
also for maps defined only on the kernel; its proof uses $D$ only through the
split retraction, so it too applies to every unital $D\subseteq k$. Every
$D\subseteq k$ is a set.

\begin{proof}[Proof of (i), (ii) and (iv) given (iii)]
(i) is \cref{ogl:el:prop:split}. (ii): perfectness is
\cref{ogl:el:prop:normal}, and $e_{12}(\omega)\ne1$. (iv): a homomorphism
$\psi:E_n(A)\to H$ restricts to the trivial map on $P_n$ by (iii), so it
factors uniquely through $\ct$ in the split decomposition; restriction to the
constant subgroup gives the factor, and for every $g$ the matrix
$g\,\ct(g)^{-1}$ lies in $P_n$. Conversely every homomorphism $E_n(D)\to H$
composes with $\ct$. Over the field $k$ ordinary elimination gives
$E_n(k)=\SL_n(k)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[First proof of (iii): rescaled set fields \src{07}]
By \cref{ogl:el:lem:normalgen} it suffices to kill each conjugate
$g\,e_{ih}(x)g^{-1}$ with $g\in E_n(A)$, $0\ne x\in\Pi_k$, $i\ne h$. Let
$\psi:P_n\to H$. Choose an index $j\notin\{i,h\}$ and take $\tau,L$ from
\cref{ogl:el:lem:ready}. Since $P_n$ is normal,
$a\mapsto\psi(g\,e_{ij}(\tau a)g^{-1})$ is defined on $L$, and $|L|>|H|$ gives
distinct $a,b\in L$ with equal images; by \eqref{ogl:el:eq:steinberg}
$\psi(g\,e_{ij}(\tau(a-b))g^{-1})=1$. Put $d=x/(\tau(a-b))\in\Pi_k$. The
conjugated commutator identity
\[
 \bigl[g\,e_{ij}(\tau(a-b))g^{-1},\ g\,e_{jh}(d)g^{-1}\bigr]
 =g\,e_{ih}(\tau(a-b)d)g^{-1}=g\,e_{ih}(x)g^{-1}
\]
is an identity between elements of $P_n$, and applying $\psi$ kills the normal
generator. The conjugator $g$ need not lie in $P_n$: only the two conjugated
root elements and their commutator, all in the domain, are fed to $\psi$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Second proof of (iii): division by nearby monomials \src{06}]
By (i), $P_n$ is generated by the $e_{ij}(c)$, $c\in\Pi_k$, without conjugates.
Let $\varphi:P_n\to H$, $c\ne0$, and fix a third index $h$. Take $\varepsilon$
from \cref{ogl:el:lem:absorb} and a set $T\subset(0,\varepsilon)$ that does not
inject into $H$ (its Hartogs ordinal, or a cardinal $>|H|$, indexes such a
set). The elements $e_{ih}(\omega^\alpha)$, $\alpha\in T$, lie in $P_n$ and
cannot have distinct images, so for some $\alpha>\beta$ in $T$,
$\varphi(e_{ih}(\omega^\alpha-\omega^\beta))=1$. With
$d=\omega^\alpha-\omega^\beta$ and $b=c/d\in\Pi_k$,
\[
 \varphi(e_{ij}(c))=\varphi\bigl([e_{ih}(d),e_{hj}(b)]\bigr)
 =\bigl[1_H,\varphi(e_{hj}(b))\bigr]=1_H .
\]
Every generator, hence every finite word, is killed.
\end{proof}

The third route turns a group homomorphism into a ring homomorphism. It rests
on an abstract lemma about groups generated by root maps. For a commutative
ring $I$, possibly without unit, write $x_{ij}$ ($1\le i\ne j\le n$) for maps
from $I$ into a group; the letters $x_{ij}$ always carry two indices and are
not the elements $x\in\Pi_k$ of the proofs above.

\begin{theorem}[Root-kernel reconstruction \src{11}]\label{ogl:ker:thm:rootkernel}
Let $n\ge3$, let $I$ be a commutative ring, possibly without unit and possibly
a proper class, and let $\Sigma$ be a group generated by additive
homomorphisms $x_{ij}:(I,+)\to\Sigma$, $i\ne j$, with
\begin{equation}\label{ogl:ker:eq:abstractcomm}
 [x_{ih}(a),x_{hj}(b)]=x_{ij}(ab)\qquad(i,h,j\text{ distinct},\ a,b\in I).
\end{equation}
For a homomorphism $\varphi:\Sigma\to H$ to a set-sized group put
$J_\varphi=\{a\in I:\varphi(x_{ij}(a))=1\text{ for all }i\ne j\}$. Then
$J_\varphi$ is an ideal of $I$, and there are a set-sized ring $R_\varphi$,
possibly without unit, and a surjective additive and multiplicative map
$\vartheta_\varphi:I\to R_\varphi$ with kernel $J_\varphi$ and
\[
 |R_\varphi|\le|H|^{n(n-1)} .
\]
Moreover $R_\varphi=0$ if and only if $\varphi$ is trivial.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Each condition defining $J_\varphi$ is the kernel of an additive
homomorphism, so $J_\varphi$ is an additive subgroup. For $a\in J_\varphi$,
$b\in I$ and $i\ne j$ choose $h\notin\{i,j\}$; then
$\varphi(x_{ij}(ab))=[\varphi(x_{ih}(a)),\varphi(x_{hj}(b))]=[1,\varphi(x_{hj}(b))]=1$.
This holds for every ordered pair, so $ab\in J_\varphi$, and commutativity gives
a two-sided ideal. Put
$\vartheta_\varphi(a)=(\varphi(x_{ij}(a)))_{i\ne j}\in H^{n(n-1)}$ and
$R_\varphi=\vartheta_\varphi(I)$, a subclass of a set and hence a set. By
additivity, $\vartheta_\varphi(a)=\vartheta_\varphi(b)$ if and only if
$a-b\in J_\varphi$. Define
$\vartheta_\varphi(a)+\vartheta_\varphi(b)=\vartheta_\varphi(a+b)$ and
$\vartheta_\varphi(a)\vartheta_\varphi(b)=\vartheta_\varphi(ab)$; these are
well defined because $J_\varphi$ is a subgroup and an ideal, and the ring
axioms are inherited from $I$. If $R_\varphi=0$, every generator of $\Sigma$
is killed, so $\varphi$ is trivial; the converse is clear.
\end{proof}

The ring structure on $R_\varphi$ is transported from $I/J_\varphi$; it is not
the componentwise product in the group $H^{n(n-1)}$, which carries no ring
structure. No set of class-sized cosets is formed: the tuple image is the
carrier (11).

\begin{corollary}[Transfer from rings to groups \src{11}]\label{ogl:ker:cor:transfer}
In the setting of \cref{ogl:ker:thm:rootkernel}:
\begin{enumerate}
\item if every additive multiplicative map from $I$ to a set-sized ring is
zero, every homomorphism from $\Sigma$ to a set-sized group is trivial;
\item if $\kappa$ is an uncountable cardinal and every additive multiplicative
map from $I$ to a ring of cardinality $<\kappa$ is zero, every homomorphism
from $\Sigma$ to a group of cardinality $<\kappa$ is trivial;
\item if $I^2=I$ then $\Sigma$ is perfect, and if every element of $I$ is a
single product $bc$, each $x_{ij}(a)$ is a single commutator of root elements.
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
(i) Apply the hypothesis to $\vartheta_\varphi$. (ii) If $|H|<\kappa$ then
$|H|^{n(n-1)}<\kappa$: it is finite for finite $H$ and equals $|H|$ for
infinite $H$. (iii) For $a=\sum_{\ell=1}^rb_\ell c_\ell$ and $h\notin\{i,j\}$,
$x_{ij}(a)=\prod_\ell[x_{ih}(b_\ell),x_{hj}(c_\ell)]$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Normal generation \src{11}]\label{ogl:ker:lem:closure}
Let $U$ be a subgroup of a group $G$ and $N$ its normal closure. If every
homomorphism $U\to H$ is trivial for a given group $H$, so is every
homomorphism $N\to H$. If $U$ is perfect, so is $N$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
$N$ is generated by the conjugates $gUg^{-1}$, $g\in G$. For $f:N\to H$, the map
$u\mapsto f(gug^{-1})$ is a homomorphism $U\to H$, hence trivial. Each
$gUg^{-1}$ is perfect when $U$ is, so it lies in $[N,N]$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Third proof of (iii): a ring rebuilt from the root maps \src{11}]
Let $U=\langle e_{ij}(a):a\in\Pi_k,\ i\ne j\rangle$. The restrictions
$x_{ij}=e_{ij}|_{\Pi_k}$ are additive and satisfy
\eqref{ogl:ker:eq:abstractcomm} by \eqref{ogl:el:eq:steinberg}, so
\cref{ogl:ker:thm:rootkernel} applies with $I=\Pi_k$ and $\Sigma=U$. Every
additive multiplicative map from $\Pi_k$ to a set-sized ring is zero: this is
part (iii) of the ring theorem \replabel{osq:thm:universal} of the sibling
report, which source 11 reproves (its Theorem~3.3) with the division
identity of \cref{ogl:el:lem:absorb} in the form $c/(\omega^\alpha-\omega^\beta)\in\Pi_k$
for $0<\beta<\alpha<\delta$, where $m\delta<s$ for all $s\in\supp c$ and
ordinary $m\ge1$, proving strong summability of the quotient as well as
positivity, and with the exponents $\delta/(\xi+2)$, $\xi<|R|^+$, to force a
collision in the target ring $R$. By \cref{ogl:ker:cor:transfer}(i) every
homomorphism from $U$ to a set-sized group is trivial. $P_n$ is the normal
closure of $U$ (\cref{ogl:el:lem:normalgen}), and \cref{ogl:ker:lem:closure}
kills every homomorphism from $P_n$ to $H$. By (i) $U=P_n$, so the last step is
not needed here; source 11 does not use (i), and kills a map on the kernel
separately on each conjugate of $U$.
\end{proof}

The first two proofs do not construct a ring map from $A$ to $H$ or to an
endomorphism ring; both apply to arbitrary abstract group maps on $P_n$, and for
$k=\C$ neither needs an order on coefficients, only positivity of exponents
(06, 07). The third proof does build a ring, but from the root maps of the
given group homomorphism, not from any ring map on $A$, so it too applies to
arbitrary abstract group maps defined only on the kernel (11). Neither of the
first two proofs uses the other's stronger ingredients: 06's needs only
single-product divisibility, gaps and the geometric inverse, and 07's needs the
field automorphism $\Phi_h$ and a set field closed under inverses. The third
is not independent of them in mechanism: it imports the ring theorem, whose
proofs use the same collision identities (\cref{ogl:el:sec:collision}); what
it adds is the general transfer \cref{ogl:ker:cor:transfer}, which also gives
the Steinberg and cardinal versions below
(\cref{ogl:ker:thm:steinberg,ogl:ker:thm:fragmentgroup}). The recovery of
coefficient algebra from root commutators has substantial precedent
\cite{Voronetsky}; source 11 claims neither that idea nor the
normal-generation machinery, only its elementary finite-tuple form for
cardinality and nonunital parameter rings.
Perfectness alone would kill only abelian images; the cardinal collision kills
images in finite simple groups and in arbitrary nonabelian groups as well. The
theorem does not make $P_n$ trivial: its tautological representation on the
proper-class space $F^n$ is faithful and has no set-sized target (06, 07).

\begin{corollary}[Actions and linear representations \src{06, 07, 11}]\label{ogl:el:cor:actions}
For $n\ge3$, every action of $P_n$ on a set is trivial, and every action of
$E_n(A)$ on a set factors uniquely through $\ct$. Every linear representation of
$P_n$ on a set-sized vector space over a set-sized field, of any dimension, is
trivial; the same holds for representations on set-sized abelian groups or on
any structure whose automorphism group is a set.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
An action on a set $X$ is a homomorphism to the set-sized group $\Sym(X)$; for
linear representations use the invertible linear maps of the space.
\end{proof}

Source 11 proves the same (its Theorem~7.1 and Corollary~7.2) and adds two
points. $P_n$ has no nontrivial transitive action on a set with more than one
point. Restriction to constant matrices and pullback by $\ct$ identify the
representations of $E_n(A)$ on set-sized vector spaces with those of
$E_n(D)$, intertwiners included: a linear map intertwines two pulled-back
representations exactly when it intertwines their restrictions to the
constant subgroup, since every matrix acts through its constant term. Here
``arithmetic'' means only this factorization; the representations of
$\SL_n(D)$ are not classified, nor asserted to be algebraic (11).

\begin{remark}[The ring theorem as a consequence \mergetag]\label{ogl:el:rem:ring}
\Cref{ogl:el:thm:invisible} implies the universal set-sized quotient theorem
\replabel{osq:thm:universal} of the sibling report for coefficient fields
$K\in\{\R,\C\}$ and every unital $D\subseteq K$. \emph{Proof.} Let $S$ be a
set-sized unital ring, possibly noncommutative, and $\psi:A\to S$ a unital ring
homomorphism. Entrywise application of $\psi$ is multiplicative on matrices and
sends $e_{ij}(a)$ to $1+\psi(a)E_{ij}\in\GL_n(S)$, so it restricts to a
homomorphism $E_n(A)\to\GL_n(S)$ into a set-sized group, with $n=3$. By
\cref{ogl:el:thm:invisible}(iii) it kills $e_{12}(x)$ for $x\in\Pi_k$, so
$\psi(x)=0$ and $\psi=\psi|_D\circ\ct$; this is part~(ii) of the ring theorem.
For an additive multiplicative map $\varphi:A\to S$ into a possibly nonunital
set-sized ring, let $S^+=\Z\times S$ be the unitalization, with
$(m,s)(m',s')=(mm',ms'+m's+ss')$; then $m+x\mapsto(m,\varphi(x))$ ($m\in\Z$,
$x\in\Pi_k$) is a unital ring homomorphism from $\A_{\Z,k}=\Z\oplus\Pi_k$ to the
set-sized ring $S^+$, so $\varphi(\Pi_k)=0$ by the unital case; this gives parts
(i) and (iii). A set-sized unital module $M$ gives a unital map
$A\to\End_\Z(M)$, which gives part (iv). \qed

The implication is logical, not an independent proof: both proofs of
\cref{ogl:el:thm:invisible} use the same collision identities as the ring
theorem (\cref{ogl:el:sec:collision}). The group theorem is strictly stronger
in scope: an abstract group homomorphism on $E_n(A)$ or on $P_n$ need not be
induced by a ring or module map, so the theorem also strengthens
\replabel{osq:cor:faithful} and \replabel{osq:prop:matrices}, which concern
representations coming from ring maps. The ring theorem is proved in the
sibling report for every set field $K$; the group theorem is stated here only
for $k\in\{\R,\C\}$, as in the sources.

The converse implication is source 11's: by \cref{ogl:ker:cor:transfer}(i)
and \cref{ogl:ker:lem:closure}, part (iii) of the ring theorem implies
\cref{ogl:el:thm:invisible}(iii) (the third proof above). For
$k\in\{\R,\C\}$ the group theorem (iii) and the nonunital ring theorem
\replabel{osq:thm:universal}(iii) therefore imply each other through finite
reductions. Neither implication is an independent proof of its conclusion.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[An abstract collision criterion \src{07}]\label{ogl:el:prop:criterion}
Let $A$ be a commutative ring, $I\lhd A$, $n\ge3$, and $E_n(A,I)$ the normal
closure of $e_{ij}(I)$ in $E_n(A)$. Fix a set-sized group $H$. Suppose that for
each $0\ne x\in I$ there are a set $\mathcal P$ with $|\mathcal P|>|H|$ and
elements $u_p\in I$ ($p\in\mathcal P$) and $v_{pq}\in I$ ($p\ne q$) with
\begin{equation}\label{ogl:el:eq:abstractcollision}
 (u_p-u_q)v_{pq}=x .
\end{equation}
Then every homomorphism $E_n(A,I)\to H$ is trivial.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For a normal generator $g\,e_{ih}(x)g^{-1}$ choose a third index $j$. The images
of $g\,e_{ij}(u_p)g^{-1}$ cannot all be distinct; a collision at $p\ne q$
kills $g\,e_{ij}(u_p-u_q)g^{-1}$, and its commutator with
$g\,e_{jh}(v_{pq})g^{-1}$ is $g\,e_{ih}(x)g^{-1}$.
\end{proof}

The first proof supplies the criterion with $u_a=\tau a$ and
$v_{ab}=x/(\tau(a-b))$, the second with $u_\alpha=\omega^\alpha$ and
$v_{\alpha\beta}=x/(\omega^\alpha-\omega^\beta)$. The criterion does not require
$A$ to be a field, $A/I$ to split, or $H$ to be linear; the splitting was used
only to identify $E_n(A,I)$ with the whole reduction kernel. Source 07 proposes
it as the first target for a formalization: it isolates all size and support
input in \eqref{ogl:el:eq:abstractcollision}.

\section{A separating proper-class congruence tower}\label{ogl:el:sec:tower}

A group with no small images might be mistaken for a simple group; the kernels
give an explicit counterexample to that inference (06). Fix $A=\Oz$ or $\GOz$
and $n\ge3$. For each positive surreal $a$ put
\begin{equation}\label{ogl:el:eq:Pa}
 P_n^{(a)}=\{g\in P_n:\ g-1_n\in M_n(\omega^aA)\},
\end{equation}
the kernel on $P_n$ of reduction modulo the principal ideal $\omega^aA$, used
only as a congruence relation.

\begin{theorem}[A non-simple invisible group with a large residual tower \src{06}]\label{ogl:el:thm:tower}
For every $a>0$, $P_n^{(a)}$ is a nontrivial proper normal subgroup of $P_n$,
and $P_n^{(b)}\subsetneq P_n^{(a)}$ for $0<a<b$. Moreover
\[
 \bigcap_{a\in\No^{>0}}P_n^{(a)}=1,\qquad
 \bigcap_{a\in S}P_n^{(a)}\ne1\quad\text{for every set }S\subset\No^{>0}.
\]
Each quotient $P_n/P_n^{(a)}$ is nontrivial and perfect, has no nontrivial
homomorphism to a set-sized group, and cannot itself be set-sized.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Matrices congruent to $1$ modulo an ideal form a normal subgroup of $\GL_n(A)$;
intersecting with $P_n$ gives normality. $e_{12}(\omega^a)\in P_n^{(a)}$, while
$e_{12}(\omega^{a/2})\notin P_n^{(a)}$ since $\omega^{-a/2}\notin A$. For $b>a$,
$\omega^{b-a}\in A$ gives the inclusion, and $e_{12}(\omega^a)$ witnesses
strictness. If $g\ne1$, pick a nonzero entry $f$ of $g-1$; for $a>\ddeg f$,
$\omega^{-a}f$ has negative leading exponent, so $g\notin P_n^{(a)}$. For a set
$S$ choose $b$ above $S$ (\cref{ogl:lem:gap}); then $e_{12}(\omega^b)$ lies in
every $P_n^{(a)}$, $a\in S$, including $S=\varnothing$. A homomorphism from
$P_n/P_n^{(a)}$ to a set-sized group composes to one from $P_n$, hence is
trivial; a nontrivial set-sized quotient would contradict this through its
identity map. Perfectness passes to quotients.
\end{proof}

The tower separates individual matrices only when all surreal scales are
allowed. This is a set/class distinction, not a numerical size assigned to
$P_n$ (06).

\section{The finite side and the rotation groups}\label{ogl:el:sec:finiteside}

The same mechanism applies on the side of infinitesimals. Let $\Ofin(k)$ be
the ring of finite elements of $F$ ($k=\R$ for $\No$, $k=\C$ for $\SC$),
$\mm_k$ its infinitesimals, and $\st:\Ofin(k)\to k$ the standard part, a
surjective ring homomorphism with kernel $\mm_k$ and section $k\subset\Ofin(k)$.
No source states the following group analogue of the finite-side ring theorem
\replabel{osq:thm:finite}; it was observed in the placement dossier and is
added here with a complete proof.

\begin{proposition}[Universal set-sized quotient of $\SL_n$ of the finite surreals \mergetag]\label{ogl:el:prop:finiteside}
Let $n\ge3$ and $Q_n=\ker(\st:\SL_n(\Ofin(k))\to\SL_n(k))$. Every class
homomorphism from $Q_n$ to a set-sized group is trivial, and every class
homomorphism $\SL_n(\Ofin(k))\to H$ to a set-sized group factors uniquely through
$\st$. Thus $\SL_n(k)$ is the universal set-sized quotient of $\SL_n(\Ofin(k))$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
\emph{$\Ofin(k)$ is local with maximal ideal $\mm_k$.} If $x$ is finite and
$\st x\ne0$, then $|x|\ge|\st x|/2$, so $x^{-1}\in F$ is finite; elements of
$\mm_k$ are not units, and $\mm_k$ is an ideal.

\emph{$\SL_n(\Ofin(k))=E_n(\Ofin(k))$.} Let $g\in\SL_n(\Ofin(k))$. Since
$\det g\notin\mm_k$, some entry of the first column is a unit; elementary row
operations move a unit to position $(1,1)$ (add that row to the first if needed:
if $g_{11}\in\mm_k$ and $g_{r1}$ is a unit, the new $(1,1)$ entry
$g_{11}+g_{r1}$ is a unit), then clear the first column and the first row. By
induction $g$ becomes $\diag(u_1,\dots,u_n)$ with $\prod u_i=1$, and
$\diag(u,u^{-1})=e_{12}(u)e_{21}(-u^{-1})e_{12}(u)\,
\bigl(e_{12}(1)e_{21}(-1)e_{12}(1)\bigr)^{-1}$ (Whitehead's identity, valid
over any commutative ring) makes the diagonal matrix elementary.

\emph{$Q_n$ is the normal closure of the $e_{ij}(\mm_k)$.} This is the argument
of \cref{ogl:el:lem:normalgen} with the split surjection $\st$ in place of
$\ct$ and $E_n(\Ofin(k))=\SL_n(\Ofin(k))$, $E_n(k)=\SL_n(k)$.

\emph{Killing.} Let $\psi:Q_n\to H$, and consider a normal generator
$g\,e_{ih}(\varepsilon)g^{-1}$ with $g\in\SL_n(\Ofin(k))$ and
$0\ne\varepsilon\in\mm_k$; let $a=-\ddeg\varepsilon>0$. For
$0<\gamma<\delta<a$, $\varepsilon/(\omega^{-\gamma}-\omega^{-\delta})\in\mm_k$
(\replabel{osq:lem:finitediff}: the denominator has leading exponent
$-\gamma$, so the quotient has leading exponent $\gamma-a<0$). Choose a third
index $j$ and a set $T\subset(0,a)$ that does not inject into $H$ (for an
ordinal $\Lambda$ that does not inject into $H$, the exponents
$a/\omega^{\xi+1}$, $\xi<\Lambda$, are distinct and lie in $(0,a)$).
The elements $g\,e_{ij}(\omega^{-\gamma})g^{-1}$, $\gamma\in T$, lie in $Q_n$, so
two of them, for $\gamma<\delta$, have equal images, and
$\psi(g\,e_{ij}(d)g^{-1})=1$ for $d=\omega^{-\gamma}-\omega^{-\delta}\in\mm_k$.
With $b=\varepsilon/d\in\mm_k$,
$[g\,e_{ij}(d)g^{-1},g\,e_{jh}(b)g^{-1}]=g\,e_{ih}(\varepsilon)g^{-1}$ is an
identity in $Q_n$, so the generator is killed.

\emph{Factorization.} A homomorphism $\varphi:\SL_n(\Ofin(k))\to H$ kills $Q_n$,
and $g\,\st(g)^{-1}\in Q_n$ gives $\varphi=\varphi|_{\SL_n(k)}\circ\st$;
uniqueness follows from surjectivity of $\st$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Comparison with the surreal rotation groups]\label{ogl:el:rem:e3}
The euclidean-three-space report proves that every homomorphism from
$\SO(3,\No)$ to a set-sized group factors through standard part
(\replabel{e3:cut:thm:smallquotient}), and the same for $\SO(n,\No)$, $n\ge3$,
and $\SU(n,\SC)$, $n\ge2$ (\replabel{e3:cut:thm:allranks}), while it fails for
the circle (\replabel{e3:cut:prop:circlecounter}). Those theorems and
\cref{ogl:el:thm:invisible} are parallel instances of one phenomenon, a
universal set-sized quotient obtained by killing a kernel through a supply of
scales, but neither implies the other. The rings differ (all of $\No$, whose
compact groups have finite entries, against $\Oz$); the kernels differ
(infinitesimal matrices under $\st$ against purely infinite matrices under
$\ct$); and the mechanisms differ (normal closure at valuation cuts in a compact
group followed by Givens elimination, against Steinberg commutators with
collisions). $\SO(3,\Oz)$ is finite (\cref{ogl:alg:sec:examples}), so the
rotation group has trivial omnific kernel, and a homomorphism on $E_n(\Oz)$ says
nothing about $\SO(n,\No)$. \Cref{ogl:el:prop:finiteside} sits between the two:
it concerns the finite side like the rotation theorems but uses the elementary
mechanism. It does not imply the rotation theorems either, since a homomorphism
defined on the subgroup $\SO(n,\No)\subseteq\SL_n(\Ofin(\R))$ need not extend. The
abelian boundaries also correspond: the circle characters of
\replabel{e3:cut:prop:circlecounter}, the characters of $\UT_n(\Pi_k)$ in
\cref{ogl:alg:sec:unipotent}, and the rank-two countable detection of
\cref{ogl:el:sec:countable}.
\end{remark}

\section{Sharp boundaries of the argument}\label{ogl:el:sec:boundaries}

\subsection{A fixed Hahn workspace is not the full class}

Let $\Gamma$ be a nonzero set-sized divisible ordered abelian group, write
large monomials $X^\gamma$, and put $\Pi_k(\Gamma)=\{f:\supp f\subseteq\Gamma^{>0}\}$
and $\A_{D,k}(\Gamma)=D\oplus\Pi_k(\Gamma)$ inside the Hahn field with set-sized
reverse-well-ordered supports; these rings are sets.

The geometric dichotomy of \cref{ogl:alg:part} persists (06): the real Hahn
field over a divisible group is real closed and the complex one is
algebraically closed \cite[Corollary~4]{Poonen}; unit and bounded-coordinate
rigidity are unchanged; and a positive exponent gives a nonconstant parameter.
So the proofs of \cref{ogl:alg:thm:real,ogl:alg:thm:complex} apply to the
Hahn-cone rings in place of $\BR,\BC$, with integral constant rings as before.

Invisibility cannot persist: the purely infinite elementary group of a
nontrivial set-sized workspace is itself a set-sized nontrivial group, and its
identity homomorphism is faithful. The full-class proofs need arbitrarily many
scales below a support gap (06), and the compression of
\cref{ogl:el:lem:compress} produces a field that need not lie in any
preassigned set-sized subfield or birthday-bounded fragment (07).

\begin{proposition}[Local cardinal form \src{06}]\label{ogl:el:prop:local}
Fix $0\ne c\in\Pi_k(\Gamma)$ and $0<\delta<\supp c$. Let $T\subseteq\Gamma^{>0}$
satisfy $m\alpha<\delta$ for all $\alpha\in T$ and ordinary $m\ge1$. For every
homomorphism from the group generated by the purely infinite elementary
matrices of rank $n\ge3$ over this workspace to a group $H$ with $|T|>|H|$,
every root element $e_{ij}(c)$ maps to the identity.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The second proof of \cref{ogl:el:thm:invisible} applies verbatim: for the two
colliding exponents $\alpha>\beta$, $\alpha+m(\alpha-\beta)\le(m+1)\alpha<\delta$,
so $c/(X^\alpha-X^\beta)$ lies in the workspace with strictly positive
support. No normality or perfectness in the workspace is needed.
\end{proof}

This is a conditional lower bound on target size, not an assertion that every
exponent group supplies such a $T$: for an Archimedean group no positive
$\alpha$ has all $m\alpha<\delta$ (06). \Cref{ogl:el:prop:criterion} can be
applied at a set-sized stage for targets smaller than an available parameter
family; an optimal threshold there requires further information about support
bounds and the exponent group (07; \cref{ogl:q:thresholds}). For the rings of
supports of size $<\kappa$ over $\bigoplus_{\alpha<\kappa}\Q$ and for the
sibling report's other set-sized models this information is available, and the
optimal threshold is determined in
\cref{ogl:ker:prop:exact,ogl:ker:cor:fivemodels} \mergetag.

\subsection{Finite supports change the answer}

Let $\Pi_k^{\mathrm{fin}}$ consist of the purely infinite normal forms with
finite support and $A^{\mathrm{fin}}=D+\Pi_k^{\mathrm{fin}}$; the exponent class
is still all of $\No$.

\begin{proposition}[A visible finite-support kernel \src{06, 11}]\label{ogl:el:prop:finsupport}
The coefficient sum $\sum_sa_s\omega^s\mapsto\sum_sa_s$ is a ring homomorphism
$A^{\mathrm{fin}}\to k$, and for $n\ge3$ it maps the group generated by the
purely infinite elementary matrices onto the set-sized group $\SL_n(k)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Sums and products involve finite supports, so expanding a product and summing
coefficients gives multiplicativity; it is evaluation $\omega\mapsto1$ on the
group ring $k[\No]$. For $r\in k$ and $a>0$ the purely infinite $r\omega^a$
maps to $r$, so every $e_{ij}(r)$ is in the image, and these generate
$\SL_n(k)$.
\end{proof}

The finite-support ideal still has the single-product property, so its
elementary kernel is perfect and normal; nevertheless it has a large ordinary
image. Infinite supports supply the exact divisor \eqref{ogl:el:eq:absorption},
which finite truncations cannot replace (06). Source 11 proves the
nontriviality part (its Proposition~11.3: the same evaluation maps the
finite-support kernel nontrivially to $E_n(k)$, since $e_{12}(\omega)\mapsto
e_{12}(1)$) and notes that the evaluation does not extend to the full ring, by
the ring theorem. This is the group form of the
finite-support augmentation in the sibling report's support threshold
(\replabel{osq:thm:support}).

\subsection{Rank two and the full special linear group}

All three proofs need a third index to convert a killed additive root parameter
into an arbitrary multiplicative one (the third needs it to make the common root
kernel an ideal), so none says anything about rank two; and the theorem
concerns $E_n(A)$, not $\SL_n(A)$, and uses no stable-range theorem for an
omnific ring (06, 07, 11). Source 06 left both issues open, and so does
source 11 (its Questions~11.1 and 11.2), whose pin already contained 07's
answer in this report; 11 states $n\ge3$ as a proved range, not as a claim
that $n=2$ is a sharp negative boundary. Rank two is
settled by source 07 in the next sections: invisibility fails there
(\cref{ogl:el:thm:countable}). Whether the universal set-sized quotient of
$\SL_n(\Oz)$, $n\ge3$, is $\SL_n(\Z)$ remains open (\cref{ogl:q:sln}); for
$n=2$, $\SL_2(\Oz)\ne E_2(\Oz)$ (\cref{ogl:el:thm:nonel}).
\section{Two rows: an amalgam detected by finite and infinite points}\label{ogl:el:sec:amalgam}

From here to the end of \cref{ogl:el:sec:stabilization} the source is 07, and $(D,k)$ is $(\Z,\R)$ or
$(\Z[i],\C)$, $A=\A_{D,k}$, $K_D=\Frac D$. The proof of
\cref{ogl:el:thm:invisible} cannot be repeated with two rows, because it uses a
third index. The two-row group is identified explicitly instead. The argument
is a specialized elementary form of Cohn's classical two-row normal-form method
\cite{Cohn}; it also gives the Gaussian presentation needed later.

\subsection{The upper triangular subgroup}
Write $G_D=\SL_2(D)$,
\[
 C_D=\Bigl\{\begin{pmatrix}u&d\\0&u^{-1}\end{pmatrix}:u\in D^\times,\ d\in D\Bigr\},
 \qquad
 B_D=\Bigl\{\begin{pmatrix}u&r\\0&u^{-1}\end{pmatrix}:u\in D^\times,\ r\in A\Bigr\},
\]
$U(x)=e_{12}(x)$ and $U(\Pi_k)=\{U(x):x\in\Pi_k\}$. By \cref{ogl:lem:units},
$B_D$ is the whole upper triangular subgroup of $\SL_2(A)$; each member is
uniquely $U(x)c$ with $x\in\Pi_k$ and $c=\ct$ of it in $C_D$. For $c$ with
diagonal $u,u^{-1}$,
\begin{equation}\label{ogl:el:eq:Caction}
 cU(x)c^{-1}=U(u^2x),\qquad\text{so}\qquad B_D=U(\Pi_k)\rtimes C_D .
\end{equation}
The action is trivial for $D=\Z$ and by the signs $\pm1$ for $D=\Z[i]$. Both
$G_D$ and $B_D$ lie in $E_2(A)$, they generate it (every upper elementary matrix
is in $B_D$, and $SU(x)S^{-1}=e_{21}(-x)$ with
$S=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\in G_D$), and
$G_D\cap B_D=C_D$.

\subsection{A projective separation argument}
In $\PP^1(F)=F\cup\{\infty\}$ let $P_{\mathrm{fin}}=\{z\in F:|z|<m\text{ for
some ordinary }m\}$ be the finite points and $P_\infty$ the rest (infinite
elements and $\infty$), with the fractional-linear action of determinant-one
matrices.

\begin{lemma}[Alternation of finite and infinite points \src{07}]\label{ogl:el:lem:pingpong}
\leavevmode
\begin{enumerate}
\item Every $b\in B_D\setminus C_D$ maps $P_{\mathrm{fin}}$ into $P_\infty$ and
fixes $\infty$.
\item Every $g=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\in
G_D\setminus C_D$ maps $P_\infty$ into $P_{\mathrm{fin}}$, has $c\ne0$, and
$\st(gz)=a/c\in K_D$ for $z\in P_\infty$ (exactly $a/c$ at $z=\infty$).
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
(i) Write $b=U(x)c'$ with $0\ne x\in\Pi_k$, $c'\in C_D$: $c'$ preserves finite
points and fixes $\infty$, and adding the nonzero purely infinite $x$ makes a
finite point infinite. (ii) $c\ne0$ since $g$ is not upper triangular; its pole
$-d/c$ is constant, hence not in $P_\infty$. For infinite $z$,
\begin{equation}\label{ogl:el:eq:mobiusresidue}
 \frac{az+b}{cz+d}-\frac ac=\frac{bc-ad}{c(cz+d)}=-\frac1{c(cz+d)},
\end{equation}
which is infinitesimal.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[The two-row amalgam \src{07}]\label{ogl:el:thm:amalgam}
The inclusions induce an isomorphism $E_2(A)\cong G_D*_{C_D}B_D$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Surjectivity is generation. For injectivity use reduced words of the amalgam:
a nontrivial reduced word is conjugate to a nonidentity element of one factor or
to a cyclically reduced word
$g_1b_1g_2b_2\cdots g_mb_m$ with $g_j\in G_D\setminus C_D$,
$b_j\in B_D\setminus C_D$. These finite-word rules are valid for class groups:
words are finite, coset representatives are sets, and each shortening step
reduces the finite length. Apply the matrix of the cyclic word to $\infty$:
$b_m$ fixes it, $g_m$ sends it into $P_{\mathrm{fin}}$, $b_{m-1}$ back into
$P_\infty$, and so on (\cref{ogl:el:lem:pingpong}); the final image is finite,
so the matrix is not the identity. Nonidentity elements of the factors are
nonidentity matrices, and conjugation preserves this.
\end{proof}

No convergence, continued infinite fraction or action on a set-sized tree is
involved, and the Gaussian case needs no order on the coefficients (07). The
proof used only that the constant subgroup is $\SL_2(D)$, that nonconstant upper
triangular translations are infinite, that all units are constant, and that a
non-upper-triangular constant matrix sends infinite points to finite ones. So it
applies to the subgroup of $\SL_2(K_D(t))$ generated by $G_D$ and
$B'_D=\{U(tq)c:q\in\Q,\ c\in C_D\}$, using degree at infinity or the injective
embedding $K_D(t)\to F$, $t\mapsto\omega$:
\begin{equation}\label{ogl:el:eq:targetamalgam}
 \langle G_D,B'_D\rangle\cong G_D*_{C_D}B'_D .
\end{equation}

\section{The two-row kernel as a free product}\label{ogl:el:sec:freeproduct}

$G_D$ acts transitively on $\PP^1(K_D)$ with stabilizer $C_D$ at $\infty$ (a
point is represented by a coprime pair $(a,c)\in D^2$, which B\'ezout completes
to a first column in $\SL_2(D)$), so $G_D/C_D\cong\PP^1(K_D)$, a countable set.
Choose a transversal $\cT\subset G_D$ of these left cosets containing $1$.

\begin{theorem}[Kernel decomposition \src{07}]\label{ogl:el:thm:freeproduct}
There is an isomorphism $P_2\cong\mathop{*}_{t\in\cT}\Pi_k^{(t)}$, each
$\Pi_k^{(t)}$ a copy of $(\Pi_k,+)$ embedded by $x\mapsto tU(x)t^{-1}$, and
\[
 E_2(A)\cong\Bigl(\mathop{*}_{t\in\cT}\Pi_k^{(t)}\Bigr)\rtimes G_D,
\]
where, if $gt=t'c$ with $t'\in\cT$, $c\in C_D$ of first diagonal entry $u$, the
action sends $x$ in factor $t$ to $u^2x$ in factor $t'$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $\cF$ be the free product with inclusions $\iota_t$, and let $G_D$ act by
$g\cdot\iota_t(x)=\iota_{t'}(u^2x)$ when $gt=t'c$. Each $u^2=\pm1$ gives an
automorphism of the factor; coset factorization is unique, and if $g_2t=t'c_2$,
$g_1t'=t''c_1$ then $g_1g_2t=t''c_1c_2$ with multiplier the product, so this is
an action. In $\cF\rtimes G_D$, $\iota_1(\Pi_k)$ and $C_D$ form
$\Pi_k\rtimes C_D$ with the action \eqref{ogl:el:eq:Caction}, which gives a
homomorphism from $B_D$ agreeing with $G_D$ on $C_D$, hence
$G_D*_{C_D}B_D\to\cF\rtimes G_D$. Conversely send $G_D$ to its constant
matrices and $\iota_t(x)$ to $tU(x)t^{-1}$; the defining relation of the action
is \eqref{ogl:el:eq:Caction}. The two maps are inverse on generators; under the
isomorphism, constant reduction is the projection to $G_D$, whose kernel is
$\cF$. Combine with \cref{ogl:el:thm:amalgam}.
\end{proof}

For $D=\Z$ every $u^2=1$ and the action permutes factors indexed by
$\PP^1(\Q)$. In the Gaussian case a change of representative can act on a
factor by a sign; omitting it would misdescribe the semidirect product (07).

\begin{corollary}[Internal abelianization and torsion \src{07}]\label{ogl:el:cor:kernelab}
$P_2^{\ab}\cong\bigoplus_{q\in\PP^1(K_D)}(\Pi_k,+)$. The group $P_2$ is
torsion-free and not perfect.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
A free product abelianizes to the direct sum of the abelianized factors, here
abelian and nonzero. A cyclically reduced word with one syllable lies in a
torsion-free factor; with several syllables its powers concatenate without
cancellation. Conjugation preserves finite order.
\end{proof}

Torsion-freeness also follows, by a different route, from source 11's trace
argument, which applies to the constant-term kernel of $\GL_n$ for every $n$
(\cref{ogl:ker:thm:torsionfree}).

This internal abelianization must not be confused with the image of $P_2$ in
$E_2(A)^{\ab}$, where the constant group can identify or kill classes
(\cref{ogl:el:sec:abelian}).

\section{Countable representations separating finite sets}\label{ogl:el:sec:countable}

The needed additive functionals can be built without a basis of the
proper-class vector space $\Pi_k$ (07).

\begin{lemma}[A set-based additive functional \src{07}]\label{ogl:el:lem:functional}
For finitely many nonzero $x_1,\dots,x_m\in\Pi_k$ there is a $\Q$-linear class
map $\ell:\Pi_k\to\Q$ with $\ell(x_j)\ne0$ for all $j$, factoring through
finitely many coefficient extractions into the set $k^r$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Select for each $j$ one exponent where $x_j$ has a nonzero coefficient; list the
selected exponents $s_1,\dots,s_r$ and put
$\mathsf p(x)=(\coeff{s_1}{x},\dots,\coeff{s_r}{x})\in k^r$. Each $\mathsf p(x_j)\ne0$.
Let $W\subseteq k^r$ be the finite-dimensional $\Q$-span of these vectors with
basis $w_1,\dots,w_d$, and $f_M(w_h)=M^{h-1}$; $f_M(\mathsf p(x_j))$ is a
nonzero polynomial in $M$, so some integer $M$ avoids the finitely many roots.
Extend $f_M$ $\Q$-linearly to $k^r$ by extending a basis of the set $W$, and set
$\ell=f_M\circ\mathsf p$.
\end{proof}

This is an existence construction with set choice, not a computability claim
about arbitrary real or complex coefficients (07).

\begin{proposition}[Additive specialization \src{07}]\label{ogl:el:prop:specialize}
For every additive $\ell:\Pi_k\to\Q$ there is a unique homomorphism
$\rho_\ell:E_2(A)\to\SL_2(K_D(t))$ with $\rho_\ell(g)=g$ for $g\in G_D$ and
$\rho_\ell(U(x))=U(t\ell(x))$ for $x\in\Pi_k$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
On $B_D=U(\Pi_k)\rtimes C_D$ send $U(x)c\mapsto U(t\ell(x))c$. Root addition is
preserved, and compatibility with the $C_D$-action is $\ell(u^2x)=u^2\ell(x)$,
true since $u^2=\pm1$. This agrees with the identity on $C_D$;
\cref{ogl:el:thm:amalgam} extends it, and generation gives uniqueness.
\end{proof}

In the Gaussian case $\ell$ need not be complex-linear or satisfy
$\ell(ix)=i\ell(x)$: diagonal conjugation only involves $i^2=-1$. This is why
Gaussian countable representations survive although the Gaussian abelian
characters behave differently (07).

\begin{theorem}[One countable target separates finite patterns \src{07}]\label{ogl:el:thm:countable}
For every finite $\mathcal S\subset E_2(A)$ some $\rho_\ell$ is injective on
$\mathcal S$. In particular every nonidentity element of $E_2(A)$ is detected by
a homomorphism into the fixed countable group $\SL_2(K_D(t))$ that is the
identity on $\SL_2(D)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Keep nontrivial each $a^{-1}b$, $a\ne b$ in $\mathcal S$. Write them in reduced
amalgam form; the $B_D\setminus C_D$ syllables are $U(x)c$ with $x\ne0$, finitely
many. Choose $\ell$ nonzero on all these $x$ (\cref{ogl:el:lem:functional}).
Under $\rho_\ell$, $G_D\setminus C_D$ syllables are unchanged and
$B_D\setminus C_D$ syllables go to $B'_D\setminus C_D$, so reduced words stay
reduced in \eqref{ogl:el:eq:targetamalgam} and are nontrivial; one-factor words
are preserved as well. $K_D(t)$ is countable.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[A commutator needing a nonabelian detector \src{07}]\label{ogl:el:ex:commutator}
Let $\ell$ extract the coefficient of $\omega^1$ (followed by a $\Q$-linear map
taking $1$ to $1$). Then
\[
 \rho_\ell\bigl([U(\omega),e_{21}(\omega)]\bigr)=[U(t),e_{21}(t)]
 =\begin{pmatrix}1+t^2+t^4&-t^3\\t^3&1-t^2\end{pmatrix}\ne I_2 ,
\]
a countable nonabelian image of a commutator in $P_2$, killed of course by every
abelian image; valid over both coefficient rings.
\end{example}

\begin{corollary}[No universal set-sized quotient in rank two \src{07}]\label{ogl:el:cor:nouniversal}
Neither $E_2(A)$ nor $P_2$ has a universal set-sized group quotient.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
If $q:E_2(A)\to H$ were one, every $\rho_\ell$ would factor through $q$, so
$\ker q=1$ by \cref{ogl:el:thm:countable}; but $U(\Pi_k)$ contains more than
$|H|$ distinct monomials. Restricting the $\rho_\ell$ to $P_2$ gives the same for
$P_2$.
\end{proof}

There is no contradiction between residual countability and proper-class size:
the separating maps are not a set-indexed family whose product is a faithful
set-sized representation, and no single faithful countable representation is
asserted (07).

\section{Finite quotients and the detection trichotomy}\label{ogl:el:sec:finite}

\begin{lemma}[Divisibility kills finite root images \src{07}]\label{ogl:el:lem:finite}
Every homomorphism from $(\Pi_k,+)$ to a finite group is trivial.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
With $m=|H|$, $x/m\in\Pi_k$ and $f(x)=f(x/m)^m=1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[All finite images are arithmetic \src{07}]\label{ogl:el:prop:finitefactor}
For every $n\ge2$ every homomorphism from $E_n(A)$ to a finite group factors
uniquely through $\ct$, and every homomorphism from $P_2$ to a finite group is
trivial.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
A finite-target map kills each $e_{ij}(\Pi_k)$ (\cref{ogl:el:lem:finite}) and
hence its normal closure $P_n$ (\cref{ogl:el:lem:normalgen}). For $P_2$ use the
free product (\cref{ogl:el:thm:freeproduct}).
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Finite detection in the constant group \src{07}]\label{ogl:el:lem:ordinaryrf}
$\SL_n(D)$ is residually finite for $n\ge2$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
If $g\ne1$, a nonzero entry $a$ of $g-1$ is not in $mD$ for a suitable ordinary
$m\ge2$ (for Gaussian $a$, take $m$ larger than both integer coordinates), and
reduction to $\SL_n(D/mD)$ does not kill $g$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Finite and set residuals \src{07}]\label{ogl:el:thm:residuals}
For every $n\ge2$, $\resfin E_n(A)=P_n$. The set residual (the class of elements
killed by every homomorphism to a set-sized group) satisfies
$\resset E_2(A)=1$ and $\resset E_n(A)=P_n$ for $n\ge3$. For the kernels,
$\resfin P_2=P_2$, $\resset P_2=1$ and $\resset P_n=P_n$ for $n\ge3$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Finite maps kill $P_n$ (\cref{ogl:el:prop:finitefactor}), and elements outside
$P_n$ survive a finite map (\cref{ogl:el:lem:ordinaryrf}). The set residuals
follow from \cref{ogl:el:thm:countable,ogl:el:thm:invisible}, and those of the
kernels also from the last clause of \cref{ogl:el:prop:finitefactor}.
\end{proof}

The finite quotient theory of $E_n(A)$ is exactly that of $\SL_n(D)$; its
profinite completion, defined by this set-indexed system, is that of
$\SL_n(D)$. No congruence subgroup property is involved: arbitrary finite
quotients are identified, and congruence reduction was used only for
\cref{ogl:el:lem:ordinaryrf} (07). For $n\ge3$ source 11 proves the finite and
set residual statements again (its Theorem~9.1, by the same reduction modulo
$m$), and for $\Oz$ it then identifies the completion with
$\SL_n(\widehat\Z)$ by importing the congruence subgroup theorem
(\cref{ogl:ker:cor:profinite}); that corollary is the only place in the
report where a congruence subgroup property enters.

\begin{theorem}[The detection trichotomy \src{07}]\label{ogl:el:thm:trichotomy}
Let $n\ge2$ and $1\ne g\in E_n(A)$. Exactly the applicable alternative holds:
\begin{enumerate}
\item if $\ct(g)\ne1$, some finite group homomorphism detects $g$;
\item if $n=2$ and $g\in P_2$, no finite group homomorphism detects $g$, a
countable one does, and the least cardinality of a detecting target is
$\aleph_0$, also for homomorphisms defined only on $P_2$;
\item if $n\ge3$ and $g\in P_n$, no homomorphism to any set-sized group detects
$g$, also for homomorphisms defined only on $P_n$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\Cref{ogl:el:lem:ordinaryrf,ogl:el:prop:finitefactor,ogl:el:thm:countable,ogl:el:thm:invisible}.
The last alternative is the nonexistence of any set-sized detecting target, not
an ``infinite cardinal larger than all cardinals''.
\end{proof}

\section{Abelian characters: a real--Gaussian contrast}\label{ogl:el:sec:abelian}

\begin{proposition}[The real abelianization, a classical specialization \src{07}]\label{ogl:el:prop:realab}
$E_2(\Oz)^{\ab}\cong\Z/12\oplus(\Pi,+)$. For every $s\in\No^{>0}$ there is a
surjective homomorphism $\chi_s:E_2(\Oz)\to(\R,+)$ with $\chi_s(\SL_2(\Z))=0$ and
$\chi_s(U(x))=\coeff{s}{x}$ for $x\in\Pi$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For a split group $\cF\rtimes G$, the abelianization is $G^{\ab}$ plus the
$G$-coinvariants of $\cF^{\ab}$. By
\cref{ogl:el:thm:freeproduct,ogl:el:cor:kernelab}, $\cF^{\ab}$ is a direct sum
of copies of $\Pi$ on which $\SL_2(\Z)$ acts by permuting the indices
transitively without twist; the coinvariants are one copy of $\Pi$ (sum of the
finitely many nonzero coordinates). And $\SL_2(\Z)^{\ab}=\Z/12$: from the
presentation with generators $S,U(1)$ and relations $S^4=1$, $S^2=(SU(1))^3$,
an abelian quotient has $[S]=-3[U(1)]$ and $12[U(1)]=0$ and nothing more.
Project the second summand to its $\omega^s$-coefficient for $\chi_s$;
$\chi_s(U(r\omega^s))=r$.
\end{proof}

The formula is not a novelty claim (07). It is the omnific case of Cohn's
abelianization theorem \cite{Cohn} in the formulation of Mirzaii and Torres
P\'erez \cite{MirzaiiTorres}: their Corollary~3.3 (credited to Cohn) gives
$E_2(R)^{\ab}\cong R/M$ for rings universal for $\mathrm{GE}_2$, with $M$ the
additive subgroup generated by $x(a^2-1)$ and $3(b+1)(c+1)$ ($x\in R$,
$a,b,c\in R^\times$); their Example~3.4(i) gives $R/M=R/12\Z$ when
$R^\times=\{\pm1\}$; and their Example~1.1(vi) records Cohn's theorem that
discretely ordered rings are universal for $\mathrm{GE}_2$. These numbers
were checked against the arXiv version~3 of \cite{MirzaiiTorres} for this
report. Those statements concern rings that are sets; for the class ring $\Oz$,
discretely ordered with units $\pm1$, the proof above is self-contained. The
quotient is by the additive subgroup $12\Z$, not by the ideal $12\Oz=12\Z+\Pi$,
which would wrongly discard the purely infinite summand (07).

\begin{proposition}[Gaussian ambient abelianization \src{07}]\label{ogl:el:prop:gaussianab}
Constant reduction induces $E_2(\GOz)^{\ab}\cong\SL_2(\Z[i])^{\ab}$. Every
homomorphism from $E_2(\GOz)$ to an abelian group kills $P_2$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
With $d(i)=\diag(i,-i)$, $d(i)U(y)d(i)^{-1}=U(-y)$, and since $x/2\in\Pi_\C$,
\begin{equation}\label{ogl:el:eq:gaussiancomm}
 [d(i),U(-x/2)]=U(x)\qquad(x\in\Pi_\C).
\end{equation}
Conjugating by $S$ handles lower roots, and normal closure gives
$P_2\subseteq[E_2,E_2]$. The constant inclusion is an inverse on
abelianizations.
\end{proof}

No numerical description of $\SL_2(\Z[i])^{\ab}$ is needed or claimed. The
comparison is new in 07: purely infinite Gaussian information is absent from
every abelian image but visible in countable nonabelian images
(\cref{ogl:el:thm:countable}). This does not make $P_2$ perfect: $d(i)\notin P_2$,
and $P_2^{\ab}$ is still the large sum of \cref{ogl:el:cor:kernelab}; for $n\ge3$
the commutators of \cref{ogl:el:prop:normal} have both entries in $P_n$.

\section{Cusp residues, non-elementary unipotents, and stabilization}\label{ogl:el:sec:stabilization}

\begin{proposition}[The finite cusp-residue obstruction \src{07}]\label{ogl:el:prop:cuspresidue}
If $h\in E_2(A)$ and $h\cdot\infty$ is finite, then $\st(h\cdot\infty)\in K_D$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By \cref{ogl:el:thm:amalgam} write
$h=a_0U(x_1)a_1\cdots a_{m-1}U(x_m)a_m$ with nonzero $x_j\in\Pi_k$, interior
$a_j\in G_D\setminus C_D$ and endpoint $a_0,a_m\in G_D$ (absorb triangular
constant parts into neighbours, move interior triangular constants through upper
roots by \eqref{ogl:el:eq:Caction}, merge adjacent roots, and shorten when a
parameter vanishes; the process is finite). If $m=0$ the claim is clear. If
$m\ge1$, $a_m$ sends $\infty$ to $\infty$ or to a point of $K_D$, $U(x_m)$ sends
that point into $P_\infty$, each interior $a_j$ sends $P_\infty$ into
$P_{\mathrm{fin}}$ with standard part in $K_D$, and the next root sends it back
into $P_\infty$. If $a_0\in C_D$ the final point is in $P_\infty$, contrary to
finiteness; otherwise \cref{ogl:el:lem:pingpong}(ii) applies.
\end{proof}

The obstruction is stronger than looking at constant reduction: a matrix can
reduce to the identity and still fail it, because a ratio of its large entries
has a nonarithmetic finite standard part. The polynomial pattern
$\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1+xy&x^2\\-y^2&1-xy\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$ is Cohn's
example of a non-elementary matrix over $k[x,y]$ \cite[Section~7, p.~26]{Cohn};
neither it nor the block identity below is claimed as new (07).

\begin{theorem}[A non-elementary omnific unipotent \src{07}]\label{ogl:el:thm:nonel}
Let $\alpha\in k\setminus K_D$, $a>0$, and
\[
 Q_\alpha=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&1\\-\alpha^2&-\alpha\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad M_\alpha=I_2+\omega^{2a}Q_\alpha .
\]
Then $Q_\alpha^2=0$, $\det M_\alpha=1$, $\ct(M_\alpha)=I_2$, $M_\alpha$ has
infinite order, and $M_\alpha\in\SL_2(A)\setminus E_2(A)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The square and determinant are polynomial identities; the nonconstant entries
have exponent $2a>0$ and coefficients in $k$. With $T=\omega^{2a}$,
$M_\alpha\cdot\infty=(1+\alpha T)/(-\alpha^2T)=-1/\alpha-1/(\alpha^2T)$ is finite
with standard part $-1/\alpha\notin K_D$, so \cref{ogl:el:prop:cuspresidue}
excludes $E_2(A)$. Finally $M_\alpha^m=I_2+m\omega^{2a}Q_\alpha\ne I_2$.
\end{proof}

For $a=1/2$ and $\alpha=\sqrt2$,
\begin{equation}\label{ogl:el:eq:exampleM}
 M=\begin{pmatrix}1+\sqrt2\,\omega&\omega\\-2\omega&1-\sqrt2\,\omega\end{pmatrix}
 \in\SL_2(\Oz)\setminus E_2(\Oz),
\end{equation}
and the same matrix is non-elementary over $\GOz$ since $\sqrt2\notin\Q(i)$.
This needs no factorization theory of omnific integers. It is consistent with the
omnific Diophantine report's non-Euclidean phenomena (pairs without a gcd,
\replabel{odg:thm:nogcd}), and with its rationality theorem for primitive
constant directions (\replabel{odg:thm:realray}), which concerns constant real
projective points; the first column of $M_\alpha$ is not one.

\begin{lemma}[A finite block identity \src{07}]\label{ogl:el:lem:block}
Over any commutative ring, for a column $v$ and a row $w$ of length two with
$wv=0$,
\[
 \Bigl[\begin{pmatrix}I_2&v\\0&1\end{pmatrix},
 \begin{pmatrix}I_2&0\\w&1\end{pmatrix}\Bigr]
 =\begin{pmatrix}I_2+vw&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The inverses replace $v,w$ by their negatives; multiply the four blocks, and the
terms containing $wv$ vanish.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[One-step stabilization \src{07}]\label{ogl:el:thm:stabilize}
With $z=\omega^a$, $v=z(1,-\alpha)^{\mathsf T}$, $w=z(\alpha,1)$,
$X=e_{13}(z)e_{23}(-\alpha z)$ and $Y=e_{31}(\alpha z)e_{32}(z)$, one has
$X,Y\in P_3$ and $[X,Y]=\diag(M_\alpha,1)$. Hence this nonidentity matrix is
killed by every homomorphism from $P_3$ or $E_3(A)$ to a set-sized group.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
All parameters lie in $\Pi_k$. $X$ and $Y$ are the block matrices of
\cref{ogl:el:lem:block}, $wv=z^2(\alpha-\alpha)=0$ and $vw=z^2Q_\alpha$. Apply
\cref{ogl:el:thm:invisible}.
\end{proof}

The commutator is an eight-factor elementary word. The identity itself does
not depend on surreals; the surreal content is the coefficient ring, the
residue obstruction before stabilization and the invisibility after it (07).

\begin{corollary}[A strict failure of extension \src{07}]\label{ogl:el:cor:noextend}
Let $j:E_2(A)\to E_3(A)$, $g\mapsto\diag(g,1)$. If $\rho:E_2(A)\to H$, $H$
set-sized, detects a nonidentity element of $P_2$, then $\rho$ does not extend
along $j$ to a homomorphism into $H$, nor into any set-sized group containing
$H$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
$j(P_2)\subseteq P_3$, which every set-target homomorphism kills
(\cref{ogl:el:thm:invisible}); an injective enlargement of $H$ preserves
$\rho(g)\ne1$.
\end{proof}

For example $U(\omega)$ is detected in two rows by a map to $U(t)$, but its
block inclusion into three rows is killed by every set-sized representation. The
embedding is injective; what changes is the class of homomorphisms permitted by
the extra relations (07).

The two parts of this report meet at one point. In \cref{ogl:lat:sec:reduction}
bases of $\A^n$ are changed by $\GL_n(\A)$; \cref{ogl:el:thm:nonel} shows that
already $\SL_2(\Oz)$ is not reached by elementary moves, while
\cref{ogl:lat:thm:nolll} obstructs the \emph{existence} of reduced bases whatever
group is used.

\section{The kernel for \texorpdfstring{$n\ge3$}{n >= 3}: torsion, center and normal subgroups}\label{ogl:ker:sec:structure}

This section and the next two are source 11's, with two merge results at the
end of \cref{ogl:ker:sec:fragments}. Throughout them $(D,k)$ is $(\Z,\R)$ or
$(\Z[i],\C)$, $A=\A_{D,k}$, and $P_n$ is the kernel
\eqref{ogl:el:eq:kernel}; for $n\ge3$, $P_n=\langle e_{ij}(\Pi_k)\rangle$ and
$E_n(A)=P_n\rtimes\SL_n(D)$ (\cref{ogl:el:thm:invisible}). Source 11 writes
$U_n(\Pi_k)$ for the root-generated subgroup and $N_n(A)$ for the kernel,
deliberately never identifies them, and proves its structural statements for
both. By \cref{ogl:el:prop:split} (06) they are one group, so the statements are
printed for $P_n$; none of 11's proofs uses the identification.

\subsection{Torsion and center}

\begin{theorem}[Torsion-free constant-term kernels \src{11}]\label{ogl:ker:thm:torsionfree}
For every $n\ge1$ the group $\ker\bigl(\ct:\GL_n(A)\to\GL_n(D)\bigr)$ is
torsion-free. In particular $P_n$ is torsion-free for every $n\ge2$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $g^r=1_n$ with $r\ge1$ and $\ct(g)=1_n$. Work in the algebraically closed
field $\SC$, which contains both coefficient cases. The polynomial $X^r-1$ has
the $r$ distinct ordinary complex $r$th roots of unity as its roots, and the
minimal polynomial of $g$ divides it; so $g$ is diagonalizable over $\SC$ with
eigenvalues $\zeta_1,\dots,\zeta_n\in\C$. Hence $\tr g=\sum_j\zeta_j$ is an
ordinary complex number, in particular finite; it lies in $A$, whose finite
elements are those of $D$ (\cref{ogl:lem:units}(iii)). So
$\tr g=\ct(\tr g)=\tr\ct(g)=n$. Each $\zeta_j$ has modulus one, so its real part
is at most $1$, and the real parts sum to $n$; hence every $\zeta_j=1$, and the
diagonalizable $g$ is $1_n$.
\end{proof}

For $n=2$ this is a second route to the torsion-freeness of $P_2$ in
\cref{ogl:el:cor:kernelab}, where 07 derives it from the free product. The
theorem concerns the constant-term kernel only; the constant subgroup
$\SL_n(D)$ has torsion.

\begin{theorem}[Centerlessness \src{11}]\label{ogl:ker:thm:center}
For $n\ge3$, $P_n$ has trivial center.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $g$ be central and fix $0\ne p\in\Pi_k$. Since $e_{ij}(p)=1_n+pE_{ij}\in P_n$
and $p\ne0$ in the field $F$, $gE_{ij}=E_{ij}g$ for all $i\ne j$. Comparing
entries, $g_{ai}\delta_{jb}=\delta_{ai}g_{jb}$: with $b=j$ and $a\ne i$ every
off-diagonal entry vanishes, and with $a=i$, $b=j$ one gets $g_{ii}=g_{jj}$. So
$g=\lambda1_n$ with $\lambda,\lambda^{-1}\in A$, hence $\lambda\in D^\times$
(\cref{ogl:lem:units}(ii)), and $\ct(g)=1_n$ forces $\lambda=1$.
\end{proof}

$P_n$ is nonabelian: $[e_{12}(\omega),e_{23}(\omega)]=e_{13}(\omega^2)\ne1_n$.

\begin{corollary}[Set-sized normal subgroups and conjugacy classes \src{11}]\label{ogl:ker:cor:normalsets}
For $n\ge3$, $P_n$ has no nontrivial set-sized normal subgroup, and the
conjugacy class in $P_n$ of every nonidentity element is a proper class.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
If $S\lhd P_n$ is a set, conjugation is a homomorphism $P_n\to\Aut(S)$ into a
set-sized group, trivial by \cref{ogl:el:thm:invisible}(iii); so $S$ is central
and $S=1$ by \cref{ogl:ker:thm:center}. If the conjugacy class of $x\ne1$ were a
set $X$, conjugation would give a homomorphism $P_n\to\Sym(X)$, again trivial;
then $x$ would be central.
\end{proof}

This is consistent with the tower of \cref{ogl:el:thm:tower}: its members
$P_n^{(a)}$ are nontrivial proper normal subgroups, and so are proper classes;
invisibility does not make $P_n$ simple.

\begin{proposition}[Finite subgroups are detected by constant terms \src{11}]\label{ogl:ker:prop:finitesubgroups}
Let $n\ge3$. Every finite subgroup $\Theta\le E_n(A)$ is mapped injectively into
$\SL_n(D)$ by $\ct$. The isomorphism types of finite subgroups of $E_n(A)$ are
exactly those of $\SL_n(D)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The kernel of $\ct|_\Theta$ is a finite subgroup of the torsion-free group
$P_n$ (\cref{ogl:ker:thm:torsionfree}), hence trivial. Conversely the constant
inclusion embeds every finite subgroup of $\SL_n(D)$.
\end{proof}

The statement concerns isomorphism types and injectivity. It is not claimed
that every finite subgroup of $E_n(A)$ is conjugate to a constant subgroup;
that would need a separate analysis of splittings over finite groups (11).

\subsection{A principal congruence chain and common congruence elements}

For $0\ne a\in A$ put $P_n\langle a\rangle=\{g\in P_n:g-1_n\in M_n(aA)\}$, so that
$P_n^{(a)}=P_n\langle\omega^a\rangle$ in \eqref{ogl:el:eq:Pa}. As there, the
ideal $aA$ is used only as a congruence relation; no quotient ring is asserted to
be a set.

\begin{theorem}[A chain at $1+\omega$ \src{11}]\label{ogl:ker:thm:filtration}
Let $n\ge3$. The subgroups $P_n\langle(1+\omega)^m\rangle$, $m\ge0$, are normal in
$P_n$, $P_n\langle1\rangle=P_n$, and
\[
 P_n\langle(1+\omega)^m\rangle\supsetneq P_n\langle(1+\omega)^{m+1}\rangle
 \qquad(m\ge0),
\]
while $e_{12}(\omega^\omega)$ lies in every one of them.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Matrices congruent to $1_n$ modulo an ideal form a normal subgroup of
$\GL_n(A)$, which gives normality and the inclusions. The parameter
$p_m=(1+\omega)^m\omega^{1/2}$ has support $\{j+1/2:0\le j\le m\}$, so
$e_{12}(p_m)\in P_n\langle(1+\omega)^m\rangle$. If it lay in the next subgroup,
$\omega^{1/2}/(1+\omega)$ would lie in $A$; its leading exponent is $-1/2$, which
is impossible for a nonzero element of $A$. For $m\ge1$,
\begin{equation}\label{ogl:ker:eq:persistent}
 \frac{\omega^\omega}{(1+\omega)^m}
 =\sum_{r\ge0}(-1)^r\binom{m+r-1}{r}\,\omega^{\omega-m-r}\in\Pi_k ,
\end{equation}
since $(1+\omega)^{-m}=\omega^{-m}(1+\omega^{-1})^{-m}$; the exponents
$\omega-m-r$ are positive and strictly decreasing, and multiplying by
$(1+\omega)^m$ checks the identity coefficientwise. So $\omega^\omega\in(1+\omega)^mA$
for every $m$.
\end{proof}

The comparison with \cref{ogl:el:thm:tower} is instructive. The tower uses the
monomial ideals $\omega^aA$ over the whole class of scales $a>0$ and separates
the elements of $P_n$; the chain above is countable, uses powers of a
non-monomial element, and does not separate. The second phenomenon is general
for sets of principal congruences:

\begin{proposition}[Common root elements for a set of congruences \src{11}]\label{ogl:ker:prop:commoncongruence}
Let $(a_s)_{s\in S}$ be a set-indexed family of nonzero elements of $A$. There is
$h_0>0$ such that $\omega^h/a_s\in\Pi_k$ for all $s\in S$ and all $h>h_0$.
Consequently, for $n\ge3$, $i\ne j$ and $h>h_0$,
$e_{ij}(\omega^h)\in\bigcap_{s\in S}P_n\langle a_s\rangle$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Each $a_s^{-1}\in F$ has a set support, and the union $T$ of these supports is a
set. Choose $h_0>-t$ for all $t\in T$ (\cref{ogl:lem:gap}); then every exponent
$h+t$ of $\omega^h/a_s$ is positive for $h>h_0$. Hence
$\omega^h=a_s(\omega^h/a_s)\in a_sA$, and $e_{ij}(\omega^h)\in P_n$.
\end{proof}

This generalizes the set clause of \cref{ogl:el:thm:tower} from the monomials
$\omega^a$ to arbitrary nonzero elements. It says nothing about the
intersection over the proper class of all principal ideals, and makes no claim
that the quotient rings $A/a_sA$ are sets (11).

\subsection{Finite quotients and the profinite completion}

By \cref{ogl:el:thm:residuals} (07; for $n\ge3$ also 11), $\resfin E_n(A)=P_n$,
and by \cref{ogl:el:thm:invisible}(iv) every finite quotient of $E_n(A)$ is a
finite quotient of $\SL_n(D)$ composed with $\ct$. The source is a class group,
so its profinite completion needs a convention; following 11 it is the inverse
limit of its finite quotients, and since the whole diagram of finite quotients
is that of the set group $\SL_n(D)$, this is an ordinary profinite group.

\begin{corollary}[The profinite completion \src{11}]\label{ogl:ker:cor:profinite}
For $n\ge3$ there are canonical isomorphisms
\[
 \widehat{E_n(\Oz)}\cong\widehat{\SL_n(\Z)}\cong\SL_n(\widehat\Z),
 \qquad
 \widehat{E_n(\GOz)}\cong\widehat{\SL_n(\Z[i])}.
\]
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The first isomorphism in each case identifies the finite quotients with those of
the constant subgroup. For the second real isomorphism, the congruence subgroup
theorem of Bass, Milnor and Serre \cite{BMS} says that for $n\ge3$ the
principal congruence subgroups of $\SL_n(\Z)$ are cofinal among its subgroups of
finite index. Reduction $\SL_n(\Z)\to\SL_n(\Z/m\Z)$ is surjective, since
elementary elimination works over each local factor of $\Z/m\Z$ and elementary
parameters lift to integers. So the completion is the inverse limit of the
$\SL_n(\Z/m\Z)$, which is $\SL_n(\widehat\Z)$.
\end{proof}

The last step is a deep imported theorem, not reproved here; the preceding
factorization is proved. No isomorphism of $\widehat{E_n(\GOz)}$ with
$\SL_n(\widehat{\Z[i]})$ is asserted: congruence kernels need care in the
totally imaginary case, which the classical source treats separately from
fields with a real embedding (11).

\section{Steinberg groups}\label{ogl:ker:sec:steinberg}

No other statement about Steinberg groups or $K_2$ appears in the collection.
For a commutative unital ring $B$ and $n\ge3$, $\St_n(B)$ is the group with
generating symbols $x_{ij}(a)$, $a\in B$, $i\ne j$, and the Steinberg relations
\begin{align}
 x_{ij}(a)x_{ij}(b)&=x_{ij}(a+b),\label{ogl:ker:eq:st1}\\
 [x_{ij}(a),x_{lm}(b)]&=1\qquad(i\ne m,\ j\ne l),\label{ogl:ker:eq:st2}\\
 [x_{ih}(a),x_{hj}(b)]&=x_{ij}(ab)\qquad(i,h,j\text{ distinct}).\label{ogl:ker:eq:st3}
\end{align}
$\pi_B:\St_n(B)\to E_n(B)$, $x_{ij}(a)\mapsto e_{ij}(a)$, is a surjective
homomorphism. The symbols $x_{ij}$ are the universal instance of the root maps
of \cref{ogl:ker:thm:rootkernel}. For a proper-class ring, 11 uses finite words
and finite derivations from the relations, and represents an equivalence class
of words by Scott's trick, as the set of equivalent words of least rank; the
carrier is a class of sets with well-defined operations, and no class of proper
classes is formed. Only the presentation and its elementary universal property
are used; no theorem on the kernel of $\pi_B$ or on universal central
extensions is needed.

\begin{lemma}[The split Steinberg kernel \src{11}]\label{ogl:ker:lem:stkernel}
Let $B=D_0\oplus I$ be a unital commutative ring with a ring retraction
$c:B\to D_0$ and $I=\ker c$. Then $c$ induces a split surjection
$\St_n(B)\to\St_n(D_0)$ whose kernel is the normal closure of the $x_{ij}(a)$,
$a\in I$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The inclusion $D_0\hookrightarrow B$ and $c$ induce maps of presentations whose
composite on $\St_n(D_0)$ is the identity. The normal closure $N'$ lies in the
kernel. Modulo $N'$, \eqref{ogl:ker:eq:st1} gives $x_{ij}(d+a)\equiv x_{ij}(d)$,
so every word is congruent to the image of its constant-term word; if that word
is the identity in $\St_n(D_0)$, so is its image in $\St_n(B)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Universal set-sized quotient of the Steinberg group \src{11}]\label{ogl:ker:thm:steinberg}
Let $n\ge3$ and $P_n^{\St}=\ker\bigl(\ct:\St_n(A)\to\St_n(D)\bigr)$. The sequence
$1\to P_n^{\St}\to\St_n(A)\to\St_n(D)\to1$ is split exact, $P_n^{\St}$ is perfect,
every homomorphism from $P_n^{\St}$ to a set-sized group is trivial, and every
homomorphism from $\St_n(A)$ to a set-sized group factors uniquely through
$\St_n(D)$. Moreover $\pi_A(P_n^{\St})=P_n$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $V\le\St_n(A)$ be generated by the $x_{ij}(a)$, $a\in\Pi_k$. Relations
\eqref{ogl:ker:eq:st1} and \eqref{ogl:ker:eq:st3} are the hypotheses of
\cref{ogl:ker:thm:rootkernel}; with the ring theorem
\replabel{osq:thm:universal}(iii), \cref{ogl:ker:cor:transfer} kills every
set-sized image of $V$ and, with \cref{ogl:prop:factor}, makes $V$ perfect. By
\cref{ogl:ker:lem:stkernel} $P_n^{\St}$ is the normal closure of $V$, and
\cref{ogl:ker:lem:closure} transfers both properties. Factorization is as in
\cref{ogl:el:thm:invisible}(iv). Finally $\pi_A$ maps the generators of $V$ onto
the root elements $e_{ij}(\Pi_k)$ and is onto $E_n(A)$, so it maps the normal
closure of $V$ onto the normal closure of those root elements, which is $P_n$
(\cref{ogl:el:lem:normalgen}).
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[No $K$-theoretic conclusion \src{11}]\label{ogl:ker:rem:noK}
The quotient $\St_n(D)$ is a set (countable here: its alphabet is countable and
its words are finite), and it must not be replaced by $\SL_n(D)$ without
accounting for the kernel of $\pi_D$. $P_n^{\St}$ is the kernel of the constant
term, not of $\pi_A:\St_n(A)\to E_n(A)$, which is not computed. The theorem does
not calculate $K_2$, assert a universal central extension in any unstable rank,
establish an excision theorem, or claim anything about algebraic $K$-theory in
general; nor does it assert that $P_n^{\St}$ is centerless or torsion-free: the
proofs of \cref{ogl:ker:thm:torsionfree,ogl:ker:thm:center} are about matrices
and are not transferred.
\end{remark}

\section{Set-sized fragments and exact thresholds}\label{ogl:ker:sec:fragments}

In a fixed set-sized ring the identity map is a faithful set-sized
representation, so only cardinal bounds can survive
(\cref{ogl:el:sec:boundaries}). Source 11 replaces the class of scales by a
regular cardinal. Fix an uncountable regular cardinal $\kappa$ and let
$\Gamma_\kappa=\bigoplus_{\alpha<\kappa}\Q e_\alpha$, ordered by the sign of
the coefficient at the least index where it is nonzero; so $e_\alpha>0$ and
$me_\beta<e_\alpha$ for $\alpha<\beta$ and ordinary $m\ge1$. Let
$\Pi_{<\kappa}$ be the set of Hahn series in $X$ over $\Gamma_\kappa$ with
coefficients in $k$ whose support is a reverse well-ordered subset of
$\Gamma_\kappa^{>0}$ of cardinality $<\kappa$, and $R_{<\kappa}=D\oplus\Pi_{<\kappa}$.
The map $X^g\mapsto\omega^{\iota(g)}$, with $\iota(e_\alpha)=\omega^{-\alpha}$
for the ordinal $\alpha$, embeds $R_{<\kappa}$ in $A$; there are two levels of
exponents, and $X^{e_\alpha}=\omega^{\omega^{-\alpha}}$, for instance
$X^{e_0}=\omega$ and $X^{e_1}=\omega^{1/\omega}$. This is the ring $R^{11}_\kappa$
of the sibling report, with its ideal $\Pi^{11}_\kappa$, the same group
(\replabel{osq:prop:lex}) and the same embedding
(\replabel{osq:rel:lem:embedding}); source 11 describes it only as related to
that report's constructions and proves the facts it needs directly. Put
$P_{n,<\kappa}=\ker\bigl(\ct:E_n(R_{<\kappa})\to E_n(D)\bigr)$ (11's
$N_{n,\kappa,D}$).

\subsection{Cardinal rigidity below \texorpdfstring{$\kappa$}{kappa}}

\begin{lemma}[Closure and small scales \src{11}]\label{ogl:ker:lem:fragment}
\leavevmode
\begin{enumerate}
\item $R_{<\kappa}$ is a set-sized subring of $A$, $\ct$ retracts it onto $D$,
and its units are $D^\times$.
\item For every $S\subseteq\Gamma_\kappa^{>0}$ with $|S|<\kappa$ there is
$\beta<\kappa$ with $me_\beta<s$ for all $s\in S$ and ordinary $m\ge1$.
\item Every element of $\Pi_{<\kappa}$ is a product of two elements of
$\Pi_{<\kappa}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
(i) Unions and sumsets of fewer than $\kappa$ exponents have fewer than $\kappa$
elements, so Hahn multiplication preserves the support bound; positivity and the
multiplicativity of $\ct$ are unchanged, the coefficient field and exponent
group are sets, and the leading-exponent argument of \cref{ogl:lem:units}(ii)
gives the units. (ii) The least nonzero coordinate indices of the members of
$S$ are fewer than $\kappa$ ordinals below $\kappa$, hence bounded below $\kappa$
by regularity; for $\beta$ above all of them, the leading coordinate of each
$s\in S$ is positive and occurs before $\beta$, so $s$ dominates every
$me_\beta$. (iii) For $p\ne0$ take $\beta$ from (ii) for $S=\supp p$ and write
$p=X^{e_\beta}(X^{-e_\beta}p)$.
\end{proof}

Parts (i) and (ii) are also \replabel{osq:rel:lem:embedding}(iii) and (ii).

\begin{lemma}[No small ring image of the fragment ideal \src{11}]\label{ogl:ker:lem:fragmentring}
Every additive multiplicative map from $\Pi_{<\kappa}$ to a ring of cardinality
$<\kappa$ is zero.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Let $\rho$ be such a map and $0\ne p\in\Pi_{<\kappa}$; take $\beta$ from
\cref{ogl:ker:lem:fragment}(ii) for $\supp p$ and put $\delta=e_\beta$. The
$\kappa$ exponents $e_\gamma$, $\beta<\gamma<\kappa$, lie in $(0,\delta)$, so two
of the monomials $X^{e_\gamma}$ have the same image; call their exponents
$\eta<\varepsilon$. By the division lemma \replabel{osq:lem:division} (11's
Lemma~3.1, whose proof uses only the ordered-group inequalities
$\varepsilon+r(\varepsilon-\eta)<(r+1)\delta<s$, reverse well ordering and Hahn
multiplication), $p=(X^\varepsilon-X^\eta)q$ with $q$ of positive support of
cardinality at most $\max(\aleph_0,|\supp p|)<\kappa$, since $\kappa$ is
uncountable. So $q\in\Pi_{<\kappa}$ and $\rho(p)=\rho(X^\varepsilon-X^\eta)\rho(q)=0$.
\end{proof}

Regularity bounds the leading indices in \cref{ogl:ker:lem:fragment}(ii);
uncountability keeps the countable geometric tail within the support bound. 11
does not extend the lemma to singular cardinals or to finite supports. It is the
nonunital form, for this ring, of the sibling report's coinitiality theorem
\replabel{osq:thm:coinitial} (through \replabel{osq:prop:lex}), and it is
weaker than that report's exact ring threshold \replabel{osq:prop:card11}; the
merge uses the latter in \cref{ogl:ker:prop:exact}.

\begin{theorem}[Cardinal elementary rigidity \src{11}]\label{ogl:ker:thm:fragmentgroup}
Let $n\ge3$. Every homomorphism $P_{n,<\kappa}\to H$ with $|H|<\kappa$ is
trivial, and every homomorphism $E_n(R_{<\kappa})\to H$ with $|H|<\kappa$
factors uniquely through $\ct:E_n(R_{<\kappa})\to\SL_n(D)$. The group
$P_{n,<\kappa}$ is nontrivial, nonabelian, torsion-free, centerless and perfect.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $U_{<\kappa}=\langle e_{ij}(\Pi_{<\kappa})\rangle$. By
\cref{ogl:ker:cor:transfer}(ii) and \cref{ogl:ker:lem:fragmentring} it has no
nontrivial image of cardinality $<\kappa$, and by
\cref{ogl:ker:cor:transfer}(iii) and \cref{ogl:ker:lem:fragment}(iii) it is
perfect. $P_{n,<\kappa}$ is its normal closure, by the split-kernel argument of
\cref{ogl:el:lem:normalgen} (11's Lemma~5.1, for any split ring), and
\cref{ogl:ker:lem:closure} transfers both properties; factorization is as in
\cref{ogl:el:thm:invisible}(iv). (By \cref{ogl:el:prop:normal} and
\cref{ogl:ker:lem:fragment}(iii), in fact $U_{<\kappa}=P_{n,<\kappa}$.) The
monomial $X^{e_0}$ gives $e_{12}(X^{e_0})\ne1_n$ and
$[e_{12}(X^{e_0}),e_{23}(X^{e_0})]=e_{13}(X^{2e_0})\ne1_n$. Torsion-freeness holds
because $P_{n,<\kappa}$ lies in the kernel of \cref{ogl:ker:thm:torsionfree}. The
proof of \cref{ogl:ker:thm:center} applies inside $R_{<\kappa}$, whose root
matrices are present and whose units are $D^\times$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Cardinal Steinberg rigidity \src{11}]\label{ogl:ker:cor:fragmentst}
Let $n\ge3$. Every homomorphism from $\St_n(R_{<\kappa})$ to a group of
cardinality $<\kappa$ factors through $\St_n(D)$. Its constant-term kernel
$P_{n,<\kappa}^{\St}$ is perfect and has no nontrivial image in a group of
cardinality $<\kappa$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Apply \cref{ogl:ker:cor:transfer}(ii),(iii) and \cref{ogl:ker:lem:fragmentring}
to the subgroup generated by the $x_{ij}(\Pi_{<\kappa})$, and take its normal
closure by \cref{ogl:ker:lem:stkernel,ogl:ker:lem:closure}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Non-simple fragments \src{11}]\label{ogl:ker:prop:fragmentfiltration}
Let $n\ge3$, $t_0=X^{e_0}$ and $t_1=X^{e_1}$. The subgroups
$\{g\in P_{n,<\kappa}:g-1_n\in M_n((1+t_1)^mR_{<\kappa})\}$, $m\ge0$, are normal
and strictly decreasing, and $e_{12}(t_0)$ lies in all of them. The group
$P_{n,<\kappa}$ is linear over the set field $\Frac(R_{<\kappa})$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Normality follows from reduction modulo an ideal. The parameter
$(1+t_1)^mX^{e_1/2}$ witnesses strictness, since $X^{e_1/2}/(1+t_1)$ has leading
exponent $-e_1/2<0$. For $m\ge1$,
$t_0/(1+t_1)^m=\sum_{r\ge0}(-1)^r\binom{m+r-1}{r}X^{e_0-(m+r)e_1}\in\Pi_{<\kappa}$:
the exponents are positive in the lexicographic order and the support is
countable. Finally $R_{<\kappa}$ is a set-sized integral domain, and the matrix
inclusion into its fraction field is faithful.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Concrete cardinals, actions and representations}

\begin{proposition}[The countable-support case \src{11}]\label{ogl:ker:prop:continuum}
For $\kappa=\aleph_1$ and $n\ge3$, $|R_{<\aleph_1}|=|P_{n,<\aleph_1}|=\mathfrak c$, and
$P_{n,<\aleph_1}$ has no nontrivial homomorphism to a countable group.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$|\Gamma_{\aleph_1}|=\aleph_1\le\mathfrak c$, and a countable support with a
countable list of coefficients is coded in a set of size
$\mathfrak c^{\aleph_0}=\mathfrak c$. The series $rX^{e_0}$, $r\in\R$, give
$\mathfrak c$ distinct root matrices in $P_{n,<\aleph_1}$, and finite matrices
over a ring of size $\mathfrak c$ are at most $\mathfrak c$ in number. The last
assertion is \cref{ogl:ker:thm:fragmentgroup}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Action thresholds \src{11}]\label{ogl:ker:prop:actioncard}
Let $n\ge3$. Every action of $P_{n,<\kappa}$ on a set $X$ with $|\Sym(X)|<\kappa$
is trivial, in particular for finite $X$ and for infinite $X$ with
$2^{|X|}<\kappa$; every action of $E_n(R_{<\kappa})$ on such a set factors
through $\SL_n(D)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Apply \cref{ogl:ker:thm:fragmentgroup} to the target $\Sym(X)$. For infinite
$|X|=\lambda$, $|\Sym(X)|=2^\lambda$: $\lambda^\lambda\le(2^\lambda)^\lambda=2^\lambda$,
and swapping the members of any subfamily of $\lambda$ disjoint pairs gives
$2^\lambda$ permutations.
\end{proof}

A target of cardinality $<\kappa$ and an action on a set of cardinality
$<\kappa$ are different conditions; the action threshold uses the cardinality of
the symmetric group (11).

\begin{corollary}[Ordinary groups invisible to bounded actions \src{11}]\label{ogl:ker:cor:boundedactions}
For every infinite cardinal $\lambda$ there is an explicit set-sized nontrivial,
torsion-free, centerless, perfect group with no nontrivial action on any set of
cardinality at most $\lambda$: $P_{3,<\kappa}$ with $D=\Z$ and
$\kappa=(2^\lambda)^+$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
A successor cardinal $\mu^+$ is regular: a cofinal family of at most $\mu$
ordinals below $\mu^+$ would have union of size at most $\mu\cdot\mu=\mu$. For
$|X|\le\lambda$, $2^{|X|}\le2^\lambda<\kappa$. Apply
\cref{ogl:ker:thm:fragmentgroup,ogl:ker:prop:actioncard}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Finite-dimensional complex representations \src{11}]\label{ogl:ker:cor:complexreps}
For $\kappa=\mathfrak c^+$ and $n\ge3$, every finite-dimensional complex
representation of $P_{n,<\kappa}$ is trivial, and every such representation of
$E_n(R_{<\kappa})$ factors through $\SL_n(D)$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
$|\GL_m(\C)|=\mathfrak c<\kappa$ for $m\ge1$; the case $m=0$ is trivial.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[No overstatement at $\aleph_1$ \src{11}]\label{ogl:ker:rem:aleph1}
The absence of a countable image in \cref{ogl:ker:prop:continuum} does not by
itself exclude an action on a countably infinite set, since $|\Sym(\N)|=\mathfrak c$,
nor every finite-dimensional complex representation. The larger cardinals in
\cref{ogl:ker:cor:boundedactions,ogl:ker:cor:complexreps} are intentional.
The exact threshold $\mathfrak c$ at $\aleph_1$ obtained in
\cref{ogl:ker:prop:exact} does not change this: $\Sym(\N)$ and $\GL_m(\C)$ have
cardinality exactly $\mathfrak c$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Exact thresholds}

\begin{proposition}[An exact-threshold criterion \src{11}]\label{ogl:ker:prop:exactthreshold}
Let $I$ be a set-sized commutative ring, possibly without unit, of infinite
cardinality $\theta$, such that every nonzero additive multiplicative map from
$I$ to a ring has image of cardinality at least $\theta$. Let $A_0=D_0\oplus I$
be a split unital commutative ring with $I\ne0$ an ideal and $|D_0|\le\theta$,
and let $n\ge3$ and $N=\ker\bigl(E_n(A_0)\to E_n(D_0)\bigr)$. Then
$|N|=\theta$, every homomorphism from $N$ that does not kill a given
nonidentity element has a target of cardinality at least $\theta$, and the
identity of $N$ attains this bound for every nonidentity element. In particular
the least cardinality of a group receiving a nontrivial homomorphic image of $N$
is $\theta$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$N$ is the normal closure of $U=\langle e_{ij}(I)\rangle$ by the split-kernel
argument (\cref{ogl:el:lem:normalgen}; 11's Lemma~5.1). If $f:N\to H$ is
nontrivial, it is nontrivial on some conjugate $gUg^{-1}$, and
$u\mapsto f(gug^{-1})$ is a nontrivial homomorphism $U\to H$. By
\cref{ogl:ker:thm:rootkernel} there is a nonzero ring image $R_\varphi$ of $I$ with
$\theta\le|R_\varphi|\le|H|^{n(n-1)}$; as $\theta$ is infinite, $H$ is infinite and
$|H|\ge\theta$. The root matrices inject $I$ into $N$, and $N\subseteq M_n(A_0)$
has at most $\theta$ elements, so $|N|=\theta$.
\end{proof}

Source 11 notes that the hypothesis is stronger than
\cref{ogl:ker:lem:fragmentring} and does not claim that its fragments have exact
threshold $\kappa$ or $\kappa^{<\kappa}$: any application must verify the ring
hypothesis. The sibling report's field bound verifies it for $R_{<\kappa}$. The
next two results are the merge's.

\begin{proposition}[Exact thresholds for the fragments \mergetag]\label{ogl:ker:prop:exact}
Let $\kappa$ be uncountable and regular and $n\ge3$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Every nonzero additive multiplicative map from $\Pi_{<\kappa}$ to a ring
(not necessarily unital or commutative) has image of cardinality at least
$\kappa^{<\kappa}$, and $|\Pi_{<\kappa}|=|R_{<\kappa}|=\kappa^{<\kappa}$.
\item For every $1\ne g\in P_{n,<\kappa}$ the least cardinality of a group $H$
admitting a homomorphism $P_{n,<\kappa}\to H$ that does not kill $g$ is exactly
$\kappa^{<\kappa}$; the same holds for homomorphisms defined on
$E_n(R_{<\kappa})$. Every homomorphism from $E_n(R_{<\kappa})$ to a group of
cardinality $<\kappa^{<\kappa}$ factors uniquely through $\ct$, and every action
of $P_{n,<\kappa}$ on a finite set, or on an infinite set $X$ with
$2^{|X|}<\kappa^{<\kappa}$, is trivial.
\item The least cardinality of a group detecting a nonidentity element of
$P^{\St}_{n,<\kappa}$, by a homomorphism defined on $P^{\St}_{n,<\kappa}$, is also
exactly $\kappa^{<\kappa}$.
\item For $\kappa=\aleph_1$ the value is $\mathfrak c$: $P_{n,<\aleph_1}$ has no
nontrivial homomorphic image of cardinality below $\mathfrak c$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
(i) Let $\rho:\Pi_{<\kappa}\to S$ be additive and multiplicative with
$\rho(x)\ne0$. By \cref{ogl:ker:lem:fragment}(ii) there is $\gamma=e_\beta>0$
strictly below $\supp x$; put $c=\gamma/2\in\Gamma_\kappa$. Then
$X^{\gamma}\in\Pi_{<\kappa}$, and $y=X^{-\gamma}x$ has positive support of the
same cardinality, so $y\in\Pi_{<\kappa}$; since
$\rho(X^\gamma)\rho(y)=\rho(x)\ne0$, $\rho(X^{2c})\ne0$. Let
$\Gamma_\kappa^{\ll c}=\{h\in\Gamma_\kappa:m|h|<c\text{ for all ordinary }m\ge1\}$
(the sibling report's $H_c(\Gamma)$) and let $\mathbb L_c$ be the set of Hahn
series over $\Gamma_\kappa$ with coefficients in $k$ and fewer than $\kappa$
exponents, all in $\Gamma_\kappa^{\ll c}$. By \replabel{osq:prop:card11} (with
$K=k$) and its proof, $\mathbb L_c$ is a field with at least $\kappa^{<\kappa}$
elements. For $f\in\mathbb L_c$ every exponent $c+h$ of $X^cf$ is positive, since
$|h|<c$, and there are fewer than $\kappa$ of them; so
$X^c\mathbb L_c\subseteq\Pi_{<\kappa}$. For distinct $f,f'\in\mathbb L_c$ the
element $X^c/(f-f')$ lies in $X^c\mathbb L_c\subseteq\Pi_{<\kappa}$, and in
$\Pi_{<\kappa}$
\[
 (X^cf-X^cf')\cdot\frac{X^c}{f-f'}=X^{2c}.
\]
If $\rho(X^cf)=\rho(X^cf')$, additivity and multiplicativity give
$\rho(X^{2c})=0$, a contradiction. So $f\mapsto\rho(X^cf)$ is injective on
$\mathbb L_c$ and $|\rho(\Pi_{<\kappa})|\ge\kappa^{<\kappa}$. This is the
scaled-field collision lemma \replabel{osq:lem:collision}(i), whose proof, as
that report notes, uses only that the source is closed under addition and
multiplication and contains $X^c\mathbb L_c$; it therefore applies to maps
defined on $\Pi_{<\kappa}$ alone. Since $|k|=\mathfrak c\le\max(\kappa,\mathfrak c)$,
\replabel{osq:prop:card11} gives $|R_{<\kappa}|=\kappa^{<\kappa}$; and
$|\Pi_{<\kappa}|\ge\kappa^{<\kappa}$ by the case $\rho=\mathrm{id}$.

(ii) Apply \cref{ogl:ker:prop:exactthreshold} with $I=\Pi_{<\kappa}$,
$\theta=\kappa^{<\kappa}$ and $D_0=D$, which is countable. A homomorphism on
$E_n(R_{<\kappa})$ not killing $g$ restricts to one on $P_{n,<\kappa}$, and the
inclusion $E_n(R_{<\kappa})\subseteq\GL_n(R_{<\kappa})$, a group of cardinality
$\kappa^{<\kappa}$, detects $g$. A homomorphism from $E_n(R_{<\kappa})$ to a
group of cardinality $<\kappa^{<\kappa}$ therefore kills $P_{n,<\kappa}$, and
factors uniquely through $\ct$ by the split decomposition
$E_n(R_{<\kappa})=P_{n,<\kappa}\rtimes\SL_n(D)$ as in
\cref{ogl:el:thm:invisible}(iv). For actions, $|\Sym(X)|$ is finite or
$2^{|X|}$ (proof of \cref{ogl:ker:prop:actioncard}).

(iii) Let $f:P^{\St}_{n,<\kappa}\to H$ not kill $g$. By
\cref{ogl:ker:lem:stkernel}, $P^{\St}_{n,<\kappa}$ is the normal closure of the
subgroup $V$ generated by the $x_{ij}(\Pi_{<\kappa})$, so $f$ is nontrivial on a
conjugate of $V$, and composing with conjugation gives a nontrivial homomorphism
$V\to H$. By \eqref{ogl:ker:eq:st1}, \eqref{ogl:ker:eq:st3} and
\cref{ogl:ker:thm:rootkernel}, $\Pi_{<\kappa}$ has a nonzero ring image of
cardinality at most $|H|^{n(n-1)}$, so $|H|\ge\kappa^{<\kappa}$ by (i). Conversely
$\St_n(R_{<\kappa})$ is a quotient of the free group on $n(n-1)|R_{<\kappa}|=
\kappa^{<\kappa}$ symbols, so its kernel has at most $\kappa^{<\kappa}$ elements,
and its identity map detects $g$.

(iv) $\aleph_1^{<\aleph_1}=\aleph_1^{\aleph_0}$, and
$2^{\aleph_0}\le\aleph_1^{\aleph_0}\le(2^{\aleph_0})^{\aleph_0}=2^{\aleph_0}$.
\end{proof}

When $\kappa^{<\kappa}>\kappa$, for instance for $\kappa=\aleph_1$ when the
continuum hypothesis fails, the exact value is strictly larger than the bound
$\kappa$ of \cref{ogl:ker:thm:fragmentgroup}. The value agrees with the size
$\mathfrak c$ of \cref{ogl:ker:prop:continuum}. 11's non-claim about exact
thresholds remains a true description of 11; the value is obtained here by
combining 11's criterion with \replabel{osq:prop:card11} and the scaled-field
collision, which 11 did not use.

The same argument applies to the other set-sized rings of the sibling report.

\begin{corollary}[Exact thresholds in the sibling report's set-sized models \mergetag]\label{ogl:ker:cor:fivemodels}
Let $A_0$ be one of the five set-sized rings of \replabel{osq:thm:thresholds}, or
one of the Gaussian versions named there, with constant ring $D_0$, and let
$n\ge3$. For every nonidentity $g$ in $\ker\bigl(\ct:E_n(A_0)\to E_n(D_0)\bigr)$
the least cardinality of a group detecting $g$, by a homomorphism defined on this
kernel or on $E_n(A_0)$, is exactly $|A_0|$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
In the notation of the sibling report's \replabel{osq:sec:fixed},
$A_0=D_0+\Pi(\mathbb F)$ for a subfield $\mathbb F$ of a Hahn field over an
ordered set group $\Gamma$ containing the coefficients and every monomial, with
$\Pi(\mathbb F)$ the elements of positive support (the report writes $F$; here
$F$ is the ambient field $\No$ or $\SC$). The proof of
\replabel{osq:thm:thresholds} establishes, for each of these rings, that
$\Gamma$ is divisible, that every nonzero $x\in\Pi(\mathbb F)$ has a positive
strict lower bound $\gamma\in\Gamma$ of its support, and that for every $c>0$ the
field $L_c(\mathbb F)$ of members of $\mathbb F$ supported in
$\Gamma^{\ll c}=\{h\in\Gamma:m|h|<c\text{ for all ordinary }m\ge1\}$ has exactly $|A_0|$ elements, while $X^cL_c(\mathbb F)\subseteq\Pi(\mathbb F)$
(\replabel{osq:prop:fieldbound}(i)). With $c=\gamma/2$,
$X^\gamma\in\Pi(\mathbb F)$ and $X^{-\gamma}x\in\Pi(\mathbb F)$, the argument of \cref{ogl:ker:prop:exact}(i) shows
that every nonzero additive multiplicative map from $\Pi(\mathbb F)$ to a ring
has image of cardinality at least $|A_0|$. Moreover
$|\Pi(\mathbb F)|=|A_0|$, which is infinite, since
$X^cL_c(\mathbb F)\subseteq\Pi(\mathbb F)$, and $|D_0|\le|A_0|$. Apply
\cref{ogl:ker:prop:exactthreshold} with $\theta=|A_0|$, and use the inclusion into
$\GL_n(A_0)$ for homomorphisms defined on $E_n(A_0)$.
\end{proof}

Together, \cref{ogl:ker:prop:exact,ogl:ker:cor:fivemodels} answer
\cref{ogl:q:thresholds} for $n\ge3$ in these rings: the least detecting target
cardinal of every nonidentity relative elementary matrix is the cardinality of
the ring, as for ring and module targets in \replabel{osq:thm:thresholds}. Other
exponent groups and support restrictions remain open.
\part{Shortest vectors and missing infima}\label{ogl:lat:part}

This part is source 10's. An ordinary positive-definite quadratic form on $\Z^n$
has a shortest nonzero vector: a positive lower bound by the Euclidean norm
combines with the finiteness of bounded subsets of $\Z^n$. Over $\Oz$ neither
positive definiteness nor uniform separation settles the question. The mechanism
is visible in one example: for infinitesimal $\varepsilon>0$ let
\begin{equation}\label{ogl:lat:eq:introexample}
 \cE_\varepsilon(x,y)=(x-\sqrt2\,y)^2+\varepsilon(x^2+y^2),\qquad(x,y)\in\Oz^2 .
\end{equation}
No nonzero ordinary integer vector lies on the kernel $x=\sqrt2\,y$ of the
dominant form, but for every $\gamma>0$ the vector
$\omega^\gamma(\sqrt2,1)/\sqrt3$ has omnific coordinates, lies on that line and
has energy $\varepsilon\omega^{2\gamma}$; making $\gamma$ smaller never produces
a finite vector. This creates a missing infimum. Unimodular vectors behave
differently: $\ct$ sends a unimodular omnific vector to a nonzero unimodular
ordinary vector, an admissible competitor, whereas the constant term of a
nonzero vector may be zero.

Throughout, $(k,D,F,\A)$ is $(\R,\Z,\No,\Oz)$ or $(\C,\Z[i],\SC,\GOz)$;
conjugation is trivial in the real row and fixes $\No$ in the complex row, and
$\norm{x}^2=\sum_\ell\overline{x_\ell}x_\ell$. \emph{Terminology.} A vector is
\emph{unimodular} if its coordinates generate the unit ideal. Source 10 calls
such vectors ``primitive''; the omnific Diophantine report uses
\emph{primitive} for the weaker ``no common nonunit divisor''
(\replabel{odg:thm:realray}), so the word is not used for 10's notion here. The
energy is written $\cE$, since $E_n$ is the elementary group.

\section{Positive forms contract under constant-term descent}\label{ogl:lat:sec:descent}

For an ordinary symmetric or Hermitian positive-semidefinite matrix $Q$ write
$q(x)=x^*Qx$, on $k^n$ and on $F^n$. The ordinary spectral theorem gives an
ordinary $B$ with $Q=B^*B$, so $q(x)=\norm{Bx}^2$; nonnegativity and the kernel
persist after scalar extension to $F$.

\begin{theorem}[Constant-term contraction \src{10}]\label{ogl:lat:thm:contraction}
For $x\in\A^n$ and ordinary $a\in k^n$,
$q(x-a)\ge q(\ct(x)-a)$, with equality exactly when $x-\ct(x)$ lies in the
scalar-extended kernel of $Q$; otherwise $q(x-a)$ is positive infinite while
$q(\ct(x)-a)$ is an ordinary real.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Write $x=z+j$ with $z\in D^n$, $j\in\Pi_k^n$ (\cref{ogl:lem:units}). Then
$B(x-a)=B(z-a)+Bj$ with $B(z-a)$ ordinary and $Bj$ purely infinite. If $Bj\ne0$
one coordinate is infinite (\cref{ogl:lem:units}(iii)), its squared modulus is
positive infinite and the others are nonnegative. Finally $Bj=0$ iff $Qj=0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Strict descent for a positive family \src{10}]\label{ogl:lat:cor:strictdescent}
Let $Q_0,\dots,Q_m$ be ordinary positive-semidefinite with common kernel $0$ and
$\lambda_0,\dots,\lambda_m$ positive surreals. For
$\cE(x)=\sum_i\lambda_ix^*Q_ix$ and $a\in k^n$, $\cE(x-a)\ge\cE(\ct(x)-a)$ with
equality exactly when $x\in D^n$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Each difference in \cref{ogl:lat:thm:contraction} is nonnegative, and for
$j=x-\ct(x)\ne0$ at least one is positive.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[What the argument does not assert \src{10}]\label{ogl:lat:rem:notorder}
$\ct$ is not order preserving on $\Bk$, the contraction is not a Pythagorean
decomposition (mixed terms may be nonzero), and the weights $\lambda_i$ need not
lie in $\Bk$, so applying $\ct$ to the energy itself is not legitimate. The
argument works because an ordinary sum-of-squares map either annihilates the
whole positive-exponent part or makes the norm infinite. For closest points the
contraction always preserves admissibility; for shortest \emph{nonzero} vectors
it need not, since $x\ne0$ does not imply $\ct(x)\ne0$; for unimodular vectors
it does (\cref{ogl:lat:prop:unidescent}).
\end{remark}

For a definite $Q$ the contraction gives a Tarski-free proof that omnific points
on a finite level of an ordinary definite form are ordinary, the conclusion of
\replabel{odg:cor:definite} in the omnific Diophantine report.

\section{Separated positive pencils}\label{ogl:lat:sec:pencil}

\begin{definition}[Separated positive pencil \src{10}]\label{ogl:lat:def:pencil}
Let $n\ge1$, $m\ge0$. A separated positive pencil consists of ordinary
positive-semidefinite matrices $Q_0,\dots,Q_m$ and positive surreals
$\varepsilon_0,\dots,\varepsilon_m$ with $\varepsilon_{j+1}\ll\varepsilon_j$.
Put $q_j(x)=x^*Q_jx$,
\[
 K_j=\bigcap_{i<j}\ker_kQ_i,\qquad L_j=K_j\cap D^n,\qquad K_0=k^n,
\]
and require $K_m\ne0$ and $K_{m+1}=0$. The energy is
$\cE(x)=\sum_{j=0}^m\varepsilon_jq_j(x)$ for $x\in F^n$. For an ordinary
subspace $K$, $K_F$ is its scalar extension. Repeated kernels and zero forms are
allowed.
\end{definition}

The condition $K_m\ne0=K_{m+1}$ says that the last index is the first at which
the cumulative kernel vanishes; no finer forms are appended after it, and this
convention is part of the scope of the exact minimum formula below. For $m=0$ it
says $Q_0$ is positive definite (10).

\begin{lemma}[Coercivity at the last scale \src{10}]\label{ogl:lat:lem:coercive}
There are ordinary $c,C>0$ with
$c\varepsilon_m\norm{x}^2\le\cE(x)\le C\varepsilon_0\norm{x}^2$ on $F^n$, and $q_m$
is positive definite on $K_m$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
$S=\sum_jQ_j$ is positive definite; choose $cI\le S\le CI$ over $k$, which
persists over $F$ by sum-of-squares factorization, and use
$\varepsilon_m\le\varepsilon_j\le\varepsilon_0$. A vector of $K_m$ killed by
$q_m$ lies in $K_{m+1}=0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Exact lexicographic comparison \src{10}]\label{ogl:lat:lem:lex}
For ordinary $a,z,z'\in k^n$, the sign of $\cE(z-a)-\cE(z'-a)$ is the sign of
the first nonzero entry of $\bigl(q_j(z-a)-q_j(z'-a)\bigr)_{j=0}^m$, and the
energies are equal if all entries vanish.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
If the first nonzero entry is $d_j$, divide by $\varepsilon_j$: the result is
$d_j$ plus the infinitesimal $\sum_{i>j}(\varepsilon_i/\varepsilon_j)d_i$.
\end{proof}

This is exact for fixed ordinary vectors, not uniform in all ordinary integer
coordinates; an infinitesimal weight times any one ordinary real is
infinitesimal, which is central to the Pell example (10).

\section{The unrestricted shortest-vector dichotomy}\label{ogl:lat:sec:shortest}

For a nonempty class $S\subseteq\No^{>0}$ let
$\LB(S)=\{b\in\No_{\ge0}:b\le s\text{ for all }s\in S\}$. A subclass $T\subseteq S$
is coinitial if every $s\in S$ has some $t\in T$ with $t\le s$. A surreal
infimum is a greatest lower bound in $\No$, not an adjoined gap. Let
$\mathscr V=\{\cE(x):x\in\A^n\setminus\{0\}\}$.

\begin{theorem}[Minimum or exact missing-infimum cut \src{10}]\label{ogl:lat:thm:main}
For a separated positive pencil exactly one of the following holds.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item If $L_m\ne0$, then $\mu=\min\{q_m(z):z\in L_m\setminus\{0\}\}$ is an
attained positive ordinary real, $\min\mathscr V=\varepsilon_m\mu$, and the
minimizers are the finitely many $z\in L_m\setminus\{0\}$ with $q_m(z)=\mu$.
\item If $L_m=0$, then $\cE(x)/\varepsilon_m$ is positive infinite for every
nonzero $x\in\A^n$, and $\LB(\mathscr V)=\varepsilon_m(\Ofin)_{\ge0}$. In
particular $\mathscr V$ has neither a minimum nor a surreal infimum.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
If $x\notin D^n$, $\cE(x)/\varepsilon_m\ge c\norm{x}^2$ is positive infinite
(\cref{ogl:lem:units,ogl:lat:lem:coercive}). If $x\in D^n\setminus K_m$, some
$q_i(x)>0$ with $i<m$ is an ordinary positive real and
$\cE(x)/\varepsilon_m\ge(\varepsilon_i/\varepsilon_m)q_i(x)$ is infinite. If
$x\in L_m$, $\cE(x)=\varepsilon_mq_m(x)$.

(a) $q_m$ is positive definite on $K_m$, so the ordinary lattice vectors with
$q_m(z)\le q_m(z_0)$, for one $z_0\in L_m\setminus\{0\}$, have bounded norm and
are finitely many; their least positive value is $\mu$. The classification gives
the rest.

(b) The classification makes every nonnegative finite multiple of
$\varepsilon_m$ a lower bound. Conversely let $b\ge0$ with $h=b/\varepsilon_m$
infinite; choose ordinary $w\in K_m$ with $q_m(w)=1$ and $\gamma>0$ with
$2\gamma<\ddeg h$. Then $x=\omega^\gamma w\in\Pi_k^n\setminus\{0\}$, the earlier
forms vanish on it, and $\cE(x)=\varepsilon_m\omega^{2\gamma}\ll\varepsilon_mh=b$,
so $b$ is not a lower bound. The class $\varepsilon_m(\Ofin)_{\ge0}$ has no
greatest member ($b+\varepsilon_m$ is larger), and a greatest lower bound would
be nonnegative, so no infimum exists.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Improvement of every set-sized family \src{10}]\label{ogl:lat:thm:simultaneous}
Assume $L_m=0$. For every set-indexed family
$(x_s)_{s\in S}\subseteq\A^n\setminus\{0\}$ there are an ordinary $w\in K_m$ with
$q_m(w)=1$ and $\gamma>0$ such that $x=\omega^\gamma w$ satisfies
$\cE(x)\ll\cE(x_s)$ for every $s$. Consequently $\mathscr V$ has no set-sized
coinitial subclass.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Each $h_s=\cE(x_s)/\varepsilon_m$ is positive infinite; \cref{ogl:lem:gap}(iii)
gives $\gamma>0$ with $\omega^{2\gamma}\ll h_s$ for all $s$. The construction
applies directly to a set of energy values, through their leading exponents,
without choosing vector representatives.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[No ordinary-factor approximation \src{10}]\label{ogl:lat:cor:noapprox}
If $L_m=0$, for every nonzero $y\in\A^n$ there is a nonzero $x\in\A^n$ with
$\cE(x)<c\,\cE(y)$ for every ordinary $c>0$.
\end{corollary}

The witness is a single monomial vector; no sum or limit is taken, and replacing
the missing infimum by the limit of a chosen ordinary sequence would be
incorrect. In the full surreal field no countable list of improving vectors is
coinitial in $\mathscr V$ (10). The monomial witness $\omega^\gamma w$ is the
mechanism of the homogeneous cone criterion and the zero Pell fiber of the
omnific Diophantine report (\replabel{odg:thm:cone},
\replabel{odg:prop:zeropell}).

\section{Recovering the kernel flag from low energies}\label{ogl:lat:sec:lowflag}

For $0\le j\le m$ let $\mathrm{Low}_j=\{x\in\A^n:\cE(x)\ll\varepsilon_j\}$, with
$0\in\mathrm{Low}_j$. (Source 10 writes $V_j$; the letter is left to the radical
flag of the Hahn--Tate report.)

\begin{theorem}[Field span and integral trace of small energies \src{10}]\label{ogl:lat:thm:lowflag}
For every $j$: $\mathrm{Low}_j\subseteq(K_{j+1})_F$,
$\Span_F\mathrm{Low}_j=(K_{j+1})_F$, and $\ct(\mathrm{Low}_j)=L_{j+1}$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For $x\in\mathrm{Low}_j$ and $i\le j$,
$0\le q_i(x)\le\cE(x)/\varepsilon_j\in\mm$; by the sum-of-squares argument of
\cref{ogl:lat:thm:contraction}, $q_i(x)$ is an ordinary nonnegative real or
positive infinite, so it is $0$. This gives the inclusion, and applying ordinary
matrices and $\ct$ gives $\ct(x)\in L_{j+1}$. Every $z\in L_{j+1}$ has
$\cE(z)/\varepsilon_j=\sum_{i>j}(\varepsilon_i/\varepsilon_j)q_i(z)\in\mm$. For
the span, take an ordinary basis $w_\ell$ of $K_{j+1}$; each
$h_\ell=\cE(w_\ell)/\varepsilon_j$ is positive infinitesimal, and for
$0<2\gamma<-\ddeg h_\ell$ (all $\ell$) the vectors $\omega^\gamma w_\ell$ lie in
$\mathrm{Low}_j$ and span $(K_{j+1})_F$.
\end{proof}

An irrational kernel can have a large field span of small-energy vectors and a
small integral trace; in the binary example the trace is $0$ (10).

\section{Unimodular vectors}\label{ogl:lat:sec:unimodular}

Let $\Um(\A^n)$ be the unimodular vectors: those $x$ with $\sum_\ell a_\ell x_\ell=1$
for some $a_\ell\in\A$.

\begin{proposition}[Unimodular descent \src{10}]\label{ogl:lat:prop:unidescent}
If $x\in\Um(\A^n)$ then $\ct(x)\in\Um(D^n)$, and $\Um(D^n)\subseteq\Um(\A^n)$.
Hence $\cE(x)\ge\cE(\ct(x))$ on $\Um(\A^n)$, strictly unless $x$ is ordinary; the
energies of ordinary unimodular vectors form a countable coinitial subset of the
unimodular energies; and every unimodular minimizer, if any, is ordinary.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Apply the ring homomorphism $\ct$ to a relation $\sum a_\ell x_\ell=1$; a relation
over $D$ is one over $\A$. Then use \cref{ogl:lat:cor:strictdescent} at target
$0$; $D^n$ is countable.
\end{proof}

The converse ``$\ct(x)$ unimodular implies $x$ unimodular'' for arbitrary
$x\in\A^n$ is neither needed nor asserted (10). Descent of unimodularity under
$\ct$ is the mechanism of the omnific Diophantine report's unimodular Fermat
theorem and rational-direction theorem
(\replabel{odg:thm:unimodularfermat}, \replabel{odg:thm:realray}).

\begin{corollary}[Countable coinitiality \src{10}]\label{ogl:lat:cor:unicountable}
If the unimodular energy has no minimum, its coinitiality is exactly $\aleph_0$.
In particular \cref{ogl:lat:thm:simultaneous} fails if every vector in it is
required to be unimodular.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
\Cref{ogl:lat:prop:unidescent} gives a countable coinitial set; a finite one
would contain a minimum.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Discreteness of a projected lattice]\label{ogl:lat:lem:projection}
Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional real vector space, $\Lambda$ a full lattice in
$V$ and $T:V\to W$ real linear. Then $T(\Lambda)$ is discrete iff
$\ker T=\Span_\R(\ker T\cap\Lambda)$. If $T(\Lambda)$ is not discrete, it has
nonzero points arbitrarily close to $0$, it is not closed, and there is
$h\in\overline{T(\Lambda)}\setminus T(\Lambda)$. The same holds for
complex-linear maps and Gaussian lattices, through the underlying real spaces.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The equivalence is \replabel{tate:theta:lem:latticeprojection} of the Hahn--Tate
report (for $\Lambda=\Z^d$, which is the general case after choosing a basis);
source 10 proves it independently by the same saturation and rank count. The
remaining assertions are 10's. A nondiscrete subgroup contains nonzero
$g_j\to0$; passing to a subsequence with $g_j/\norm{g_j}\to u$, for real $t$ let
$a_j$ be an integer nearest to $t/\norm{g_j}$; then $a_jg_j\to tu$, so a closed
such subgroup would contain $\R u$, contradicting countability. For the complex
case, a complex subspace is spanned over $\C$ by its Gaussian integral vectors
iff it is spanned over $\R$ by them in real coordinates, because the Gaussian
lattice and its intersection with a complex subspace are closed under
multiplication by $i$.
\end{proof}

Let $r$ be the largest index in $\{0,\dots,m\}$ with $L_r\ne0$ (it exists since
$L_0=D^n$), $\Lambda=L_r$, $W=\Span_k\Lambda$ and $W'=W\cap K_{r+1}$ (10's $H$).
By maximality $K_{r+1}\cap D^n=0$, and $\Lambda$ is a full lattice in $W$ (in real
coordinates in the complex case).

\begin{theorem}[Exact unimodular attainment criterion \src{10}]\label{ogl:lat:thm:unimodular}
For a separated positive pencil the following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\cE$ has a minimum on $\Um(\A^n)$;
\item $\cE$ has a minimum on $D^n\setminus\{0\}$;
\item $q_r$ is positive definite on $W$;
\item $W'=0$.
\end{enumerate}
When they hold, the minimizers in (i) and (ii) are the same finite set of
ordinary unimodular vectors, obtained by minimizing $q_r$ on $\Lambda\setminus\{0\}$
and breaking ties lexicographically by $q_{r+1},\dots,q_m$. When they fail, the
unimodular energies have coinitiality $\aleph_0$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The kernel of $q_r$ on $W\subseteq K_r$ is $W'$, so (iii)$\Leftrightarrow$(iv).
A nonzero ordinary vector outside $K_r$ has a positive earlier coefficient and
is worse than every fixed nonzero vector of $\Lambda$ (\cref{ogl:lat:lem:lex}), so
ordinary minimization may be restricted to $\Lambda\setminus\{0\}$.

If $W'=0$, $q_r$ has a positive minimum on $\Lambda\setminus\{0\}$ attained at
finitely many vectors, and lexicographic tie-breaking leaves a finite nonempty
set of minimizers on $D^n\setminus\{0\}$. Each is unimodular: $D$ is Euclidean,
and dividing a non-unimodular ordinary vector by a nonunit gcd $g$ gives a
nonzero ordinary vector of energy $\cE(z)/|g|^2<\cE(z)$. So (ii) holds, and
\cref{ogl:lat:prop:unidescent} gives (i) with no extra nonordinary minimizers.

If $W'\ne0$, factor $q_r|_W=\norm{T\cdot}^2$ with an ordinary $T$ of kernel $W'$.
Since $W'\cap\Lambda=0$, \cref{ogl:lat:lem:projection} makes $T(\Lambda)$
nondiscrete, while $q_r(z)>0$ on $\Lambda\setminus\{0\}$ because $L_{r+1}=0$; so
$q_r$ has infimum $0$ and no minimum there. Dividing by the coordinate gcd keeps
vectors in $\Lambda$, makes them unimodular and does not increase $q_r$, so
arbitrarily small positive $q_r$ values are attained by unimodular vectors, and
any ordinary candidate in $\Lambda$ is beaten (\cref{ogl:lat:lem:lex}); candidates
outside $K_r$ were already worse. So there is no ordinary nonzero or unimodular
minimum, and \cref{ogl:lat:prop:unidescent} excludes a nonordinary one. The
coinitiality is \cref{ogl:lat:cor:unicountable}.
\end{proof}

The criterion uses the span of the actual integral intersection, not the whole
deepest kernel, and can hold when \cref{ogl:lat:thm:main} gives unrestricted
nonattainment (\cref{ogl:lat:ex:unimodularonly}).

\section{Closest points}\label{ogl:lat:sec:closest}

\subsection{What is already in the Hahn--Tate report}\label{ogl:lat:sub:tate}

Part~II of the Hahn--Tate uniformization report
(\fn{docs/surcomplex/hahn-tate-uniformization}, \replabel{tate:part:theta}),
present in the collection at 10's pin, studies lexicographically valued
quadratic lattice energies $\cE_j(n)=\frac12\cB_j(n,n)+b_j(n)$ with
Amini--Nicolussi higher-rank forms \cite{AN}. It already contains the ordinary
backbone of this section:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item the lattice-projection lemma \replabel{tate:theta:lem:latticeprojection}
(\cref{ogl:lat:lem:projection}) and the one-level quadratic criterion
\replabel{tate:theta:lem:onelevel};
\item the exact flag criterion \replabel{tate:theta:thm:flag} and its corollary
\replabel{tate:theta:cor:domain}: for an AN-admissible form, the compatible
linear terms are exactly those giving an energy with a global lattice minimum;
\item the parity bound: at most $2^g$ minimizers, attained
(\replabel{tate:theta:thm:minimizers}, with the example $\sum_jn_j(n_j-1)$);
\item the Pell example \replabel{tate:theta:ex:irrational}: the form
$\bigl((m-\sqrt2n)^2,\,m^2+n^2\bigr)$ and the Pell vectors
$p_k+q_k\sqrt2=(1+\sqrt2)^k$, along which the lexicographic energies strictly
decrease.
\end{itemize}
Source 10 cites the Lean module that formalizes part of that report
(\fn{Surreal/Algebra/LatticeEnergyCertificate.lean}) but not the report itself;
its repository search for ``shortest vector'' found no hit, which is literally
true and misleading (Appendix~\ref{ogl:app:stale}). These results are cited here, not
reprinted; 10's own proofs are kept only where they prove more (the
non-closedness in \cref{ogl:lat:lem:projection}, the converse in
\cref{ogl:lat:thm:closestflag}, the Gaussian bounds, arbitrary surreal targets)
or where the omnific statement needs them.

The dictionary is as follows. For ordinary PSD forms $q_j$ on all of $k^n$ and an
ordinary center $a$, the profile $z\mapsto(q_j(z-a))_{j=0}^m$ on $D^n=\Z^n$ (real
case) is, up to additive constants, a lexicographic energy with $r=m+1$
coordinates, $\cB_{j+1}(z,z')=2z^{\mathsf T}Q_jz'$ and
$b_{j+1}(z)=-2a^{\mathsf T}Q_jz$ (the Hahn--Tate forms are indexed from $1$). Its
radical flag \replabel{tate:theta:eq:flag} is $V_j=K_j$, ending with
$V_{m+1}=K_{m+1}=0$; the global radical condition
\replabel{tate:theta:eq:globalradical} holds automatically because
$K_{j+1}\subseteq\ker Q_j$; and the compatibility
$b_{j+1}(V_{j+1})=-2a^{\mathsf T}Q_jK_{j+1}=0$ holds for every center for the same
reason. AN admissibility then amounts to rationality of every $K_j$. The Gaussian case is
the real case for the rank-$2n$ lattice $\Z[i]^n\cong\Z^{2n}$ (a Hermitian PSD form
is a real PSD form on the underlying space, with the same kernel).

\subsection{Reduction to an ordinary lexicographic problem}

For $a\in F^n$ a closest point is a minimizer of $\cE(x-a)$ on $\A^n$, with no
nonzero or unimodular restriction.

\begin{proposition}[Exact reduction for an ordinary target \src{10}]\label{ogl:lat:prop:closest}
Every closest point to $a\in k^n$, if any, is ordinary, and it is exactly a
lexicographic minimizer over $z\in D^n$ of $\bigl(q_0(z-a),\dots,q_m(z-a)\bigr)$.
Equivalently, with $S_{-1}=D^n$ and $S_j=\argmin_{z\in S_{j-1}}q_j(z-a)$, a
closest point exists iff every stage is nonempty, and then $S_m$ is the finite
set of closest points.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Strict descent (\cref{ogl:lat:cor:strictdescent}) gives ordinarity and
\cref{ogl:lat:lem:lex} the lexicographic description. On $S_m$ all $q_j(z-a)$
are fixed, their sum is an ordinary positive-definite form, so $\norm{z-a}$ is
bounded and $S_m$ finite.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Rational flags}

Call an ordinary subspace $K\subseteq k^n$ \emph{$D$-rational} if
$K=\Span_k(K\cap D^n)$: ordinary rationality for $k=\R$, Gaussian rationality
for $k=\C$; no rationality of a displayed basis or matrix is required.

\begin{theorem}[Closest points for every ordinary center \src{10}; sufficiency also in the Hahn--Tate report]\label{ogl:lat:thm:closestflag}
For a separated positive pencil, every $a\in k^n$ has a closest point in $\A^n$
iff every prefix kernel $K_j$ is $D$-rational. If so, after stage $j$ the staged
sets of \cref{ogl:lat:prop:closest} are finite unions of cosets of $L_{j+1}$. If
not, an ordinary center can be chosen for which one stage has an unattained
ordinary infimum $0$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Sufficiency.} By \cref{ogl:lat:prop:closest} it is the existence of a
lexicographic minimum of the profile on $D^n$, which is
\replabel{tate:theta:cor:domain} with \replabel{tate:theta:thm:flag} under the
dictionary of \cref{ogl:lat:sub:tate}. The coset description is 10's induction,
which is also the inductive step of the proof of \replabel{tate:theta:thm:flag}:
on a coset $v+L_j$ of $S_{j-1}$, the stage-$j$ function
$\ell\mapsto\norm{B_j(v-a)+B_j\ell}^2$ has kernel $K_{j+1}$ on $K_j$, which is
rational, so $B_jL_j$ is a lattice (\cref{ogl:lat:lem:projection}) and the
minimum is attained at finitely many image points, each with preimage one coset
of $L_{j+1}$ (\replabel{tate:theta:lem:onelevel}); retaining the smallest value
over the finitely many cosets proves the induction, and $L_{m+1}=0$ makes the
final set finite.

\emph{Necessity} \src{10}. Let $K_{j+1}$ be the first irrational kernel, so $K_j$
is rational and $L_j$ is a full lattice in $K_j$. With $q_j=\norm{B_j\cdot}^2$,
$G=B_jL_j$ is not discrete (\cref{ogl:lat:lem:projection}); choose
$h\in\overline G\setminus G$. Since $B_jK_j$ is closed and contains $\overline G$,
there is ordinary $a\in K_j$ with $B_ja=h$. For $z\in L_j$ the earlier
$q_i(z-a)$ vanish and $q_j(z-a)=\norm{B_jz-h}^2>0$ has infimum $0$ and no
minimum, while ordinary $z\notin K_j$ are worse than every fixed candidate in
$L_j$. So no ordinary lexicographic minimizer exists, and strict descent
excludes nonordinary closest points.
\end{proof}

The converse, an explicit bad center for an irrational flag, is not in the
Hahn--Tate report, which proves nonsummability for non-admissible forms rather
than the absence of a minimum for some center. Both 10 and that report credit the
rational-filtration mechanism to higher-rank Voronoi theory \cite{AN}.

\begin{corollary}[Nearest-point multiplicity \src{10}]\label{ogl:lat:cor:parity}
For an ordinary target with closest points, their number is at most $2^n$ in
the real case and $4^n$ in the Gaussian case, and both bounds are attained.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The real bound is the midpoint argument of \replabel{tate:theta:thm:minimizers}:
distinct minimizers $z,z'$ congruent modulo $2D^n$ would have midpoint in $D^n$
and $\cE\bigl(\frac{z+z'}2-a\bigr)=\frac{\cE(z-a)+\cE(z'-a)}2-\frac{\cE(z-z')}4$
smaller. The Gaussian bound is the same bound for the rank-$2n$ lattice
$\Z[i]^n$. For the Euclidean form with one weight, the target
$(1/2,\dots,1/2)$ has $2^n$ closest integer points and the target with every
coordinate $(1+i)/2$ has $4^n$ closest Gaussian points (10).
\end{proof}

The parity argument is a standard finite-lattice argument; source 10 includes
it as a consequence, not as a priority claim.

\subsection{Arbitrary surreal targets}

Taking the standard part of a target and using its ordinary closest points is not
enough: infinitesimal displacements can decide between branches before the next
energy scale (10). For $a\in F^n$ split supports coordinatewise:
\begin{equation}\label{ogl:lat:eq:targetsplit}
 a=a^++a^0+a^-,\qquad a^+\in\Pi_k^n,\quad a^0\in k^n,\quad a^-\in\mm_k^n,
\end{equation}
so $b=a^0+a^-$ is finite with standard part $a^0$. This is a support
decomposition, not a claim that $\ct$ is a homomorphism on $F$.

\begin{lemma}[Matching the target's infinite part \src{10}]\label{ogl:lat:lem:finitetarget}
For $a\in F^n$, $x\in\A^n$, $y=x-a^+$ and $z=\ct(y)\in D^n$,
$\cE(x-a)\ge\cE(a^++z-a)=\cE(z-b)$, strictly unless $x=a^++z$. Every closest
point to $a$ has purely infinite part exactly $a^+$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
$y\in\A^n$; write $y=z+j$ and $q_i=\norm{B_i\cdot}^2$. $B_i(z-b)$ is finite and
$B_ij$ purely infinite; if $B_ij\ne0$, $\norm{B_i(y-b)}^2$ is infinite and exceeds
$\norm{B_i(z-b)}^2$. All weighted differences are nonnegative, and one is
positive when $j\ne0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Finite candidates for every target \src{10}]\label{ogl:lat:thm:alltargets}
Assume every prefix kernel is $D$-rational. For every $a^0\in k^n$ there is a
finite nonempty $C(a^0)\subseteq D^n$, depending only on $a^0$ and
$q_0,\dots,q_m$, such that for every $a\in F^n$ with decomposition
\eqref{ogl:lat:eq:targetsplit} the closest points of $a$ are exactly
\[
 a^++\argmin_{z\in C(a^0)}\cE(z-a^0-a^-).
\]
$|C(a^0)|\le2^n$ (real) or $4^n$ (Gaussian), and $C(a^0)$ does not depend on
$a^-$ or on the separated weights. Consequently closest points exist for every
$a\in F^n$ iff they exist for every $a\in k^n$ iff every prefix kernel is
$D$-rational.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Build a finite tree of cosets: the root is $D^n$; at level $j$, for each retained
coset $v+L_j$, minimize $q_j(z-a^0)$ on it and retain \emph{all} minimizing
cosets of $L_{j+1}$ as children (finitely many and at least one, by the proof of
\cref{ogl:lat:thm:closestflag}). Unlike the staged sets, no parent is discarded
because another has a smaller value. At level $m+1$ the cosets are singletons;
$C(a^0)$ is the set of leaves.

Every ordinary $z$ is dominated by a leaf for the target $b=a^0+a^-$: if $z$ is
in a retained parent $v+L_j$ but not in a minimizing child, pick $z'$ in one; for
$i<j$, $q_i(z-b)=q_i(z'-b)$ exactly because $z-z'\in L_j$; the difference
$q_j(z-b)-q_j(z'-b)$ has positive ordinary standard part
$q_j(z-a^0)-q_j(z'-a^0)$; later differences are finite, so after division by
$\varepsilon_j$ their weighted sum is infinitesimal and $\cE(z-b)>\cE(z'-b)$.
Repeating reaches a leaf, strictly better unless $z$ was a leaf.
\Cref{ogl:lat:lem:finitetarget} handles nonordinary candidates.

For the bound let $d_j=\dim_kK_j-\dim_kK_{j+1}$. The children of a parent are
closest points in the projected lattice $B_jL_j$ of real rank $d_j$ or $2d_j$;
two congruent modulo twice that lattice would have a better midpoint, as in
\cref{ogl:lat:cor:parity}; so each parent has at most $2^{d_j}$ or $4^{d_j}$
children, and $\sum_jd_j=n$. The equivalences follow from
\cref{ogl:lat:thm:closestflag}.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[The ordinary minimizer set is too small \src{10}]\label{ogl:lat:ex:targetperturb}
Let $0<\varepsilon\in\mm$, $\eta=\sqrt\varepsilon$, and
$\cE(x,y)=x^2+\varepsilon(y-x/2)^2$; the flag is rational. The ordinary target
$a^0=(1/2,1/4)$ has the unique closest point $(0,0)$, but the tree retains
$C(a^0)=\{(0,0),(1,0),(1,1)\}$. For $a=(1/2+\eta,1/4)$,
$\cE((1,0)-a)-\cE((0,0)-a)=-2\eta+\varepsilon(1/4-\eta/2)<0$ and
$\cE((1,1)-a)-\cE((1,0)-a)=\varepsilon\eta>0$, so the unique closest point is
$(1,0)$: a branch losing the ordinary comparison must be kept.
\end{example}

This is a finite mathematical reduction, not an algorithm for arbitrary surreal
inputs; constructing or comparing unrestricted surreals is a separate
representational problem (10).

\section{A binary model with two different missing cuts}\label{ogl:lat:sec:pell}

Consider, in both rows,
\begin{equation}\label{ogl:lat:eq:binary}
 \cE_\varepsilon(x,y)=|x-\sqrt2\,y|^2+\varepsilon(|x|^2+|y|^2),\qquad
 0<\varepsilon\in\mm,
\end{equation}
with matrix
$Q_\varepsilon=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1+\varepsilon&-\sqrt2\\-\sqrt2&2+\varepsilon\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$,
$\det Q_\varepsilon=\varepsilon(3+\varepsilon)>0$. Here $K_1=k(\sqrt2,1)$, $K_2=0$,
and $K_1\cap D^2=0$ since $\sqrt2\notin\Q(i)$. The form and the Pell vectors
below are those of \replabel{tate:theta:ex:irrational}, where the lexicographic
energy $\bigl((m-\sqrt2n)^2,m^2+n^2\bigr)$ is shown to decrease strictly along the
Pell vectors, so that its theta series is not Hahn summable. Source 10 uses the
same example for a different purpose: to compare two omnific lower cuts.

\begin{theorem}[Unrestricted binary cut \src{10}]\label{ogl:lat:thm:binarycut}
The nonzero-vector energies of \eqref{ogl:lat:eq:binary} have nonnegative lower
bounds exactly $\varepsilon(\Ofin)_{\ge0}$, no surreal infimum and no set-sized
coinitial family; every set-sized collection of nonzero candidates is improved by
some $x_\gamma=\omega^\gamma(\sqrt2,1)/\sqrt3$, with
$\cE_\varepsilon(x_\gamma)=\varepsilon\omega^{2\gamma}$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\Cref{ogl:lat:thm:main,ogl:lat:thm:simultaneous} with $q_1=|x|^2+|y|^2$.
\end{proof}

Define ordinary integers by $p_0=1$, $q_0=0$, $p_{n+1}=p_n+2q_n$,
$q_{n+1}=p_n+q_n$. By induction
\begin{equation}\label{ogl:lat:eq:pell}
 p_n\pm q_n\sqrt2=(1\pm\sqrt2)^n,\qquad p_n^2-2q_n^2=(-1)^n,\qquad
 p_nq_{n+1}-q_np_{n+1}=(-1)^n ,
\end{equation}
so consecutive $u_n=(p_n,q_n)$ form an ordinary unimodular basis and every $u_n$
is unimodular over $\Oz$ and $\GOz$, with
\begin{equation}\label{ogl:lat:eq:pellenergy}
 \cE_\varepsilon(u_n)=(\sqrt2-1)^{2n}+\varepsilon(p_n^2+q_n^2).
\end{equation}
These identities come from the recurrence, not from a classification of Pell
solutions over the omnific integers (10); Pell rigidity in the omnific
Diophantine report (only ordinary solutions at nonzero levels) is consistent
with them.

\begin{theorem}[Unimodular cut and countable coinitiality \src{10}]\label{ogl:lat:thm:pellcut}
For \eqref{ogl:lat:eq:binary} the nonnegative lower bounds of the unimodular
energies are exactly $\mm_{\ge0}$; there is no infimum and no minimum, and
$(\cE_\varepsilon(u_n))_{n\in\N}$ is strictly decreasing and coinitial in the
unimodular energies, in both rows.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For $x\in\Um(\A^2)$ and $z=\ct(x)$, $z$ is a nonzero ordinary unimodular vector and
$r_z=|z_1-\sqrt2z_2|^2>0$ is an ordinary real, so
$\cE_\varepsilon(x)\ge\cE_\varepsilon(z)>r_z$ (\cref{ogl:lat:thm:contraction}); every
nonnegative infinitesimal is a lower bound. A positive noninfinitesimal $b$
exceeds an ordinary $r>0$; choose $n$ with $(\sqrt2-1)^{2n}<r/2$, and since
$p_n^2+q_n^2$ is ordinary, $\cE_\varepsilon(u_n)<r<b$. The class $\mm_{\ge0}$ has no
greatest member. For coinitiality apply this with $r<r_z$. Consecutive energies
differ in the dominant coefficient by a positive ordinary real and in the rest by
an ordinary multiple of $\varepsilon$, so the sequence decreases
(\cref{ogl:lat:lem:lex}).
\end{proof}

For a fixed positive ordinary weight the second term of
\eqref{ogl:lat:eq:pellenergy} eventually stops the improvement; for infinitesimal
$\varepsilon$ no ordinary index reaches that balance, and substituting an
infinite omnific index into the recurrence is not part of its definition and
would not justify a limit (10).

With $\varepsilon=\omega^{-1}$ the two cuts differ visibly:
$\LB(\text{all nonzero energies})=\omega^{-1}(\Ofin)_{\ge0}$ and
$\LB(\text{unimodular energies})=\mm_{\ge0}$. For example $\omega^{-1/2}$ bounds
every unimodular energy but not $\cE(x_{1/8})=\omega^{-3/4}$. Every unrestricted
witness with small $\gamma$ beats the whole Pell sequence, yet it is not
unimodular, since its constant term is $0$. ``A coinitial Pell family'' is
correct for unimodular energies and false for unrestricted ones (10).

\section{Three attainment properties with strict implications}\label{ogl:lat:sec:examples}

\begin{corollary}[A strict hierarchy \src{10}]\label{ogl:lat:cor:hierarchy}
For separated positive pencils: closest points for every target in $F^n$
$\Rightarrow$ a shortest nonzero vector $\Rightarrow$ a shortest unimodular
vector; neither implication reverses.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
If every prefix kernel is rational, $K_m\ne0$ contains a nonzero integral vector
and \cref{ogl:lat:thm:main} applies; its ordinary minimizers are unimodular,
since division by a nonunit gcd would give a shorter nonzero vector. Strictness:
the examples below.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[All three properties \src{10}]\label{ogl:lat:ex:rational}
$\cE(x,y)=x^2+\varepsilon y^2$ on $\Oz^2$: all kernels rational, shortest vectors
$\pm(0,1)$ of energy $\varepsilon$; the Gaussian version has the four unit
multiples of $(0,1)$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[A shortest vector without universal closest points \src{10}]\label{ogl:lat:ex:shortnotclosest}
On $\Oz^3$ let $\cE=(z-\sqrt2y)^2+\varepsilon(x^2+y^2+z^2)$. The last kernel is
$K_1=\Span_\R\{(1,0,0),(0,1,\sqrt2)\}$ with $K_1\cap\Z^3=\Z(1,0,0)$, so the
shortest vectors are $\pm(1,0,0)$ with minimum $\varepsilon$; but $K_1$ is not
rational. For the center $(0,0,1/2)$ the dominant term on integer candidates is
$(z-\sqrt2y-1/2)^2$, with infimum $0$ never attained: $\Z+\sqrt2\Z$ contains the
arbitrarily small nonzero $(1-\sqrt2)^n$, so it is dense, and $1/2\notin\Z+\sqrt2\Z$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Unimodular attainment without unrestricted attainment \src{10}]\label{ogl:lat:ex:unimodularonly}
On $\Oz^3$ let $\cE=(z-\sqrt2y)^2+\varepsilon x^2+\varepsilon^2(y^2+z^2)$. Then
$K_1=\Span_\R\{(1,0,0),(0,1,\sqrt2)\}$, $K_2=\R(0,1,\sqrt2)$, $L_1=\Z(1,0,0)$ and
$L_2=0$; the unrestricted energies have no infimum, with lower cut
$\varepsilon^2(\Ofin)_{\ge0}$. For the unimodular criterion $r=1$, $W=\R(1,0,0)$ and
$W\cap K_2=0$: the shortest unimodular vectors are exactly $\pm(1,0,0)$, of energy
$\varepsilon$. Directly: an ordinary vector off the $x$-axis has positive
dominant coefficient, ordinary unimodular vectors on the axis are $\pm(1,0,0)$,
and nonordinary unimodular vectors are worse than their constant terms. The
Gaussian analogue has four unimodular minimizers.
\end{example}

The binary form \eqref{ogl:lat:eq:binary} has neither minimum
(\cref{ogl:lat:sec:pell}).

\section{Reduction obstructions}\label{ogl:lat:sec:reduction}

Nonattainment can rule out the existence of the reduced basis itself, not only
the termination of an algorithm. A basis of $\A^n$ means the columns of a matrix
in $\GL_n(\A)$; an invertible matrix over $F$ is not a change of basis of the
same $\A$-module (10).

\begin{lemma}[What Gauss inequalities would imply \src{10}]\label{ogl:lat:lem:gauss}
Over $\Oz$, with $\beta(u,v)=u^{\mathsf T}Qv$ the bilinear form of the energy
$\cE$, a basis with $\cE(b_1)\le\cE(b_2)$ and $2|\beta(b_1,b_2)|\le\cE(b_1)$ has
$b_1$ as a shortest nonzero vector.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For $s,t\in\Oz$ not both $0$,
$\cE(sb_1+tb_2)\ge(s^2-|st|+t^2)\cE(b_1)$. If one coefficient vanishes the other
has modulus at least $1$ (\cref{ogl:lem:units}); otherwise
$s^2-|st|+t^2=(|s|-|t|)^2+|st|\ge1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[No Gauss-reduced basis \src{10}]\label{ogl:lat:cor:nogauss}
A binary separated positive pencil with $L_m=0$, for instance
\eqref{ogl:lat:eq:binary}, has no Gauss-reduced $\Oz$-basis.
\end{corollary}

For a real basis $b_1,\dots,b_n$ perform finite Gram--Schmidt orthogonalization,
$b_j=b_j^*+\sum_{i<j}\mu_{ji}b_i^*$ with $\Delta_i=\cE(b_i^*)>0$ (10 writes $D_i$;
all divisions are in $\No$). The $\delta$-LLL conditions \cite{LLL} are
\begin{equation}\label{ogl:lat:eq:lll}
 |\mu_{ji}|\le\tfrac12\ (i<j),\qquad
 \Delta_j+\mu_{j,j-1}^2\Delta_{j-1}\ge\delta\Delta_{j-1}\ (j\ge2).
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[No ordinary-parameter LLL basis \src{10}]\label{ogl:lat:thm:nolll}
Let $\cE$ be a separated positive pencil on $\Oz^n$ with $L_m=0$. For no ordinary
$\delta\in(1/4,1]$ does an $\Oz$-basis satisfy \eqref{ogl:lat:eq:lll}.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
With $c=\delta-1/4\in(0,3/4]$ ordinary, the size bound and the Lov\'asz inequality
give $\Delta_j\ge c\Delta_{j-1}\ge c^{j-1}\Delta_1$. For nonzero
$v=\sum a_ib_i$, $a_i\in\Oz$, with $a_k$ the last nonzero coefficient, the
$b_k^*$-coefficient of $v$ is $a_k$, so
$\cE(v)\ge a_k^2\Delta_k\ge\Delta_k\ge c^{n-1}\cE(b_1)$, using $|a_k|\ge1$ and
$c\le1$. The factor $c^{n-1}$ is ordinary, contradicting
\cref{ogl:lat:cor:noapprox} for $b_1$.
\end{proof}

This is an existence obstruction. No assertion is made about a modified
reduction notion with different constants or an infinitesimal gap
$\delta-1/4$ (10). It applies also to \cref{ogl:lat:ex:unimodularonly}, which has
a shortest unimodular vector: LLL conditions control every nonzero module vector,
including the non-unimodular monomial vectors.

For $F=\SC$ use $\langle u,v\rangle=u^*Qv$, conjugate-linear in the first
argument, $\mu_{ji}=\langle b_i^*,b_j\rangle/\Delta_i$, and the explicitly stated
conditions
\begin{equation}\label{ogl:lat:eq:gaussianlll}
 |\Re\mu_{ji}|\le\tfrac12,\quad|\Im\mu_{ji}|\le\tfrac12\ (i<j),\qquad
 \Delta_j+|\mu_{j,j-1}|^2\Delta_{j-1}\ge\delta\Delta_{j-1}\ (j\ge2),
\end{equation}
stated to avoid relying on a convention for complex LLL parameters (10).

\begin{theorem}[Gaussian reduction obstruction \src{10}]\label{ogl:lat:thm:gaussianlll}
For a separated positive pencil on $\GOz^n$ with $L_m=0$, no $\GOz$-basis
satisfies \eqref{ogl:lat:eq:gaussianlll} for an ordinary $\delta\in(1/2,1]$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The size conditions give $|\mu_{j,j-1}|^2\le1/2$; with $c=\delta-1/2\in(0,1/2]$,
$\Delta_j\ge c^{j-1}\Delta_1$, and $|a_k|\ge1$ gives $\cE(v)\ge c^{n-1}\cE(b_1)$,
contradicting \cref{ogl:lat:cor:noapprox}.
\end{proof}

\section{Closed, uniformly separated Euclidean lattices}\label{ogl:lat:sec:euclidean}

For ordinary real $\alpha$ and positive $\varepsilon$ let
\[
 T_{\alpha,\varepsilon}=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{1+\varepsilon}&-\alpha/\sqrt{1+\varepsilon}\\
 0&\sqrt{\varepsilon(1+\alpha^2+\varepsilon)/(1+\varepsilon)}\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad
 T_{\alpha,\varepsilon}^*T_{\alpha,\varepsilon}
 =\begin{pmatrix}1+\varepsilon&-\alpha\\-\alpha&\alpha^2+\varepsilon\end{pmatrix},
\]
with positive surreal square roots and determinant
$\sqrt{\varepsilon(1+\alpha^2+\varepsilon)}\ne0$. The module
$\mathcal L_\varepsilon=T_{\sqrt2,\varepsilon}\A^2\subseteq F^2$ satisfies
$\norm{T_{\sqrt2,\varepsilon}x}^2=\cE_\varepsilon(x)$, so the binary example is a
literal Euclidean lattice problem in surreal coordinates (10).

\begin{proposition}[Uniform separation without a shortest vector \src{10}]\label{ogl:lat:prop:closed}
For positive infinitesimal $\varepsilon$, $\mathcal L_\varepsilon$ is uniformly
separated and closed in the $\No$-valued Euclidean topology, yet its nonzero
distances to $0$ have neither a minimum nor a surreal infimum.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$\cE_\varepsilon(x)\ge\varepsilon\norm{x}^2$ and a nonzero $x\in\A^2$ has a
coordinate of modulus at least $1$, so distinct points are at distance at least
$\rho=\sqrt\varepsilon$. For $p\notin\mathcal L_\varepsilon$ the ball
$B(p,\rho/3)$ contains at most one lattice point $\ell$, and the ball of radius
$\min(\rho/3,\norm{p-\ell}/2)$ contains none; so the complement is open, without
any set-indexed limit. Squaring is an order isomorphism of the nonnegative
surreals, so \cref{ogl:lat:thm:binarycut} transfers.
\end{proof}

\Cref{ogl:lat:lem:coercive} gives uniform separation for every pencil; the
missing ingredient for an attained minimum is ordinary compactness and
finiteness, not positive definiteness. No contradiction to a Minkowski theorem
with a surreal volume is claimed, since no such volume theory is constructed;
$\mathcal L_\varepsilon$ is a proper-class module, and its two generators are a
basis over $\A$, not over $\Z$ (10).

\section{Scope and sharp boundaries}\label{ogl:lat:sec:scope}

\paragraph{The full surreal class matters.} The failure of an infimum and the
failure of set-sized coinitiality are distinct; the latter uses a positive
exponent below an arbitrary set and must not be transferred to a fixed Hahn field
(10). In the Hahn field $k((t^\R))$ ($t$ positive infinitesimal, well-ordered
supports) let $A^{\mathrm{Hahn}}=D+k((\R_{<0}))$, the series with nonpositive
exponents and constant coefficient in $D$ (here $k((\R_{<0}))$ denotes series
supported in $\R_{<0}$, not a field). For the binary form with
$\varepsilon=t^\eta$, $\eta>0$ real, the vectors
$v_n=t^{-1/n}(\sqrt2,1)/\sqrt3$ have energies $t^{\eta-2/n}$; every nonzero energy
divided by $\varepsilon$ is still positive infinite, and if its leading term is
$ct^{-d}$, $d>0$, then $n$ with $2/n<d$ gives $\cE(v_n)/\cE(x)$ infinitesimal. So
$(v_n)$ is a countable coinitial family there; the energies still have no
infimum. In $\No$ the exponent $\gamma=1/\omega$ lies below every $1/n$, and
$\omega^{1/\omega}(\sqrt2,1)/\sqrt3$ defeats the whole sequence under $t=\omega^{-1}$.

\paragraph{Ordinary coefficient forms are essential.} For the positive-definite
surreal form $q(x,y)=(x-\omega y)^2+y^2$ and $v=(\omega,1)\in\Oz^2$, $q(v)=1$ but
$q(\ct v)=q(0,1)=\omega^2+1$: contraction fails as strongly as possible. This form
is even reduced by the unimodular $\Oz$-basis $(1,0),(\omega,1)$ (both energies
$1$, orthogonal), so it is not asserted that all positive-definite surreal forms
lack reduced bases. A general asymptotic expansion of a positive-definite matrix
need not have positive-semidefinite coefficients and cannot be inserted into the
hypotheses (10).

\paragraph{What is and is not uniform.} \Cref{ogl:lat:thm:alltargets} handles
every target, but it does not say that infinitesimal perturbations leave the
nearest point unchanged (\cref{ogl:lat:ex:targetperturb}). The unrestricted
theorem improves every set-sized family, not a family indexed by the whole class,
and in the unimodular problem even a countable coinitial family cannot be
improved simultaneously by another unimodular vector. The LLL obstructions need
an ordinary parameter strictly above the critical value (10).

\section{Questions}\label{ogl:sec:questions}

\begin{question}[The full special linear groups \src{06, 07, 11}]\label{ogl:q:sln}
For $n\ge3$, is $\SL_n(\Z)$ the universal set-sized quotient of $\SL_n(\Oz)$, and
$\SL_n(\Z[i])$ that of $\SL_n(\GOz)$? More generally, what can set-sized groups
detect on $\SL_n(\A_{D,k})$ outside $E_n(\A_{D,k})$, and can the cusp-residue
obstruction be organized into a classification of elementary orbits or of
extensions of two-row representations?
\end{question}
\emph{Status: open.} Non-elementary matrices and relative algebraic $K$-theory
are the additional obstruction (06). \Cref{ogl:el:thm:nonel} shows that the
issue cannot be bypassed by declaring all unimodular matrices elementary, and
\cref{ogl:el:thm:stabilize} shows only that one family becomes invisible after
one stabilization; it does not classify the remaining obstructions (07). Source
11 asks the first part again (its Question~11.1) and notes that elementary
generation, or an independent elimination of the unstable obstruction, would
settle it; neither step is hidden in any proof here.

\begin{question}[Rank two \src{06, 11}]\label{ogl:q:ranktwo}
Does an analogue of kernel invisibility hold for $E_2$?
\end{question}
\emph{Status: answered negatively by 07} (\cref{ogl:el:thm:countable,ogl:el:thm:trichotomy}):
every nonidentity element of $P_2$ is detected by a countable group, although no
finite group detects it. Source 11 asks for the set-sized images of $E_2(\Oz)$,
$E_2(\GOz)$ and their kernels (its Question~11.2), at a pin where this answer
was already in the report: there is no universal set-sized quotient
(\cref{ogl:el:cor:nouniversal}), finite images are arithmetic
(\cref{ogl:el:prop:finitefactor}), and one countable group separates finite
sets (\cref{ogl:el:thm:countable}).

\begin{question}[Other root systems \src{07, 11}]\label{ogl:q:roots}
For which elementary Chevalley-type groups over $\A_{D,k}$ does a collision force
the whole relative kernel to have no set-sized images, and which low-rank systems
keep additive specializations like \cref{ogl:el:prop:specialize}?
\end{question}
\emph{Status: open.} Other root systems have extra root factors and integer
structure constants in their commutator formulas, which a formal analogy does not
control (07). Source 11 suggests the same for elementary symplectic and other
split Chevalley groups through root-kernel reconstruction, and notes that root
lengths and commutator constants must be addressed; it also leaves the kernel of
$\St_n(\A_{D,k})\to E_n(\A_{D,k})$ uncomputed (\cref{ogl:ker:rem:noK}).

\begin{question}[Thresholds at set-sized stages \src{07; 06}]\label{ogl:q:thresholds}

cardinal detecting a prescribed nonzero relative elementary matrix?
\end{question}
\emph{Status: partly answered} \mergetag. \Cref{ogl:el:prop:criterion,ogl:el:prop:local}
give lower bounds; a matching upper bound needs an actual detecting

\item Nothing in this report is formalized in Lean; \fn{docs/FORMALIZATION.md}
maps none of its labels.
\item The verification of the Mirzaii--Torres and Cohn citations and of the Milne
theorem numbers checks citations, not proofs; 06's reference to real rank and

checked.

sibling report (\replabel{osq:prop:card11}, \replabel{osq:lem:collision} and,
for \cref{ogl:ker:cor:fivemodels}, the properties established in the proof of

independent of the collision identities of the other two: it imports the ring

\item The material added from source 11 was not reviewed beyond this

\section{Verification suites}\label{ogl:app:verification}

Four finite programs accompany the report, as shipped (byte-identical to the
deliveries). The first three need Python~3 and SymPy (the recorded runs used
Python 3.13.5 and SymPy 1.14.0); source 11's needs only the Python standard
library (version 3.10 or later). All four were rerun for this report on copies
in a scratch directory with Python 3.14.4 (and SymPy 1.14.0).
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \fn{code/06-algebraic-groups-verification.py} (06): 1,108 exact checks in ten
families (908 elementary-identity checks in ranks $3$--$6$, 18 for the two
orthogonal examples \eqref{ogl:alg:eq:complexexample} and
\eqref{ogl:alg:eq:realexample}, 139 unitriangular instances, 43 geometric
remainders and finite-support augmentations). It prints to standard output and
writes a file only with \texttt{--output}; the recorded run is
\fn{data/06-algebraic-groups-verification-results.txt}.
\item \fn{code/07-matrix-dichotomy-checks.py} (07): 23 checks, including 120 exact
reduced alternating words in the countable target, the Steinberg identities,
\eqref{ogl:el:eq:gaussiancomm}, the commutator of \cref{ogl:el:ex:commutator},
the determinant and square-zero identities of $M_\alpha$, and the stabilized
commutator. It always writes \fn{checks-results.json} next to itself; the recorded
run is \fn{data/07-matrix-dichotomy-checks-results.json} with
\fn{data/07-matrix-dichotomy-checks-output.txt}. The rerun reproduced it except
for the recorded Python version.
\item \fn{code/10-shortest-vectors-verify.py} (10): six groups of checks (the
Cholesky identity, the Pell recurrence and 81 norms, determinants and gcds for
indices $0$--$80$, the Gram--Schmidt identity, the three-dimensional examples, and
the perturbed-target differences of \cref{ogl:lat:ex:targetperturb}); standard
output only, recorded in \fn{data/10-shortest-vectors-verification-output.txt}.
\item \fn{code/11-matrix-shadows-verify.py} (11): 2,111 deterministic exact checks
(seed 20260923) in twelve families, with exact fractions and finite Laurent
polynomials over the lexicographic group $\Q\oplus\Q$: root additivity (272),
root inverses (272), root commutators (840), constant-term multiplication and
nonnegative supports (200 each), word inverses, conjugated commutators,
constant terms of conjugated roots and matrix constant-term multiplication (40
each), exact geometric remainders and positivity of the quotient supports (81
each, the identity
$(X^{2e_1}-X^{e_1})\sum_{r=0}^MX^{e_0-(r+2)e_1}=X^{e_0}-X^{e_0-(M+1)e_1}$), and the
Whitehead diagonal identity over rational units (5). It prints its results and
always overwrites \fn{verification_results.json} next to itself (under the shipped
name, \fn{code/verification_results.json}); the recorded run is
\fn{data/11-matrix-shadows-verification_results.json}, and the rerun reproduced
its content exactly.
\end{itemize}
These programs check finite identities and examples. They do not verify Hahn
summability, support gaps, class-size or cardinal arguments, algebraic-group
structure, the reduced-word arguments, or any minimization or coinitiality
theorem, which are proved in the text.
