\begin{abstract}
The omnific integers $\Oz=\Pi\oplus\Z$ form a discretely ordered subring of
the surreal field whose arithmetic is neither ordinary integer arithmetic
nor a routine nonstandard enlargement of it. We organize their theory
around the split ring retraction $\ct:\Oz\to\Z$ that takes a normal form to
its constant coefficient. This gives an exact transfer theorem for
polynomial equations with ordinary integer coefficients, a classification of
finite quotients, and a complete solution criterion for linear systems.

The Diophantine results distinguish existence from the structure of
solutions. At a nonzero constant level, the omnific fiber of a product of
affine linear forms is described exactly by a real common kernel; in
particular binary forms with at least two distinct projective linear factors,
Pell equations and number-field norm forms have no new omnific points.
Support-preserving Euler derivations prove the same rigidity for every
separated equation $ax^m+by^n=c$ with $abc\ne0$ and $m,n\ge2$, including
$y^2=x^3+1$, and for nonzero Fermat triples of exponent at least three that
generate the unit ideal. In contrast, every represented nonzero level of a
nondegenerate indefinite integral quadratic form in at least three variables
carries explicit injective polynomial families through each integer point,
and we prove an exhaustive quadratic-level dichotomy that includes degenerate
forms. Pell rigidity combined with the three-square or the four-square
theorem gives single quartic equations defining the ordinary integers inside
$\Oz$. Further results concern non-atomic factorization, full-class common
divisors, bounded semialgebraic rigidity, primitive and projective points, a
failed existential induction instance, and failures of residue lifting.

This report merges three independently written manuscripts; it states which
source proved each result, prints shared results once, and keeps every
source's limitations. It is an AI-assisted draft: it has not been refereed,
and nothing in it has been formalized in Lean.
\end{abstract}

\section{Scope, results, and conventions}\label{odg:sec:scope}

Omnific integers are the surreal numbers with no negative exponents in
Conway normal form and with an ordinary integer constant coefficient.
The definition is classical, as are their discreteness and integer-part
property \cite{Conway,Gonshor,LM}. This report develops their arithmetic
with a particular emphasis on polynomial equations.

Three questions must be kept separate. Does an equation have any solution?
Does it have a genuinely infinite solution? Does it have such a solution
with a nonvanishing, positivity or primitivity condition? For example,
every system of equations over $\Z$ has an omnific solution exactly when it
has an ordinary integer solution. Nevertheless, the equation $X^n+Y^n=Z^n$
has positive infinite omnific solutions for every ordinary $n\geq2$. There
is no contradiction: the constant coefficients of these solutions are all
zero. On the other hand, every omnific solution of
\[
 x^2-2y^2=1,\qquad y^2=x^3+17,\qquad x^7-y^5=3
\]
has ordinary integer coordinates; these conclusions concern the entire
solution classes, not just their constant terms.

The central decomposition is
\begin{equation}\label{odg:eq:splitintro}
 \Oz=\Z\oplus\Pi,\qquad \ct:\Oz\longrightarrow\Z,
 \qquad \ct(a+b)=\ct(a)+\ct(b),\quad \ct(ab)=\ct(a)\ct(b),
\end{equation}
where $\Pi$ is the ideal of normal forms supported on strictly positive
exponents; coefficients of elements of $\Pi$ may have either sign. The
decomposition is a splitting of additive groups and a ring retraction, not a
direct product decomposition of rings. In particular, $\Pi$ has no identity.

\subsection{Principal results}\label{odg:sub:results}

The structural results of
Sections~\ref{odg:sec:basic}--\ref{odg:sec:class} explain why ordinary
congruences see only the constant coefficient, although the fraction field
of the full class $\Oz$ is all of $\No$. The main Diophantine statements are
the following. Bracketed numbers name the source manuscripts
(\cref{odg:sub:provenance}).

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\arabic*)},leftmargin=*,itemsep=3pt]
\item \textbf{Equational transfer} \src{01, 02, 05}. For every finite system
over $\Z$, $\V(\Oz)$ is nonempty exactly when $\V(\Z)$ is nonempty
(\cref{odg:thm:transfer}). This does not include inequations or order
conditions (\cref{odg:prop:notdiophantine}).
\item \textbf{Constant-product rigidity} \src{01, 02, 05}. A nonzero
constant product of normal forms with no negative exponents has only
constant factors. At a nonzero constant level, the omnific points of a
product of affine linear forms are exactly the integer points plus purely
infinite vectors in the real common kernel of the linear parts
(\cref{odg:thm:decomposable}). Binary forms with two distinct projective
factors over $\C$ at a level in $\C^\times$ have only constant points in the
complex nonnegative-support ring (\cref{odg:thm:binary}); Pell equations with
$D\ne0$ and number-field norm forms are rigid
(\cref{odg:cor:pell,odg:thm:norm}).
\item \textbf{Separated powers and unimodular Fermat} \src{05}. For real
$a,b,c\ne0$ and $m,n\ge2$, every nonnegative-support solution of
$ax^m+by^n=c$ is constant (\cref{odg:thm:separated}). For $n\ge3$, a nonzero
Fermat triple generating the unit ideal is constant
(\cref{odg:thm:unimodularfermat}); the threshold is sharp.
\item \textbf{Quadratic-level dichotomy} \src{01, 02}. For $q\in\Z[X_1,\ldots,X_n]$
quadratic homogeneous and $0\ne c\in\Z$, all solutions are ordinary in
the nondegenerate definite case and in nondegenerate dimension at most two.
In every other case each integer point lifts to an injectively parametrized
infinite family (\cref{odg:thm:quadclassification}). For indefinite forms in
at least three variables, $(\Pi,+)$ embeds in the kernel of
$\SO(q,\Oz)\to\SO(q,\Z)$ (\cref{odg:cor:unipotent}).
\item \textbf{Quartic definitions of $\Z$} \src{01, 05}. The equation
\[
 (U^2-2V^2-1)^2+
 \Bigl(U-\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_i^2-W_1^2-W_2^2-W_3^2\Bigr)^2=0
\]
with five existentially quantified auxiliary variables defines exactly
$\Z^n$ inside $\Oz^n$ (\cref{odg:thm:guard}); a variant with six auxiliary
variables and four squares defines $\Z$ (\cref{odg:thm:standarddef}). The
graph of $\ct$ and the ideal $\Pi$ are first-order definable, and an explicit
existential induction instance fails (\cref{odg:thm:induction}).
\item \textbf{Primitive and projective phenomena} \src{01, 02, 05}. There
are infinite unimodular Pythagorean triples. A constant real projective
direction has a primitive omnific representative precisely when it is
rational (\cref{odg:prop:pythagoras,odg:thm:realray}).
\item \textbf{Bounded and homogeneous geometry} \src{01, 02}. Bounded
semialgebraic sets have only integer omnific points
(\cref{odg:thm:bounded}); a homogeneous system has a nonzero omnific point
exactly when it has a nonzero real point, and for integer coefficients this
is decidable (\cref{odg:thm:cone}).
\end{enumerate}

These statements are proved here, but proving a statement here is not a
claim that nobody has previously proved it. The constant-term formalism is
classical, and several consequences are natural applications of it. Each
source names its own most distinctive contributions (\cref{odg:app:nonclaims});
no exhaustive historical-priority claim is made.

The \emph{universal set-sized quotient theorem}, that every unital ring
homomorphism from the full class $\Oz$ to a set-sized ring is
$x\mapsto\ct(x)1$, was proved by source 05 and is printed with its proof in
the sibling report \fn{docs/surreal/set-sized-quotients-of-omnific-integers}.
It is quoted here as \cref{odg:fact:settarget}, together with the elementary
special cases proved by source 01.

\subsection{Class size and foundations}\label{odg:sub:foundations}

The full surreal field and $\Oz$ are proper classes. We work in a customary
class theory such as NBG, with the usual background for surreal normal forms;
source 02 notes that one may equally read everything relative to universes,
with ``set-sized'' replaced by ``small in the specified lower universe''. Each
individual surreal has a \emph{set-sized} support. Every polynomial, system,
matrix, or tuple in this report has ordinary finite size. Quantifiers over
$\Oz$ are class quantifiers expressed through their defining set-theoretic
formulas, not an appeal to a set containing all surreals.

Choice for sets is used to extend a rational linear functional on a
set-sized exponent group (\cref{odg:lem:detection}). No proof printed in this
report uses global choice. Source 05 works in NBG with global choice, which it
uses only to pick a cardinal in the proof of the set-sized quotient theorem;
the Hartogs ordinal of the target ring serves there without choice, since the
pigeonhole step needs only that no injection from that ordinal into the target
exists (\cref{odg:rem:hartogs}).

Most proofs can be carried out in a fixed set-sized Hahn field. We identify
the arguments that need the stronger fact that \emph{every set} of surreal
exponents has upper bounds and every set of positive surreal exponents has a
strictly positive lower bound (\cref{odg:sec:class}). One must not silently
assume these properties for a fixed ordered exponent group
(\cref{odg:sec:formal}).

\paragraph{Sign convention.}
Exponents are those of Conway normal forms: supports are reverse well
ordered, and $\omega^\gamma$ is \emph{infinite} for $\gamma>0$, even for
infinitesimal $\gamma>0$. Thus
\[
 \Pi=\{x\in\No:\supp(x)\subseteq\No^{>0}\}\quad(\text{$\omega$-exponents}).
\]
In the Hahn convention $t=\omega^{-1}$, which is the convention of the
trigonometry report (\fn{docs/surcomplex/trigonometry},
equation \replabel{trigonometry:eq:split}), every exponent changes sign:
$\omega^\gamma=t^{-\gamma}$, supports become well ordered, the valuation is
$v=-\ddeg$, and the same class is
\[
 \Pi=\{x\in\No:\supp_t(x)\subseteq\No^{<0}\}\quad(\text{$t$-exponents}).
\]
So ``strictly positive exponents'' here and ``negative $t$-exponents'' there
describe one class, and ``no positive exponents'' in the $t$-convention is
``no negative exponents'' here. Correspondingly $\BR$ is the ring of
\emph{nonpositive} valuation, not the valuation ring of finite elements.
Using the wrong convention would invalidate the unit computation
(\cref{odg:prop:units}) and every rigidity proof below.

The repository's foundations report defines $\Oz=\Pi\oplus\Z$ with the same
letter $\Pi$ (\fn{docs/foundations-and-computation/foundations}, label
\replabel{found:eq:omnific}); the notation here agrees with it. Other
repository names for the same class are $\No^{\uparrow}$ and
$\No_{\mathrm{PI}}$ in other reports. The sources used $\mathcal J$ (01) and
$\mathcal I$ (02, 05); the complete per-source rename table is
\cref{odg:tab:renames}.

\section{The normal-form rings and the integer part}\label{odg:sec:basic}

\subsection{Normal forms and three related rings}

We use the standard normal-form theorem: each surreal has a unique
expression
\begin{equation}\label{odg:eq:nf}
 x=\sum_{\gamma\in S}r_\gamma\omega^\gamma,
 \qquad r_\gamma\in\R\setminus\{0\},
\end{equation}
where $S\subseteq\No$ is a reverse-well-ordered set: every nonempty subset
has a greatest member. Conversely such a normal form represents a surreal.
Addition is coefficientwise, multiplication is Hahn convolution (the sum of
two reverse-well-ordered supports is reverse well ordered, and each exponent
of a product receives finitely many contributions), and the sign of a
nonzero normal form is the sign of its leading coefficient
\cite{Conway,Gonshor}. The field $\No$ is real closed.

\begin{definition}\label{odg:def:rings}
Let
\begin{align*}
 \Pi&=\{x\in\No:\supp(x)\subseteq\No^{>0}\},\\
 \BR&=\R\oplus\Pi,\\
 \Oz&=\Z\oplus\Pi,\\
 \BC&=\BR[i]\subseteq\No[i].
\end{align*}
The zero series belongs to $\Pi$. For an element of any of these rings,
$\ct(x)$ denotes its coefficient at exponent zero. For
$x=a+ib\in\BC$, this is $\ct(a)+i\ct(b)$. Put $\Pi_{\C}=\Pi+i\Pi$, so
$\BC=\C\oplus\Pi_{\C}$.
\end{definition}

Thus $\sqrt2\,\omega$, $\omega/17$, $-\omega^\omega+3$,
$\omega^{1/\omega}$ and $\sum_{k\geq1}\omega^{1/k}$ are omnific integers.
The numbers $\omega+\tfrac12$ and $\omega+\omega^{-1}$ are not. The word
``integer'' restricts the constant coefficient and excludes infinitesimal
tails; it does not require all coefficients to lie in $\Z$.
``Purely infinite'' is a support condition, not a sign condition: $-\omega\in\Pi$,
while $\omega-3\notin\Pi$. The normal-form definition of $\Oz$ is equivalent to
Conway's simplicity characterization by the cut $\{x-1\mid x+1\}$
\cite{Conway,Gonshor}; no proof below uses that characterization.

For every positive surreal exponent $\gamma$, even an infinitesimal one,
the Conway monomial $\omega^\gamma$ exceeds every ordinary positive
real number. This is a normal-form order fact about the omega-map;
replacing that map by an unstated analytic expression
$\exp(\gamma\log\omega)$ would invalidate the support arguments below.

\begin{proposition}[Rings and retraction \src{01, 02, 05}]\label{odg:prop:ring}
$\BR$, $\BC$, and $\Oz$ are integral domains, and $\BR$, $\Oz$ are ordered
subrings of $\No$. The class $\Pi$ is an ideal of $\BR$ and $\Oz$, and is
an $\R$-vector space; $\Pi_{\C}$ is an ideal of $\BC$.
Constant-coefficient extraction is a surjective ring homomorphism from
$\BR$ to $\R$, from $\BC$ to $\C$, and from $\Oz$ to $\Z$. In the last case
its kernel is $\Pi$, the inclusion $\Z\hookrightarrow\Oz$ is a section, and
$\Oz/\Pi\cong\Z$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Nonnegative exponents remain nonnegative under addition of exponents. A
strictly positive exponent plus a nonnegative exponent is strictly
positive. These observations and Hahn convolution prove the ring and ideal
claims. In a product of two supported normal forms, exponent zero can
arise only as $0+0$. Therefore $\ct(xy)=\ct(x)\ct(y)$, and the constant
coefficient of a product of elements of $\Oz$ is an integer. Additivity is
coefficientwise. Real scalar multiplication does not change a support except
when the scalar is zero. Surjectivity and the section are supplied by
constant series. All rings embed in a field, so have no zero divisors.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Two misleading analogies]\label{odg:rem:notvaluation}
The map $\ct$ is not a ring homomorphism on all of $\No$:
$\ct(\omega)=\ct(\omega^{-1})=0$ but $\ct(1)=1$. The repository records the
same warning for coefficient extraction at zero on $\No$
(\replabel{diff:warn:ct} in the differential-equations report, and
\fn{docs/NOTATION.md}); the map $\ct$ of this report is the restriction of
that extraction to $\Oz$, where it \emph{is} multiplicative. Also $\BR$ is not
the ring of finite surreals. The latter permits negative exponents but no
positive exponents; $\BR$ has the opposite support restriction. Taking the
standard part of a finite element and taking the constant coefficient of an
omnific integer are different constructions, although both are multiplicative
on their own domains. Finally, $\ct$ is not order preserving:
$\omega-5>0$ but $\ct(\omega-5)=-5$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Leading degree, units, and finite elements}

For nonzero $x\in\No[i]$, combine real and imaginary coefficients and put
\[
 \ddeg(x)=\max\supp(x),\qquad \lc(x)=[\omega^{\ddeg(x)}]x,
 \qquad \ddeg(0)=-\infty.
\]
On $\BC\setminus\{0\}$ the degree lies in $\No^{\geq0}$.

\begin{lemma}[Degree \src{01, 02, 05}]\label{odg:lem:degree}
For nonzero normal forms $x,y$ with real or complex coefficients,
\[
 \ddeg(xy)=\ddeg(x)+\ddeg(y),\qquad
 \lc(xy)=\lc(x)\lc(y),
\]
\[
 \ddeg(x+y)\le\max\{\ddeg x,\ddeg y\},
\]
with equality in the last relation when $\ddeg x\ne\ddeg y$. An element of
$\BC$ of degree zero is a nonzero complex constant.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
If $\gamma$ and $\delta$ are the respective largest exponents, every
contributing exponent of $xy$ is at most $\gamma+\delta$. Equality requires
both exponents to be largest: making either smaller makes their sum smaller.
Thus the coefficient at $\gamma+\delta$ is the nonzero product of leading
coefficients. The statements about sums follow from cancellation at the
greatest exponent. Finally, support in $[0,0]$ contains only zero.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Units and finite elements \src{01, 02, 05}]\label{odg:prop:units}
The unit groups are
\[
 \BR^\times=\R^\times,\qquad
 \BC^\times=\C^\times,\qquad \Oz^\times=\{1,-1\}.
\]
The omnific integers bounded in absolute value by some ordinary real number
are exactly the ordinary integers; in particular $\Oz\cap\R=\Z$.
Consequently $\Oz$ is discretely ordered, with least positive element $1$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
If $xy=1$ in $\BC$, nonnegativity and additivity of degree force both
degrees to be zero. This gives the first two unit statements; in $\Oz$ the
constant units are precisely the units of $\Z$.

A nonconstant element of $\Oz$ has positive leading exponent, so its
absolute value exceeds every positive real number by normal-form order.
A constant element of $\Oz$ is an integer. Hence no element lies strictly
between $0$ and $1$, and every positive element is at least $1$.
\end{proof}

Notice that $\omega/2$ is an omnific integer even though $1/2$ is not.
This is no conflict with discreteness: $\omega/2$ is infinite, and
there are still no omnific integers in the interval $(0,1)$.

The unit computation is stronger than saying that an infinite factor has
large real absolute value: complex leading coefficients can cancel in sums,
but they cannot cancel the unique leading coefficient in a product. It will
do much of the Diophantine work.

\subsection{Local workspaces}

\begin{lemma}[Set-sized workspaces \src{05}]\label{odg:lem:workspace}
Any set-sized family of normal forms belongs to a common Hahn field whose
exponents lie in a set-sized ordered divisible group $H\subseteq\No$. One can
take $H$ to be the rational span of the union of the supports.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The union of the supports is a set, and so is its rational span. Normal
forms supported in $H$ constitute the real or complex Hahn field with
exponents in $H$. The Hahn field operations preserve this exponent group,
including inverses of nonzero series.
\end{proof}

We write $\mathcal B_{\R,H}$, $\mathcal B_{\C,H}$ and $\Oz_H$ for the
nonnegative-support subrings of this workspace. Most proofs below, including
all the main equation-rigidity theorems, take place in one such set-sized
ring and need no proper-class derivation or basis. By contrast, a positive
surreal smaller than every member of a specified set of positive exponents
need not belong to a preassigned $H$; the results of \cref{odg:sec:class}
genuinely use the full exponent class, and are marked there.

\subsection{The exact omnific floor}

Every surreal admits a unique decomposition
\[
 x=x_{>0}+r+\varepsilon,
 \qquad x_{>0}\in\Pi,\quad r\in\R,\quad
 \supp(\varepsilon)\subseteq\No^{<0}.
\]
Here $\varepsilon$ is infinitesimal, possibly zero.

\begin{theorem}[Integer-part formula \src{01, 02, 05}]\label{odg:thm:floor}
There is exactly one $a\in\Oz$ such that $a\leq x<a+1$. It is
\begin{equation}\label{odg:eq:floor}
 \lfloor x\rfloor_{\Oz}=
 \begin{cases}
 x_{>0}+\lfloor r\rfloor,&r\notin\Z\text{ or }\varepsilon\geq0,\\
 x_{>0}+r-1,&r\in\Z\text{ and }\varepsilon<0.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For $r\notin\Z$, its distances from the two adjacent integers are
strictly positive real numbers, and the infinitesimal $\varepsilon$ cannot
cross either boundary. If $r$ is an integer and $\varepsilon\ge0$, the
remainder is $\varepsilon\in[0,1)$. If $r$ is an integer and $\varepsilon<0$,
the remainder after subtracting $x_{>0}+r-1$ is $1+\varepsilon\in(0,1)$.
Two possible floors would differ by an omnific integer strictly between $-1$
and $1$, so would coincide by discreteness.
\end{proof}

For example,
\[
 \lfloor\omega-\omega^{-1}\rfloor_{\Oz}=\omega-1,
 \qquad \lfloor\omega+\tfrac32-\omega^{-1}\rfloor_{\Oz}=\omega+1,
 \qquad \lfloor-\omega^{-1}\rfloor_{\Oz}=-1.
\]
Merely discarding negative exponents and flooring the constant gives the
wrong answer at a negative infinitesimal displacement from an integer.

\status{The integer-part assertion is classical. Ehrlich--Kaplan prove the
stronger initiality characterization: for an initial subfield $K$ of
$\No$, $\Oz\cap K$ is its unique initial integer part
\cite[Lemma 11.1]{EK}. No alternative integer part is introduced. The formula
agrees with the trigonometry report's construction of the integer part, which
takes ``the appropriate side when $f$ is infinitesimally adjacent to an
integer'' (text following \replabel{trigonometry:eq:split}).}

\begin{proposition}[One-variable rigidity \src{02, 05}]\label{odg:prop:univariate}
If $P\in\R[T]$ is nonzero, every root of $P$ in $\BR$ belongs to $\R$.
In particular, the roots in $\Oz$ of a nonzero $P\in\Z[T]$ are exactly its
ordinary integer roots.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
If a root $x$ were nonconstant, $\alpha=\ddeg(x)>0$. For nonzero
coefficients $a_j$, the terms $a_jx^j$ have distinct degrees $j\alpha$, so
the highest-degree term cannot cancel. Thus $P(x)\ne0$. A constant root in
$\Oz$ is an integer.
\end{proof}

Every infinite omnific integer is therefore transcendental over $\R$. This
does not make a tuple of infinite omnific integers algebraically independent:
$(\omega,\omega^2)$ is a counterexample. Infinite coefficients change the
problem: $X^2-\omega^2$ has the infinite roots $\pm\omega$.

\section{Divisibility, residues, and quotients}\label{odg:sec:residues}

\subsection{Division by ordinary integers}

\begin{theorem}[Ordinary moduli \src{01, 02, 05}]\label{odg:thm:finitequotients}
For every nonzero ordinary integer $n$ and $x\in\Oz$,
\begin{equation}\label{odg:eq:nOz}
 n\Oz=\Pi+n\Z,\qquad
 n\mid x\ \Longleftrightarrow\ n\mid\ct(x),\qquad
 \Oz/n\Oz\cong\Z/n\Z.
\end{equation}
In particular, for every ordinary prime $p$,
\begin{equation}\label{odg:eq:divkernel}
 \bigcap_{n\geq1}n\Oz=\Pi=\bigcap_{k\geq1}p^k\Oz .
\end{equation}
If $\ct(x)\ne0$, the largest ordinary $k$ with $p^k\mid x$ is the $p$-adic
order of $\ct(x)$; if $\ct(x)=0$, every power of every ordinary prime
divides $x$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Necessity in the divisibility criterion follows by applying $\ct$ to $x=ny$.
Multiplication by a nonzero real scalar preserves $\Pi$ and is invertible
on $\Pi$, so $n\Pi=\Pi$. If $x=m+j$ with $m\in\Z$, $j\in\Pi$, then
$x/n=m/n+j/n$ lies in $\Oz$ exactly when $m/n$ is an integer. Reduction of
$\ct$ modulo $n$ is therefore a surjection with kernel $n\Oz$. The only
ordinary integer divisible by all positive integers, or by every power of one
prime, is zero, which gives \eqref{odg:eq:divkernel} and the statement on
$p$-adic orders.
\end{proof}

This theorem solves every ordinary congruence. For instance,
$\sqrt2\,\omega+14$ is even and is congruent to $2$ modulo $3$; $\omega$ is
divisible by every positive ordinary integer and $\omega+1$ is odd. The
whole infinite part is invisible to these tests.

The statement does \emph{not} extend to arbitrary omnific divisors.
For example, $\ct(\omega+2)$ divides $\ct(2)$ in $\Z$, but $\omega+2$
does not divide $2$ in $\Oz$. More generally:

\begin{proposition}[Divisors of integers \src{05}]\label{odg:prop:finitedivisors}
If $d\in\Oz$ divides a nonzero ordinary integer $N$, then $d$ is an
ordinary integer divisor of $N$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Write $de=N$. Neither factor is zero, so $|e|\ge1$ by discreteness and
$|d|\le|N|$. Hence $d\in\Z$ by \cref{odg:prop:units}; similarly $e\in\Z$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Ordinary primes and mixed gcds \src{02, 05}]\label{odg:cor:mixedgcd}
Every positive ordinary prime $p$ is a prime element of $\Oz$, and $p\Oz$ is
maximal. For $0\ne n\in\Z$ and $x\in\Oz$,
\[
 (n,x)=\gcd(n,\ct(x))\,\Oz,
\]
so an ordinary gcd of $n$ and $\ct(x)$ is a gcd of $n$ and $x$ in $\Oz$. The
Chinese remainder theorem for pairwise coprime ordinary moduli holds in $\Oz$
with exactly the ordinary residue rings.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The quotient by $p\Oz$ is the field $\Z/p\Z$. If $x=m+j$ with $j\in\Pi$,
then $x-n(j/n)=m$, so $(n,x)=(n,m)$, which an ordinary B\'ezout identity
identifies. A generator of a two-generated principal ideal is a gcd of its
generators in any domain. For coprime moduli, ordinary B\'ezout coefficients
give comaximality, and the quotient descriptions give the Chinese remainder
isomorphism.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Positive-characteristic quotients \src{01, 02, 05}]\label{odg:cor:charideals}
Every proper ideal $\mathfrak a\subset\Oz$ containing a nonzero ordinary
integer is $n\Oz$ for a unique ordinary $n\geq2$; it is prime exactly when
$n$ is an ordinary prime. Every unital homomorphism from $\Oz$ to a finite
ring kills $\Pi$ and factors through $\ct$, and every nonzero finite quotient
of $\Oz$ is a ring $\Z/n\Z$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
If $m\in\mathfrak a$ with $0\ne m\in\Z$, then $\Pi=m\Pi\subseteq\mathfrak a$.
Thus $\mathfrak a$ is the inverse image of an ideal of $\Oz/\Pi=\Z$, namely
$n\Z$ with $n>0$, and properness gives $n\geq2$. Equation~\eqref{odg:eq:nOz}
identifies its inverse image with $n\Oz$; primality is that of $n\Z$. If a
finite target has characteristic $m$, then each $j\in\Pi$ is $m(j/m)$ and
maps to zero. A finite nonzero unital ring has positive characteristic, so the
last assertion follows.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Completions}

All finite-index ideals have just been classified, so the profinite
completion, interpreted as the set-sized inverse limit over ordinary $n\ge1$,
is
\begin{equation}\label{odg:eq:profinite}
 \widehat{\Oz}\cong\varprojlim_n\Z/n\Z=\widehat\Z,
 \qquad \varprojlim_k \Oz/p^k\Oz\cong\Z_p .
\end{equation}
The completion maps are the composites of $\ct$ with the canonical maps of
$\Z$, with kernel $\Pi$ in both cases by \eqref{odg:eq:divkernel}. Thus these
congruence topologies on $\Oz$ are not Hausdorff and $\Oz$ is not residually
finite \src{01, 02, 05}. For example $\omega$, $\sqrt2\,\omega$ and $0$ have
identical images in every finite quotient. The separated quotient is
$\Oz/\Pi=\Z$; completion does not preserve the infinite part.

\subsection{Homomorphisms to \texorpdfstring{$\Z$}{Z} and to set-sized rings}

\begin{proposition}[Uniqueness of the retraction \src{01}]\label{odg:prop:canonicalct}
The constant-term map is the unique unital ring homomorphism $\Oz\to\Z$.
Every unital endomorphism $\psi:\Oz\to\Oz$ preserves $\Pi$ and satisfies
$\ct\circ\psi=\ct$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For a homomorphism $h:\Oz\to\Z$, the integer $h(j)$ is divisible by every
positive integer whenever $j\in\Pi$. Hence $h(j)=0$. Since $h$ fixes
ordinary integers, $h=\ct$. An endomorphism preserves divisibility by
each ordinary integer, so preserves their common divisibility kernel
\eqref{odg:eq:divkernel}. It fixes $\Z$ pointwise, which proves the final
identity.
\end{proof}

This does not say that every endomorphism is the identity: the composite of
$\ct$ with the inclusion $\Z\hookrightarrow\Oz$ is a noninjective unital
endomorphism \src{05}.

\status{The divisible-kernel and residue-map perspective belongs to
classical $\Z$-group theory; see \cite[Sections 2 and 4]{JerabekMLQ}.
Here it has a concrete ring-theoretic realization because $\ct$ is
multiplicative. The finite-quotient and completion statements above are
proved directly.}

Source 05 proved a far-reaching generalization of
\cref{odg:prop:canonicalct,odg:cor:charideals}. It is proved, with the sharper
theory of set-sized quotients assembled from several manuscripts, in the
sibling report \fn{docs/surreal/set-sized-quotients-of-omnific-integers};
we quote it without proof.

\begin{fact}[Universal set-sized quotient; proved by source 05, printed in the sibling report]\label{odg:fact:settarget}
Let $S$ be a set-sized unital ring, not necessarily commutative, and let
$\varphi:\Oz\to S$ be a unital class ring homomorphism. Then
$\varphi(x)=\ct(x)\,1_S$ for every $x\in\Oz$. Consequently every set-sized
quotient ring of $\Oz$ is isomorphic to $\Z$ (kernel $\Pi$) or to $\Z/n\Z$
(kernel $n\Oz$) for an ordinary $n\ge1$.
\end{fact}

Source 05's proof applies pigeonhole to the monomials
$\omega^{\delta/\omega^{\alpha+1}}$ for ordinals $\alpha$ beyond the size of
$S$, and kills a positive monomial $\omega^\delta$ by the exact Hahn identity
$(\omega^a-\omega^b)\sum_{k\ge0}\omega^{\delta-a-k(a-b)}=\omega^\delta$, whose
quotient lies in $\Pi$ when $0<b<a\le\delta/\omega$; it then uses
\cref{odg:thm:commondivisor} below to kill all of $\Pi$.

\begin{remark}[Foundations of the cited theorem]\label{odg:rem:hartogs}
Source 05 chooses an infinite cardinal $\kappa>|S|$, using the global choice
of its NBG setting. The Hartogs ordinal of $S$, the least ordinal admitting no
injection into $S$, can be used instead: the pigeonhole step needs only that
the family $(\omega^{\delta/\omega^{\alpha+1}})_\alpha$ indexed by that ordinal
does not inject into $S$. So neither global choice nor choice for sets is
needed for \cref{odg:fact:settarget}.
\end{remark}

\Cref{odg:prop:canonicalct} is the case $S=\Z$ of \cref{odg:fact:settarget}
together with its consequence for endomorphisms (apply it to
$\ct\circ\psi$), and \cref{odg:cor:charideals} is the case of finite $S$;
the elementary divisibility proofs above are independent second routes. The
theorem does not apply to class-sized targets such as $\Oz$ itself, and it is
a full-class statement: the subring $\Z+\omega\R[\omega]$ of $\Oz$ admits
evaluation at many real values, and such maps do not extend to $\Oz$
\src{05}. Source 05 also records that, by \cref{odg:fact:settarget}, the
quotient $\Oz/(f)$ by an infinite prime $f$ with $\ct(f)=\pm1$ (for example
the prime of \cite[Theorem B]{LM} in \cref{odg:sub:factorization}) is not
set-sized; that consequence is likewise printed in the sibling report.

\section{Full-class support: common divisors, clearing, factorization}\label{odg:sec:class}

The next results use all surreal exponents, not merely the exponents in one
fixed Archimedean or finite-rank group. This is an important source of
arithmetic differences between full omnific integers and restricted series
rings (\cref{odg:sec:formal}).

\subsection{One monomial divides a whole set}

\begin{lemma}[Set-sized exponent bounds \src{01, 02, 05}]\label{odg:lem:setbounds}
If $E\subset\No^{>0}$ is a set, there is a surreal $\delta>0$ such
that $\delta<e$ for every $e\in E$. Every set of surreals also has a
strict upper bound.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
A surreal separating the cut $\{0\mid E\}$ gives the first assertion, and
a separator of $\{E\mid\varnothing\}$ the second; their option collections
are sets. The assertions also hold for the empty set. These are applications
of the set-cut property, not an assertion of an infimum or supremum. No
single $\delta$ works for the entire proper class $\No^{>0}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Common monomial divisibility \src{01, 02, 05}]\label{odg:thm:commondivisor}
For every set $S\subseteq\Pi$ there is $\delta>0$ such that
\[
 \omega^\delta\mid s\quad\text{and}\quad
 s/\omega^\delta\in\Pi\qquad(s\in S).
\]
In particular, each nonzero element of $\Pi$ is a product of two nonzero
elements of $\Pi$, both nonunits of $\Oz$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The union of the supports of the elements of $S$ is a set $E$ of strictly
positive surreal exponents. Choose $\delta$ as in
\cref{odg:lem:setbounds}. Dividing by $\omega^\delta$ shifts every exponent
$e$ to $e-\delta>0$, preserving the reverse well-order of each support.
The quotient is therefore in $\Pi$. For a nonzero $s$, both factors are
nonzero elements of positive degree, and neither is a unit.
\end{proof}

Even the series $\sum_{k\geq1}\omega^{1/k}$ has such a divisor. The
positive exponent $\delta$ may be infinitesimal relative to every real
$1/k$, for example $\delta=1/\omega$. This is why the proof cannot be copied
into a Hahn ring with exponent group $\R$.

\begin{corollary}[The purely infinite ideal \src{01, 02, 05}]\label{odg:cor:Pglobal}
The ideal $\Pi$ satisfies $\Pi^2=\Pi$, and each element of $\Pi$ is a single
product of two elements of $\Pi$. It is not generated as an ideal by any set
of elements; in particular it is not finitely generated. Consequently
$\Pi/\Pi^2=0$ although $\Pi\ne0$, $\Oz/\Pi^k\cong\Z$ for every ordinary
$k\ge1$, and the $\Pi$-adic completion of $\Oz$ is $\Z$. No nonzero element
of $\Pi$ is a finite product of irreducibles of $\Oz$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The inclusion $\Pi^2\subseteq\Pi$ follows from the ideal property. For
$j\ne0$, the factorization in \cref{odg:thm:commondivisor} has both factors
in $\Pi$, proving the reverse inclusion. Hence $\Pi^k=\Pi$ for all $k\ge1$ and
the inverse system $\Oz/\Pi^k$ is constantly $\Z$.

Suppose a set $S\subseteq\Pi$ generated $\Pi$. Choose $\delta>0$
strictly below every exponent in the supports of its elements. Each finite
$\Oz$-linear combination of elements of $S$ has support strictly above
$\delta$: multiplication by an element of $\Oz$ only adds nonnegative
exponents. But $\omega^{\delta/2}\in\Pi$ does not have such a support.

Finally, if $j=p_1\cdots p_k\ne0$ with every $p_i$ irreducible, then
$0=\ct(j)=\prod_{i=1}^k\ct(p_i)$ would put some $p_i$ in $\Pi$, and that
factor would be reducible by \cref{odg:thm:commondivisor}.
\end{proof}

The factorization $j=2(j/2)$ already proves that a nonzero $j\in\Pi$ is
reducible, even in a fixed exponent workspace; the stronger content of
\cref{odg:thm:commondivisor} is a common \emph{infinite monomial} divisor for
an entire set, with all quotients still in $\Pi$. The vanishing of
$\Pi/\Pi^2$ does not say that the infinite part is small; it says that this
particular quotient cannot detect it. The hypotheses of a finitely generated
Nakayama argument are not present \src{02}.

\begin{corollary}[Nilpotent tests \src{02}]\label{odg:cor:nilpotent}
Let $\phi:\Oz\to R$ be a ring homomorphism and $\mathfrak n\subseteq R$ a
nilpotent ideal with $\phi(\Pi)\subseteq\mathfrak n$. Then $\phi(\Pi)=0$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
If $\mathfrak n^N=0$, then
$\phi(\Pi)=\phi(\Pi^N)\subseteq\mathfrak n^N=0$ by \cref{odg:cor:Pglobal}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Factorization}\label{odg:sub:factorization}

\Cref{odg:cor:Pglobal} is an explicit failure of atomic factorization, not
merely a failure of uniqueness of factorization. It leaves room for
nonconstant prime elements having nonzero constant coefficient.

\begin{proposition}[Irreducibles \src{01, 02, 05}]\label{odg:prop:irreduciblect}
The constant irreducibles of $\Oz$ are exactly the ordinary signed primes.
Every nonconstant irreducible has constant coefficient $1$ or $-1$.
The latter condition is not sufficient for irreducibility. In particular,
$\Oz$ is neither atomic nor a unique factorization domain.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Constant irreducibles are classified by \cref{odg:prop:finitedivisors}. If a
nonconstant element $a$ has $|\ct(a)|>1$, choose an ordinary prime $p$
dividing its constant coefficient. Then $a=p(a/p)$ by
\cref{odg:thm:finitequotients} is a factorization into two nonunits: the
second factor remains nonconstant. If $\ct(a)=0$, the factorization
$a=2(a/2)$ has the same property. This leaves only constant coefficients
$\pm1$. Finally, $(\omega+1)^2$ has constant coefficient $1$ and
$\omega^2-1=(\omega-1)(\omega+1)$ has constant coefficient $-1$; both are
reducible.
\end{proof}

In particular $\omega$ has no finite irreducible factorization, not for lack
of factors: it is divisible by every ordinary positive integer and by every
$\omega^\delta$ with $0<\delta<1$. Genuine infinite primes exist. The series
$1+\sum_{k\ge1}\omega^{1/k}$ is prime by earlier results reviewed by
L'Innocente and Mantova \cite[Section 1.3]{LM} (irreducibility by Berarducci,
primality by Pitteloud, and primality in $\Oz$ by
Biljakovi\'c--Kochetov--Kuhlmann, as credited there), and
$\omega^{\sqrt2}+\omega+1$ is prime by their Theorem~B \cite[Theorem B]{LM},
which answers a question of Gonshor. Their general theory uses substantially
finer invariants than the degree employed here; no result of this report
depends on these primality theorems.

\subsection{Clearing arbitrary set-sized families of denominators}

\begin{theorem}[Global monomial clearing \src{01, 02, 05}]\label{odg:thm:fractions}
For every set $S\subseteq\No$ there is $h>0$ such that
\[
 \omega^h\in\Pi,\qquad \omega^h x\in\Pi\quad(x\in S).
\]
Consequently $\Frac(\Oz)=\No$, where the equality means that every surreal
is a quotient of two omnific integers with nonzero denominator.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Take the set $E$ obtained by uniting the supports of all elements of $S$.
Choose $h>0$ strictly greater than every $-e$ for $e\in E$.
Multiplication by $\omega^h$ shifts every exponent to $h+e>0$, so all
shifted normal forms belong to $\Pi$. Applying this to $S=\{x\}$
gives $x=(\omega^h x)/\omega^h$. The reverse inclusion holds because $\Oz$
is a subring of the field $\No$.
\end{proof}

A support unbounded below in a chosen real exponent group is still a set of
surreal exponents and can be shifted above zero by a new, larger surreal
exponent. The theorem does not assert that the required monomial lies in the
original fixed Hahn workspace.

\begin{corollary}[Projective clearing and common multiples \src{01, 02, 05}]\label{odg:cor:projectiveclear}
Every point of $\mathbb P^n(\No)$ has homogeneous coordinates in
$\Pi^{n+1}$; more generally, any set-sized collection of coordinate tuples
can be cleared by a single positive monomial, and a common zero of
homogeneous polynomials remains a common zero after this rescaling. Every
set $S$ of nonzero omnific integers has a nonzero common multiple; in fact
some nonzero $M\in\Oz$ lies in $a^k\Oz$ for every $a\in S$ and ordinary
$k\ge1$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Apply \cref{odg:thm:fractions} to the set of all coordinates and multiply
all tuples by the resulting nonzero scalar; homogeneity preserves the
equations. For the last claim apply \cref{odg:thm:fractions} to the set
$\{a^{-k}:a\in S,\ k\ge1\}$ and take $M=\omega^h$; then $M/a^k\in\Pi$.
\end{proof}

This is projective \emph{representability}, not primitivity: the coordinates
of such a representative always have a common nonunit divisor, and
\cref{odg:sec:primitive} shows that some real projective directions have no
primitive omnific representative at all. The common-multiple statement is an
existence theorem, not an algorithm; nor is there one omnific integer
divisible by every member of the proper class of nonzero omnific integers.

\subsection{Division with remainder is not a terminating Euclidean algorithm}

\begin{proposition}[Ordered division \src{01, 02, 05}]\label{odg:prop:division}
For $a,b\in\Oz$ with $b>0$, there are unique $q,r\in\Oz$ such that
$a=bq+r$ and $0\leq r<b$, namely $q=\lfloor a/b\rfloor_{\Oz}$ and
$r=a-bq$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The floor inequalities of \cref{odg:thm:floor}, multiplied by $b$, give the
remainder bounds. Conversely those bounds force $q\leq a/b<q+1$, so floor
uniqueness determines $q$ and then $r$.
\end{proof}

Thus the familiar division step exists. Termination of repeated division
is the missing ingredient: the positive order on $\Oz$ is not well founded.

\begin{example}[A Euclidean algorithm that never stops \src{01, 02, 05}]\label{odg:ex:euclid}
Let $\rho=\sqrt2-1\in(0,1)$ and start with $r_0=\sqrt2\,\omega$,
$r_1=\omega$. The first quotient is $1$, the remainder is $r_2=\rho\,\omega$,
and every subsequent quotient is $2$:
\[
 r_k=\rho^{k-1}\omega\qquad(k\geq1),\qquad
 r_{k-1}=2r_k+r_{k+1}\quad(k\ge2),
\]
because $1-2\rho=\rho^2$ and $1/\rho=\sqrt2+1\in(2,3)$. Every remainder is
positive and infinite, so the ordinary finite Euclidean procedure never
terminates. (Source 05 starts at $(\omega,\rho\omega)$ and sees the same tail.)
A decreasing remainder is not a well-founded measure here.
\end{example}

\begin{theorem}[Pairs without a gcd \src{02}, generalizing \src{01}]\label{odg:thm:nogcd}
Let $0\ne t\in\Pi$ and let $r\in\R\setminus\Q$. The pair $(t,rt)$ has no
greatest common divisor in $\Oz$, in the divisibility sense. In particular,
$\Oz$ is neither a GCD domain nor a B\'ezout domain.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Take any common divisor $d\in\Oz$. Since $t\ne0$, also $d\ne0$, and there
are nonzero $u,v\in\Oz$ with $t=du$ and $rt=dv$. Cancellation in $\No$
gives $v=ru$. Taking constants in $\BR$ yields $\ct(v)=r\ct(u)$ with both
constants ordinary integers, so irrationality of $r$ forces both to be zero:
$u,v\in\Pi$.

By \cref{odg:thm:commondivisor} applied to $\{u,v\}$, choose $\delta>0$
with $u/\omega^\delta,v/\omega^\delta\in\Pi$. Then $d'=d\,\omega^\delta$ is
another common divisor of $t,rt$, and $d\mid d'$. But $d'\nmid d$, since the
quotient would be $\omega^{-\delta}\notin\Oz$. Thus every common divisor can
be strictly enlarged in the divisibility order, and none is a gcd. A
B\'ezout domain would supply a gcd for each pair, namely a generator of the
ideal the pair generates.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[A second route for the pair $(\sqrt2\,\omega,\omega)$ \src{01}]\label{odg:rem:nogcd01}
Source 01 proves the instance $t=\omega$, $r=\sqrt2$ by degrees alone. Let
$d\ne0$ be a common divisor, $\theta=\ddeg(d)$; then $0\le\theta\le1$. If
$\theta=1$, both quotients are degree-zero omnific integers, hence nonzero
ordinary integers, and their ratio would make $\sqrt2$ rational. So
$\theta<1$; with $\eta=(\theta+1)/2$, $\omega^\eta$ is a common divisor (both
quotients have exponent $1-\eta>0$) that does not divide $d$, since its degree
is larger. This route needs only the exponent $(\theta+1)/2$, so it works in
any workspace with a divisible exponent group, whereas the general
\cref{odg:thm:nogcd} uses the full-class shift of
\cref{odg:thm:commondivisor}.
\end{remark}

\Cref{odg:ex:euclid} shows at once why numerical descent is insufficient and
why a division algorithm need not produce a gcd: its input has none.

\subsection{Non-Noetherian and non-normal behavior}

\begin{proposition}[Chains and integral closure \src{01, 02, 05}]\label{odg:prop:notnormal}
There is a strictly ascending chain of principal ideals
\[
 (\omega)\subsetneq(\omega^{1/2})\subsetneq
 (\omega^{1/4})\subsetneq\cdots,
\]
so $\Oz$ satisfies neither the ascending chain condition on principal
ideals nor the Noetherian condition. The full ring $\Oz$ is not integrally
closed in its fraction field: both $\sqrt2$ and $\sqrt{\omega^2+1}$ are
integral over $\Oz$, lie in $\Frac(\Oz)$, and are not in $\Oz$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Each generator is the square of the next, so the inclusions hold. They are
strict because the reverse quotients $\omega^{-1/2^{k+1}}$ have negative
exponents. The number $\sqrt2=(\sqrt2\,\omega)/\omega$ satisfies $T^2-2=0$
and is not an integer \src{02, 05}. Since $\No$ is real closed, let
$r=\sqrt{\omega^2+1}>0$; it lies in $\Frac(\Oz)=\No$ and satisfies
$T^2-(\omega^2+1)=0$. If $r\in\Oz$, then $(r-\omega)(r+\omega)=1$ makes both
factors units, hence elements of $\{1,-1\}$ with equal signs; their difference
$2\omega$ would be $0$, a contradiction \src{01}.
\end{proof}

In particular the full omnific ring is not a UFD or a PID. Its factorization
theory cannot be replaced by an ordinary Euclidean algorithm or by an
unstated gcd assumption. The modern theory in \cite{LM} is substantially
subtler. Conway's refinement question asks whether $ab=cd$ in $\Oz$ always
admits $a=ef$, $b=gh$, $c=eg$, $d=fh$ with $e,f,g,h\in\Oz$; this is a
refinement property, not atomicity or the existence of gcds, and none of the
preceding arguments refutes it. \Cref{odg:app:repo} records why no current
open-status claim about it is made.

\section{Equational transfer and linear Diophantine equations}\label{odg:sec:transfer}

\subsection{Existence is exactly ordinary existence}

For a finite family of polynomials $F_1,\ldots,F_r$ over a ring $R$, write
$\V_R(F_1,\ldots,F_r)$ for its simultaneous zero set in $R^n$. All
exponents of the variables are ordinary nonnegative integers and only
finitely many monomials occur; equations with variable exponents, surreal
exponentiation, or class-sized sums are not polynomial systems in this sense.

\begin{theorem}[Retraction theorem for equations \src{01, 02, 05}]\label{odg:thm:transfer}
Let $F_1,\ldots,F_r\in\Z[X_1,\ldots,X_n]$. Coordinatewise constant
coefficient gives a retraction, with the inclusion as section,
\[
 \V_{\Oz}(F_1,\ldots,F_r)\longrightarrow
 \V_\Z(F_1,\ldots,F_r).
\]
In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{odg:eq:existencetransfer}
 \V_{\Oz}(F_1,\ldots,F_r)\ne\varnothing
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \V_\Z(F_1,\ldots,F_r)\ne\varnothing.
\end{equation}
The same equivalence holds for positive existential formulas in the ring
language, evaluated at ordinary integer parameters.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For every polynomial $F$ over $\Z$ and $\mathbf x\in\Oz^n$,
$\ct(F(\mathbf x))=F(\ct(x_1),\ldots,\ct(x_n))$, because $\ct$ is a ring
homomorphism fixing all coefficients. Thus the constant tuple of an omnific
solution is an integer solution. Conversely, an integer solution is already
an omnific solution, and applying $\ct$ to it does nothing.

A positive existential formula is built from equalities using finite
conjunction, finite disjunction, and existential quantification. Ring
homomorphisms preserve such formulas, by induction on their formation, with
witnesses mapped coordinatewise. Apply this to $\ct$ and to the inclusion of
$\Z$ in $\Oz$.
\end{proof}

The theorem answers a question about existence, not about cardinalities or
complete solution sets. For $\mathbf a\in\V_\Z$, its
\emph{constant-coefficient fiber} is
\begin{equation}\label{odg:eq:fiber}
 \V_{\mathbf a}(\Oz)=\{\mathbf a+\mathbf u:\mathbf u\in\Pi^n,\
 F_j(\mathbf a+\mathbf u)=0\ \text{for all }j\}.
\end{equation}
For $Y=X^2$ the fiber above $(0,0)$ already contains $(t,t^2)$ for every
$t\in\Pi$. These are not infinitesimal deformations: every nonzero coordinate
of $\mathbf u$ is infinite. We call a system \emph{rigid} when every fiber is
its ordinary base point, that is, when $\V_{\Oz}=\V_\Z$ \src{02}.

\begin{corollary}[Hilbert's tenth problem \src{01, 02, 05}]\label{odg:cor:H10}
There is no algorithm deciding, from a finite list of ordinary integer
polynomials, whether it has a common zero in $\Oz$. The yes-instances are
exactly the ordinary-integer yes-instances of Hilbert's tenth problem, and
form a computably enumerable set that is complete for computably enumerable
sets under many-one reductions.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Equation~\eqref{odg:eq:existencetransfer} identifies the two decision
problems on exactly the same finite inputs. The undecidability and
completeness of the ordinary problem are the Davis--Putnam--Robinson--%
Matiyasevich (MRDP) theorem and its standard consequences
\cite{Matiyasevich,MatiyasevichBook,Poonen,Scholarpedia}; one passes between
the natural-number and integer formulations by the four-square
representation of nonnegative integers, and conjunctions are combined as sums
of squares. Enumerating ordinary integer tuples semidecides the omnific
yes-instances; no enumeration of the proper class $\Oz$ is involved.
\end{proof}

This corollary concerns \emph{ordinary integer coefficients}. A decision
problem allowing arbitrary omnific coefficients requires an explicit
finite representation scheme and is a different question
(\cref{odg:sec:coefficients}).

\subsection{Why signs and nonvanishing do not transfer}

Consider $(X+1)^2-2(Y+1)^2=0$. It has no solution in $\N^2$, since $X+1$
and $Y+1$ would be positive integers with ratio $\sqrt2$. However,
$X=\sqrt2\,\omega-1$, $Y=\omega-1$ is a solution with both coordinates
positive in $\Oz$. Its constant tuple is $(-1,-1)$, an ordinary solution of
the equation without positivity constraints.

\begin{proposition}[Retraction closure \src{01, 02, 05}]\label{odg:prop:notdiophantine}
If $D\subseteq\Oz^n$ is defined by a positive existential ring formula with
ordinary integer parameters, then $\mathbf x\in D$ implies
$\ct(\mathbf x)\in D$. Consequently none of
\[
 \Oz\setminus\{0\},\qquad \Oz^{>0},\qquad \Oz^{\geq0}
\]
is so definable.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Apply $\ct$ to the free variables and to all existential witnesses, as in
\cref{odg:thm:transfer}. But $\omega$ is nonzero and positive while
$\ct(\omega)=0$; and $\omega-1>0$ whereas $\ct(\omega-1)=-1<0$.
\end{proof}

Thus ``nonzero'' cannot be harmlessly added to an equational transfer
statement, and the retraction is not an elementary embedding of ordered
rings. Encoding nonvanishing by $XY=1$ would restrict $X$ to $\{1,-1\}$,
not to all nonzero omnific integers. The qualification on coefficients
matters as well: a predicate with a fixed infinite parameter need not be
preserved by a map that changes that parameter. For coefficients outside
$\Z$, specialization gives a necessary residual system whose solvability need
not lift (\cref{odg:sec:coefficients}).

\subsection{A complete solution criterion for linear systems}

\begin{theorem}[Smith normal form over the omnific integers \src{01, 02, 05}]\label{odg:thm:smith}
Let $M\in\Z^{m\times n}$ and $\mathbf b\in\Oz^m$. Choose unimodular
integer matrices $U,V$ with
\[
 UMV=\diag(d_1,\ldots,d_r,0),\qquad d_i>0,
\]
in rectangular Smith normal form, and put $\mathbf c=U\mathbf b$.
Then $M\mathbf x=\mathbf b$ is solvable in $\Oz^n$ if and only if
\begin{equation}\label{odg:eq:smithcriterion}
 d_i\mid\ct(c_i)\quad(1\leq i\leq r),
 \qquad c_i=0\quad(r<i\leq m).
\end{equation}
All solutions are $\mathbf x=V\mathbf y$, where
$y_i=c_i/d_i$ for $1\le i\le r$ and $y_{r+1},\ldots,y_n\in\Oz$ are
arbitrary.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Unimodular integer matrices and their inverses act on omnific tuples.
The substitution $\mathbf x=V\mathbf y$ transforms the system into
$\diag(d_1,\ldots,d_r,0)\mathbf y=\mathbf c$. For each nonzero diagonal entry,
division is possible precisely under \cref{odg:thm:finitequotients}. Zero rows
require the full equation $c_i=0$, not merely its constant coefficient. The
remaining coordinates are unconstrained.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Ordinary right-hand sides \src{05}]\label{odg:thm:linear}
Let $M\in\Z^{m\times n}$ and $\mathbf b\in\Z^m$. All solutions in $\Oz^n$
of $M\mathbf x=\mathbf b$ are exactly
\[
 \mathbf x=\mathbf a+\mathbf u,
 \qquad \mathbf a\in\Z^n,\quad M\mathbf a=\mathbf b,
 \quad \mathbf u\in\Pi^n,\quad M\mathbf u=0.
\]
If $\mathbf v_1,\ldots,\mathbf v_e\in\Q^n$ is a basis of
$\ker(M:\Q^n\to\Q^m)$, the last condition is equivalent to
$\mathbf u=\sum_{j}h_j\mathbf v_j$ with uniquely determined $h_j\in\Pi$.
In particular, if $M$ has full column rank, all solutions are ordinary.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Separate each coordinate into its integer constant and its member of $\Pi$.
The integer and positive-support components of $M\mathbf x-\mathbf b$
vanish separately. Gaussian elimination over $\Q$ works with entries in the
$\Q$-vector space $\Pi$, since nonzero rationals act invertibly on it;
applying the same row operations and free-variable parametrization as over
$\Q$ gives the basis description and its uniqueness.
\end{proof}

For example, every solution of $2x+3y=1$ is $x=-1+3t$, $y=1-2t$ with
$t\in\Oz$, and every solution of $6x+10y=14$ is $x=4+5t$, $y=-1-3t$ with
$t\in\Oz$. The same formulas as over $\Z$ work, but the parameter class is
larger. The equation $2x=\omega+1$ has no solution, whereas $2x=\omega+2$ has
the unique solution $x=\omega/2+1$.

\section{Rigidity of constant products: binary forms, Pell, norms}\label{odg:sec:rigidity}

\subsection{The constant-product principle}

\begin{lemma}[Constant products \src{01, 02, 05}]\label{odg:lem:constantproduct}
If $b_1,\ldots,b_s\in\BC$ and $b_1\cdots b_s=c\in\C^\times$, then every
$b_i$ belongs to $\C^\times$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Each $b_i$ has inverse $c^{-1}\prod_{j\ne i}b_j$ in $\BC$; apply
\cref{odg:prop:units}. Equivalently, their nonnegative degrees have sum
zero, so every degree is zero.
\end{proof}

The importance of $\BC$ is that linear factors may have irrational or
complex coefficients. They need not themselves be omnific integers, but they
have no negative exponents, so the unit argument applies.

\begin{theorem}[Exact fibers of decomposable equations \src{02}; case (b) \src{01}]\label{odg:thm:decomposable}
Let
\[
 F(\mathbf X)=\prod_{j=1}^{s}L_j(\mathbf X)^{e_j},\qquad
 L_j(\mathbf X)=\beta_j+\ell_j(\mathbf X),
\]
where $\beta_j\in\C$, each $\ell_j$ is a complex linear form in
$n$ variables, and each $e_j\ge1$ is an ordinary integer (a constant factor
can be absorbed into one $L_j$). Fix $c\in\C^\times$ and put
\[
 \mathcal K=\{\mathbf v\in\R^n:\ell_j(\mathbf v)=0\text{ for every }j\}.
\]
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)},leftmargin=*]
\item The solutions of $F(\mathbf x)=c$ in $\Oz^n$ are exactly
\begin{equation}\label{odg:eq:decomposablefiber}
 \mathbf x=\mathbf a+\mathbf u,\qquad \mathbf a\in\Z^n,
 \quad F(\mathbf a)=c,
 \quad \mathbf u\in\Pi^n,
 \quad \ell_j(\mathbf u)=0\ \text{for all }j.
\end{equation}
Equivalently, at each exponent the real coefficient vector of $\mathbf u$
lies in $\mathcal K$; if $\mathbf v_1,\ldots,\mathbf v_e$ is a real basis of
$\mathcal K$, the fiber above $\mathbf a$ is
$\mathbf a+\{\sum_k t_k\mathbf v_k:t_k\in\Pi\}$. If $\mathcal K=0$, all
omnific solutions are ordinary.
\item If the linear parts $\ell_1,\ldots,\ell_s$ have column rank $n$ over
$\C$, then every solution $\mathbf x\in\BC^n$ of $F(\mathbf x)=c$ lies in
$\C^n$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For $\mathbf x\in\BC^n$, every $L_j(\mathbf x)$ is in $\BC$, and if
$F(\mathbf x)=c\ne0$, \cref{odg:lem:constantproduct} makes each
$L_j(\mathbf x)$ constant.

(a) Write $\mathbf x=\mathbf a+\mathbf u$ coordinatewise with
$\mathbf a\in\Z^n$ and $\mathbf u\in\Pi^n$. Then
$L_j(\mathbf x)=L_j(\mathbf a)+\ell_j(\mathbf u)$, where the first summand is
constant and the second lies in $\Pi_{\C}$; uniqueness of normal forms
forces $\ell_j(\mathbf u)=0$, and specialization gives $F(\mathbf a)=c$.
Conversely those conditions give $L_j(\mathbf a+\mathbf u)=L_j(\mathbf a)$ and
hence $F(\mathbf a+\mathbf u)=c$. At each exponent of the (set-sized,
reverse-well-ordered) union of the coordinate supports, the equations
$\ell_j(\mathbf u)=0$ say exactly that the real coefficient vector
$\mathbf u_\gamma\in\R^n$ at that exponent satisfies $\ell_j(\mathbf u_\gamma)=0$
for all $j$, that is, lies in $\mathcal K$. Expressing each coefficient vector in the basis
$\mathbf v_k$ produces $t_k$ whose supports lie in the same union; if
$\mathcal K=0$, every coefficient vector vanishes.

(b) Select $n$ of the $\ell_j$ whose coefficient matrix is invertible over
$\C$, subtract the constant terms and solve this ordinary linear system:
all coordinates are complex constants.
\end{proof}

The kernel hypothesis in (a) is exactly what matters. A single-factor form
can have infinite fibers,
\[
 (x-y)^2=1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x=y\pm1,\qquad y\in\Oz,
\]
and the two-variable equation $X^d=1$ forces $X$ to be constant but says
nothing about $Y$. On the zero level, even independent factors permit
infinite points, since the unit argument cannot be applied to a product
equal to zero: $(\sqrt2\,\omega)^2-2\omega^2=0$.

\subsection{Binary forms, Pell equations, and conics}

\begin{theorem}[Binary-form rigidity \src{05}, generalizing \src{01, 02}]\label{odg:thm:binary}
Let $F\in\C[X,Y]$ be homogeneous with at least two distinct projective
linear factors over $\C$, and let $c\in\C^\times$. If $x,y\in\BC$ satisfy
$F(x,y)=c$, then $x,y\in\C$. In particular, for $F\in\Z[X,Y]$ and
$c\in\Z\setminus\{0\}$,
\[
 \{(x,y)\in\Oz^2:F(x,y)=c\}
 =\{(x,y)\in\Z^2:F(x,y)=c\}.
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Factor $F=\alpha\prod_{j=1}^dL_j$ over $\C$ with $\alpha\in\C^\times$ and
nonzero linear forms repeated according to multiplicity. Two distinct
projective factors have linearly independent coefficient vectors, so the
linear parts have rank two; apply \cref{odg:thm:decomposable}(b). Constant
omnific coordinates are integers.
\end{proof}

Sources 01 and 02 proved the statement for $F\in\Z[X,Y]$, $c\in\Z$ and
$\Oz$-points; the complex form is 05's. The hypothesis excludes a pure power
$\alpha L^d$, and that exclusion is necessary: fixing $L(x,y)$ to a constant
leaves a free infinite direction in the kernel of $L$. The requirement
$c\ne0$ is also necessary, since zero levels contain lines through the
origin. The hypothesis is weaker than squarefreeness in degree at least
three: $X^2Y$ qualifies.

\begin{corollary}[Thue levels and equal powers \src{01, 02, 05}]\label{odg:cor:thue}
If an integer binary form has at least two distinct projective factors,
its nonzero ordinary constant levels have no additional omnific points.
This applies to every irreducible binary form over $\Q$ of degree at least
two, hence to Thue equations, and to $x^d-y^d=c$ with $d\ge2$ and
$c\in\Z\setminus\{0\}$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
In characteristic zero an irreducible binary form of degree at least two has
distinct projective roots over $\C$, and $X^d-Y^d$ has distinct linear
factors. Apply \cref{odg:thm:binary}.
\end{proof}

The conclusion identifies the omnific solution class with the classical
integer solution set; it is not a new proof of ordinary finiteness, gives no
bound for the ordinary solutions, and supplies no algorithm for them. No
finiteness theorem of Thue or Baker is used.

\begin{corollary}[Pell rigidity \src{01, 02, 05}]\label{odg:cor:pell}
For ordinary integers $D\ne0$ and $c\ne0$, every solution in $\Oz^2$ of
$x^2-Dy^2=c$ is an ordinary integer solution. In particular
\[
 \{(u,v)\in\Oz^2:u^2-2v^2=1\}=\{(u,v)\in\Z^2:u^2-2v^2=1\}.
\]
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Over $\C$, the factors $X-\sqrt D\,Y$ and $X+\sqrt D\,Y$ are distinct
because $D\ne0$. Apply \cref{odg:thm:binary}.
\end{proof}

Source 02 states this corollary for $D>0$ only; its proof, like those of 01
and 05, uses only $D\ne0$, and the conclusion holds whether or not $D$ is a
square. ``Rigid'' means no new points, not a finite number of old points:
the classical Pell equation has ordinary solutions of arbitrarily large
finite size, but no single infinite omnific solution, and there is no ``Pell
solution at index $\omega$'' obtained by substituting an omnific index into a
finite recurrence. This is a first warning against treating $\Oz$ as an
elementary extension of ordinary integer arithmetic.

\begin{example}[The full class for $D=2$, $c=1$ \src{05}]\label{odg:ex:pell2}
Let $X_k+Y_k\sqrt2=(3+2\sqrt2)^k$ for ordinary $k\ge0$. Every omnific
solution of $x^2-2y^2=1$ is $(\pm X_k,\pm Y_k)$ with independent signs.
Indeed, \cref{odg:cor:pell} first makes $x,y$ ordinary. After changing signs
suppose $x,y>0$; then $y\ge2$, since $y=1$ would need $x^2=3$.
Multiplication by $3-2\sqrt2$ gives the integer Pell pair
$X=3x-4y$, $Y=3y-2x$ with $X>0$ (as $x>\sqrt2\,y>4y/3$) and $0\le Y<y$ (as
$x^2=2y^2+1\le9y^2/4$ and $x>y$). Ordinary descent on $y$ reaches $(1,0)$,
and reversing it gives the asserted powers.
\end{example}

In the full surreal field the contrast is visible without solving a
recurrence \src{01}. For $D>0$, $c\ne0$ and $s\in\No^\times$, the pair
$x=(s+c/s)/2$, $y=(s-c/s)/(2\sqrt D)$ satisfies $x^2-Dy^2=c$. Taking
$s=\omega$ produces negative-exponent terms, so neither coordinate is an
omnific integer, and discarding those terms changes the norm from $c$ to
zero. A surreal solution cannot be turned into an omnific solution by
unverified truncation.

\begin{proposition}[The zero Pell fiber \src{01}]\label{odg:prop:zeropell}
If $D$ is a positive nonsquare ordinary integer, the solutions of
$x^2-Dy^2=0$ in $\Oz^2$ are exactly $(\pm\sqrt D\,t,t)$ with $t\in\Pi$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Factor in $\No$ to obtain $x=\pm\sqrt D\,y$. Write $y=m+j$ with $m\in\Z$ and
$j\in\Pi$. The constant coefficient of $x$ is $\pm\sqrt D\,m$, which is an
integer only if $m=0$. Conversely, $\Pi$ is an $\R$-vector space, so all
displayed pairs belong to $\Oz^2$ and solve the equation.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Nonsingular central conics \src{05}]\label{odg:cor:conics}
Let $F(X,Y)=q(X,Y)+\ell(X,Y)+d\in\R[X,Y]$, where the quadratic form $q$ has
rank two, and let $\mathbf h\in\R^2$ be the center, so that
$F(\mathbf h+\mathbf u)=q(\mathbf u)+F(\mathbf h)$. If $F(\mathbf h)\ne0$,
every solution of $F=0$ in $\BR^2$ is constant; for integer coefficients,
every omnific solution is ordinary.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
A rank-two binary quadratic form has two distinct projective factors over
$\C$. The translated coordinates $\mathbf u=(x,y)-\mathbf h$ lie in $\BR^2$
and satisfy $q(\mathbf u)=-F(\mathbf h)\ne0$. Apply \cref{odg:thm:binary}
and translate back.
\end{proof}

This covers ellipses and hyperbolas with a nonzero centered level.
Parabolas behave differently: $Y=X^2$ has the infinite family $(t,t^2)$.

\begin{remark}[Power differences with a common factor \src{01}]\label{odg:rem:powers}
Source 01 proved that every omnific solution of $x^m-y^n=c\ne0$ is ordinary
when $d=\gcd(m,n)>1$: the binary form $X^d-Y^d$ in $X=x^{m/d}$, $Y=y^{n/d}$ is
rigid by \cref{odg:thm:binary}, and an element of $\Oz$ with an ordinary
constant positive power is ordinary (by degree additivity if the power is
nonzero, by the domain property otherwise). Source 01 stated explicitly that
this argument does not classify coprime cases such as $x^2-y^3=1$. All
exponent pairs are covered by \cref{odg:thm:separated} of source 05.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Number-field norm equations}

\begin{theorem}[Norm-form rigidity \src{01, 02}]\label{odg:thm:norm}
Let $K/\Q$ be a number field of degree $d$, let $e_1,\ldots,e_d$ be a
$\Q$-basis, and let $N(\mathbf X)=N_{K/\Q}\bigl(\sum_{j=1}^d e_jX_j\bigr)$.
For every $c\in\Q^\times$, every solution $\mathbf x\in\Oz^d$ of
$N(\mathbf x)=c$ has all coordinates in $\Z$. If the basis consists of
algebraic integers (for example a power basis of an algebraic integer
$\theta$ generating $K$, or an integral basis), then $N\in\Z[\mathbf X]$,
and the statement applies to the usual integer norm equations.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The $d$ embeddings $\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_d:K\to\C$ give
\[
 N(\mathbf X)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}
 \Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sigma_i(e_j)X_j\Bigr).
\]
The embedding matrix $(\sigma_i(e_j))$ is invertible: for a power basis
$1,\theta,\ldots,\theta^{d-1}$ of a primitive element of the separable
extension $K/\Q$ it is the Vandermonde matrix of the distinct conjugates of
$\theta$, and a basis change over $\Q$ preserves invertibility. The forms
therefore have zero common kernel and \cref{odg:thm:decomposable} applies.
If the $e_j$ are algebraic integers, each coefficient of $N$ is fixed by the
Galois group of a normal closure, hence rational, and is an integer
polynomial in conjugates of the $e_j$, hence an algebraic integer; so it is
an ordinary integer \src{02}.
\end{proof}

For example, consider the norm form for $K=\Q(\sqrt[3]{2})$:
\[
 N(X+Y\sqrt[3]{2}+Z\sqrt[3]{4})=X^3+2Y^3+4Z^3-6XYZ.
\]
For every $c\in\Z\setminus\{0\}$, every omnific solution of
$X^3+2Y^3+4Z^3-6XYZ=c$ is an ordinary integer triple. This is a
three-variable rigidity statement on an unbounded real surface, unlike the
indefinite quadratic forms of \cref{odg:sec:quadratic}: dimension alone does
not force infinite omnific points. The identity follows from the product over
the three conjugates or from the determinant of multiplication by
$X+Y\theta+Z\theta^2$; the suites of 01 and 02 check it exactly.

\subsection{An algebraic-geometric formulation}

Let $V$ be an affine complex variety whose coordinate ring $\C[V]$ is
generated, as a $\C$-algebra, by units. Every $\BC$-point of $V$ is
then a constant $\C$-point \src{01, 02}. Indeed, a $\C$-algebra map
$\C[V]\to\BC$ sends each generating unit to an element of
$\BC^\times=\C^\times$, so its entire image lies in $\C$. This includes
closed subvarieties of an algebraic torus $(\C^\times)^r$ in their
very-affine coordinate presentation. The norm argument is a concrete
linear-coordinate instance: a nonzero product relation forces its factors
into a constant torus. It is not a claim that every affine variety is
constant over $\BC$; the affine line has the full class $\BC$ of points.

\section{Euler derivations: separated powers and unimodular Fermat}\label{odg:sec:derivations}

Binary forms can be factored into linear forms, but equations such as
$Y^2=X^3+17$ cannot be handled that way. The following local tool, due to
source 05, gives short arguments without any finite-support hypothesis.

\subsection{Support-preserving Euler derivations}

Fix a set-sized ordered divisible group $H\subseteq\No$ and a $\Q$-linear
map $\lambda:H\to\R$. On the real or complex Hahn field with exponents in
$H$, define
\begin{equation}\label{odg:eq:euler}
 \partial_\lambda\Bigl(\sum_{h\in H}a_h\omega^h\Bigr)
   =\sum_{h\in H}\lambda(h)a_h\omega^h.
\end{equation}
This is a local, freely chosen Euler derivation. It is not a canonical
surreal derivation and not the Berarducci--Mantova derivation.

\begin{lemma}[Euler derivations \src{05}]\label{odg:lem:derivation}
The map $\partial_\lambda$ is a derivation, kills every real or complex
constant, and never introduces a new supported exponent; so
$\ddeg(\partial_\lambda f)\le\ddeg(f)$ whenever $\partial_\lambda f\ne0$.
It restricts to derivations of $\mathcal B_{\R,H}$ and $\mathcal B_{\C,H}$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The new support is a subset of the old one and is thus reverse well
ordered. Additivity is immediate, and $\lambda(0)=0$ kills constants. For a
fixed exponent $h$, the coefficient convolution is finite and
$\lambda(a+b)=\lambda(a)+\lambda(b)$, so
\[
 [\omega^h]\partial_\lambda(fg)
 =\sum_{a+b=h}(\lambda(a)+\lambda(b))f_a g_b
 =[\omega^h]\bigl((\partial_\lambda f)g+f(\partial_\lambda g)\bigr).
\]
This proves the Leibniz rule coefficientwise; no interchange of conditionally
convergent sums is involved. The degree and restriction statements follow
from support containment.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Detection of nonconstants \src{05}]\label{odg:lem:detection}
Every nonconstant element of a Hahn field with exponent group $H$ is
detected by some $\partial_\lambda$. If $f\in\mathcal B_{\C,H}$ has positive
leading exponent $\alpha$, one may choose $\lambda(\alpha)=1$; then
$\ddeg(\partial_\lambda f)=\alpha$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Choose a nonzero supported exponent $h$. A $\Q$-linear functional on $\Q h$
taking $h$ to $1$ extends to $H$ by extending a rational basis; $H$ is a
set, so choice for sets suffices. The derivative has nonzero coefficient at
$h$. Choosing the leading exponent gives the final assertion.
\end{proof}

For $\partial=\partial_\lambda$ define the two-term Wronskian
$W_\partial(f,g)=f\,\partial g-g\,\partial f$. For $f\ne0$ it vanishes exactly
when $\partial(g/f)=0$; it need not detect whether $g/f$ is an absolute
constant for one fixed $\partial$, which is why \cref{odg:lem:detection}
lets the derivation be chosen for the given ratio.

\begin{lemma}[A strict Wronskian bound \src{05}]\label{odg:lem:wronskian}
For nonzero $f,g\in\mathcal B_{\C,H}$ with $W_\partial(f,g)\ne0$,
$\ddeg W_\partial(f,g)\le\ddeg f+\ddeg g$. If $\ddeg f=\ddeg g=\alpha$, the
inequality is strict: $\ddeg W_\partial(f,g)<2\alpha$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The weak bound follows from \cref{odg:lem:derivation,odg:lem:degree}. If the
leading coefficients of $f,g$ are $a,b$ at exponent $\alpha$, the two
possible contributions at $2\alpha$ are $a\lambda(\alpha)b$ and
$b\lambda(\alpha)a$, which cancel; every other contribution has smaller
exponent. A nonzero Wronskian has a greatest supported exponent, by reverse
well-ordering, so its degree is strictly below $2\alpha$; one does not replace
that maximum by an unattained supremum.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Separated powers}

\begin{theorem}[Separated-power rigidity \src{05}]\label{odg:thm:separated}
Let $a,b,c\in\R\setminus\{0\}$ and let $m,n\ge2$ be ordinary integers.
Every solution in $\BR^2$ of
\begin{equation}\label{odg:eq:separated}
 a x^m+b y^n=c
\end{equation}
has $x,y\in\R$. Consequently, if $a,b,c$ are nonzero ordinary integers,
every solution in $\Oz^2$ is an ordinary integer solution.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
If one coordinate is constant, the other is constant by
\cref{odg:prop:univariate}. Suppose both are nonconstant, with
$\alpha=\ddeg x>0$ and $\beta=\ddeg y>0$. Cancellation of the positive leading
degree in \eqref{odg:eq:separated} forces $m\alpha=n\beta$. Take a common
set-sized workspace $H$ for $x,y$ (\cref{odg:lem:workspace}) and choose
$\partial=\partial_\lambda$ with $\lambda(\alpha)=1$, so $\partial x\ne0$.
Differentiating gives
\begin{equation}\label{odg:eq:separatedderivative}
 amx^{m-1}\partial x+bny^{n-1}\partial y=0,
\end{equation}
and since $x\ne0$ also $\partial y\ne0$.

We work in $\mathcal B_{\R,H}$, in which $a,b,c,m,n$ are units. Modulo the
principal ideal $(x^{m-1})$, \eqref{odg:eq:separated} says $by^n=c$, so the
image of $y$ is a unit; reducing \eqref{odg:eq:separatedderivative} then
shows that $\partial y$ vanishes in the quotient. Equivalently
$\partial y=x^{m-1}q$ with $q\in\mathcal B_{\R,H}$, and $q\ne0$ because
$\partial y\ne0$. Nonnegative degree and \cref{odg:lem:derivation} give
\[
 (m-1)\alpha\le\ddeg(\partial y)\le\beta=\tfrac mn\alpha .
\]
Since $\alpha>0$, this gives $mn-m-n\le0$, that is $(m-1)(n-1)\le1$, which
for $m,n\ge2$ forces $m=n=2$. In that case $aX^2+bY^2$ has two distinct
projective linear factors over $\C$ because $ab\ne0$, and
\cref{odg:thm:binary} contradicts the assumed nonconstancy. Constant omnific
coordinates are integers.
\end{proof}

The quotient-ring step replaces any appeal to unique factorization: if $y$
becomes a unit modulo $x^{m-1}$, a product of $\partial y$ with a unit can
vanish there only if $\partial y$ itself does. The argument is valid in a
non-Noetherian ring and for supports of arbitrary set-sized order type.

\begin{remark}[A direct divisibility identity \mergetag]\label{odg:rem:separatedidentity}
The divisibility $x^{m-1}\mid\partial y$ also follows from an explicit
identity. Put $f=ax^m+by^n$. For every derivation $\partial$,
\[
 a\,x^{m-1}\bigl(n\,x\,\partial y-m\,y\,\partial x\bigr)
 =n\,f\,\partial y-y\,\partial f,
\]
as one sees by expanding
$\partial f=amx^{m-1}\partial x+bny^{n-1}\partial y$: the right side is
$anx^m\partial y+bny^n\partial y-amx^{m-1}y\,\partial x-bny^{n}\partial y$.
On a solution of \eqref{odg:eq:separated}, $f=c$ and $\partial f=0$, so
$nc\,\partial y=x^{m-1}\cdot a(nx\,\partial y-my\,\partial x)$, and since $nc$
is a unit of $\mathcal B_{\R,H}$, $x^{m-1}$ divides $\partial y$. This route
avoids the quotient ring. The suite of 05 checks a related identity only
for $a=1$, $b=-1$; the identity above was checked symbolically for general
$a,b$ and $2\le m,n\le7$ when this report was assembled.
\end{remark}

\begin{corollary}[Mordell and Fermat curves \src{05}]\label{odg:cor:mordell}
The following equations have no infinite omnific solutions; their omnific
solution classes equal their ordinary integer solution sets:
\begin{gather*}
 x^m-y^n=c\quad(m,n\ge2,\ c\in\Z\setminus\{0\}),\\
 y^2=x^3+k\quad(k\in\Z\setminus\{0\}),\qquad
 x^n+y^n=1\quad(n\ge2).
\end{gather*}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Use $(a,b)=(1,-1)$ or $(-1,1)$ as appropriate in
\cref{odg:thm:separated}, and $(a,b,c)=(1,1,1)$ for the last equation.
\end{proof}

In particular $Y^2=X^3+1$, which source 01 listed as an open question
(\cref{odg:sub:settled}), has only its ordinary integer points in $\Oz$. The
theorem reduces the omnific problem to ordinary integers; it does not by
itself bound or list the integer solutions. A finite computation can verify
particular solutions of $y^2=x^3+1$, such as $(-1,0)$, $(0,\pm1)$, $(2,\pm3)$,
but a finite search is not a proof that such a list is complete, and the
suite of 05 checks only the displayed identities.

The condition $c\ne0$ cannot be removed: $x=t^n$, $y=t^m$ solves
$x^m-y^n=0$ for every $t\in\Oz$. Nor can an exponent equal one: $y=x^m-c$ is
an infinite family. The theorem treats fixed ordinary exponents and says
nothing about variable omnific exponents. A general elliptic equation
$y^2=x^3+ax+b$ with $a\ne0$ is outside it: the differentiated equation has
additional terms, and the divisibility by a high power of $x$ no longer
follows. This is a boundary of the proof, not a reason to extend the
statement to all elliptic curves.

\subsection{Unimodular Fermat rigidity}\label{odg:sub:fermat}

The homogeneous Fermat equation has abundant infinite solutions
(\cref{odg:ex:fermatscaling}), none of them primitive. An explicit unit-ideal
condition excludes nonconstant solutions for every exponent at least three.
We use the following terms, which sources 01 and 05 define identically in
substance.

\begin{definition}[Primitive and unimodular \src{01, 05}]\label{odg:def:primitive}
A nonzero tuple $(x_1,\ldots,x_r)$ over a commutative ring $R$ is
\emph{unimodular} if $\sum_iu_ix_i=1$ for some $u_i\in R$, that is,
$(x_1,\ldots,x_r)=R$. Over the domain $\Oz$ it is \emph{primitive} if its
entries have no common nonunit divisor.
\end{definition}

Unimodularity implies primitivity. The converse is not assumed in the
non-PID ring $\Oz$, and no B\'ezout hypothesis is used; whenever a tuple is
claimed unimodular, an explicit identity is given.

\begin{lemma}[Pairwise comaximality \src{05}]\label{odg:lem:pairwise}
Let $R$ be a commutative ring and suppose $x^n+y^n=z^n$ with $n\ge1$ and
$(x,y,z)=R$. Then $(x,y)=(y,z)=(z,x)=R$, and powers of any of these pairs
are again comaximal.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
In $R/(x,y)$ the element $z$ is nilpotent, as $z^n=0$, and a unit, because a
unit-ideal witness reduces to $wz=1$. The quotient is therefore zero. The
other pairs are similar. If $au+bv=1$, the binomial expansion of
$(au+bv)^{r+s-1}$ lies in $(u^r,v^s)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Unimodular Fermat rigidity \src{05}]\label{odg:thm:unimodularfermat}
Let $n\ge3$ be an ordinary integer. Suppose $x,y,z\in\BC$ are nonzero,
\[
 x^n+y^n=z^n,\qquad (x,y,z)=\BC.
\]
Then $x,y,z\in\C$. In particular, a nonzero unimodular Fermat triple in $\Oz$
has ordinary integer coordinates.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
First suppose that all ratios among $x,y,z$ are complex constants: $x=\mu z$,
$y=\nu z$ with $\mu,\nu\in\C^\times$. A unit-ideal witness $ux+vy+wz=1$ gives
$z(\mu u+\nu v+w)=1$, so $z$ is a unit of $\BC$, and $z$, hence $x,y$, are
constants by \cref{odg:prop:units}.

It remains to rule out a nonconstant ratio. Choose a set-sized workspace $H$
containing the supports of $x,y,z$ and of a unit-ideal witness; the ratios
$u=x/z$ and $v=y/z$ lie in the Hahn field over $H$ and satisfy $u^n+v^n=1$.
If one of $u,v$ is constant, the other is algebraic over $\C$, hence equal to
one of the complex roots of its polynomial, because that polynomial splits
over $\C$ and the Hahn field has no zero divisors. So both are nonconstant.
By \cref{odg:lem:detection}, choose $\partial$ with $\partial v\ne0$;
differentiating $u^n+v^n=1$ gives $\partial u\ne0$.

All three Wronskians are nonzero. Indeed
$W_\partial(y,z)=-z^2\,\partial v$ and $W_\partial(z,x)=z^2\,\partial u$. If
$W_\partial(x,y)=0$, then $\partial u/u=\partial v/v=h$, say, and
differentiating $u^n+v^n=1$ gives $nh(u^n+v^n)=nh=0$, so $h=0$, contrary to
$\partial v\ne0$.

Differentiating the Fermat equation gives
$x^{n-1}\partial x+y^{n-1}\partial y=z^{n-1}\partial z$. Multiplying by $z$
and using $z^n=x^n+y^n$, then multiplying by $y$ and using $y^n=z^n-x^n$,
gives the two relations
\[
 x^{n-1}W_\partial(z,x)=y^{n-1}W_\partial(y,z),\qquad
 -x^{n-1}W_\partial(x,y)=z^{n-1}W_\partial(y,z).
\]
By \cref{odg:lem:pairwise}, applied in $\mathcal B_{\C,H}$, there are
$r,s$ with $rx^{n-1}+sy^{n-1}=1$; multiplying by $W_\partial(y,z)$ and using
the first relation gives
$W_\partial(y,z)=x^{n-1}\bigl(rW_\partial(y,z)+sW_\partial(z,x)\bigr)$. The
same argument with the roles of the comaximal pairs exchanged, in both
relations, gives
\begin{equation}\label{odg:eq:fermatdiv}
 x^{n-1}\mid W_\partial(y,z),\quad
 y^{n-1}\mid W_\partial(z,x),\quad
 z^{n-1}\mid W_\partial(x,y)
\end{equation}
in $\mathcal B_{\C,H}$. Let $\alpha$ be the greatest of the three coordinate
degrees and $\beta,\gamma$ the other two. If $\alpha=0$, all coordinates are
constants. If $\alpha>0$, the divisibility in \eqref{odg:eq:fermatdiv} for
the coordinate of degree $\alpha$ and the weak Wronskian bound give
\[
 (n-1)\alpha\le\beta+\gamma\le2\alpha .
\]
For $n>3$ this is impossible. For $n=3$ it forces $\beta=\gamma=\alpha$, and
the relevant Wronskian then has degree strictly less than $2\alpha$ by
\cref{odg:lem:wronskian}, contradicting its divisibility by a nonzero element
of degree $2\alpha$. Hence a nonconstant ratio is impossible. A unit-ideal
witness in $\Oz$ is also one in $\BC$, and constant omnific elements are
ordinary integers.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[An omnific form of Fermat's Last Theorem \src{05}]\label{odg:cor:flt}
For every ordinary integer $n\ge3$ there is no triple of nonzero omnific
integers with $x^n+y^n=z^n$ and $ux+vy+wz=1$ for some $u,v,w\in\Oz$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
By \cref{odg:thm:unimodularfermat} such a triple is ordinary, and the
classical theorem of Wiles and Taylor--Wiles excludes it
\cite{Wiles,TaylorWiles}. Its positive-integer formulation also excludes
nonzero signed triples: for even exponents take absolute values, and for odd
exponents move negative terms to the other side.
\end{proof}

The rigidity theorem is the argument supplied by source 05; the final
nonexistence imports the classical theorem. The unimodular Pythagorean family
of \cref{odg:prop:pythagoras} is infinite, so the threshold $n\ge3$ is sharp.
Zero coordinates are excluded intentionally: $(1,0,1)$ is a unimodular
constant solution for every $n$. The theorem uses actual B\'ezout witnesses
and actual quotient rings; it makes no assertion about triples that merely
lack a common nonunit divisor (\cref{odg:q:primitivefermat}).

\section{The quadratic-level dichotomy}\label{odg:sec:quadratic}

Binary Pell rigidity might suggest that a nonzero constant quadratic level
never contains infinite points. This is false as soon as there is enough
indefinite dimension. The following result gives a precise classification.

\begin{theorem}[Quadratic-level dichotomy \src{01}; case (iii) also \src{02}]\label{odg:thm:quadclassification}
Let $n\geq1$, let $q\in\Z[X_1,\ldots,X_n]$ be a homogeneous quadratic
form, and let $c\in\Z\setminus\{0\}$. Write
\[
 V_{\Oz}=\{\mathbf x\in\Oz^n:q(\mathbf x)=c\},\qquad
 V_\Z=\{\mathbf a\in\Z^n:q(\mathbf a)=c\}.
\]
If $V_\Z$ is empty, so is $V_{\Oz}$. Otherwise the following alternatives
are exhaustive.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)},leftmargin=*]
\item If $q$ is nondegenerate and is either definite or of dimension at
most two, then $V_{\Oz}=V_\Z$.
\item If $q$ is degenerate, every $\mathbf a\in V_\Z$ lies on an
injective affine-linear family $\mathbf a+t\mathbf v$, $t\in\Pi$,
in $V_{\Oz}$, with every $t\ne0$ giving an infinite point.
\item If $q$ is nondegenerate, indefinite, and $n\geq3$, every
$\mathbf a\in V_\Z$ lies on an injective quadratic polynomial family
$\mathbf a+t\mathbf v+t^2\mathbf w$, $t\in\Pi$, in $V_{\Oz}$, with every
$t\ne0$ giving an infinite point. The family is injective on all of $\No$,
so every such fiber contains a proper class of points.
\end{enumerate}
In alternatives \textup{(ii)} and \textup{(iii)}, the constant tuple of
every point in the family is $\mathbf a$.
\end{theorem}

We first prove the geometric construction needed in the indefinite case.
All vectors and bilinear forms in it are ordinary real objects; only the
parameter is later allowed to be surreal.

\begin{lemma}[A nilpotent quadratic isometry \src{01, 02}]\label{odg:lem:transvection}
Let $B$ be a symmetric bilinear form on $\R^n$, with $q(x)=B(x,x)$. Suppose
$e,u\in\R^n$ satisfy
\[
 e\ne0,\qquad q(e)=0,\qquad B(e,u)=0,\qquad k=q(u)\ne0,
\]
and put $N(x)=B(x,e)u-B(x,u)e$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{odg:eq:Nidentities}
 N^2(x)=-kB(x,e)e,\qquad N^3=0,\qquad B(Nx,y)+B(x,Ny)=0.
\end{equation}
For a parameter $t$ in any field extension of $\R$, the polynomial map
\begin{equation}\label{odg:eq:transvection}
 U_t=\id+tN+\frac{t^2}{2}N^2,\qquad
 U_t(x)=x+t\bigl(B(x,e)u-B(x,u)e\bigr)-\frac{k}{2}t^2B(x,e)e,
\end{equation}
satisfies
\[
 q(U_t(x))=q(x),\qquad \det U_t=1,\qquad U_sU_t=U_{s+t},\qquad
 U_t^{-1}=U_{-t}.
\]
If $t\in\Pi$, all matrix entries of $U_t$ lie in $\Oz$ and its entrywise
constant coefficient is the identity. If $\alpha=B(a,e)\ne0$, the curve
$t\mapsto U_t(a)$ is injective.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
From $B(e,e)=B(e,u)=0$ one has $N(e)=0$ and $N(u)=-ke$, which gives the
first two identities of \eqref{odg:eq:Nidentities}. Directly,
$B(Nx,y)=B(x,e)B(u,y)-B(x,u)B(e,y)=-B(x,Ny)$.

For preservation, put $\alpha=B(x,e)$ and $\beta=B(x,u)$. In
\eqref{odg:eq:transvection} the added vector is
$t\alpha u-\bigl(t\beta+\tfrac{k}{2}t^2\alpha\bigr)e$. Its scalar product with
$x$, multiplied by two, is $-kt^2\alpha^2$, because the two linear terms
cancel. Its own quadratic value is $kt^2\alpha^2$, since $e$ is isotropic and
orthogonal to $u$. Their sum is zero.

Multiplying these polynomials in $N$ and using $N^3=0$ gives
$U_sU_t=U_{s+t}$ and hence $U_tU_{-t}=\id$ over $\R[t]$. The determinant is
therefore a unit of $\R[t]$, a nonzero real constant, and evaluation at $t=0$
makes it $1$. All identities remain valid after substituting a surreal $t$.
Every entry of $U_t$ is an entry of $\id$ plus a real combination of $t$ and
$t^2$; for $t\in\Pi$ that difference lies in $\Pi$.

Finally, for $\alpha=B(a,e)\ne0$ and $\beta=B(a,u)$,
\[
 U_t(a)=a+t v+t^2 w,
 \quad v=\alpha u-\beta e,
 \quad w=-\frac{k\alpha}{2}e .
\]
The real vectors $u,e$ are independent (as $q(u)\ne0=q(e)$), so $v,w$ are
independent: $w$ is a nonzero multiple of $e$ and $v$ has nonzero
$u$-component. Their independence persists over a field extension because
some $2\times2$ real minor is nonzero. If $U_s(a)=U_t(a)$ with $s\ne t$, then
$v+(s+t)w=0$, a contradiction.
\end{proof}

Source 01 wrote this map as a transvection $T_t$ and source 02 as the
nilpotent operator $U_T$ with $\id+TN+\tfrac{T^2}{2}N^2$; they are the same
map. No infinite exponential series is used: the expression is a finite
polynomial because $N^3=0$.

\begin{lemma}[An isotropic direction visible from a nonzero point \src{01, 02}]\label{odg:lem:nullchoices}
If $q$ is a nondegenerate indefinite real quadratic form of dimension at
least three and $a\in\R^n$ is nonzero, there exist $e,u$ as in
\cref{odg:lem:transvection} with $B(a,e)\ne0$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The real isotropic vectors span $\R^n$. In a real basis in which $q$ has
positive unit vectors $p_i$ and negative unit vectors $n_j$ (both types
exist by indefiniteness), each $p_i\pm n_j$ is isotropic, and sums and
differences recover every basis vector. Since the nonzero functional
$B(a,-)$ cannot vanish on a spanning set, some isotropic $e$ has
$B(a,e)\ne0$.

Nondegeneracy gives $f_0$ with $B(e,f_0)=1$. Replacing it by
$f=f_0-\tfrac12q(f_0)e$ makes $q(f)=0$ while retaining $B(e,f)=1$.
The plane spanned by $e,f$ is nondegenerate, so its orthogonal complement is
nondegenerate of dimension $n-2\geq1$ and contains $u$ with $q(u)\ne0$
(otherwise polarization would make its form zero). This $u$ is orthogonal to
$e$.
\end{proof}

Source 01 stated this lemma for $q(a)\ne0$; its proof, like source 02's, uses
only $a\ne0$.

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{odg:thm:quadclassification}]
The empty-set assertion is \cref{odg:thm:transfer}.

Suppose first that $q$ is definite and $\mathbf x\in V_{\Oz}$ has an
infinite coordinate. Let $\gamma>0$ be the largest leading exponent of its
nonzero coordinates and $r_i$ the coefficient of $\omega^\gamma$ in $x_i$
(zero when absent). The real vector $\mathbf r$ is nonzero, and the
coefficient of $\omega^{2\gamma}$ in $q(\mathbf x)$ is exactly
$q(\mathbf r)\ne0$, contradicting $q(\mathbf x)=c$. Thus every coordinate is
finite and hence an ordinary integer. In dimension two, nondegeneracy means
that the binary quadratic has two distinct linear factors over $\C$, so
\cref{odg:thm:binary} applies. A nondegenerate one-dimensional form is
definite. This proves (i).

For (ii), choose a nonzero real vector $\mathbf v\in\rad(B)$. For every $t$,
$q(\mathbf a+t\mathbf v)=q(\mathbf a)=c$. If $t\in\Pi$, the coordinates of
$t\mathbf v$ lie in $\Pi$, so $\mathbf a+t\mathbf v\in\Oz^n$ with constant
tuple $\mathbf a$. The map is injective because $\mathbf v\ne0$, and for
$t\ne0$ some coordinate has positive degree.

For (iii), $\mathbf a\ne0$ since $c\ne0$. Choose $e,u$ by
\cref{odg:lem:nullchoices} and use \cref{odg:lem:transvection}. Each
coordinate of $U_t(\mathbf a)$ is an integer constant plus real multiples of
$t$ and $t^2$, so it lies in $\Oz$ with constant coefficient $a_i$ for
$t\in\Pi$. Injectivity was proved for all surreal $t$. If $t\ne0$, its degree
is positive, and in a coordinate where $w_i\ne0$ the quadratic term has degree
$2\ddeg(t)$, strictly larger than that of the linear term, so the point is
infinite. Since $\Pi$ contains $\omega^\gamma$ for every surreal $\gamma>0$,
the fiber contains a proper class of points.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Explicit families}

For positive ordinary integers $D,D'$ and $t\in\Pi$, set \src{01}
\begin{equation}\label{odg:eq:hyperboloid}
 x=1+\frac{D'}2t^2,\qquad
 y=\frac{D'}{2\sqrt D}t^2,\qquad z=t.
\end{equation}
All three coordinates are omnific integers, and $x^2-Dy^2-D'z^2=1$: the two
quartic terms cancel, as do the two quadratic terms. For $t\ne0$ every
coordinate is infinite. The tuple is unimodular, since
\begin{equation}\label{odg:eq:hyperunimodular}
 1=x-\Bigl(\frac{D'}2t\Bigr)z,
 \qquad \frac{D'}2t\in\Oz .
\end{equation}
This illustrates a sharp contrast:
\begin{gather*}
 x^2-Dy^2=1\ \text{has only ordinary points},\\
 x^2-Dy^2-D'z^2=1\ \text{has infinite unimodular points}.
\end{gather*}
There is no appeal to convergence, analytic continuation, or infinite
iteration; every expression is a finite polynomial.

For the Lorentz form $q(x,y,z)=x^2-y^2-z^2$ with Gram matrix
$G=\diag(1,-1,-1)$, take $e=(1,1,0)$ and $u=(0,0,1)$ \src{02}. Then
$q(u)=-1$, $N(x,y,z)=(z,z,x-y)$, and
\begin{equation}\label{odg:eq:lorentzmatrix}
 U_t=
 \begin{pmatrix}
 1+t^2/2&-t^2/2&t\\
 t^2/2&1-t^2/2&t\\
 t&-t&1
 \end{pmatrix},
 \qquad
 U_t^{\mathsf T}GU_t=G,\quad \det U_t=1,\quad U_sU_t=U_{s+t}.
\end{equation}
For $t\in\Pi$ this is a one-parameter family of omnific Lorentz matrices with
constant coefficient the identity. Acting on $(1,0,0)$ gives
$(1+t^2/2,\,t^2/2,\,t)$, and replacing $t$ by $2s$ gives the
denominator-free identity
\[
 (1+2s^2)^2-(2s^2)^2-(2s)^2=1,
\]
valid for every $s\in\Oz$, with constant fiber $(1,0,0)$ whenever
$s\in\Pi$. For $s=\omega$ the point is $(1+2\omega^2,2\omega^2,2\omega)$; the
squares contain terms of degrees four, two and zero, and the nonconstant
terms cancel exactly. There is no negative-exponent tail. This is concretely
how an extra negative square evades the Pell rigidity mechanism.

\begin{corollary}[An invisible unipotent subgroup \src{02}]\label{odg:cor:unipotent}
For every nondegenerate indefinite integral quadratic form $q$ in at least
three variables, there is an injective group homomorphism
\[
 (\Pi,+)\longrightarrow
 \ker\bigl(\SO(q,\Oz)\xrightarrow{\ \ct\ }\SO(q,\Z)\bigr),
 \qquad t\longmapsto U_t.
\]
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Choose a nonzero real test vector $a$ and use \cref{odg:lem:nullchoices} to
construct $e,u$. \Cref{odg:lem:transvection} gives a homomorphism with
entries in $\Oz$, determinant one, preservation of $q$, and constant
coefficient $\id$. If $U_s=U_t$, apply both to $a$; injectivity of
$t\mapsto U_t(a)$ gives $s=t$. No ordinary point on a prescribed level is
needed.
\end{proof}

Here $\SO(q,\Oz)$ means determinant-one matrices over $\Oz$ preserving the
integral quadratic polynomial $q$; entrywise $\ct$ lands in $\SO(q,\Z)$.
This subgroup is invisible to every finite quotient of the coefficient ring
(\cref{odg:cor:charideals}), yet it is a proper class. It concerns indefinite
forms only; for definite forms see \cref{odg:cor:orthogonal}.

\subsection{What the classification does and does not say}

The classification concerns the presence of genuinely new points on a
\emph{represented nonzero level} \src{01, 02}. It does not decide whether the
integer level is represented in the first place. Nor does it classify all
infinite points or claim that every omnific point of a higher-dimensional
quadric lies on the constructed orbit: a displayed curve is a rigorously
constructed subset of the full fiber. Source 02's version covers nondegenerate
forms only and excludes degenerate forms and the zero level; source 01's
alternative (ii) covers degenerate forms.

For zero levels, homogeneity gives a different mechanism. An indefinite
real quadratic form has real isotropic directions, and multiplying any
such direction by an element of $\Pi$ produces omnific points with zero
constant tuple. Such points can exist even when there is no nonzero
ordinary integer isotropic vector.

\section{Bounded geometry, leading forms, and orthogonal matrices}\label{odg:sec:obstructions}

Multiplicative rigidity is not the only mechanism. A different one comes
from boundedness and the discreteness of $\Oz$.

\subsection{Bounded sets}

\begin{proposition}[Positive bounds \src{01, 05}]\label{odg:prop:squaresbound}
If $a_1,\ldots,a_n$ are positive ordinary reals, $m_1,\ldots,m_n\ge1$ are
ordinary integers, $x_1,\ldots,x_n\in\Oz$, and
\[
 \sum_i a_i x_i^{2m_i}=C\in\R,
\]
then every $x_i$ is an ordinary integer. In particular, an omnific point on
a sphere of ordinary finite radius is an ordinary integer point.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Each summand is nonnegative and at most $C$. If $C<0$ there is no solution;
otherwise this gives an ordinary real bound on each $|x_i|$, and
\cref{odg:prop:units} applies.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Bounded semialgebraic rigidity \src{02}]\label{odg:thm:bounded}
Let $X\subseteq\R^m$ be a bounded semialgebraic set defined by a finite
formula with real coefficients, and let $X(\No)$ be the class defined by the
same formula in $\No$. Then $X(\No)\cap\Oz^m=X\cap\Z^m$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Choose an ordinary real $M>0$ with $|x_i|\le M$ for all $\mathbf x\in X$.
This universal implication is a first-order sentence over $\R$. Given a
candidate point of $X(\No)$, generate a field from its coordinates and $\R$
and take its real closure inside $\No$; this is a set-sized real closed
subfield. Tarski's quantifier elimination makes the inclusion of $\R$ into it
elementary \cite{Tarski}, so the bound transfers and every omnific
coordinate is bounded by $M$, hence an ordinary integer by
\cref{odg:prop:units}. An integer tuple satisfies the formula in the
extension exactly when it does over $\R$, again by elementarity.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Definite levels \src{01, 02}]\label{odg:cor:definite}
For a positive or negative definite real quadratic form $q$ and a real $c$,
every omnific point of $q(\mathbf x)=c$ is an ordinary integer tuple.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
A nonempty real level set of a definite form is bounded, and
\cref{odg:thm:bounded} applies. Alternatively, the leading-coefficient
argument in the proof of \cref{odg:thm:quadclassification}(i) applies
verbatim to any real level $c$ \src{01}.
\end{proof}

For instance, $x^2+y^2=1$ has exactly $(\pm1,0),(0,\pm1)$ as omnific
solutions, and so has $x^4+y^4=1$. These concern a fixed finite level; the
homogeneous Pythagorean relation lets all coordinates grow together.
Positivity does \emph{not} imply being a sum of squares in $\Oz$
(\cref{odg:rem:foursquares}).

\subsection{Orthogonal and symmetric matrices}

\begin{corollary}[Euclidean orthogonal group \src{01, 02}]\label{odg:cor:orthogonal}
For every ordinary $n\ge1$, $\OO_n(\Oz)=\OO_n(\Z)$ is the group of signed
permutation matrices, of order $2^nn!$, and $\SO_n(\Oz)$ consists of the
signed permutation matrices of determinant one.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
For an orthogonal matrix, the sum of the squares in each row is $1$, so
every entry is ordinary by \cref{odg:prop:squaresbound}. An integer row of
squared norm one has exactly one nonzero entry, equal to $\pm1$, and
orthogonality of different rows forces those entries into different
columns. Conversely each signed permutation matrix is orthogonal.
\end{proof}

Requiring \emph{surreal} entries gives the large rotation group of a real
closed field, whereas \emph{omnific} entries in standard Euclidean
coordinates leave a finite group. This is not a claim that matrix groups over
$\Oz$ are finite: $\SL_2(\Oz)$ contains all $\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&t\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$,
$t\in\Oz$, and indefinite forms have the unipotent subgroup of
\cref{odg:cor:unipotent}. The Euclidean group is rigid because of
positivity.

\begin{corollary}[Symmetric matrix rigidity \src{01}]\label{odg:cor:symmetric}
Let $M$ be a symmetric matrix over $\Oz$. If $\tr(M^2)$ is an ordinary real
constant, every entry of $M$ is an ordinary integer. In particular, this
holds if its characteristic polynomial has ordinary integer coefficients.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
For symmetric $M$, $\tr(M^2)=\sum_i m_{ii}^2+2\sum_{i<j}m_{ij}^2$, so
\cref{odg:prop:squaresbound} applies. When the size is at least two,
$\tr(M^2)=(\tr M)^2-2e_2(M)$, where $e_2(M)$, the sum of the principal
$2\times2$ minors, is up to sign a coefficient of the characteristic
polynomial, as is $\tr M$; for size one the assertion is immediate.
\end{proof}

\subsection{No real point at infinity}

\begin{theorem}[Leading-homogeneous obstruction \src{01}]\label{odg:thm:infinity}
Let $F_1,\ldots,F_r\in\Z[X_1,\ldots,X_n]$ be nonconstant polynomials,
and let $H_j$ be the top homogeneous part of $F_j$. If the $H_j$ have
no common nonzero real zero, then every common zero of the $F_j$ in
$\Oz^n$ lies in $\Z^n$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Suppose a common zero $\mathbf x$ has an infinite coordinate. Let
$\gamma>0$ be the largest leading exponent of its coordinates and
$\mathbf r\ne0$ the real vector of coefficients of $\omega^\gamma$. If
$d_j=\deg F_j$, the coefficient at $\omega^{d_j\gamma}$ in $F_j(\mathbf x)$ is
$H_j(\mathbf r)$: a product attains that exponent only by taking exponent
$\gamma$ in each of $d_j$ factors, and terms of lower ordinary total degree
have exponent strictly less than $d_j\gamma$. So every $H_j(\mathbf r)=0$,
contrary to the hypothesis. All coordinates are therefore finite and hence
ordinary integers.
\end{proof}

The hypothesis is a sufficient absence-of-real-directions-at-infinity
condition for the chosen system. Its converse is false: a Pell equation has
real directions at infinity when $D>0$, but its nonzero fibers are rigid by
factorization. Initial-form obstructions and constant-product obstructions
detect different mechanisms.

\section{Defining the ordinary integers}\label{odg:sec:guard}

The retraction $\ct$ detects ordinary coefficients but is not a symbol
of the ring language. Pell rigidity shows that the embedded copy of $\Z$ is
nevertheless detected by a single polynomial equation with existential
auxiliary variables. Two versions were found independently.

\subsection{Pell coordinates as finite bounds}

Define ordinary Pell solutions by
\begin{equation}\label{odg:eq:pellsequence}
 X_k+Y_k\sqrt2=(3+2\sqrt2)^k,\qquad
 (X_{k+1},Y_{k+1})=(3X_k+4Y_k,\,2X_k+3Y_k),\qquad k\geq0,
\end{equation}
with $(X_0,Y_0)=(1,0)$.

\begin{lemma}[Pell witnesses \src{01, 05}]\label{odg:lem:pellsequence}
For every ordinary $k\ge0$, $X_k^2-2Y_k^2=1$; for $k\ge1$ both coordinates
are positive and $X_k\ge3^k$. Moreover $X_{2j}\equiv1$ and
$X_{2j+1}\equiv3\pmod8$, so both parity subsequences are unbounded.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Direct expansion gives $(3X+4Y)^2-2(2X+3Y)^2=X^2-2Y^2$, and ordinary
induction proves the identity, positivity and $X_{k+1}\ge3X_k$. From
$X_k=\tfrac12\bigl((3+2\sqrt2)^k+(3-2\sqrt2)^k\bigr)$ one gets the recurrence
$X_{k+2}=6X_{k+1}-X_k$ with $X_0=1$, $X_1=3$; modulo $8$ it gives the
alternating residues $1,3,1,3,\ldots$ (as $6\cdot3-1\equiv1$ and
$6\cdot1-3\equiv3$).
\end{proof}

\subsection{The five-auxiliary-variable guard}

We use the Legendre--Gauss three-square theorem: a nonnegative ordinary
integer $N$ is a sum of three integer squares if and only if it is not of the
form $4^a(8b+7)$ with $a,b\geq0$. It is imported, not reproved; see the
exposition \cite{Shiu} and the formal development in Isabelle's Archive of
Formal Proofs \cite{ThreeSquares}.

\begin{lemma}[A three-square gap below a Pell coordinate \src{01}]\label{odg:lem:pellgap}
For every ordinary nonnegative integer $S$, there are ordinary integers
$u,v,w_1,w_2,w_3$ such that
\[
 u^2-2v^2=1,\qquad u=S+w_1^2+w_2^2+w_3^2.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Choose the residue of $u$ modulo $8$ according to the following table; each
choice is realized by an unbounded subsequence of the $X_k$
(\cref{odg:lem:pellsequence}).
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c*{8}{r}}
\toprule
$S\bmod8$&0&1&2&3&4&5&6&7\\
chosen $u\bmod8$&1&3&3&1&1&3&1&1\\
$(u-S)\bmod8$&1&2&1&6&5&6&3&2\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Take $u$ in that subsequence so large that $N=u-S>0$. Its residue is one of
$1,2,3,5,6$, so it is neither divisible by $4$ nor congruent to $7$
modulo $8$, and cannot be $4^a(8b+7)$. The three-square theorem supplies the
$w_j$, and the corresponding Pell coordinate supplies $v$.
\end{proof}

For each ordinary $n\geq1$, define the integer polynomial
\begin{equation}\label{odg:eq:guardpoly}
 \Psi_n(\mathbf X,U,V,\mathbf W)
 =(U^2-2V^2-1)^2
 +\Bigl(U-\sum_{i=1}^nX_i^2-W_1^2-W_2^2-W_3^2\Bigr)^2,
\end{equation}
of total degree four in $n+5$ variables.

\begin{theorem}[Uniform quartic integrality guard \src{01}]\label{odg:thm:guard}
For every $\mathbf x\in\Oz^n$,
\begin{equation}\label{odg:eq:guarddef}
 \mathbf x\in\Z^n
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \exists u,v,w_1,w_2,w_3\in\Oz\quad
 \Psi_n(\mathbf x,u,v,w_1,w_2,w_3)=0.
\end{equation}
Every coordinate of every zero of $\Psi_n$ in $\Oz^{n+5}$ is an ordinary
integer. The number of auxiliary variables is five, independently of $n$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Suppose $\Psi_n=0$ in $\Oz$. A sum of two squares in an ordered ring vanishes
only when both squares vanish, so
\begin{equation}\label{odg:eq:guardrelations}
 u^2-2v^2=1,
 \qquad u=\sum_i x_i^2+w_1^2+w_2^2+w_3^2.
\end{equation}
By Pell rigidity (\cref{odg:cor:pell}), $u,v\in\Z$. The second equation
gives $u\geq0$ and bounds the square of each $x_i$ and $w_j$ by the ordinary
number $u$, so each is an ordinary integer by \cref{odg:prop:units}.
Conversely, for $\mathbf x\in\Z^n$ put $S=\sum_i x_i^2$ and choose the
witnesses of \cref{odg:lem:pellgap}.
\end{proof}

For $x=1$, one witness is $u=3$, $v=2$, $(w_1,w_2,w_3)=(1,1,0)$; for $x=2$,
$u=17$, $v=12$, $(w_1,w_2,w_3)=(3,2,0)$. The assertions for all integers rely
on \cref{odg:lem:pellgap}, not on examples or a finite computation.

\subsection{The four-square definition}

\begin{theorem}[A quartic definition of $\Z$ \src{05}]\label{odg:thm:standarddef}
For $t\in\Oz$, the following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)},leftmargin=*]
\item $t\in\Z$;
\item there exist $x,y,z_1,z_2,z_3,z_4\in\Oz$ with
\begin{equation}\label{odg:eq:standardsystem}
 x^2-2y^2=1,\qquad
 x-t^2=z_1^2+z_2^2+z_3^2+z_4^2;
\end{equation}
\item the quartic equation
\begin{equation}\label{odg:eq:standardquartic}
 (x^2-2y^2-1)^2+
 \Bigl(x-t^2-\sum_{j=1}^{4}z_j^2\Bigr)^2=0
\end{equation}
has a solution in its six existential variables over $\Oz$.
\end{enumerate}
Every witness to \eqref{odg:eq:standardsystem} has all six coordinates in
$\Z$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
If \eqref{odg:eq:standardsystem} holds, Pell rigidity makes $x,y$ ordinary,
and $0\le t^2\le x$ bounds $t$, so $t\in\Z$; each $z_j^2$ is bounded by the
ordinary integer $x-t^2$, so every $z_j$ is ordinary. Conversely, for
$t\in\Z$ choose $X_k\ge t^2$ by \cref{odg:lem:pellsequence}; the nonnegative
integer $X_k-t^2$ is a sum of four squares by Lagrange's theorem, which is
available in Mathlib as \fn{Nat.sum_four_squares} \cite{FourSquares}.
In an ordered ring a sum of two squares vanishes exactly when both do, so
\eqref{odg:eq:standardsystem} and \eqref{odg:eq:standardquartic} are
equivalent. The polynomial has total degree four, integer coefficients and no
infinite parameters.
\end{proof}

Write $\Std(t)$ for the existential formula \eqref{odg:eq:standardquartic}
and $\Std_n(\mathbf x)$ for \eqref{odg:eq:guarddef}. Replacing $t^2$ by
$\sum_{i=1}^nt_i^2$ in \eqref{odg:eq:standardsystem} defines $\Z^n$ with six
auxiliary variables by the same proof. Here ``standard'' refers precisely to
the embedded $\Z$; it is not an extra predicate of the language.

\begin{remark}[Comparison of the two guards]\label{odg:rem:guards}
Source 01's guard is uniform in $n$ with five auxiliary variables and needs
the three-square theorem; source 05's uses one more auxiliary variable and
Lagrange's four-square theorem, and source 01 had noted this simpler variant
without writing it out. Neither source claims its count optimal
(\cref{odg:q:guard}). For formalization the difference matters: at the
Mathlib revision pinned by this repository (\texttt{81a5d257}),
\fn{Nat.sum_four_squares} is proved in
\fn{Mathlib/NumberTheory/SumFourSquares.lean}, while no three-square
theorem is present. Only source 05's definition therefore rests on a theorem
in Mathlib; source 01's would need the three-square theorem imported or
proved first. The four-square theorem is used only on an ordinary
nonnegative integer; its analogue for positive omnific integers is false
(\cref{odg:rem:foursquares}).
\end{remark}

\subsection{Definability consequences}

$\Std(\Oz)=\Z$: ordinary integer arithmetic is present not only as the
quotient $\Oz/\Pi$ but as an existentially definable subring of $\Oz$.
There is no conflict with \cref{odg:prop:notdiophantine}, because $\Z$ is
stable under $\ct$, whereas the positive cone and the nonzero locus are not.

\begin{corollary}[Definability of $\ct$ and $\Pi$ \src{01, 05}]\label{odg:cor:definablect}
The graph of $\ct$ and the ideal $\Pi$ are first-order definable in the pure
ring language of $\Oz$ without parameters:
\begin{align*}
 \ct(a)=m\quad&\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \Std(m)\land\forall k\,\bigl((\Std(k)\land k\ne0)\Rightarrow
 \exists y\ (a-m=ky)\bigr),\\
 j\in\Pi\quad&\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \forall k\,\bigl((\Std(k)\land k\ne0)\Rightarrow\exists y\ (j=ky)\bigr).
\end{align*}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The right sides say that $m\in\Z$ and that $a-m$ (respectively $j$) is
divisible by every nonzero ordinary integer; by \eqref{odg:eq:divkernel}
this means $a-m\in\Pi$ (respectively $j\in\Pi$), which for $m\in\Z$ is
equivalent to $\ct(a)=m$.
\end{proof}

These definitions use a universal quantifier and an implication. Neither
source claims an existential (Diophantine) definition of $\Pi$ or of the
graph of $\ct$ (\cref{odg:q:existentialPi}).

\begin{remark}[An abstract principle \src{01}]\label{odg:rem:abstractguard}
The proof of \cref{odg:thm:guard} needs less than all surreal numbers. Let
$R$ be any discretely ordered ring containing $\Z$ in which every solution of
$U^2-2V^2=1$ is ordinary. Every element of $R$ bounded by an ordinary integer
is ordinary, by discreteness and a finite comparison with the integers in the
bounding interval; so the same proof shows that $\Psi_n$ defines $\Z^n$ in
$R^n$, and likewise for \eqref{odg:eq:standardquartic}. The omnific rings
considered here satisfy the Pell hypothesis by \cref{odg:cor:pell}.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Sums of squares and induction}

\begin{remark}[Squares and Peano arithmetic \src{01, 02, 05}]\label{odg:rem:foursquares}
The positive omnific integer $\omega-1$ is not a sum of any finite number of
squares in $\Oz$: applying $\ct$ to such an identity gives
$-1=\sum_j m_j^2$ with $m_j\in\Z$. So the nonnegative cone of $\Oz$ does not
satisfy Peano arithmetic, in which Lagrange's theorem is provable. This is
compatible with the classical fact that integer parts of real closed fields
satisfy open induction \cite{Shepherdson,LM}; open induction is not full
arithmetic.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[A failed existential induction instance \src{05}]\label{odg:thm:induction}
On the nonnegative part of $\Oz$, consider the formula
\begin{equation}\label{odg:eq:phi}
 \Phi(t):\quad
 \exists x,y\ge0\ \bigl(x^2=2y^2+1\ \wedge\ x>t\ \wedge\ y>0\bigr),
\end{equation}
existential with a quantifier-free ordered-ring matrix. Over $\Oz_{\ge0}$,
\[
 \Phi(0),\qquad
 \forall t\ge0\ (\Phi(t)\Rightarrow\Phi(t+1)),
 \qquad \neg\Phi(\omega),
\]
and $\Phi(t)$ holds exactly for the ordinary nonnegative integers $t$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The pair $(3,2)$ witnesses $\Phi(0)$. Given witnesses $x,y$ for $\Phi(t)$,
set $x'=3x+4y$, $y'=2x+3y$. The identity of \cref{odg:lem:pellsequence}
gives $(x')^2=2(y')^2+1$; positivity and discreteness give $x,y\ge1$, so
$x'>x+1>t+1$ and $y'>0$. This proves the successor step for every omnific
$t$. Pell rigidity makes every witness ordinary, so $x$ cannot exceed an
infinite $t$ such as $\omega$. Conversely an ordinary nonnegative $t$ has a
larger positive ordinary Pell witness by \cref{odg:lem:pellsequence}. By
\cref{odg:prop:units} these are the only two kinds of nonnegative omnific
integers.
\end{proof}

By Shepherdson's theorem, an integer part of a real closed ordered field
satisfies open induction, that is, induction for quantifier-free formulas
\cite{Shepherdson}. In a divisible set-sized Hahn workspace $H$, the ring
$\Oz_H$ is an integer part of the real closed field of real Hahn series with
exponents in $H$ (\cref{odg:thm:floor}); so the full $\Oz$ satisfies the same
schema, by applying the theorem in a workspace containing the finitely many
parameters and any proposed counterexample \src{05}. There is no conflict with
\cref{odg:thm:induction}: $\Phi$ is existential, not quantifier free. The
sentence ``every nonnegative $t$ has a larger positive Pell solution'' is
true over $\Z$ and false over $\Oz$; thus $\Oz$ is not an elementary extension
of $\Z$ as an ordered ring.

\begin{remark}[An induction failure in the pure ring language \mergetag]\label{odg:rem:ringinduction}
Existential induction also fails in the pure ring language, without order:
the existential formula $\Std(t)$ of \cref{odg:thm:standarddef} satisfies
$\Std(0)$ and $\forall t\,(\Std(t)\Rightarrow\Std(t+1))$ over $\Oz$ but not
$\Std(\omega)$, because it defines $\Z$, which contains $0$ and is closed
under $t\mapsto t+1$, and $\omega\notin\Z$. In particular the formula
$\Std(t)$ also shows that $\Oz$ is not an elementary extension of $\Z$ in the
ring language: the sentence $\forall t\,\Std(t)$ holds in $\Z$ and fails in
$\Oz$.
\end{remark}

\section{Infinite families, primitivity, and projective points}\label{odg:sec:primitive}

\subsection{Polynomial curves through integer points}

\begin{proposition}[Finite polynomial lifting \src{01, 02, 05}]\label{odg:prop:polylift}
Let $F_1,\ldots,F_r\in\R[X_1,\ldots,X_n]$, and let
$P_1(T),\ldots,P_n(T)\in\R[T]$ satisfy
\[
 F_j(P_1(T),\ldots,P_n(T))=0\quad(1\leq j\leq r),
 \qquad P_i(0)\in\Z\quad(1\leq i\leq n).
\]
Then $\mathbf P(t)\in\Oz^n$ solves the system for every $t\in\Pi$, with
constant tuple $\mathbf P(0)$. If some $P_i$ is nonconstant, every
$t\in\Pi\setminus\{0\}$ produces an infinite point.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Every nonconstant term of $P_i(t)$ lies in $\Pi$, since $\Pi$ is an ideal and
a real vector space, so the constant coefficient is exactly $P_i(0)$.
Polynomial identities hold under substitution into any field extension. If
$P_i$ has degree $d>0$ and $\ddeg(t)=\gamma>0$, its top term has degree
$d\gamma$, strictly larger than every other term's degree.
\end{proof}

No integrality is required of the nonconstant coefficients: for example
$(1+t^2/2,\,t^2/2,\,t)$ is omnific for every $t\in\Pi$ although
$1/2\notin\Oz$. The finiteness of the polynomials matters, however.
Evaluating an infinite real power series at an infinite omnific argument is
not licensed by this proposition; the Taylor expansion of $\sqrt{1+T}$ is not
an admissible way to assert that $\sqrt{1+\omega}$ lies in $\Oz$. Polynomial
maps supply a systematic sufficient condition for nontrivial fibers; they do
not exhaust all normal-form constructions, and no such exhaustion is assumed
(but see \cref{odg:thm:specialization} for finite supports).

\subsection{Infinite solutions of homogeneous equations}

\begin{theorem}[Homogeneous cone criterion \src{01}; part (b) \src{02}]\label{odg:thm:cone}
Let $F_1,\ldots,F_r\in\R[X_1,\ldots,X_n]$ be homogeneous of positive
degrees.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)},leftmargin=*]
\item Their common zero locus has a nonzero point in $\Oz^n$ if and only
if it has a nonzero point in $\R^n$.
\item If the $F_j$ have ordinary integer coefficients, the
equivalence in (a) persists with any fixed combination of coordinate sign and
nonvanishing conditions preserved by positive scaling, and these existence
problems are decidable.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
(a) A real nonzero common zero $\mathbf a$ gives the omnific common zero
$\omega\mathbf a\in\Pi^n$ by homogeneity. Conversely, take a nonzero common
zero $\mathbf x\in\Oz^n$. If every coordinate is constant, it is a real
point. Otherwise let $\gamma>0$ be the maximum of the coordinates' leading
degrees and $r_i=[\omega^\gamma]x_i$; the vector $\mathbf r$ is nonzero. For
$F_j$ homogeneous of degree $d_j$, the coefficient at $\omega^{d_j\gamma}$
in $F_j(\mathbf x)$ is $F_j(\mathbf r)$, since a product attains that exponent
only by taking exponent $\gamma$ in every factor. So $\mathbf r$ is a nonzero
real common zero. This route uses no quantifier elimination.

(b) A real solution $\mathbf r$ gives $\omega\mathbf r$, which has the same
signs and zero pattern. Conversely an omnific solution lies, with $\R$, in a
set-sized real closed subfield of $\No$ (generate a field and take its real
closure inside $\No$). Quantifier elimination makes the inclusion of $\R$
elementary \cite{Tarski}, so the existential statement with its finite sign
conditions already holds over $\R$; quantifier elimination is an effective
decision procedure for this finite formula with integer coefficients.
\end{proof}

The leading-vector route of (a) does not preserve sign or nonvanishing
conditions on coordinates of lower degree, which is why (b) uses Tarski. Any
homogeneous solution over $\No$, not only over $\R$, can be multiplied by a
common positive monomial into $\Pi^n$ preserving signs and zero patterns
(\cref{odg:cor:projectiveclear}) \src{02, 05}. Neither statement decides
affine equations: homogenizing an affine equation and requiring the new
coordinate to be nonzero allows division in $\No$, not in $\Oz$.

\begin{example}[Fermat scaling \src{01, 02, 05}]\label{odg:ex:fermatscaling}
For every ordinary integer $n\geq2$ and $0<t\in\Pi$,
\begin{equation}\label{odg:eq:fermatscaling}
 x=t,\qquad y=t,\qquad z=2^{1/n}t
\end{equation}
is a positive infinite solution of $x^n+y^n=z^n$; for $t=\omega$ this is
$\omega^n+\omega^n=(2^{1/n}\omega)^n$. The irrational coefficient is
permitted by the definition of $\Oz$. All constant coefficients are zero,
consistent with \cref{odg:thm:transfer}, whose specialization is $(0,0,0)$;
it does not refute Fermat's Last Theorem about nonzero ordinary integers. The
coordinates are divisible by every ordinary integer and have a common
infinite monomial divisor (\cref{odg:thm:commondivisor}), so these solutions
are not primitive and must not be cited as resolving a primitive Fermat
problem.
\end{example}

\subsection{Unimodular Pythagorean triples}

\begin{proposition}[Infinite unimodular Pythagorean triples \src{01, 05}]\label{odg:prop:pythagoras}
For every $t\in\Pi$, put $x=t^2-1$, $y=2t$, $z=t^2+1$. Then
$x^2+y^2=z^2$ and the triple is unimodular. For $t\ne0$ all three
coordinates are infinite, and for $t>0$ they are positive.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The identity is the ordinary polynomial identity
$(t^2-1)^2+4t^2=(t^2+1)^2$. Two unit-ideal witnesses are
\begin{equation}\label{odg:eq:pythbezout}
 \frac{t^2}{2}z-\Bigl(\frac{t^2}{2}+1\Bigr)x=1\ \src{01},\qquad
 \frac t2\,y-x=1\ \src{05},
\end{equation}
with $t^2/2,\,t/2\in\Pi\subseteq\Oz$. Positive leading degree gives the
assertion about infinite size.
\end{proof}

The constant tuple is $(-1,0,1)$, not the zero tuple; that is exactly where
the common-divisor obstruction for $\Pi^3$ stops applying. Infinite
solutions therefore need not arise by scaling an ordinary tuple, and
exponent two differs from exponents at least three
(\cref{odg:thm:unimodularfermat}). More generally
$(u^2-v^2)^2+(2uv)^2=(u^2+v^2)^2$ for all $u,v\in\Oz$ \src{02}.

\subsection{Which real projective directions have primitive representatives?}

A point $[r_0:\cdots:r_n]\in\mathbb P^n(\R)$ is \emph{rational} if
it has homogeneous coordinates in $\Q$, equivalently a nonzero integer
coordinate tuple.

\begin{theorem}[Rationality of primitive constant directions \src{01}]\label{odg:thm:realray}
For a real projective point $p=[r_0:\cdots:r_n]$, the following are
equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)},leftmargin=*]
\item $p$ is rational;
\item $p$ has a unimodular representative in $\Oz^{n+1}$;
\item $p$ has a primitive representative in $\Oz^{n+1}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For (i)$\Rightarrow$(ii), choose an integer representative with ordinary gcd
one; the integer B\'ezout identity makes it unimodular over $\Oz$.
(ii)$\Rightarrow$(iii) is immediate. For (iii)$\Rightarrow$(i), write a
primitive representative as $z_i=\lambda r_i$ with $\lambda\in\No^\times$
and choose $r_j\ne0$. Since $z_j\in\Oz\subseteq\BR$ and division by the
nonzero real $r_j$ preserves $\BR$, $\lambda\in\BR$. Put $c=\ct(\lambda)$;
then $\ct(z_i)=cr_i\in\Z$ for all $i$. If $c=0$, every $z_i$ lies in $\Pi$,
so $2$ divides every coordinate, contradicting primitivity. Hence $c\ne0$ and
$(cr_0,\ldots,cr_n)$ is a nonzero integer representative of $p$.
\end{proof}

For example, $[1:\sqrt2]$ has omnific representatives such as
$(\omega,\sqrt2\,\omega)$ but no primitive, hence no unimodular,
representative; source 02 proved the unimodular half of this example
directly. The theorem concerns real projective points; it is not a
classification of all points of $\mathbb P^n(\No)$, and neither monomial
clearing nor the existence of a homogeneous solution answers the
primitive-representative question.

\subsection{Finite supports specialize to polynomial arcs}

The rigidity theorems above do not depend on finite supports. Finite-support
expressions nevertheless admit an additional reduction \src{05}.

\begin{lemma}[Finite ordered specialization \src{05}]\label{odg:lem:specialization}
Let $H$ be a finitely generated subgroup of an ordered abelian group and
$E\subseteq H$ finite. There is an additive map $\ell:H\to\Z$ with
$\ell(a)>\ell(b)$ whenever $a,b\in E$ and $a>b$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
A finitely generated torsion-free abelian group is isomorphic to $\Z^r$. In
such coordinates, form the finite set of vectors $a-b$ for $a,b\in E$ with
$a>b$; they are positive in the original order. Zero is not in their convex
hull in $\R^r$: otherwise, since the vectors are integral, the rational
linear system describing such a convex combination would have a rational
solution (a nonempty polytope given by rational equations, nonnegativity and
a coordinate-sum equation has a rational vertex, obtained by setting a
maximal set of coordinates to zero and solving the remaining independent
rational equations), and clearing denominators would give a nontrivial sum of
positive group elements equal to zero. Strict separation of this compact
convex set from zero gives a real linear functional positive on all these
vectors; a small rational perturbation keeps the finitely many strict
inequalities, and clearing denominators gives an integer-valued functional.
If there are no strict comparisons, $\ell=0$ works.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Finite-support solutions specialize to arcs \src{05}]\label{odg:thm:specialization}
Suppose $\mathbf x\in\Oz^n$ has finite normal-form supports and satisfies a
finite polynomial system over $\Z$. There are real polynomials
$f_1(T),\ldots,f_n(T)$ satisfying the same polynomial identities with
$f_i(0)=\ct(x_i)$, and every nonconstant $x_i$ gives a nonconstant $f_i$. A
prescribed finite list of nonzero polynomial expressions in $\mathbf x$ can
be kept nonzero, with the same eventual signs as $T\to+\infty$ as their
surreal signs.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $E$ contain zero, all exponents of the coordinates, and all exponents of
the prescribed expressions after expansion; it is finite. Let $H$ be its
integer span and choose $\ell$ by \cref{odg:lem:specialization}. The
group-algebra map $\R[H]\to\R[T,T^{-1}]$, $\omega^h\mapsto T^{\ell(h)}$, is a
ring homomorphism. Positive coordinate exponents map to positive integers,
so coordinate images are polynomials with the same constant coefficients;
injectivity of $\ell$ on $E$ prevents cancellation of distinct supported
terms, in particular of leading terms. Polynomial identities are preserved,
and each prescribed expression keeps its leading coefficient and hence its
eventual sign for large positive $T$.
\end{proof}

With \cref{odg:prop:polylift} (substituting $t\in\Pi$ for $T$), this gives an
exact equivalence between the existence of a finite-support nonconstant
solution and of a nonconstant real polynomial arc with integer constant
coordinates. It does not turn infinite-support solutions into polynomial
arcs.

For a finite-support implementation one may store an element as a map from
exact exponents to nonzero exact real coefficients; the essential operations
are coefficientwise addition, exponent addition in convolution, combination
of equal exponents, and deciding integrality of the constant coefficient.
Rational exponents with real algebraic coefficients give a practical
decidable representation; general symbolic reals or arbitrary surreal
exponents need further equality and order oracles. Useful exact certificates
are polynomial identities, B\'ezout witnesses such as \eqref{odg:eq:pythbezout},
standard-modulus quotients, and finite normal-form obstructions such as the
first negative term in \eqref{odg:eq:binomial} \src{05}.

\section{Equations with omnific coefficients}\label{odg:sec:coefficients}

For $P\in\Oz[X_1,\ldots,X_n]$ let $\overline P\in\Z[X_1,\ldots,X_n]$ apply
$\ct$ to every coefficient. Then \src{02, 05}
\[
 \ct(P(\mathbf x))=\overline P(\ct x_1,\ldots,\ct x_n),\qquad
 P(\mathbf x)=0\ \Longrightarrow\ \overline P(\ct\mathbf x)=0 .
\]
The reverse implication need not hold, because the coefficients are no longer
fixed by $\ct$.

\subsection{Quadratic equations}

\begin{theorem}[Square-discriminant criterion \src{02}]\label{odg:thm:quadraticformula}
For $b,c\in\Oz$, the equation $x^2+bx+c=0$ has a root in $\Oz$ if and only
if $\Delta=b^2-4c$ is a square in $\Oz$. If $y^2=\Delta$ with $y\in\Oz$, its
roots are $(-b\pm y)/2$, and both are omnific.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
A root gives $(2x+b)^2=\Delta$. Conversely, suppose $y^2=\Delta$.
Specializing and reducing modulo $4$ gives $\ct(y)^2\equiv\ct(b)^2\pmod4$.
Integer squares are $0$ or $1$ modulo $4$, so $\ct(y)$ and $\ct(b)$ have the
same parity, and \cref{odg:thm:finitequotients} shows that $-b\pm y$ are
divisible by two in $\Oz$. The usual identity verifies the roots.
\end{proof}

Testing whether a nonnegative surreal has an omnific square root is not the
same as testing whether it has a surreal square root: real closedness gives
the latter, while nonnegative support and an integral constant must still be
checked for the former.

\begin{example}[Residual and leading tests that do not lift \src{01, 02, 05}]\label{odg:ex:nonsquare}
The equation $x^2=\omega^2+1$ has no solution in $\Oz$
(\cref{odg:prop:notnormal}). Nevertheless its residual equation
$\ct(x)^2=1$ is solvable, and the leading terms allow degree $1$ and leading
coefficient $\pm1$. The positive root in $\No$ has the formal binomial
expansion
\[
 \sqrt{\omega^2+1}
 =\omega+\tfrac12\omega^{-1}-\tfrac18\omega^{-3}
   +\tfrac1{16}\omega^{-5}-\cdots,
\]
whose negative exponents exclude it from $\Oz$. This is a support-controlled
Hahn expansion, not a claim of convergence in the surreal order topology. In
contrast, $\omega^2+2\omega+1=(\omega+1)^2$ is an omnific square.
\end{example}

\subsection{Initial forms}

\begin{proposition}[Initial forms of an omnific solution \src{01, 02}]\label{odg:prop:initial}
Let $P=\sum_\alpha a_\alpha\mathbf X^\alpha\in\Oz[X_1,\ldots,X_n]$ be nonzero,
let $\mathbf x\in\Oz^n$ have all coordinates nonzero, and write
$\gamma_i=\ddeg(x_i)$, $r_i=\lc(x_i)$. Discarding zero coefficients, put
\[
 W=\max_\alpha\Bigl(\ddeg(a_\alpha)+\sum_i\alpha_i\gamma_i\Bigr).
\]
If $P(\mathbf x)=0$, then
\begin{equation}\label{odg:eq:initial}
 \sum_{\ddeg(a_\alpha)+\sum_i\alpha_i\gamma_i=W}
       \lc(a_\alpha)\prod_i r_i^{\alpha_i}=0,
\end{equation}
and in particular the maximum is attained by at least two monomials.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By \cref{odg:lem:degree}, each evaluated monomial has degree
$\ddeg(a_\alpha)+\sum_i\alpha_i\gamma_i$ and leading coefficient
$\lc(a_\alpha)\prod_ir_i^{\alpha_i}$. The coefficient of $P(\mathbf x)$ at
$\omega^W$ is exactly the sum in \eqref{odg:eq:initial}, and it must vanish; a
single maximizer would contribute a nonzero product.
\end{proof}

Variables assigned zero can first be substituted out. For ordinary
coefficients this is a weighted initial polynomial over $\R$ with surreal
weights. The constant-coefficient equation and the initial equation constrain
opposite ends of a normal form; they are complementary necessary tests, not a
lifting criterion, as \cref{odg:ex:nonsquare} shows: the missing information
lies in the full tail of a potential root.

\subsection{Two-term roots and failed lifting}

\begin{proposition}[A two-term root obstruction \src{05}]\label{odg:prop:binomial}
Let $a\in\R_{>0}$, $\gamma>0$, $b\in\Z$, and let $n\ge2$ be ordinary. The
equation $X^n=a\omega^\gamma+b$ has a solution in $\Oz$ if and only if $b=0$.
When $b=0$ its positive solution is $a^{1/n}\omega^{\gamma/n}$, together with
its negative when $n$ is even.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The right side is positive and infinite. Its positive $n$th root in $\No$ has
the formal binomial expansion
\begin{equation}\label{odg:eq:binomial}
 a^{1/n}\omega^{\gamma/n}
 \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\binom{1/n}{k}
 \Bigl(\frac ba\Bigr)^k\omega^{-k\gamma},
\end{equation}
whose exponents strictly decrease; the formal power-series binomial identity
shows it is an $n$th root in the Hahn field, positivity gives uniqueness among
positive roots, and in an ordered field the only other real root is its
negative when $n$ is even. If $b\ne0$, the $k=1$ coefficient is nonzero at
the exponent $\gamma/n-\gamma<0$, which no other term has, so neither root is
omnific. If $b=0$ the root is the single positive monomial displayed.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[A simple residue root need not lift \src{05}]\label{odg:ex:hensel}
Let $f(T)=T^2-T-\omega$. Modulo $\Pi$ it becomes $T(T-1)$, and the residue
root $0$ is simple, with derivative $-1$, a unit. Nevertheless $f$ has no root
in $\Oz$: its roots in $\No$ are
\[
 \tfrac12\pm\bigl(\omega^{1/2}+\tfrac18\omega^{-1/2}
 -\tfrac1{128}\omega^{-3/2}+\cdots\bigr),
\]
with constant coefficient $\tfrac12$ and negative exponents. Hensel-style
intuition fails for $\Oz\to\Z$: the kernel $\Pi$ is not contained in the
Jacobson radical, since $-\omega\in\Pi$ but $1+\omega$ is not a unit, and
$\Pi^2=\Pi$, so there is no decreasing nilpotent-error filtration. This is a
failure of the simple-root lifting property itself.
\end{example}

For general coefficients, a viable approach must inspect actual normal-form
supports. Matching constant terms is necessary, and occasionally useful, but
is not a sufficient solvability criterion (\cref{odg:q:roots}).

\section{Fixed Hahn workspaces and a formalization route}\label{odg:sec:formal}

\subsection{Which results are local in the exponent group?}

For a set-sized ordered abelian group $\Gamma$, work in a Hahn field whose
monomials are written $\omega^\gamma$ with reverse-well-ordered supports, and
define \src{01, 02}
\[
 \Oz_\Gamma=\Z+\Bigl\{\sum_{\gamma>0}r_\gamma\omega^\gamma\Bigr\},
 \qquad
 \mathcal B_\Gamma=\R+\Bigl\{\sum_{\gamma>0}r_\gamma\omega^\gamma\Bigr\}.
\]
The displayed sums are elements of the ambient Hahn field with the specified
support restrictions; this is a convention, not an identification of every
abstract group element with a surreal exponent. When an ordered embedding of
$\Gamma$ into $\No$ is fixed, it gives the corresponding surreal workspace.

\paragraph{Local results.} The ring decompositions, the unit lemma, the
retraction, finite quotients, equational transfer, the Smith criterion,
constant-product and decomposable rigidity, binary, Pell and norm rigidity,
the quadratic constructions, the quartic guards, and all explicit
polynomial identities apply to $\Oz_\Gamma$ \src{01, 02}. Their proofs use finite algebra,
leading exponents and real coefficients, not the availability of a new
exponent outside $\Gamma$. The derivation arguments of
\cref{odg:sec:derivations} take place in the workspace of the rational span of
the relevant supports, so they apply to $\Oz_\Gamma$ after passing to the
divisible hull of $\Gamma$ \src{05}. If $\Gamma\ne0$, its positive cone
provides nonconstant parameters for the infinite-family statements; the case
$\Gamma=0$ is $\Oz_\Gamma=\Z$. The Lorentz family uses only $t$, $t^2$ and
real scalars, so one positive monomial already produces examples. If the
coefficient field is restricted below $\R$, the general indefinite lifting
additionally needs the chosen real vectors $e,u$ to be available, or an
explicit scalar extension \src{02}.

\paragraph{Full-class results.} The common-divisor theorem for an arbitrary
set of purely infinite elements, the monomial clearing theorem, $\Pi^2=\Pi$,
the non-generation of $\Pi$ by a set, $\Frac(\Oz)=\No$, the general pair
without a gcd (\cref{odg:thm:nogcd}) and the set-sized quotient theorem
(\cref{odg:fact:settarget}) require bounds that may not exist in $\Gamma$.
For instance, in $\Gamma=\R$ the set $\{1/k:k\geq1\}$ has no strictly
positive real lower bound, whereas in $\No$ it has one. A support in $\Gamma$
may also be unbounded below in $\Gamma$. These conclusions are not asserted
for $\Oz_\Gamma$; in particular one must not infer $\Pi_\Gamma^2=\Pi_\Gamma$ by
copying the proof for $\No$ \src{01, 02, 05}. Proofs that stay inside a
workspace must be distinguished from proofs that enlarge it.

\section{Questions and synthesis}\label{odg:sec:questions}

The sources list continuation problems that their proofs do not decide. None
of them claims that these problems are open in the literature, or that the
list is exhaustive. Two were settled inside the batch of manuscripts from
which this report was built.

\subsection{Questions settled by source 05}\label{odg:sub:settled}

\paragraph{Coprime exponents and $Y^2=X^3+1$ (source 01).} Source 01 asked
which curves such as $Y^2=X^3+1$ have genuinely infinite omnific points, and
proved rigidity of $x^m-y^n=c$ only for $\gcd(m,n)>1$
(\cref{odg:rem:powers}). \Cref{odg:thm:separated} of source 05 answers this
for every separated equation $ax^m+by^n=c$ with $abc\ne0$ and $m,n\ge2$; in
particular $Y^2=X^3+1$ and $x^2-y^3=1$ have only ordinary omnific points
(\cref{odg:cor:mordell}). The general part of source 01's question, a
criterion for arbitrary affine curves, remains open as
\cref{odg:q:affine}.

\paragraph{Unimodular Fermat (sources 01 and 02).} Source 01 asked for the
classification of primitive or unimodular omnific solutions of
$X^n+Y^n=Z^n$ with nonzero coordinates, $n\ge3$; source 02 asked which
homogeneous systems admit nonzero omnific solutions generating $\Oz$.
\Cref{odg:thm:unimodularfermat,odg:cor:flt} of source 05 settle the
unimodular Fermat case: there are none. The primitive case (no common nonunit
divisor) remains open as \cref{odg:q:primitivefermat}, and source 02's
question for other homogeneous systems remains open as
\cref{odg:q:homogeneous}.

\subsection{Open questions}

\begin{question}[Rigid affine varieties and curves \src{01, 02, 05}]\label{odg:q:affine}
For which affine varieties $X$ over $\Z$ does $X(\Oz)=X(\Z)$ hold? For which
affine curves does every omnific point have ordinary coordinates? A natural
next test is $Y^2=X^3+aX+b$ with $a\ne0$, outside
\cref{odg:thm:separated}.
\end{question}
\Cref{odg:thm:decomposable,odg:thm:binary,odg:thm:separated} answer this for
nonzero decomposable levels with trivial common kernel and for separated
equations, \cref{odg:thm:bounded,odg:thm:infinity} for bounded loci and
systems with no real point at infinity, and \cref{odg:thm:quadclassification}
and polynomial arcs give large opposing classes. A satisfactory criterion
should separate polynomial parametrization, torus rigidity and genuinely
algebraic branches \src{01}; it needs either a new degree inequality or a
structural analysis of possible supports \src{05}. No claim is made that all
other fibers are rigid, or that all nontrivial fibers come from real
polynomial curves \src{02}.

\begin{question}[Primitive Fermat triples \src{01, 05}]\label{odg:q:primitivefermat}
For $n\ge3$, can the unit-ideal hypothesis of
\cref{odg:thm:unimodularfermat} be weakened to ``no common nonunit
divisor''? That is, are there primitive, non-unimodular omnific solutions of
$X^n+Y^n=Z^n$ with nonzero coordinates?
\end{question}
The answer cannot be read off from ordinary integer intuition; it needs an
explicit relation between common divisors, ideal generation and the
applicable factorization theory. \Cref{odg:thm:unimodularfermat} deliberately
avoids any such relation, and the binary nonzero-fiber theorem does not
directly answer a three-variable homogeneous question \src{01}.

\begin{question}[Primitive homogeneous solutions \src{02}]\label{odg:q:homogeneous}
Which homogeneous systems admit a nonzero omnific solution whose coordinates
generate $\Oz$, and how does this depend on the chosen integral model of the
projective variety?
\end{question}
The real-solvability criterion \cref{odg:thm:cone} omits unimodularity, and
\cref{odg:thm:realray} exhibits the gap. A gcd normalization cannot be
assumed, by \cref{odg:thm:nogcd}. Fermat equations of exponent at least
three have none (\cref{odg:cor:flt}); the exponent-two Pythagorean and
indefinite quadratic cases have many (\cref{odg:prop:pythagoras},
\eqref{odg:eq:hyperunimodular}).

\begin{question}[An existential definition of $\Pi$ \src{01}]\label{odg:q:existentialPi}
Is there an existential definition of $\Pi$ over $\Oz$ with ordinary integer
parameters? More generally, which fibers or subideals of $\Pi$ are
Diophantine? Retraction stability is necessary but far from sufficient.
\end{question}

\begin{question}[Complexity of the integrality guard \src{01}]\label{odg:q:guard}
Can the number of auxiliary variables be reduced below five, with a single
quartic equation, or can a meaningful lower bound be proved? The present
constructions give explicit upper bounds only (five in \cref{odg:thm:guard},
six in \cref{odg:thm:standarddef}); other rigid arithmetic families might
serve as finite bounds.
\end{question}

\begin{question}[Roots with omnific coefficients \src{01, 02, 05}]\label{odg:q:roots}
For polynomials with finitely represented omnific coefficients, which root
questions in a chosen support class are decidable? Can one give tractable
necessary and sufficient support criteria for a root of a polynomial in
$\Oz[T]$ to be omnific?
\end{question}
\Cref{odg:thm:quadraticformula} reduces the monic quadratic case to square
membership, \cref{odg:prop:binomial} settles two-term binomials, and
\cref{odg:ex:hensel} rules out a naive lifting theorem; multiterm
cancellation needs separate analysis. Any effective claim must specify the
coefficients, the exponent representation, and whether workspace enlargement
is permitted. An unrestricted algorithm for ordinary-coefficient affine
existence would contradict \cref{odg:cor:H10}.

\begin{question}[The exact size boundary \src{05}]\label{odg:q:size}
Which set-sized exponent groups or universe-relative constructions admit
analogues of the common-monomial and set-sized quotient theorems?
\end{question}
The sibling report \fn{docs/surreal/set-sized-quotients-of-omnific-integers}
contains cardinal-threshold results on this question for specific set-sized
integer parts.

\begin{question}[Effective finite-support discovery \src{05}]\label{odg:q:search}
How far can a search for polynomial arcs and exact B\'ezout certificates,
suggested by \cref{odg:thm:specialization}, go before transfinite support
constructions are needed? It can find witnesses and expose obstructions, but
it cannot become a complete decision procedure for integer-coefficient
systems, by \cref{odg:cor:H10}.
\end{question}

\subsection{Synthesis}

The arithmetic of $\Oz$ is governed by three simultaneous facts:
\[
 \Oz/\Pi=\Z,\qquad \Oz^\times=\{\pm1\},\qquad\Frac(\Oz)=\No.
\]
The first makes ordinary congruences and equation-existence questions look
classical. The second imposes powerful rigidity on nonzero product fibers.
The third makes projective representation flexible while saying little about
primitive representatives. Growth degree, support and chosen Euler
derivations then govern whether individual solutions escape the ordinary
integers \src{05}.

For Diophantine equations the key divide is not only between finite and
infinite numbers. It is between \emph{equation existence}, which the
retraction controls; \emph{multiplicative, differential or bounded
rigidity}, which can force every coordinate to be ordinary; and
\emph{polynomial flexibility}, which creates infinite points without any
infinitesimal tail \src{02}. The difference between a Pell conic and a
three-variable indefinite quadric is structural: binary factorization forces
constants, while an additional indefinite dimension supports finite
polynomial isometries. The guards use the rigid side of this dichotomy to
recover the ordinary integers inside $\Oz$. Omnific integers preserve much
more of ordinary equational existence than their size suggests, and much less
of ordinary quantified arithmetic than their discreteness suggests
\src{05}. None of this requires unique factorization, a Euclidean termination
principle, or Conway's refinement property.

\section{Dependencies and verification}\label{odg:app:verification}

\subsection{Imported inputs}

The foundation imported from surreal theory is the existence, uniqueness,
order and multiplication of set-supported Conway normal forms, the set-cut
property, and the real closedness of $\No$ \cite{Conway,Gonshor}. The other
imported theorems are: MRDP (\cref{odg:cor:H10}); Tarski's quantifier
elimination (\cref{odg:thm:bounded}, \cref{odg:thm:cone}(b)); the three-square
theorem (\cref{odg:lem:pellgap}); Lagrange's four-square theorem
(\cref{odg:thm:standarddef}, \cref{odg:cor:H10}); Shepherdson's theorem (open
induction); the Fermat theorem of Wiles and Taylor--Wiles
(\cref{odg:cor:flt} only, after the rigidity theorem has reduced to ordinary
integers); the algebraic closedness of $\C$ (to factor binary forms; the
algebraic closedness of a class-sized surcomplex field is not used); and, as
context only, the primes of \cite{LM} and \cite[Lemma 11.1]{EK}. All other
deductions are proved here from these and finite algebra.

The principal dependency chains are:
\begin{align*}
 &\text{normal forms}\ \Longrightarrow\ \ct\text{ is a retraction}
 \ \Longrightarrow\ \text{transfer and finite quotients};\\
 &\text{normal forms}\ \Longrightarrow\ \text{constant units in }\BC
 \ \Longrightarrow\ \text{decomposable, binary and norm rigidity};\\
 &\text{local Euler derivations}+\text{binary rigidity}
 \ \Longrightarrow\ \text{separated powers and unimodular Fermat};\\
 &\text{Pell rigidity}+\text{three- or four-square theorem}
 \ \Longrightarrow\ \text{quartic definitions of }\Z;\\
 &\text{real quadratic linear algebra}+\text{support-ring algebra}
 \ \Longrightarrow\ \text{quadratic-level dichotomy};\\
 &\text{normal forms}+\text{set-cut bounds}
 \ \Longrightarrow\ \text{common divisors and clearing}.
\end{align*}
The source-05 proofs that most deserve independent review are, by its own
account, the separated-power theorem (divisibility in a quotient ring without
unique factorization), the unimodular Fermat theorem (strict leading-term
cancellation in the equal-degree Wronskian), and the set-sized quotient
theorem (positivity of every exponent in the geometric quotient; printed in
the sibling report).

that code or used the announced result. Accordingly this report does not
repeat older descriptions of the refinement conjecture as a verified
\emph{currently open} problem, does not claim to settle it, and none of its
proofs depends on its status.
