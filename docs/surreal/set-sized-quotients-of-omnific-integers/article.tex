\begin{abstract}
Write an omnific integer uniquely as a purely infinite normal form plus an
ordinary integer, and let $c_0:\Oz\to\Z$ extract that integer. We prove that
every unital homomorphism from $\Oz$ into any set-sized ring, even a
noncommutative ring with nilpotents, is $x\mapsto c_0(x)1$. More strongly,
every action of $\Oz$ on a set-sized abelian group factors through $c_0$.
The proof uses scaled copies of arbitrarily large fields inside the purely
infinite ideal and a finite multiplicative identity; it requires no
continuity or preservation of infinite sums.

We classify all set-sized quotient rings, identify the profinite quotient,
and obtain the Gaussian omnific analogue. An application of an existing
primality theorem gives a nonzero proper-class domain with no nonzero
set-sized unital representations. Explicit real-closed Hahn subfields
produce set-sized integer parts with prescribed lower cardinal thresholds.
Under $\kappa^{<\kappa}=\kappa$, the threshold is exactly $\kappa$, both for
detecting any nonzero purely infinite element and for detecting certain
first and second extension classes. Their purely infinite ideals are flat
and idempotent but nonprojective, while the quotient has flat dimension one.
The new theorem package is separated from standard Hahn-field facts and
from the literature's primality result. No claim of peer review or Lean
verification is made.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and main results}\label{sec:intro}

The omnific integers form a discretely ordered subring of the surreal
field, but their ring-theoretic size is not an incidental feature.
An individual element has a set-sized normal form, whereas the supply of
scales available below any positive surreal exponent is a proper class.
We show that these two facts together force an unexpectedly rigid answer
to a basic representation question:
\begin{center}
\emph{What can an ordinary, set-sized ring or module see of an omnific integer?}
\end{center}
The answer is exactly its ordinary integer coefficient. This is much
stronger than the familiar observation that a proper-class field cannot
embed in a set. The source here is a ring, not a field; its maps have large
kernels, and targets with zero divisors are allowed. Nor is the result a
mere consequence of divisibility: a rationally divisible ideal can act
nontrivially on many infinite set-sized modules.

\subsection{The class theorem}
An omnific integer has the form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ozform}
 x=n+\sum_{g\in S}r_g\omega^g,
 \qquad n\in\Z,\quad r_g\in\R,\quad S\subset\No^{>0},
\end{equation}
where $S$ is a set and is reverse well ordered. Put $c_0(x)=n$ and
$\I=\ker c_0$. The adjective \emph{purely infinite} describes support in
strictly positive exponents, not the sign of the element.

\begin{maintheorem}[The universal set-sized quotient]\label[maintheorem]{main:class}
For every set-sized unital ring $S$, there is exactly one unital ring
homomorphism $\Oz\to S$, namely
\[
                 x\longmapsto c_0(x)1_S.
\]
For every set-sized unital $\Oz$-module $M$, one has $\I M=0$.
Thus $\Z$-module structures and set-sized $\Oz$-module structures correspond
uniquely by restriction and inflation along $c_0$.

For Gaussian omnific integers the corresponding universal quotient is
$\GOz\to\Z[i]$. In particular, unital maps $\GOz\to S$ correspond
exactly to elements $j\in S$ satisfying $j^2=-1_S$.
\end{maintheorem}

The last assertion concerns ring maps, not necessarily maps of complex
algebras: the image of $i$ is part of the data. A source ring that is
commutative can map into a noncommutative ring; its image is, of course,
commutative. Full proofs appear in \cref{sec:collision,sec:universal}.

One consequence illustrates the force of the theorem. L'Innocente and
Mantova proved that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qintro}
                  q=\omega^{\sqrt2}+\omega+1
\end{equation}
is prime in $\Oz$ \cite{LM}. We do not reprove or claim that primality
result. We instead prove that the nonzero domain $\Oz/(q)$ has no unital
map into any nonzero set-sized ring, and no nonzero set-sized unital
module. The explanation is that $c_0(q)=1$. This application separates
primality from the existence of set-sized residue representations.

\subsection{Set-sized models and a sharp threshold}
There is a quantitative counterpart with no class-ring homological
algebra. Let $\kappa$ be an uncountable regular cardinal. In
\cref{sec:cardinal} we construct an explicit divisible subgroup
$\Gamma_\kappa\subset\No$ of cardinality $\kappa$, a real-closed Hahn
subfield $K_\kappa\subset\No$, and its omnific integer part
\[
       R_\kappa=K_\kappa\cap\Oz=\Z+I_\kappa.
\]
All these objects are sets. Supports in $K_\kappa$ have cardinality
strictly less than $\kappa$.

\begin{maintheorem}[Cardinal detection threshold]\label[maintheorem]{main:cardinal}
For every uncountable regular $\kappa$, the ring $R_\kappa$ above satisfies:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Every homomorphism $R_\kappa\to S$ with $|S|<\kappa$ factors
through $c_0:R_\kappa\to\Z$.
\item Every $R_\kappa$-module $M$ with $|M|<\kappa$ is annihilated by
$I_\kappa$.
\item $I_\kappa^2=I_\kappa$, and the least cardinality of a generating
set of $I_\kappa$ as an ideal is $\kappa$.
\end{enumerate}
If additionally $\kappa^{<\kappa}=\kappa$, then $|R_\kappa|=\kappa$.
For each $0\ne x\in I_\kappa$, the least cardinality of a ring target
admitting a homomorphism that does not kill $x$ is exactly $\kappa$.
The least cardinality of a module on which $x$ acts nontrivially is also
exactly $\kappa$.
\end{maintheorem}

The last two minima are over set-sized targets. The lower bounds hold
without the displayed cardinal-arithmetic hypothesis; that hypothesis
makes the explicit source itself a matching upper-bound witness.
The real closedness of bounded-support Hahn fields is not a novelty
claim. What matters here is the choice of a two-sided scale group,
combined with the small-target obstruction.

\subsection{Homological detection can require the same cardinal}
Write $R=R_\kappa$, $I=I_\kappa$ and $D=R/I=\Z$ in the next theorem.
All dimensions, resolutions, tensor products and extension groups are
ordinary constructions over the set-sized ring $R$.

\begin{maintheorem}[Homological reflection and hidden obstructions]
\label[maintheorem]{main:homological}
The ideal $I$ is flat, idempotent, and nonprojective. Moreover
\[
       \fd_R D=1,\qquad \pd_R D\ge2.
\]
For arbitrary $D$-modules $N,M$, inflated to $R$-modules,
\[
 \Tor_n^R(D,N)=0\quad(n>0),\qquad
 \Ext_R^n(N,M)\cong\Ext_D^n(N,M)\quad(n\ge0).
\]
In particular, $\Ext_R^n(D,M)=0$ for every $n>0$ whenever $|M|<\kappa$.
Nevertheless,
\[
 \Ext_R^1(D,I)\ne0,
\]
and there is an explicitly presented module $J$ with
$\Ext_R^2(D,J)\ne0$. If $\kappa^{<\kappa}=\kappa$, both $I$ and $J$
have cardinality $\kappa$. Consequently $\kappa$ is the exact least
size of a coefficient module detecting a nonzero extension in degree
one, and also in degree two, from $D$.
\end{maintheorem}

This does not give the exact projective dimension of $D$. It gives a
sharp size bound on two kinds of witnesses to its nonprojectivity.
The distinction is substantive: all smaller coefficient modules see
vanishing, but larger modules detect nonsplit extensions.

\section{Normal forms, coefficients, and size conventions}\label{sec:normal}

\subsection{Foundation and meaning of a class quotient}
We work in G\"odel--Bernays set theory with global choice, or an equivalent
set/class framework adequate for the usual surreal construction.
Every surreal and every individual series support is a set. The carriers
$\No$, $\Oz$, and some rings used below are proper classes. Their
operations and homomorphisms are class functions.

Statements about all homomorphisms from a class ring are understood as
statements about each such class function, not as a claim that a set of
all these functions exists. Likewise, a quotient of a class ring by a
class ideal may be represented by Scott codes: for each equivalence
class, take its representatives of least set-theoretic rank. These form
a nonempty set and give a set-valued code. Alternatively, every quotient
statement below can be read as a statement about a kernel and the
associated universal property. No class of all subclasses is required.

The only cardinality contradictions used in the class arguments are
ordinary set contradictions. Given a target set $S$, choose a cardinal
$\lambda>|S|$, explicitly construct $\lambda$ many source elements, and
force an injection of that set into $S$. This avoids assigning a
``cardinality'' to a proper class.

\subsection{Large-monomial Hahn notation}
Let $k$ be a set-sized field and let $G$ be a divisible ordered abelian
group, either a set or the class $\No$. We write
\[
 k((X^G))
\]
for series with coefficients in $k$ and \emph{set-sized, reverse-well-ordered}
support in $G$. Thus $X$ is a large-monomial symbol and
$X^{g+h}=X^gX^h$. It is not an ordinary real variable and does not denote
a choice of surreal exponential. The usual small-monomial convention
is recovered by $t^g=X^{-g}$.

Addition is coefficientwise and multiplication is finite convolution at
each exponent. The Hahn support lemma ensures that these operations are
well defined. If $f=cX^g(1+u)$ with $\supp u<0$, its inverse is
\[
 f^{-1}=c^{-1}X^{-g}\sum_{n\ge0}(-u)^n.
\]
The sum is a formal Hahn sum. All this is standard support calculus,
including for a class exponent group because each calculation uses only
a set of exponents \cite[Sections 8--10]{Berarducci}.

For real coefficients and $G=\No$, Conway's normal-form theorem
identifies $\R((X^{\No}))$ with $\No$ under $X^g\mapsto\omega^g$.
For complex coefficients the same identification, applied to the real
and imaginary coefficients, gives
\[
          \C((X^{\No}))\cong\No[i].
\]
A union of two reverse-well-ordered supports remains reverse well
ordered. Therefore the coefficientwise complexification is exact, not
an enlargement requiring additional supports.

\subsection{A uniform coefficient ring}
For a unital subring $D\subset k$, define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:generalring}
 \I_k(G)=\{f\in k((X^G)):\supp f\subset G^{>0}\},
 \qquad A_{D,k}(G)=D+\I_k(G).
\end{equation}
The zero series is included. Abbreviate $\I_k=\I_k(\No)$ and
$A_{D,k}=A_{D,k}(\No)$ when the exponent group is the full surreal class.
The two principal examples are
\[
 A_{\Z,\R}=\Oz,\qquad A_{\Z[i],\C}=\GOz.
\]
The notation $A_{D,k}(G)$ alone imposes no support-cardinality bound
other than set-sizedness. A strict bound will be written explicitly in
\cref{sec:cardinal}.

\begin{lemma}[Constant coefficient]\label[lemma]{lem:c0}
The map $c_0:A_{D,k}(G)\to D$ is a surjective ring homomorphism, with
kernel $\I_k(G)$ and with the constant-series inclusion as a section.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
All exponents in the ring are nonnegative. A sum $g+h$ of two such
exponents is zero exactly when both are zero. Hence the constant
coefficient of a product is the product of the constant coefficients.
Additivity and the asserted kernel and section follow directly from
\eqref{eq:generalring}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Not the standard-part map]
The map $c_0$ here is defined on a ring of generally infinite elements.
It is not the standard-part homomorphism on the valuation ring of finite
surreals. Constant coefficient extraction is not multiplicative on all
of $\No$: both $\omega$ and $\omega^{-1}$ have zero constant coefficient,
but their product is $1$.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}
Both $3\omega^\omega-\sqrt2\omega^{1/7}+5$ and
$-\omega+5$ map to $5$. Also,
\[
 f=\sum_{n\ge1}\omega^{1/n}\in\I,
 \qquad c_0(f)=0.
\]
There is no smallest positive real exponent in this support, but the
support has a positive surreal lower bound. This distinction is central
to the argument that follows.
\end{example}

\subsection{Uniform gaps below set supports}
\begin{lemma}[A positive gap below a set]\label[lemma]{lem:gap}
For every set $T\subset\No^{>0}$ there is $a\in\No$ such that
$0<a<t$ for every $t\in T$. Every set of surreal numbers also has a
strict upper and a strict lower bound in $\No$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For nonempty $T$, the cut $\{0\mid T\}$ is a separated set-sized cut,
and its value is a suitable $a$. For empty $T$, use $a=1$.
For an arbitrary set $U$, the cuts $\{U\mid\varnothing\}$ and
$\{\varnothing\mid U\}$ give strict upper and lower bounds. We use here
the standard set-cut existence theorem \cite[Section 3]{Berarducci}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Common monomial divisibility]\label[proposition]{prop:common}
Every set-sized family $\mathcal F\subset\I_k$ admits a positive
$a\in\No$ such that
\[
          \mathcal F\subset X^a\I_k\subset X^aA_{D,k}.
\]
Consequently $\I_k^2=\I_k$, and $\I_k$ is not generated as an ideal
of $A_{D,k}$ by any set.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The union of the supports of the members of $\mathcal F$ is a set of
positive surreals. Choose a strict positive lower bound $a$ by
\cref{lem:gap}. Translation of each support by $-a$ leaves only positive
exponents, so $X^{-a}f\in\I_k$ for every $f\in\mathcal F$.
In particular, each $f\in\I_k$ is a product of two members of $\I_k$,
namely $X^a$ and $X^{-a}f$. The reverse inclusion $\I_k^2\subset\I_k$
is automatic.

If a set $\mathcal F$ generated $\I_k$, the first part would place the
generated ideal inside $X^aA_{D,k}$. But $X^{a/2}\in\I_k$ is not in
that principal ideal, since its unique quotient by $X^a$ in the ambient
field is $X^{-a/2}$, which has a negative exponent.
\end{proof}

This proposition alone does not prove the main theorem. An idempotent
ideal may act nontrivially on a small module. The additional obstruction
comes from the number of multiplicatively related elements available
below a single scale.

\section{Scaled fields and the collision identity}\label{sec:collision}

\subsection{The finite algebraic mechanism}
\begin{lemma}[Scaled-field collision]\label[lemma]{lem:collision}
Let $A$ be a commutative subring of a field $F$, let $L\subset F$ be a
subfield, and let $0\ne m\in F$ satisfy $mL\subset A$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item For any ring homomorphism $\phi:A\to S$, if $\phi(m^2)\ne0$,
then $x\mapsto\phi(mx)$ is injective on $L$.
\item For any unital $A$-module $M$ and $v\in M$, if $m^2v\ne0$,
then $x\mapsto(mx)v$ is injective on $L$.
\end{enumerate}
The same conclusions hold on any chosen subset of $L$. The target ring
$S$ need not be commutative or reduced.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Since $1\in L$, we have $m\in A$. For distinct $x,y\in L$, the element
$m/(x-y)$ belongs to $A$, and the following is an identity in $A$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:collision}
           (mx-my)\frac{m}{x-y}=m^2.
\end{equation}
If $\phi(mx)=\phi(my)$, applying $\phi$ to this identity gives
$\phi(m^2)=0$. This proves the first assertion without cancellation in
$S$. For the second assertion, apply $m/(x-y)$ to the equality
$(mx)v=(my)v$. Commutativity in the source makes the resulting equality
$m^2v=0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Cardinal version]\label[corollary]{cor:collisioncard}
If $B\subset L$ is a set with $|B|>|S|$, then every homomorphism
$\phi:A\to S$ kills $m^2$. If $|B|>|M|$, then $m^2M=0$.
\end{corollary}

The module version is worth retaining rather than deriving it through
$\End_{\Z}(M)$. That endomorphism ring can be much larger than $M$,
whereas \cref{cor:collisioncard} depends on the size of $M$ itself.

\subsection{A proper-class field below each exponent scale}
For positive $a\in\No$, set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Ha}
 H_a=\{h\in\No:n|h|<a\text{ for every integer }n\ge1\}.
\end{equation}
We write $h\ll a$ for $h>0$ satisfying the displayed inequalities.
The comparison takes place in the additive exponent group. Thus
$h\ll a$ is an assertion about exponents, not about a ratio of the outer
monomials $X^h$ and $X^a$.

\begin{lemma}\label[lemma]{lem:Ha}
The class $H_a$ is a divisible convex subgroup of $(\No,+)$. The Hahn
class field $L_a=k((X^{H_a}))$ satisfies
\[
                     X^aL_a\subset\I_k.
\]
For every set cardinal $\lambda$, the field $L_a$ contains at least
$\lambda$ distinct elements.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
If $h,h'\in H_a$, then for every positive integer $n$,
$2n|h|<a$ and $2n|h'|<a$, so $n|h+h'|<a$.
Negation, rational division, and convexity follow immediately from
\eqref{eq:Ha}. Hence $H_a$ is a divisible convex subgroup. The usual
Hahn inversion remains within this subgroup, so $L_a$ is a field.
For every $h\in H_a$, one has $a+h>0$; translating a support by $a$
therefore proves $X^aL_a\subset\I_k$.

For ordinals $\alpha$, put
\[
             b_\alpha=\frac{a}{\omega^{\alpha+1}}.
\]
These are distinct positive elements of $H_a$: each denominator exceeds
every positive integer. The monomials $X^{b_\alpha}$ are distinct
members of $L_a$. Restriction to $\alpha<\lambda$ supplies the required
set of elements.
\end{proof}

Here $\omega^{\alpha+1}$ in the definition of $b_\alpha$ is the Conway
monomial in the \emph{exponent field} $\No$. The outer symbol $X$ is
kept separate to make this double use of surreal scales explicit.

\subsection{An explicit countable witness}\label{subsec:explicit}
The field language in \cref{lem:Ha} is conceptually useful, but the
contradiction needs much less. Let $0<c<b\ll a$, and put $d=b-c>0$.
Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:geometricwitness}
 q_{b,c}=\frac{X^a}{X^b-X^c}
        =\sum_{n\ge0}X^{a-b-nd}\in\I_k.
\end{equation}
Indeed, $b+nd\le(n+1)b<a$ for each $n$, so every exponent on the right is
positive. They strictly decrease with $n$. The family is a valid Hahn
sum with countable support, not a convergent series in any assumed
ordinary topology. Multiplication yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:explicitcollision}
       (X^{a+b}-X^{a+c})q_{b,c}=X^{2a}.
\end{equation}
For arbitrary distinct $b,c\ll a$, exchange them and change sign as
necessary. Thus every collision between the images of two of the
monomials $X^{a+b}$ forces the image of $X^{2a}$ to vanish.

A useful finite version, also checked by the supplied program, is
\begin{align}
 q_{b,c}^{(N)}&=\sum_{n=0}^N X^{a-b-nd},\notag\\
 (X^{a+b}-X^{a+c})q_{b,c}^{(N)}
      &=X^{2a}-X^{2a-(N+1)d}.\label{eq:finitewitness}
\end{align}
The nonzero remainder explains why replacing the source by a ring of
finite-support series would invalidate this proof.

\begin{remark}[No illicit interchange]
We never apply a target homomorphism term by term to
\eqref{eq:geometricwitness}. The series first denotes a single element
of the source ring. Only the finite multiplicative identity
\eqref{eq:explicitcollision} is then mapped to the target. Hence no
continuity, valuation preservation, strong additivity, or measurable
structure is assumed of the homomorphism.
\end{remark}

\section{The universal quotient and all set-sized modules}\label{sec:universal}

\begin{theorem}[Coefficient-ring form of the main theorem]
\label{thm:universal}
Let $D\subset k$ be a unital subring of a set field, and put
$A=A_{D,k}$ and $I=\I_k$. For every set-sized unital ring $S$, restriction
to $D$ and composition with $c_0$ give mutually inverse correspondences
\[
             \Hom_1(A,S)\ \longleftrightarrow\ \Hom_1(D,S).
\]
Every set-sized unital $A$-module is annihilated by $I$. Its $A$-action is
uniquely determined by the restricted $D$-action.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Fix $g>0$, let $a=g/2$, and put $m=X^a$. By \cref{lem:Ha},
$mL_a\subset A$ and $L_a$ contains a set of more than $|S|$ elements.
The cardinal collision lemma therefore implies
\[
                       \phi(X^g)=\phi(m^2)=0.
\]
This holds for every positive exponent $g$.

For arbitrary $f\in I$, \cref{prop:common} supplies $g>0$ and $h\in I$
with $f=X^gh$. Multiplicativity now gives $\phi(f)=0$. Thus $\phi$
factors through the kernel quotient $A/I=D$, and its factor is exactly
its restriction to the constant series. Conversely, a ring map
$D\to S$ composed with $c_0$ is plainly a ring map. Both constructions
preserve the identity element and are inverse.

For a set-sized module $M$, use instead a set of more than $|M|$
elements of $L_a$ and the module part of \cref{cor:collisioncard}.
It gives $X^gM=0$ for every $g>0$. Monomial divisibility then gives
$IM=0$. Finally, writing $f=c_0(f)+(f-c_0(f))$ proves
$fv=c_0(f)v$ for every $v\in M$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{main:class}]
Take $(D,k)=(\Z,\R)$ and $(\Z[i],\C)$ in
\cref{thm:universal}. There is one unital map $\Z\to S$, given by
$n\mapsto n1_S$. A unital map $\Z[i]\to S$ is exactly a choice of
$j\in S$ with $j^2=-1_S$, because $\Z[i]=\Z[T]/(T^2+1)$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The module correspondence is genuinely complete}
For sets $M,N$ equipped with $D$-module structures, inflate the actions
along $c_0$. An additive map $M\to N$ is $A$-linear exactly when it is
$D$-linear. Thus the correspondence also preserves all module maps.
For $D=\Z$, it identifies the set-sized representation theory of $\Oz$
with ordinary abelian-group theory.

This phrasing is not an assertion that unrestricted module theories over
$\Oz$ and $\Z$ are Morita equivalent. The regular module $\Oz$ has a
proper-class carrier and is not among the objects considered.
Likewise, the ideal $\I$ carries a nontrivial class-sized action. The
small-target condition is essential, not a removable technicality.

\begin{corollary}[No faithful set-sized action]\label[corollary]{cor:nofaithful}
No nonzero purely infinite omnific integer can act nontrivially on any
set-sized unital $\Oz$-module. In particular, no set-sized faithful
unital representation of $\Oz$ exists. For every set-sized abelian group
$M$, the only unital ring map $\Oz\to\End_{\Z}(M)$ is the integer-scalar
map induced by $c_0$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}[Nonunital maps]
The proof killing $I$ does not use preservation of $1$. Hence every
possibly nonunital map $\Oz\to S$ is $x\mapsto c_0(x)e$ for an
idempotent $e\in S$. Conversely, each idempotent defines such a map.
The universal quotient theorem is therefore not an artifact of the
unital convention, although statements about quotient rings and modules
below use that convention.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Localization and the full fields}
\begin{proposition}[The full field is a monomial localization]
\label[proposition]{prop:localization}
Let $\mathcal U=\{X^a:a\in\No^{>0}\}\cup\{1\}$. Then, as class rings,
\[
 \mathcal U^{-1}A_{D,k}=k((X^{\No})).
\]
In particular, $\Frac(\Oz)=\No$ and $\Frac(\GOz)=\SC$.
Neither full field admits a unital map to a nonzero set-sized ring, or a
nonzero set-sized unital module.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For any nonzero series $f$, its support is a set. Choose $a>0$ such that
$a+g>0$ for every $g\in\supp f$, using \cref{lem:gap} on the negatives
of its exponents. Then $X^af\in I$, so
$f=(X^af)/X^a$ belongs to the indicated localization. The reverse
inclusion is immediate from the ambient field.

A homomorphism of the full field, restricted to $A$, kills every $X^a$
by \cref{thm:universal}. But $X^a$ is invertible in the full field, so its
image cannot be zero unless $1_S=0$. The same argument applied to a
module shows that an invertible scalar would both annihilate the module
and act bijectively, forcing that module to be zero.
\end{proof}

\section{Quotients, finite congruences, and prime domains}\label{sec:quotients}

\subsection{All quotients that have set-sized carriers}
\begin{theorem}[Quotients and their universal small images]
\label{thm:quotients}
Let $A=A_{D,k}$, $I=\I_k$, and let $J$ be any class ideal of $A$. Set
$j=c_0(J)\subset D$. The induced map
\[
              A/J\longrightarrow D/j
\]
is the universal map from $A/J$ to a set-sized ring. Its kernel is
$(I+J)/J$. Moreover, $A/J$ has a set-sized carrier if and only if
$I\subset J$; in that event
\[
              J=c_0^{-1}(j),\qquad A/J\cong D/j.
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Surjectivity of $c_0$ shows that $j$ is an ideal of $D$.
Every map $A/J\to S$ to a set-sized ring, composed with the quotient
map, is by \cref{thm:universal} a map through $D$. Since it kills $J$,
the induced map on $D$ kills $j$ and therefore factors uniquely through
$D/j$. The preimage of $j$ under $c_0$ is $I+J$: if $c_0(a)=c_0(b)$
for some $b\in J$, then $a-b\in I$.

If $A/J$ itself is a set, apply \cref{thm:universal} to the quotient
map $A\to A/J$ to get $I\subset J$. Conversely, if $I\subset J$,
the constant coefficient identifies the quotient with $D/j$.
\end{proof}

For $\Oz$, this gives a complete list without any factorisation theory.

\begin{corollary}[The set-sized quotients of $\Oz$]\label[corollary]{cor:smallquot}
The ideals of $\Oz$ with set-sized quotient are exactly
\[
               \I\quad\text{and}\quad n\Oz\quad(n\ge1).
\]
Their quotients are respectively $\Z$ and $\Z/n\Z$.
Among these ideals, the prime ideals are $\I$ and $p\Oz$ for ordinary
positive primes $p$; the maximal ones are $p\Oz$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The ideals of $\Z$ are $n\Z$ for integers $n\ge0$.
For $n=0$ its inverse image is $\I$. For $n>0$, division of every
nonconstant coefficient by $n$ is allowed in $\I$, so
\[
                   n\Oz=n\Z+\I=c_0^{-1}(n\Z).
\]
The statements about prime and maximal ideals follow from the
corresponding elementary facts for $\Z$.
\end{proof}

The distinction between $n=0$ and $n>0$ matters: $0\Oz=0$, not $\I$.
The corollary classifies \emph{only} ideals with set-sized quotients.
It does not classify all prime or maximal class ideals of $\Oz$.

\begin{corollary}[Profinite completion]\label[corollary]{cor:profinite}
All finite unital quotient rings of $\Oz$ are $\Z/n\Z$. The inverse
limit of these finite quotients is canonically $\widehat{\Z}$, and the
canonical map
\[
                \Oz\longrightarrow\widehat{\Z}
\]
has kernel $\I$ and image the diagonal copy of $\Z$.
In particular,
\[
                       \bigcap_{n\ge1}n\Oz=\I.
\]
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Use \cref{cor:smallquot} and the usual divisibility transition maps.
An element maps to zero in every quotient exactly when its integer
constant coefficient is divisible by every positive integer, hence is
zero. The map factors through $c_0$, so its image is precisely $\Z$,
not all of $\widehat{\Z}$.
\end{proof}

The finite-congruence observation is elementary and is not by itself a
strong novelty claim. The main theorem explains why passing from finite
rings to \emph{arbitrary} set-sized rings reveals nothing further.

\subsection{A prime quotient with no nonzero set-sized representations}
\begin{corollary}[A domain invisible to nonzero set rings]
\label[corollary]{cor:primeexample}
Let $q=\omega^{\sqrt2}+\omega+1$. Then $B=\Oz/(q)$ is a nonzero
proper-class integral domain of characteristic zero. It has no unital
map into any nonzero set-sized ring and no nonzero set-sized unital
module.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The imported primality theorem of L'Innocente--Mantova \cite{LM}
says that $q$ is a prime element of $\Oz$. Hence $(q)$ is proper and
prime, and $B$ is a nonzero domain.
Because $c_0(q)=1$, \cref{thm:quotients} gives the zero ring as its
universal set-sized quotient. A unital map from the zero ring to a ring
$S$ can exist only when $S$ is also zero.

For the module assertion, inflate a set-sized $B$-module to a
$\Oz$-module. The main theorem makes $q$ act as $c_0(q)=1$, but the
quotient makes it act as zero. Thus the module is zero. If $B$ were a
set, its identity map would contradict the ring assertion, so its
carrier is a proper class.

Finally, no nonzero ordinary integer $n$ belongs to $(q)$.
Otherwise $n=qa$ would force $a=n/q$ in $\No$. This is a nonzero
infinitesimal and so cannot be omnific. Thus $\Z$ injects into $B$,
which proves characteristic zero.
\end{proof}

This is not a contradiction to familiar existence theorems for maximal
ideals of set rings. The source quotient is not a set. Nor does the
corollary state that a particular class maximal ideal does not exist.
It states that no quotient or representation into a nonzero set ring
can exist.

\begin{proposition}[The same obstruction without primality]
\label[proposition]{prop:unitconstant}
If $J$ is a class ideal of $\Oz$ containing an element with constant
coefficient $1$, then $\Oz/J$ has no nonzero set-sized unital module.
If $J$ is proper, the quotient is a nonzero proper-class ring.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The element of constant coefficient $1$ acts simultaneously as the
identity, by \cref{main:class}, and as zero, by the quotient relation.
\end{proof}

For example, $\omega+1$ is not a unit: its reciprocal is infinitesimal.
Thus $(\omega+1)$ is proper, and its quotient already gives a nonzero
class-ring example. The benefit of the more sophisticated $q$ above is
the additional conclusion that the quotient is a domain.

\subsection{What non-arithmetic prime quotients must contain}
\begin{proposition}[A scaled field in every non-arithmetic residue field]
\label[proposition]{prop:primereservoir}
Let $P$ be a prime class ideal of $A=A_{D,k}$ such that $I\not\subset P$.
There exists $a>0$ for which the class field $L_a=k((X^{H_a}))$ embeds
in $\Frac(A/P)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Choose $f\in I\setminus P$ and write $f=X^gh$ with $g>0$ and
$h\in I$. Then $X^g\notin P$. Let $a=g/2$ and $m=X^a$. Primality
ensures $\bar m\ne0$ in $A/P$. Since $mL_a\subset A$, define
\[
         \iota(x)=\frac{\overline{mx}}{\bar m}
         \qquad(x\in L_a).
\]
Additivity and preservation of $1$ are immediate. Multiplicativity
follows by reducing the source identity
$(mx)(my)=m(mxy)$ and dividing by $\bar m^2$.
For $x\ne0$, the identity $(mx)(m/x)=m^2$ shows that
$\overline{mx}\ne0$. Thus the unital field homomorphism $\iota$ is
injective.
\end{proof}

The conclusion is more informative than merely saying that the residue
field is large. It contains a specific Hahn field based on all exponent
scales infinitesimal relative to one positive $a$. This proposition
still does not classify those prime ideals or assert that every field
of this form is a residue field.

\section{Gaussian omnific integers and algebraic tests}\label{sec:complex}

\subsection{All set-sized representations of the Gaussian ring}
Every element of $\GOz$ is uniquely $a+ib$ with $a,b\in\Oz$. In terms
of normal forms it has complex coefficients at positive exponents and
a Gaussian integer coefficient at zero.
For a set ring $S$ and $j\in S$ with $j^2=-1_S$, the corresponding map
is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gaussmap}
       a+ib\longmapsto c_0(a)1_S+c_0(b)j.
\end{equation}
All such maps are obtained this way by \cref{thm:universal}.

\begin{proposition}[Finite matrix representations]\label[proposition]{prop:matrices}
Let $n\ge1$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The only unital map $\Oz\to M_n(\C)$ is
$x\mapsto c_0(x)I_n$.
\item Up to conjugacy in $\mathrm{GL}_n(\C)$, the unital maps
$\GOz\to M_n(\C)$ are indexed by $p=0,\ldots,n$, with
\[
           i\longmapsto\diag(iI_p,-iI_{n-p}).
\]
\item A unital map $\GOz\to M_n(\R)$ exists exactly when $n$ is even.
For even $n$ there is one conjugacy class, represented by $n/2$ blocks
$\begin{psmallmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{psmallmatrix}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The first assertion is \cref{main:class}. For the second, a map is
specified by a matrix $J$ with $J^2=-I_n$. Its minimal polynomial divides
$(T-i)(T+i)$, which has distinct roots over $\C$, so $J$ is diagonalizable
with eigenvalues $i$ and $-i$. Their multiplicities determine and are
determined by the conjugacy class.

Over $\R$, the matrix $J$ makes $\R^n$ a complex vector space by
$(u+iv)v_0=uv_0+vJv_0$. Hence its real dimension is even. A complex
basis gives the indicated real block matrix. Conversely those blocks
satisfy $J^2=-I_n$ and define the representation.
\end{proof}

No analytic hypothesis, norm bound, or finite-dimensional assumption
was used to kill the purely infinite coefficients. Finite dimensionality
enters only the final classification of the possible images of $i$.
For set-sized infinite-dimensional vector spaces, the same reduction to
an operator $J$ with $J^2=-1$ remains valid.

\subsection{Gaussian quotient rings}
\begin{corollary}
The ideals of $\GOz$ with set-sized quotient are exactly the inverse
images under $c_0$ of the ideals of $\Z[i]$. The quotient by the purely
infinite ideal is $\Z[i]$. Every other proper set-sized quotient is
finite and is a quotient of $\Z[i]$ by a nonzero Gaussian principal
ideal.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Apply \cref{thm:quotients} with $D=\Z[i]$. For nonzero $d\in\Z[i]$,
complex coefficient division gives
$d\GOz=d\Z[i]+\I_\C$. Finally, $\Z[i]$ is a Euclidean domain and
its quotient by $(d)$ has $|d|^2$ elements, as follows by the determinant
of multiplication by $d$ on the lattice $\Z^2$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Polynomial equations tested in set rings}
Let $A=A_{D,k}$ and let
\[
       B=A[T_1,\ldots,T_n]/(f_\nu:\nu\in E),
\]
where $n$ is finite and $E$ is a set. Apply $c_0$ to coefficients and put
\[
       B_0=D[T_1,\ldots,T_n]/(c_0(f_\nu):\nu\in E).
\]
The latter is a set-sized ring.

\begin{theorem}[Coefficient reduction for all set-algebra tests]
\label{thm:polynomialtests}
For every commutative set-sized unital ring $S$, composition with the
coefficient-reduction map gives a natural bijection
\[
                    \Hom_1(B_0,S)\cong\Hom_1(B,S).
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
A map $B\to S$ restricts on $A$ to a map through $D$, by
\cref{thm:universal}. Once that restriction is fixed, a choice of the
images of $T_1,\ldots,T_n$ satisfies $f_\nu=0$ exactly when it satisfies
the coefficient-reduced relation $c_0(f_\nu)=0$. This identifies both
sets of maps, and the construction commutes with maps of targets.
\end{proof}

For example, an omnific coefficient relation $\omega T=1$ reduces to
$0=1$ and consequently has no solution in a nonzero set ring under any
unital representation of its coefficient ring. Nevertheless it has the
solution $T=\omega^{-1}$ in the full surreal field. The theorem thus
pinpoints a failure of set-sized algebraic tests to detect certain
class-algebra phenomena. It does not assert that a scheme with a
proper-class coordinate ring is an ordinary scheme.

\section{Derivations: vanishing on small modules, not internally}
\label{sec:derivations}

The representation theorem has a first-order algebraic consequence,
but it is important not to overinterpret it. A derivation
$d:A\to M$ means an additive class function into an $A$-module satisfying
$d(xy)=x\,d(y)+y\,d(x)$.

\begin{proposition}[Reduction of derivations]\label[proposition]{prop:derivations}
Let $A=A_{D,k}$ and let $M$ be a set-sized unital $A$-module. Every
derivation $A\to M$ vanishes on $I=\I_k$. Restriction and composition
with $c_0$ give inverse correspondences
\[
                        \Der(A,M)\cong\Der(D,M).
\]
In particular, every derivation $\Oz\to M$ is zero.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The main theorem gives $IM=0$, and \cref{prop:common} gives $I=I^2$.
If $x\in I$, express it as a finite sum of products of members of $I$
(in fact a single product suffices here). Leibniz's rule makes the
derivative of every such product zero. Thus $d(I)=0$, so the additive
map factors through $D$. Its factor is a derivation because the action
on $M$ also factors through $D$. Conversely a derivation on $D$ composed
with $c_0$ satisfies Leibniz's rule on $A$. A derivation on $\Z$ vanishes:
first $d(1)=2d(1)$, and then additivity gives $d(n)=0$ for every integer.
\end{proof}

For $\GOz$, a derivation into a set module is determined by $u=d(i)$,
subject to $2iu=0$, equivalently $2u=0$. Every such $u$ defines a
derivation of $\Z[i]$ by $d(a+bi)=bu$; direct multiplication verifies
the condition. Hence there can be nonzero derivations in $2$-torsion
targets. There are none in targets with no nonzero $2$-torsion.

\subsection{A nonzero proper-class-valued derivation}
Let
\[
                 \chi:\No\longrightarrow\R
\]
extract the coefficient of $\omega^0$ from the normal form of its input.
On all of $\No$ it is additive, although not multiplicative, and
$\chi(1)=1$. Use it as an additive weight on the \emph{exponents} of an
outer normal form.

\begin{proposition}[An internal Euler-type derivation]\label[proposition]{prop:euler}
The formula
\begin{equation}\label{eq:euler}
 \mathcal D\left(\sum_g r_g\omega^g\right)
             =\sum_g \chi(g)r_g\omega^g
\end{equation}
defines a derivation $\No\to\No$. It restricts to a derivation
$\Oz\to\I$ and satisfies $\mathcal D(\omega)=\omega\ne0$.
Coefficientwise complexification gives the analogous derivation of
$\SC$ and $\GOz$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The output support is a subset of the input support, so the formula
always defines a surreal. Additivity is coefficientwise. At each fixed
exponent $u$, the coefficient of a product is a finite sum over pairs
$g+h=u$. Since $\chi(g+h)=\chi(g)+\chi(h)$, multiplying this coefficient
by $\chi(u)$ gives exactly the coefficient of
$\mathcal D(f)h+f\mathcal D(h)$. This proves Leibniz's rule.
For an omnific input no negative exponent is introduced, and the
constant coefficient is killed because $\chi(0)=0$.
Finally, \eqref{eq:euler} applied to $\omega^1$ gives $\omega$.
The complex argument is identical.
\end{proof}

There is no contradiction with \cref{prop:derivations}: the codomain
$\I$ here is a proper-class module with its internal multiplication
action. This diagonal Hahn derivation is not asserted to be the
Berarducci--Mantova derivation, nor to be compatible with surreal
exponentiation. No conclusion that all class-valued differentials
vanish follows from the small-module theorem.

\section{An explicit family of cardinal-sized integer parts}
\label{sec:cardinal}

We now replace the proper-class supply of scales by $\kappa$ scales,
and replace arbitrary set supports by supports of size less than
$\kappa$. The resulting statements are about sets inside ordinary ZFC.

\subsection{The two-sided exponent group}
Fix an uncountable regular cardinal $\kappa$. Within the additive group
of $\No$ define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Gamma}
 h_\alpha=\omega^{\alpha+1},\qquad
 \epsilon_\alpha=\omega^{-(\alpha+1)}\quad(\alpha<\kappa),
 \qquad
 \Gamma_\kappa=\Span_\Q\{h_\alpha,\epsilon_\alpha:\alpha<\kappa\}.
\end{equation}
The span consists of \emph{finite} rational linear combinations. Its
generators are distinct Conway monomials and are linearly independent
over $\Q$. Thus $\Gamma_\kappa$ is a divisible ordered abelian group of
cardinality $\kappa$.

For $\alpha<\beta$ one has
\[
       \epsilon_\beta\ll\epsilon_\alpha\ll h_\alpha\ll h_\beta.
\]
Here $u\ll v$ means $nu<v$ for every positive integer $n$. This notation
continues to refer to the group of \emph{outer exponents}.

\begin{lemma}[Simultaneous upper and lower scales]\label[lemma]{lem:kappagap}
For every $T\subset\Gamma_\kappa$ of cardinality less than $\kappa$
there is $\beta<\kappa$ such that
\[
       \epsilon_\beta\ll |t|\ll h_\beta
             \qquad(t\in T\setminus\{0\}).
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Every $t$ has a unique finite rational expression in the generators of
\eqref{eq:Gamma}. The union of the index sets occurring in these
expressions has size less than $\kappa$. Regularity bounds it below some
$\beta<\kappa$, chosen strictly beyond every occurring index.
The greatest monomial in each nonzero finite expression determines its
Archimedean size. Every generator in that expression lies strictly
between the Archimedean scales of $\epsilon_\beta$ and $h_\beta$.
A nonzero rational multiple or a finite sum with that leading monomial
has the same Archimedean size. The two required inequalities follow.
\end{proof}

Using both families in \eqref{eq:Gamma} is deliberate. The
$\epsilon_\alpha$ provide smaller positive gaps, while the $h_\alpha$
allow an entire support to be shifted above zero. A one-sided family
would not automatically establish the fraction-field assertion below.

\subsection{The bounded-support fields and integer parts}
For $k=\R$ or $\C$, let
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Kkappa}
 K_{\kappa,k}=
 \left\{f\in k((X^{\Gamma_\kappa})):
                          |\supp f|<\kappa\right\}.
\end{equation}
Write $K_\kappa=K_{\kappa,\R}$ and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Rkappa}
 I_\kappa=\{f\in K_\kappa:\supp f\subset\Gamma_\kappa^{>0}\},
 \qquad R_\kappa=\Z+I_\kappa.
\end{equation}
Map $X^g$ to $\omega^g$ to regard these as actual substructures of the
surreal and surcomplex fields.

\begin{proposition}[Field closure and integer parts]\label[proposition]{prop:fieldclosure}
The structures $K_{\kappa,\R}$ and $K_{\kappa,\C}$ are respectively
real closed and algebraically closed fields, with
$K_{\kappa,\C}=K_{\kappa,\R}[i]$. Furthermore,
\[
 R_\kappa=K_\kappa\cap\Oz,\qquad
 \Frac(R_\kappa)=K_\kappa.
\]
The ring $R_\kappa$ is a discretely ordered integer part of $K_\kappa$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For a single nonzero series, factor its leading term and invert its
near-one factor by the Hahn geometric series. If the original support
has cardinality $\mu<\kappa$, the support of the inverse is contained
in a translate of the additive monoid generated by at most
$\max(\mu,\aleph_0)$ elements. That monoid has cardinality at most
$\max(\mu,\aleph_0)<\kappa$. Sums and products also preserve the strict
support bound, proving field closure.

Here is a short localization proof of the closedness assertions, given
to separate them from the new cardinal claims. The coefficients of a
finite polynomial use a union $T$ of fewer than $\kappa$ exponents.
Let $\Delta=\Span_\Q(T)$. Then $|\Delta|<\kappa$, and the full Hahn
field $\C((X^\Delta))$ is algebraically closed by the standard
closedness theorem for divisible value groups
\cite[Corollary 4]{Poonen}. Every support in $\Delta$ has size below
$\kappa$, so this entire field lies inside $K_{\kappa,\C}$. The
polynomial therefore splits there. The real field has its leading-term
order, and its complexification is the algebraically closed field just
obtained. Hence it is real closed. The complexification assertion uses
only the union of the real and imaginary supports.

The equality with $K_\kappa\cap\Oz$ follows directly from normal forms.
For $f\in K_\kappa$, use \cref{lem:kappagap} on its support and choose
$\beta$ with $|g|<h_\beta$ for every support exponent. Then
$X^{h_\beta}f\in I_\kappa$, and $f$ is a quotient of two members of
$R_\kappa$.

Finally write $f=f_++r+f_-$ by positive, zero, and negative exponents.
Here $r\in\R$, $f_+\in I_\kappa$, and $f_-$ is infinitesimal.
Normally $z=f_++\lfloor r\rfloor$ satisfies $z\le f<z+1$.
The sole adjustment is when $r\in\Z$ and $f_-<0$: then take
$z=f_++r-1$. A noninteger real fractional part is separated from both
$0$ and $1$ by positive real amounts, so an infinitesimal cannot change
its interval. There are no elements of $R_\kappa$ strictly between $0$
and $1$: a nonzero positive-exponent leading term dominates every real,
and the remaining case is an ordinary integer. This proves both the
integer-part property and discreteness.
\end{proof}

The support-closure argument for one field operation uses only that
$\kappa$ is uncountable; the simultaneous gap lemma uses regularity.
This distinction prevents an incorrect inference that singular
bounded-support fields must fail to be fields.

\subsection{The small-target theorem at \texorpdfstring{$\kappa$}{kappa}}
\begin{theorem}[Ring and module bounds]\label{thm:kappasmall}
Every homomorphism $R_\kappa\to S$ with $|S|<\kappa$ kills $I_\kappa$.
Every unital $R_\kappa$-module $M$ with $|M|<\kappa$ is annihilated by
$I_\kappa$. The same assertions hold for $R_\kappa[i]$, with the
Gaussian purely infinite ideal and quotient $\Z[i]$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Fix $g>0$ in $\Gamma_\kappa$ and put $a=g/2$. For all sufficiently large
$\alpha<\kappa$, one has $\epsilon_\alpha\ll a$, by inspecting the
finite expression for $a$. Thus there are $\kappa$ many choices of
$b=\epsilon_\alpha$ with $b\ll a$.
For distinct choices $b,c$, the geometric witness
\eqref{eq:geometricwitness} uses only countably many exponents from
$\Gamma_\kappa$, all positive. Since $\kappa$ is uncountable, that
witness belongs to $I_\kappa$. The identity
\eqref{eq:explicitcollision} therefore holds inside $R_\kappa$.

If $\phi(X^g)\ne0$, the $\kappa$ elements $X^{a+b}$ have pairwise
distinct images in $S$, contrary to $|S|<\kappa$. For modules, if
$X^gv\ne0$, the $\kappa$ vectors $(X^{a+b})v$ are pairwise distinct,
contrary to $|M|<\kappa$. Thus every positive monomial is killed, or
annihilates $M$, respectively.

For $f\in I_\kappa$, \cref{lem:kappagap} gives a positive
$\epsilon_\beta$ below its entire support. Hence
$f=X^{\epsilon_\beta}h$ with $h\in I_\kappa$, finishing both arguments.
They use no property of the coefficient field beyond what is shared
by the real and complex versions.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Generation of the purely infinite ideal}
\begin{proposition}[An increasing chain of principal ideals]
\label[proposition]{prop:kappachain}
One has the strictly increasing union
\begin{equation}\label{eq:chain}
 I_\kappa=\bigcup_{\alpha<\kappa}X^{\epsilon_\alpha}R_\kappa.
\end{equation}
The ideal is idempotent and its least number of generators is $\kappa$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For $\alpha<\beta$, the difference
$\epsilon_\alpha-\epsilon_\beta$ is positive, so
$X^{\epsilon_\alpha}R_\kappa\subset X^{\epsilon_\beta}R_\kappa$.
The inclusion is strict because the reverse quotient of the generators
has a negative exponent. Every term of the union lies in $I_\kappa$.
Conversely, for $f\in I_\kappa$, a strict gap
$\epsilon_\alpha<\supp f$ makes $f/X^{\epsilon_\alpha}$ a member of
$I_\kappa$, proving \eqref{eq:chain}. This factorization also gives
$I_\kappa^2=I_\kappa$ and supplies a generating family of size $\kappa$.

Suppose $\mathcal F\subset I_\kappa$ has size less than $\kappa$.
Regularity makes the union of its supports have size less than $\kappa$.
Choose a positive $a\in\Gamma_\kappa$ strictly below this union.
Then the ideal generated by $\mathcal F$ is contained in
$X^aR_\kappa$, which does not contain $X^{a/2}\in I_\kappa$.
Thus no smaller family generates the ideal.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Exact cardinality under a stated hypothesis}
\begin{proposition}[Sharpness]\label[proposition]{prop:sharp}
If $\kappa^{<\kappa}=\kappa$, then
\[
 |K_{\kappa,\R}|=|K_{\kappa,\C}|=|R_\kappa|=|I_\kappa|=\kappa.
\]
For every $0\ne x\in I_\kappa$, the exact minimum sizes of a ring
representation detecting $x$ and of a module on which $x$ acts
nontrivially are both $\kappa$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The hypothesis implies $2^{\aleph_0}\le\kappa$. A series is specified
by a subset of $\Gamma_\kappa$ of size $\mu<\kappa$ and a function from
that subset to $\R$ or $\C$. The total number of possibilities is at
most
\[
          \sum_{\mu<\kappa}\kappa^\mu\kappa^\mu=\kappa.
\]
The $\kappa$ distinct positive monomials already give the reverse
inequality even for $I_\kappa$. The lower detection bounds are
\cref{thm:kappasmall}. For the upper bounds, the identity map on
$R_\kappa$ detects $x$, and the regular module $R_\kappa$ detects it by
$x\cdot1=x$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{main:cardinal}]
Combine \cref{prop:fieldclosure,thm:kappasmall,prop:kappachain,prop:sharp}.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[The first uncountable threshold under CH]
Under the continuum hypothesis, $\kappa=\aleph_1$ satisfies
$\kappa^{<\kappa}=\kappa$. Indeed every exponent below $\aleph_1$ has
countable cardinality, and
$(2^{\aleph_0})^{\aleph_0}=2^{\aleph_0}$.
The constructed integer part then has size $\aleph_1$. Every action on
a countable abelian group is an ordinary integer action, but its regular
module of size $\aleph_1$ sees all its purely infinite elements. Without
CH, the lower-bound theorem at $\aleph_1$ remains valid; the asserted
size of this particular ring need not be $\aleph_1$.
\end{example}

\section{Flat ideals and homological reflection}\label{sec:homological}

In this section $R=R_\kappa$, $I=I_\kappa$, and $D=R/I=\Z$.
Everything is set-sized. We do not form a class-sized free resolution
and then silently apply the standard derived-functor machinery to it.

\subsection{Flat, idempotent, and nonprojective}
\begin{lemma}[Projective ideals of rank one]\label[lemma]{lem:projectiveideal}
A nonzero projective ideal of a commutative domain is finitely generated.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Let $J$ be such an ideal and fix $0\ne a\in J$.
For any $R$-linear map $f:J\to R$ and any $x\in J$, linearity gives
\[
                      a f(x)=f(ax)=x f(a).
\]
As the ring is a domain, $f(a)=0$ implies $f=0$.
A projective module has a dual basis: elements $u_s\in J$ and linear
maps $f_s:J\to R$ such that
$x=\sum_s f_s(x)u_s$, with only finitely many nonzero terms for each
$x$. For $x=a$, only finitely many $f_s(a)$ are nonzero. Every other
$f_s$ is identically zero by the preceding observation. Consequently
that finite set of $u_s$ generates $J$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label[proposition]{prop:flatideal}
The ideal $I$ is flat and idempotent but not projective.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By \cref{prop:kappachain}, it is the directed union of the principal
ideals $X^{\epsilon_\alpha}R$. Each is a free rank-one $R$-module because
$R$ is a domain. A filtered colimit of flat modules is flat
\cite[Lemma 10.39.3]{StacksFlat}, so $I$ is flat.
Idempotence was proved in \cref{prop:kappachain}.
The same proposition shows that $I$ is not finitely generated.
\Cref{lem:projectiveideal} therefore excludes projectivity.
\end{proof}

The quotient sequence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:flatresolution}
                  0\longrightarrow I\longrightarrow R
                    \overset{c_0}{\longrightarrow}D\longrightarrow0
\end{equation}
is thus a flat resolution of length one, but not a projective resolution
of length one.

\subsection{Tensor products and extension groups}
\begin{lemma}\label[lemma]{lem:tensorzero}
If $N$ is a $D$-module, inflated along $c_0$, then
\[
                      I\otimes_RN=0.
\]
Consequently $\Tor_n^R(D,N)=0$ for every $n>0$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Since $IN=0$, the tensor product factors through the quotient of $I$
by $I^2$:
\[
       I\otimes_RN\cong(I/I^2)\otimes_DN=0.
\]
For completeness, the first isomorphism sends
$x\otimes n$ to $(x+I^2)\otimes n$. Products $ab$ with $a,b\in I$
are sent to zero already in the original tensor product, because
$(ab)\otimes n=b\otimes an=0$; this makes the inverse map well defined.
Applying the flat resolution \eqref{eq:flatresolution} now computes
all positive Tor groups as zero.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Derived reflection on quotient modules]
\label{thm:extreflection}
For all $D$-modules $N,M$ and all $n\ge0$, inflation induces a natural
isomorphism
\[
                    \Ext_R^n(N,M)\cong\Ext_D^n(N,M).
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Choose a projective $R$-resolution $P_\bullet\to N$.
The complex $D\otimes_RP_\bullet$ has degree-zero homology $N$ and
higher homology $\Tor_n^R(D,N)=0$ by \cref{lem:tensorzero}.
Its terms are projective $D$-modules: tensoring a direct-summand
presentation of an $R$-projective module gives a direct summand of a
free $D$-module. It is therefore a projective $D$-resolution of $N$.
Degreewise adjunction yields an isomorphism of cochain complexes
\[
 \Hom_R(P_\bullet,M)
       \cong\Hom_D(D\otimes_RP_\bullet,M).
\]
Taking cohomology proves the assertion. The usual comparison of
projective resolutions makes the resulting isomorphism natural.
\end{proof}

Equivalently, the surjection $R\to D$ is a homological ring epimorphism:
$D\otimes_RD=D$ and all higher self-Tor groups vanish. This terminology
is optional; the content needed here is completely contained in the
two preceding proofs.

\begin{corollary}[Small coefficient modules see no obstruction]
\label[corollary]{cor:smallext}
For every $R$-module $M$ with $|M|<\kappa$ and every $n>0$,
\[
                         \Ext_R^n(D,M)=0.
\]
More generally, for any two $R$-modules of size less than $\kappa$, their
extension groups are the corresponding extension groups of their
underlying abelian groups.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The small-target theorem gives $IM=0$, and similarly for the other
module when present. Apply \cref{thm:extreflection}. For the first
assertion, $D$ is free over itself, so its positive Ext groups over
$D$ vanish.
\end{proof}

The extension group in the last assertion is computed in the \emph{full}
category of $R$-modules, not merely by restricting intermediate terms
to small modules. That is why \cref{thm:extreflection} is useful: it is
an ambient derived-functor statement, not just an equivalence of small
module categories.

\subsection{Exact flat dimension and nontrivial large witnesses}
\begin{proposition}[Flat dimension]\label[proposition]{prop:dimensions}
One has $\fd_RD=1$ and $\pd_RD\ge2$. For every nonzero $x\in I$,
\[
                       \Tor_1^R(D,R/xR)\cong D.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The flat resolution \eqref{eq:flatresolution} gives $\fd_RD\le1$.
Because $R$ is a domain, multiplication by $x$ gives the free resolution
\[
             0\longrightarrow R\overset{x}{\longrightarrow}R
                      \longrightarrow R/xR\longrightarrow0.
\]
After tensoring with $D$, its first map is zero since $x\in I$.
This proves the displayed nonzero Tor group and hence $\fd_RD=1$.
If $\pd_RD\le1$, the kernel of the free surjection $R\to D$ would be
projective, by the usual comparison of length-one projective
resolutions (or Schanuel's lemma). That kernel is $I$, contradicting
\cref{prop:flatideal}. Thus $\pd_RD\ge2$.
\end{proof}

The nonzero Tor group does not contradict \cref{lem:tensorzero}:
$R/xR$ is not a $D$-module. Indeed $I$ is not contained in $xR$.
Otherwise a gap below the support of $x$ would show that $xR$ misses a
still smaller positive monomial in $I$. Under the cardinal-arithmetic
hypothesis its carrier must have size at least $\kappa$, by the
small-module theorem.

\begin{proposition}[A degree-one witness]\label[proposition]{prop:extone}
The sequence \eqref{eq:flatresolution} is nonsplit. In particular,
\[
                         \Ext_R^1(D,I)\ne0.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Suppose $s:D\to R$ were an $R$-linear section of $c_0$ and put $r=s(1)$.
Then $c_0(r)=1$. For nonzero $x\in I$, linearity and the quotient action
give $xr=s(x\cdot1)=s(0)=0$. Since $R$ is a domain, this forces $r=0$,
contradicting $c_0(r)=1$. Thus the extension represents a nonzero
Yoneda class.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[An explicit degree-two witness]\label[proposition]{prop:exttwo}
Let
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Jpresentation}
 F=\bigoplus_{\alpha<\kappa}Re_\alpha,
 \qquad \pi:F\longrightarrow I,
 \qquad \pi(e_\alpha)=X^{\epsilon_\alpha},
 \qquad J=\ker\pi.
\end{equation}
Then $\pi$ is surjective and $\Ext_R^2(D,J)\ne0$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Surjectivity follows from \eqref{eq:chain}. The exact sequence
$0\to J\to F\to I\to0$ is nonsplit, since a splitting would make $I$
a direct summand of a free module and hence projective.
It gives a nonzero class in $\Ext_R^1(I,J)$.
Applying $\Hom_R(-,J)$ to \eqref{eq:flatresolution}, and using that
$R$ is projective, yields the dimension-shifting isomorphism
\[
                     \Ext_R^1(I,J)\cong\Ext_R^2(D,J).
\]
Equivalently the nonzero degree-two class is represented by the spliced
sequence
\[
                  0\longrightarrow J\longrightarrow F
                    \longrightarrow R\longrightarrow D
                    \longrightarrow0.
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Exact size of cohomological witnesses]
\label{thm:extthreshold}
Assume $\kappa^{<\kappa}=\kappa$. For each $n\in\{1,2\}$,
\[
 \min\{|M|:M\text{ is an }R_\kappa\text{-module and }
                  \Ext_{R_\kappa}^{n}(\Z,M)\ne0\}=\kappa.
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The lower bound is \cref{cor:smallext}. The module $I$ in
\cref{prop:extone} has size $\kappa$ by \cref{prop:sharp}.
For degree two, $F$ in \eqref{eq:Jpresentation} is a direct sum of
$\kappa$ copies of a ring of size $\kappa$, so $|F|=\kappa$ and
$|J|\le\kappa$. Nonvanishing in \cref{prop:exttwo} and the lower bound
force $|J|=\kappa$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{main:homological}]
Combine \cref{prop:flatideal,lem:tensorzero,thm:extreflection,cor:smallext,prop:dimensions,prop:extone,prop:exttwo,thm:extthreshold}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The Gaussian variant}
The entire set-sized homological argument also works with
$R=R_\kappa[i]$, its complex purely infinite ideal $I$, and
$D=\Z[i]$. The domain property, principal-ideal chain, idempotence,
nonprojectivity proof, tensor calculation, and dimension shifting are
unchanged. In particular the quotient again has flat dimension one and
projective dimension at least two, and under
$\kappa^{<\kappa}=\kappa$ the degree-one and degree-two detection
thresholds are again $\kappa$.
The notation $\Ext_D^n$ in that variant means extensions of Gaussian
integer modules, not ordinary abelian-group extensions.

\section{Interpretation, boundaries, and further questions}
\label{sec:boundaries}

\subsection{What the quotient theorem does and does not say}
The content can be summarized by a contrast. As an internal ring,
$\Oz$ has all surreal scales available through its fraction field, has
a nonzero proper-class ideal $\I$, admits nonzero internal derivations,
and has non-arithmetic prime quotients. As a source of representations
on sets, it has no more scalar information than $\Z$.
The gap is caused not by ignoring limits but by finite identities that
become available once the source contains the appropriate Hahn sums.

In particular, neither $\Oz$ nor $\GOz$ can be faithfully realized as
ordinary set-sized matrix rings, rings of endomorphisms of a set,
or rings of functions on a set with values in a set ring. Such target
rings are sets, however large their cardinalities. This does not
preclude working in a sufficiently large set-sized Hahn subfield for a
specified problem. The rings $R_\kappa$ quantify precisely how much the
answer can depend on the permitted target size.

There is also an elementary categorical lesson. The map $c_0$ is
universal relative to set-sized targets, but it is not injective, and
it does not identify the class ring itself with its quotient. The
regular class representation lies outside the test category. The
set-sized construction shows a parallel phenomenon without classes:
a quotient can look homologically projective against every small
coefficient module while failing projectivity in the ambient category.

\subsection{Hypotheses that must remain visible}
The proofs use four different size restrictions, each for a different
reason. Individual surreal supports are sets so that a common positive
gap can be chosen. The full class of smaller exponent scales supplies
an arbitrarily large collision family. In the set-sized analogue,
$\kappa>\aleph_0$ admits the countable geometric witness, while
regularity bounds the union of fewer than $\kappa$ supports. Finally,
$\kappa^{<\kappa}=\kappa$ is used only to match the lower threshold with
an upper witness of exactly that cardinality.

Dropping one of these restrictions requires a new argument. In
particular, finite-support series do not contain the witness
\eqref{eq:geometricwitness}; arbitrary full supports in a fixed set
group need not have a positive gap; singular support bounds may preserve
field closure but not the simultaneous gap property used here.

The theorem gives no description of all automorphisms of $\Oz$, all
class ideals, or all factorisation patterns. It neither uses nor claims
to resolve a current refinement, GCD, or unique-factorisation conjecture.
Its only primality input is explicitly attributed. No first-order
elementarity assertion about $R_\kappa\subset\Oz$ is made, and no
ordinary set-theoretic compactness argument is applied to a proper-class
theory.

\subsection{Questions opened by this construction}
The following are questions arising from the present results, not
assertions that they are recognized longstanding open problems.

\begin{question}[Higher projective dimension]
For the explicit $R_\kappa$, what is the exact value of
$\pd_{R_\kappa}\Z$? Can the relationship between the cofinality of the
principal-ideal chain and higher projective dimensions be computed
uniformly in $\kappa$?
\end{question}
The current proof gives only a lower bound of two and exact
flat dimension one. Higher syzygies would be needed to claim more.

\begin{question}[Cardinal arithmetic without the matching hypothesis]
For an arbitrary uncountable regular $\kappa$, what is the least size
of a target detecting a fixed nonzero $x\in I_\kappa$ when
$\kappa^{<\kappa}>\kappa$? Is there a different integer-part
construction of size exactly $\kappa$ with the same lower-bound
property under weaker hypotheses?
\end{question}
The identity representation only gives an upper bound of
$|R_\kappa|$ in that situation. The theorem does not collapse the two
cardinals without an argument.

\begin{question}[Which non-arithmetic residue fields occur?]
Which class fields can occur as $\Frac(\Oz/P)$ for prime class ideals
$P$ not containing $\I$? Can the embedded field from
\cref{prop:primereservoir} be used to distinguish such primes?
\end{question}
The scaled-field embedding gives a necessary structural condition,
not an existence or classification theorem.

\subsection{Verification boundary and a formalization route}
A proof-assistant implementation can isolate the decisive algebra in a
small interface. First formalize \cref{lem:collision} for a subring of
a field and a scaled subfield, with a separate direct module version.
Then formalize the countable geometric witness in a Hahn-series ring
and the two inequalities ensuring all its exponents stay positive.
The cardinal step is an injection contradiction. Monomial divisibility
then finishes the universal factorization.

The class theorem needs an explicit universe or class interface, while
the $R_\kappa$ construction can be carried out entirely with set types
and cardinal bounds. The homological part should come last, after
proving the principal-ideal chain and avoiding any reliance on a
class-sized category of modules. None of these implementation steps was
executed here. The repository's previously checked support lemmas may
be useful prerequisites, but they are not a certificate for the
new results in this article.

\section{A compact direct proof for omnific integers}
\label{app:direct}
For reference, here is a self-contained version of the main argument
once Conway normal forms and set-cut existence are granted.

Let $\phi:\Oz\to S$ be a ring homomorphism into a set. Fix $g>0$ and
write $a=g/2$. Choose a cardinal $\lambda>|S|$. For $\alpha<\lambda$
let $b_\alpha=a/\omega^{\alpha+1}$ and
$u_\alpha=\omega^{a+b_\alpha}$. Distinct $b_\alpha$ are positive and
infinitesimal relative to $a$. The $\lambda$ images $\phi(u_\alpha)$
cannot all be distinct, so choose $\alpha\ne\beta$ with the same image.
Reorder the two indices so that $b=b_\alpha>c=b_\beta$, and put
\[
              q=\sum_{n\ge0}\omega^{a-b-n(b-c)}.
\]
Its support is strictly decreasing and positive, since
$b+n(b-c)\le(n+1)b<a$. Hence $q\in\Oz$, and direct Hahn multiplication
gives $(u_\alpha-u_\beta)q=\omega^g$. Thus $\phi(\omega^g)=0$.

Now take any purely infinite $f$. Its positive exponent support is a
set, so choose $0<h<\supp f$. Then
$f=\omega^h(\omega^{-h}f)$, with the second factor still omnific.
Therefore $\phi(f)=0$. Finally $x-c_0(x)$ is purely infinite for every
$x\in\Oz$, so $\phi(x)=c_0(x)\phi(1)$. For a unital map this is the
asserted formula. Replacing images of $u_\alpha$ by vectors
$u_\alpha v$ gives the module proof with the same identities.

\section{Exact finite checks and reproducibility}\label{app:checks}
The archive includes \texttt{verify.py}, using only Python's standard
library and exact rational arithmetic. It does not implement all
surreals, class functions, uncountable supports, projective resolutions,
or a theorem prover. It checks algebraic components that are susceptible
to indexing and sign errors.

The program represents a finite Hahn sum as a dictionary from
lexicographically ordered rational exponent vectors to rational
coefficients. It checks the finite identity \eqref{eq:finitewitness}
for many positive scales with $0<c<b\ll a$, verifies that each truncated
witness has positive decreasing support, checks multiplicativity of
constant coefficient on nonnegative supports, checks a diagonal Euler
Leibniz identity, and verifies the real complex-structure matrix blocks.
The generated \texttt{verification.json} records exact assertion counts.

The infinite witness is justified in the mathematical proof by its
support and the exact cancellation identity, not by extrapolating a
finite test. The cardinal contradictions and the nonvanishing extension
classes likewise rest on the written arguments.
