\begin{abstract}
Fix a set-sized ordered abelian group $\Gamma\ne0$ and the complex Hahn
field $K_\Gamma=\C((t^\Gamma))$. Entireness will mean strong Hahn
summability at every point of this one field. We prove that every entire
D-finite power series is a polynomial, in one or finitely many variables.
The one-variable result is stronger: a linear differential operator determines
one exterior valuation region in which strong evaluation of any formal
solution already forces the solution to be a polynomial of uniformly bounded
degree.

For dilation equations there is a precise alternative. If $q\in K_\Gamma^\times$
has infinite multiplicative order, a nonpolynomial entire solution of a
nonzero linear $q$-difference equation with polynomial coefficients exists
if and only if $\abs{v(q)}$ is an order unit of $\Gamma$. Thus a nonzero
valuation is not enough: its integer multiples must be cofinal among all
scales. The proof includes units whose residues are roots of unity and
coefficients with arbitrary well-ordered Hahn supports. A partial theta
series proves sharpness. A mixed differential--dilation extension is also
proved for nonzero, noncofinal dilation valuations.

The common mechanism is an elementary finite-step escape lemma: bounded
valuation costs in a coefficient recurrence create a strictly descending
subsequence of evaluated leading exponents. This contradicts strong
summability without replacing an arbitrary-rank valuation by a real-valued
norm. In an explicit surreal workspace with countable cofinality but no
order unit, the series $\sum_{n\ge0}\omega^{-\omega^n}z^n$ is entire but
satisfies no such pure differential or infinite-order dilation equation.
\end{abstract}

\section{The question and the principal results}

A familiar way to specify a special function is to give a finite linear
differential equation. Another is to give a finite relation between its values
at $z,qz,q^2z,\ldots$. Over the complex numbers both descriptions can produce
nonpolynomial entire functions. Over a surreal Hahn workspace, however,
``entire'' asks for evaluation not only at ordinary complex constants but
also at every infinite and infinitesimal scale available in that workspace.
The main question of this article is what a finite equation can accommodate
under this stronger global requirement.

We work with a fixed nonzero, set-sized ordered abelian group $\Gamma$ and
\[
   \K=\C((t^\Gamma)).
\]
The coefficient valuation is trivial on $\C^\times$. A series
$f(z)=\sum_{n\ge0}a_nz^n$ belongs to $\E$ when the family $(a_nx^n)_{n\ge0}$
is strongly Hahn summable for every $x\in\K$; the exact definition is in
Section~\ref{sec:support}. All differential and dilation equations below are
formal identities in $\K[[z]]$, not assertions about limits of partial sums.

A positive element $\mu\in\Gamma$ is an \emph{order unit} if
\begin{equation}\label{eq:orderunit}
  (\forall\gamma\in\Gamma)(\exists N\in\N)\quad \gamma<N\mu.
\end{equation}
Equivalently, the positive integer multiples of $\mu$ are cofinal in
$\Gamma$. The notation $\abs{\lambda}$ means $\max(\lambda,-\lambda)$ in the
ordered group. In particular, zero is not an order unit.

\begin{maintheorem}[Differential rigidity, with a uniform exterior obstruction]
\label{main:ode}
Let $0\ne L\in\K[z]\langle D_z\rangle$ be a linear differential operator,
where $D_z(a)=0$ for $a\in\K$. There are $\delta_L>0$ in $\Gamma$ and
$N_L\in\N$ such that every formal solution $Lf=0$ that is strongly evaluable
at even one $x$ with $v(x)\le-\delta_L$ has degree less than $N_L$.
Consequently,
\[
 \{f\in\E:f\text{ is D-finite over }\K(z)\}=\K[z].
\]
\end{maintheorem}

\begin{maintheorem}[Sharp dilation criterion]\label{main:q}
Let $q\in\K^\times$ have infinite multiplicative order. The following are
equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
\item There is a nonpolynomial $f\in\E$ satisfying
\[
  \sum_{\ell=0}^r p_\ell(z)f(q^\ell z)=0,
  \qquad p_\ell\in\K[z],\qquad (p_0,\ldots,p_r)\ne0.
\]
\item $\abs{v(q)}$ is an order unit of $\Gamma$.
\end{enumerate}
When \textup{(ii)} fails, each fixed equation has a uniform exterior
obstruction and a uniform polynomial degree bound, as in
Theorem~\ref{main:ode}. When \textup{(ii)} holds, an explicit partial theta
series supplies a nonpolynomial entire solution.
\end{maintheorem}

\begin{maintheorem}[Several-variable rigidity]\label{main:multi}
Let $F\in\K[[z_1,\ldots,z_d]]$ be strongly Hahn summable at every point of
$\K^d$. If its mixed partial derivatives span a finite-dimensional vector
space over $\K(z_1,\ldots,z_d)$, then $F$ is a polynomial.
\end{maintheorem}

The hypotheses distinguish two separate obstructions. Differential equations
have bounded valuation costs after passing to their coefficient recurrence.
Dilation equations can have costs increasing linearly with the index,
but only along the single scale $v(q)$. A scale which is not an order unit
cannot reach all of $\Gamma$, even if its multiples are unbounded in their
own Archimedean component. Strong entireness detects that failure.

The most substantial proposed contribution is the exact all-rank criterion
in Theorem~\ref{main:q}, together with its support-theoretic proof and the
uniform obstruction. Theorem~\ref{main:ode} and its multivariate extension
supply a complementary rigidity principle. We do not claim that every
rank-one specialization, or the partial theta witness itself, is new.

\subsection{Position relative to the repository and the literature}

The repository snapshot used here is
\begin{center}
\small\texttt{\sha}.
\end{center}
Its documentation already distinguishes fixed Hahn workspaces from the full
surcomplex class and contains a report on cofinality, factorization, and
scalar extension for entire Hahn functions~\cite{repo,repoentire}. The present
article does not claim those results anew. In particular, existence of
nonpolynomial entire functions at countable cofinality and all-class
polynomial rigidity are background, not the target of this work.

Stanley's D-finite/P-recursive correspondence is the standard algebraic
starting point~\cite{stanley}. We make the coefficient conversion explicit
because the valuations of the resulting recurrence, rather than ordinary
coefficient growth, drive the proof. Ramis's work treats growth of ordinary
complex entire solutions of linear $q$-difference equations and also studies
simultaneous differential and difference constraints~\cite{ramis}. The
arbitrary-rank strong summability condition here is a different global
condition; none of its order-unit conclusions is inferred from an ordinary
complex growth estimate.

Terminology also requires care. Hahn meromorphic functions in the analytic
setting of M\"uller and Strohmaier involve a different convergence framework
from the formal strong evaluation used here~\cite{mueller}. The
Berarducci--Mantova derivation differentiates surreal coefficients themselves;
it is not $D_z$~\cite{bm}. Work on operation-closed surreal subfields gives
further context for using set-sized workspaces~\cite{bg}, but closure under
that scalar derivation is not assumed in any theorem below.

The inspection was targeted: the repository's root documentation, report
catalogue, and entire-function report guide were read, together with the
primary sources identified above. It was not a line-by-line audit of all
repository manuscripts or an exhaustive bibliography search. The final
section records exactly what is, and is not, claimed about originality.

\section{Hahn support and what evaluation means}\label{sec:support}

An element of $\K$ is a formal sum
\[
 a=\sum_{\gamma\in S}a_\gamma t^\gamma,
 \qquad a_\gamma\in\C,
\]
where $S\subseteq\Gamma$ is well ordered and is a set. Its support consists
of the exponents with nonzero coefficient. For $a\ne0$ put
\[
 v(a)=\min\supp(a),\qquad \lc(a)=a_{v(a)},
\]
and put $v(0)=+\infty$. Products and sums are the usual Hahn operations.
Their valuation laws are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:valuationlaws}
 v(ab)=v(a)+v(b),\qquad
 v(a+b)\ge\min\{v(a),v(b)\}.
\end{equation}
If two summands have distinct valuations, equality holds in the second law.
Every nonzero ordinary complex number, and in particular every positive
integer and factorial, has valuation zero.

\begin{definition}[Strong summability]
A set-indexed family $(b_i)_{i\in I}$ in $\K$ is \emph{strongly Hahn summable}
if $\bigcup_i\supp(b_i)$ is well ordered and each exponent belongs to the
supports of only finitely many members. Its sum is then defined
coefficientwise, using finite sums in $\C$ at each exponent.
\end{definition}

This definition concerns the family before cancellation between different
indices. One cannot first cancel a non-summable family and then declare it
summable. Nor does the convergence of a complex numerical coefficient series
justify infinitely many contributions at one Hahn exponent.

\begin{lemma}[Three elementary support facts]\label{lem:support}
The following hold.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item If the leading exponents of a subsequence of nonzero family members
are strictly decreasing, the family is not strongly summable.
\item If $v(b_n)$ tends cofinally to $+\infty$, meaning that for each
$\beta\in\Gamma$ all but finitely many nonzero $b_n$ have valuation greater
than $\beta$, then $(b_n)$ is strongly summable.
\item Grouping a strongly summable family into finite blocks preserves
strong summability and its sum.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For (a), those leading exponents themselves form a descending sequence in
the union of the supports. For (b), fix an upper bound $\beta$. Only finitely
many supports can meet $(-\infty,\beta]$. A finite union of well-ordered
subsets of a linear order is well ordered. Any proposed descending sequence
in the full union would lie below its first member, so would already lie in
such a finite union, a contradiction. The same finiteness shows that an
exponent can occur in only finitely many members. For (c), each new support
is contained in the union of the old supports, and its contributing blocks
at any exponent form a finite set. Coefficientwise associativity is finite.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Sufficiency is not necessity]\label{rem:positive}
Let $0<\epsilon<H$ in $\Gamma$, with $n\epsilon<H$ for every ordinary
integer $n$. The family $(t^{n\epsilon})_{n\ge0}$ is strongly summable:
its support is an increasing sequence and each exponent occurs once. Yet
its valuations do not tend cofinally to $+\infty$. In the rank-two group
$\Q H\oplus\Q\epsilon$, this is the most elementary warning against
identifying positive valuation with topological nilpotence.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}[Entire power series]
The strong entire class $\E$ consists of the series
$f=\sum_{n\ge0}a_nz^n$ for which $(a_nx^n)_n$ is strongly summable for every
$x\in\K$. In several variables, the family to be tested is
$(a_\alpha x^\alpha)_{\alpha\in\N^d}$.
\end{definition}

Polynomial series are entire because all the evaluation families are finite.
An ordinary complex entire Taylor series need not be entire under this
definition. For example, at a monomial argument $x=t^\gamma$ the family
\begin{equation}\label{eq:monomialexp}
  \left(\frac{x^n}{n!}\right)_{n\ge0}
\end{equation}
is strongly summable exactly when $\gamma>0$: positive $\gamma$ gives distinct
increasing exponents, $\gamma=0$ gives infinitely many contributions at zero,
and negative $\gamma$ gives a descending sequence. Thus even the familiar
formal exponential provides a useful local test, not an entire function on
all of $\K$.

Theorems below use only the valuation laws, the existence of every monomial
$t^\gamma$, trivial valuation on the coefficient constants, and these support
facts. They do not require algebraic closedness, spherical completeness,
Weierstrass preparation, or an embedding of $\Gamma$ into $\R$.

\section{The finite-step escape lemma}\label{sec:escape}

The following statement isolates the mechanism used throughout the article.
It does not require a formula for the recurrence coefficients.

\begin{lemma}[Escape from bounded valuation costs]\label{lem:escape}
Suppose a sequence $(a_n)$ in $\K$ satisfies, for every $n\ge N$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:generalrecurrence}
 A_0(n)a_n+\sum_{j=1}^s A_j(n)a_{n+j}=0,
 \qquad A_0(n)\ne0.
\end{equation}
Suppose $\delta>0$ has the property that, whenever $n\ge N$, $j\ge1$, and
$A_j(n)\ne0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cost}
 v(A_0(n))-v(A_j(n))<j\delta.
\end{equation}
If $a_{n_0}\ne0$ for some $n_0\ge N$, there is an infinite sequence
$n_0<n_1<n_2<\cdots$, with $1\le n_{r+1}-n_r\le s$, such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:descent}
 v(a_{n_{r+1}})-n_{r+1}\delta
 <v(a_{n_r})-n_r\delta.
\end{equation}
Consequently, if $\sum a_nz^n$ is strongly evaluable at any $x$ with
$v(x)\le-\delta$, then $a_n=0$ for every $n\ge N$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Assume $a_n\ne0$ with $n\ge N$. The leftmost term in
\eqref{eq:generalrecurrence} is nonzero, so at least one later term is nonzero.
By \eqref{eq:valuationlaws}, one can choose $j\in\{1,\ldots,s\}$ such that
\[
 v(A_j(n)a_{n+j})\le v(A_0(n)a_n).
\]
Hence
\[
 v(a_{n+j})-v(a_n)
 \le v(A_0(n))-v(A_j(n))<j\delta.
\]
This gives the next index and \eqref{eq:descent}. Choosing, for instance,
the smallest admissible $j$ makes the recursion definite. All chosen
coefficients are nonzero, so the construction never stops.

If $\alpha=v(x)\le-\delta$, the same estimate gives
\[
 v(a_{n+j}x^{n+j})-v(a_nx^n)
 <j\delta+j\alpha\le0.
\]
The evaluated leading exponents therefore strictly decrease along the path.
Lemma~\ref{lem:support}(a) rules out strong summability. This proves the
contrapositive of the last assertion. If $s=0$, the recurrence itself gives
$a_n=0$ for all $n\ge N$, so the final conclusion still holds.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[A convenient uniform version]\label{cor:boundedcost}
If all the differences $v(A_0(n))-v(A_j(n))$ in
\eqref{eq:generalrecurrence} are bounded above by a single $B\in\Gamma$, then
any $\delta>\max\{B,0\}$ gives the conclusion of Lemma~\ref{lem:escape}.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
For $j\ge1$, $B<\delta\le j\delta$.
\end{proof}

The choice of an exterior scale is possible in every nonzero ordered abelian
group: take a finite maximum of the required bounds and then add any positive
element. No cofinal cyclic subgroup is required. This is exactly why the
lemma proves rigidity even in groups of very large rank.

There is also no assumption that the sequence of valuations has an
asymptotic slope or that cancellations are rare. Cancellations in the
recurrence are allowed completely. They can increase the valuation of a
sum, but they cannot make every term on its right have valuation larger than
the nonzero term on its left. The infinite path records only that finite
inequality, repeatedly.

\section{Polynomial recurrence coefficients stabilize}\label{sec:stabilize}

\begin{lemma}[Evaluation at ordinary integers]\label{lem:integer}
Let $0\ne P(X)=\sum_k p_kX^k\in\K[X]$ and put
$\beta(P)=\min_{p_k\ne0}v(p_k)$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:integerstable}
 v(P(n))=\beta(P)
\end{equation}
for all but finitely many nonnegative ordinary integers $n$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The polynomial $t^{-\beta(P)}P$ has coefficients of nonnegative valuation,
and at least one coefficient has valuation zero. Its coefficientwise
reduction is the nonzero complex polynomial
\[
 \overline P(X)=
 \sum_{v(p_k)=\beta(P)}\lc(p_k)X^k.
\]
At every integer $n$ for which $\overline P(n)\ne0$, the Hahn coefficient at
exponent zero of $t^{-\beta(P)}P(n)$ is nonzero. There is no smaller exponent,
so \eqref{eq:integerstable} follows. A nonzero polynomial over $\C$ has only
finitely many roots.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Uniform obstruction for P-recursive coefficients]
\label{prop:precursive}
Suppose
\begin{equation}\label{eq:precursive}
 \sum_{j=0}^s P_j(n)a_{n+j}=0
\end{equation}
for every sufficiently large integer $n$, with $P_j\in\K[X]$ and $P_0\ne0$.
There exist $N\in\N$ and $\delta>0$, determined by the recurrence, such that
strong evaluation at any $x$ with $v(x)\le-\delta$ forces
$a_n=0$ for all $n\ge N$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Discard the identically zero $P_j$ when taking minima. By
Lemma~\ref{lem:integer}, for all $n\ge N$ one has
$v(P_j(n))=\beta_j:=\beta(P_j)$, simultaneously for the finitely many
nonzero polynomials. Choose
\[
 \delta>\max\bigl(\{0\}\cup\{\beta_0-\beta_j:j\ge1,\ P_j\ne0\}\bigr).
\]
The escape lemma applies. If no $j\ge1$ remains, the recurrence directly
forces the same vanishing.
\end{proof}

When $\Gamma$ is divisible one can use the smaller sufficient threshold
\begin{equation}\label{eq:refinedthreshold}
 \delta>\max\left(\{0\}\cup
 \left\{\frac{\beta_0-\beta_j}{j}:j\ge1,\ P_j\ne0\right\}\right).
\end{equation}
This is a certified exterior obstruction, not a claim to have computed the
exact evaluation domain of each solution. The individual solutions can
have stricter restrictions or can terminate much earlier.

The residue polynomials in the proof also explain the degree bound. Choose
$N$ beyond the original starting index and beyond every nonnegative integer
root of the finitely many residue polynomials. Then any summable solution in
the exterior region has degree less than $N$. A polynomial solution of degree
at least $N$ would in any event contradict the recurrence at its last
nonzero coefficient, since $P_0(n)$ no longer vanishes there.

\section{Differential rigidity and its consequences}\label{sec:ode}

A formal series is D-finite if its derivatives span a finite-dimensional
space over $\K(z)$. Equivalently, it satisfies a nonzero homogeneous linear
ordinary differential equation with coefficients in $\K[z]$. The usual
D-finite/P-recursive equivalence is classical~\cite{stanley}; the following
calculation specifies precisely the recurrence needed here.

Write
\begin{equation}\label{eq:operator}
 L=\sum_{r=0}^R\sum_{k=0}^m c_{r,k}z^kD_z^r,
 \qquad c_{r,k}\in\K,
\end{equation}
with at least one nonzero coefficient. Put $f=\sum_{n\ge0}a_nz^n$. For
$n\ge m$, the coefficient of $z^n$ in $Lf$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:odecoeff}
 \sum_{s=-m}^R Q_s(n)a_{n+s},\qquad
 Q_s(X)=\sum_{r-k=s}c_{r,k}\fall{X+s}{r},
\end{equation}
where $\fall{Y}{r}=Y(Y-1)\cdots(Y-r+1)$ and $\fall{Y}{0}=1$.
Indeed, $z^kD_z^r$ takes the term of index $n+r-k$ to degree $n$ and
multiplies it by that falling factorial.

For a fixed shift $s$, the distinct derivative orders $r$ in
\eqref{eq:odecoeff} give polynomials of distinct degrees. Thus a shift group
containing a nonzero $c_{r,k}$ cannot cancel identically. At least one
$Q_s$ is nonzero. Let $s_0$ be the least active shift and reindex by
$u=n+s_0$. This gives
\[
 \sum_{j=0}^{R-s_0}P_j(u)a_{u+j}=0,
 \qquad P_j(X)=Q_{s_0+j}(X-s_0),\qquad P_0\ne0,
\]
for all sufficiently large $u$.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{main:ode}]
Apply Proposition~\ref{prop:precursive} to this recurrence. An entire
solution is strongly evaluable at the available monomial $t^{-\delta_L}$,
so it is a polynomial. Conversely, a polynomial is entire and is annihilated
by a sufficiently high derivative. This proves the displayed equality.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Algebraic entire series]\label{cor:algebraic}
An element of $\E$ algebraic over $\K(z)$ belongs to $\K[z]$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Let $F=\K(z,f)$ be the finite extension generated by the algebraic series.
In characteristic zero its minimal polynomial is separable. Differentiating
that polynomial expresses $D_zf$ in $F$, because its derivative with respect
to the algebraic variable is nonzero at $f$ and hence invertible in $F$.
The derivation therefore preserves $F$. All derivatives of $f$ lie in the
finite-dimensional $\K(z)$-space $F$, so $f$ is D-finite. Apply
Theorem~\ref{main:ode}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Rational differential systems]\label{cor:systems}
If a finite vector $Y$ of strongly entire formal series satisfies
$D_zY=A(z)Y$ with $A\in M_r(\K(z))$, every component of $Y$ is a polynomial.
The same conclusion holds for an inhomogeneous system with a rational
forcing vector, provided all its solution components are strongly entire.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The $\K(z)$-span of the components is stable under differentiation, so the
successive derivatives of each component span a finite-dimensional space.
For $Y'=AY+b$, append the constant component $1$ and use the matrix
$\left(\begin{smallmatrix}A&b\\0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[A sparse recurrence does not escape the obstruction]
The formal Airy-type equation $f''-zf=0$ has the recurrence
\[
 a_{n+3}=\frac{a_n}{(n+3)(n+2)}\qquad(n\ge0),\qquad a_2=0.
\]
Taking $a_0=1$ and $a_1=0$ yields infinitely many nonzero coefficients at
indices $3n$, all of valuation zero. At $x=t^{-\delta}$ their leading
exponents are $-3n\delta$, strictly decreasing for every $\delta>0$.
The gaps between nonzero coefficients do not help: the escape lemma follows
only the surviving congruence class.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[What transcendence has actually been proved]
Every nonpolynomial member of $\E$ is transcendental over $\K(z)$ and
satisfies no nonzero \emph{linear} differential equation over $\K(z)$.
This does not imply differential transcendence in the nonlinear sense.
An equation involving products of $f,f',f'',\ldots$ is outside the present
proof unless it can separately be reduced to a finite linear system.
\end{remark}

Theorem~\ref{main:ode} is stronger than merely excluding a globally extended
exponential. Its operator coefficients can themselves be arbitrary Hahn
series and can include infinite surreal scales. Nevertheless, there are
only finitely many such coefficients in the operator. Their leading
valuations provide a finite obstruction that one additional exterior scale
can defeat.

\section{Dilation equations and their coefficient recurrences}\label{sec:qrec}

For $q\in\K^\times$, write $\sigma_qf(z)=f(qz)$. Throughout the proof of
Theorem~\ref{main:q}, $q$ has infinite multiplicative order. A
\emph{linear $q$-difference equation} is an identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qeq}
 \sum_{\ell=0}^r p_\ell(z)\sigma_q^\ell f(z)=0,
 \qquad p_\ell(z)\in\K[z],
\end{equation}
with not all $p_\ell$ zero. We call a series satisfying such an equation
$q$-holonomic in this article; the definition includes the explicit
polynomial-coefficient condition.

Let $m=\max_\ell\deg p_\ell$ and write
$p_\ell(z)=\sum_{k=0}^m p_{\ell,k}z^k$. The coefficient of $z^{n+m}$ in
\eqref{eq:qeq}, for $n\ge0$, is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qrec}
 \sum_{j=0}^m P_j(q^n)a_{n+j}=0,
 \qquad
 P_j(X)=\sum_{\ell=0}^r p_{\ell,m-j}q^{\ell j}X^\ell.
\end{equation}
At least one top-degree coefficient $p_{\ell,m}$ is nonzero, so $P_0\ne0$.
Each remaining $P_j$ is either zero or a nonzero polynomial in one variable.

An infinite-order $q$ makes the powers $q^n$ distinct. Consequently,
$P_0(q^n)$ is zero for only finitely many $n$. This observation by itself is
not sufficient for the escape lemma: the valuation differences between the
$P_j(q^n)$ might still grow. The next two sections identify exactly how
these valuations behave.

If $m=0$, the equation already gives $P_0(q^n)a_n=0$ for every $n$. All
solutions are therefore polynomials even without any entireness assumption.
For the remaining cases we may assume $m\ge1$.

\section{Unit dilations, including residues that are roots of unity}
\label{sec:units}

\begin{lemma}[Eventual periodic valuation for unit dilations]\label{lem:unit}
Suppose $v(q)=0$ and $q$ has infinite multiplicative order. For every
$0\ne P\in\K[X]$, the sequence $v(P(q^n))$ is eventually periodic with
finite values. More precisely, if $\zeta=\lc(q)$ is not a root of unity,
the sequence is eventually constant; if $\zeta$ has order $h$, its eventual
period divides $h$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
First suppose $\zeta$ is not a root of unity. Set
$\beta=\min_kv(p_k)$ for $P(X)=\sum p_kX^k$. The coefficient at exponent
$\beta$ of $P(q^n)$ is
\[
 \overline P(\zeta^n),\qquad
 \overline P(X)=\sum_{v(p_k)=\beta}\lc(p_k)X^k.
\]
The polynomial $\overline P$ is nonzero, and the points $\zeta^n$ are
distinct. Thus the displayed coefficient vanishes only finitely often.
Since no exponent below $\beta$ can occur, $v(P(q^n))=\beta$ eventually.

Now suppose $\zeta$ is a root of unity of exact order $h$. Write
\[
 q=\zeta(1+u),\qquad \eta=v(u)>0,\qquad d=\lc(u).
\]
Here $u\ne0$, since otherwise $q$ itself would have finite order. For every
positive ordinary integer $n$, the finite binomial expansion gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:yn}
 y_n:=(1+u)^n-1,
 \qquad v(y_n)=\eta,\qquad \lc(y_n)=nd.
\end{equation}
There is no convergence assertion in this step: all powers used for each
fixed $n$ are finite.

Fix $r\in\{0,\ldots,h-1\}$. Expand the nonzero polynomial
\[
 P(\zeta^r(1+Y))=\sum_{k=0}^{\deg P}b_{r,k}Y^k
\]
and set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:betar}
 \beta_r=\min_{b_{r,k}\ne0}\bigl(v(b_{r,k})+k\eta\bigr).
\end{equation}
For $n\equiv r\pmod h$, one has $P(q^n)=\sum_k b_{r,k}y_n^k$.
Its coefficient at exponent $\beta_r$ equals
\begin{equation}\label{eq:residuepoly}
 R_r(n),\qquad
 R_r(T)=\sum_{v(b_{r,k})+k\eta=\beta_r}
                 \lc(b_{r,k})d^kT^k.
\end{equation}
The polynomial $R_r$ is nonzero: the indices $k$ are distinct and all
its indicated coefficients are nonzero. Outside the finitely many integer
roots of $R_r$, the leading coefficient in \eqref{eq:residuepoly} survives.
Therefore $v(P(q^n))=\beta_r$ eventually on this congruence class. There are
only finitely many classes, which proves the assertion.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Every unit dilation is globally rigid]\label{cor:unitrigid}
If $v(q)=0$ and $q$ has infinite order, every $q$-holonomic member of $\E$
is a polynomial. Each fixed equation has a uniform exterior obstruction
and a uniform degree bound.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Apply Lemma~\ref{lem:unit} simultaneously to the nonzero polynomials in
\eqref{eq:qrec}. Their valuations take only finitely many values after a
common starting index. All differences
$v(P_0(q^n))-v(P_j(q^n))$ are therefore bounded above by a single element
of $\Gamma$. Corollary~\ref{cor:boundedcost} applies.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[A residue root of unity does not make the dilation periodic]
Let $q=-(1+t^\eta)$ with $\eta>0$. For $P(X)=X-1$ and $n\ge1$,
\[
 v(P(q^n))=
 \begin{cases}
 0,&n\text{ odd},\\
 \eta,&n\text{ even}.
 \end{cases}
\]
The residue has order two, but $q$ has infinite order. The valuation sequence
is periodic, not the dilation itself. The escape argument uses the bounded
valuation costs; it does not replace $q$ by its residue.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Finite exceptional indices really occur]
For $q=1+t^\eta$ and $P(X)=X-1-7t^\eta$, the valuation of $P(q^n)$ is
$\eta$ for positive $n\ne7$, but it is $2\eta$ at $n=7$. The leading
coefficient polynomial at exponent $\eta$ is $n-7$. This explains why the
stabilization lemma must allow finitely many exceptions, even when the
residue of $q$ is $1$.
\end{example}

For an ordinary complex $q$ with $\abs{q}_{\C}\ne1$, its complex size does
not alter this conclusion: as an element of the Hahn coefficient field,
$q$ still has Hahn valuation zero. The usual complex modulus and the scale
valuation answer different questions.

\section{Nonunit dilations and the missing-scale obstruction}\label{sec:nonunits}

\begin{lemma}[Eventual affine valuations]\label{lem:affine}
Suppose $\lambda=v(q)\ne0$. For every $0\ne P\in\K[X]$, there are an
index $k_P\in\N$, a value $\beta_P\in\Gamma$, and $N_P\in\N$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:affine}
 v(P(q^n))=\beta_P+k_Pn\lambda\qquad(n\ge N_P).
\end{equation}
Here $\beta_P$ is the valuation of a coefficient of $P$, and $k_P$ is its
index.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For each nonzero term $p_kX^k$ the valuation after substitution is
$\beta_k+kn\lambda$, where $\beta_k=v(p_k)$. For two distinct indices,
the difference
\[
 \beta_k-\beta_l+(k-l)n\lambda
\]
is strictly monotone in $n$. Its sign is eventually constant and it can be
zero for at most one integer. Since there are finitely many pairs, their
relative order is fixed, with no ties, for all sufficiently large $n$.
There is then a unique minimizing index $k_P$. Its term cannot cancel with
terms of strictly larger valuation, so \eqref{eq:affine} follows.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[A rank-one shortcut that fails]
The minimizing index in Lemma~\ref{lem:affine} need not be the smallest
index when $v(q)>0$. In $\Gamma=\Q H\oplus\Q\epsilon$, with $H$ dominating
all $n\epsilon$, take $q=t^\epsilon$ and
\[
 P(X)=X+t^{-H}.
\]
The constant term has smaller valuation than $q^n$ for every $n$, however
large. Conversely, with $P(X)=1+t^{-H}X$, the nonconstant term remains the
minimum for every $n$. The proof uses eventual ordering of finitely many
affine functions, not the Archimedean assertion that $n\epsilon$ eventually
exceeds $H$.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[Noncofinal dilation scales force polynomiality]
\label{prop:noncofinal}
Suppose $\lambda=v(q)\ne0$ and $\abs{\lambda}$ is not an order unit. Every
$q$-holonomic member of $\E$ is a polynomial, with a uniform exterior
obstruction and degree bound for each fixed equation.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:affine}, the nonzero coefficient polynomials in
\eqref{eq:qrec} have, after a common starting index, valuations
\[
 v(P_j(q^n))=\beta_j+k_jn\lambda.
\]
For suitable $C>0$ in $\Gamma$ and $M\in\N$, every relevant difference is
bounded above by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:linearbound}
 v(P_0(q^n))-v(P_j(q^n))\le C+Mn\abs{\lambda}.
\end{equation}
For example, choose $C$ at least the maximum of the finitely many
$\abs{\beta_0-\beta_j}$ and choose
$M\ge\max_j\abs{k_0-k_j}$.

Since $\abs{\lambda}$ is not an order unit, there is $\eta>0$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dominated}
 n\abs{\lambda}<\eta\qquad\text{for every }n\in\N.
\end{equation}
The strict version follows from failure of cofinality: if the multiples
are bounded by $\eta$, equality for one multiple would be contradicted by
the next. In particular, $Mn\abs{\lambda}<\eta$ for every $n$ whenever
$M>0$, and the same upper bound holds trivially when $M=0$.
The right side of \eqref{eq:linearbound} is bounded by $C+\eta$.
Choose $\delta>C+\eta$ and apply the escape lemma.
\end{proof}

The proof handles two different higher-rank situations. The group might
have no order unit at all, so every possible nonzero dilation valuation is
noncofinal. Alternatively, the group might have an order unit but the
chosen $q$ might live on a smaller scale. Global $q$-holonomicity depends on
the valuation of this particular dilation, not only on the group.

\section{Sharpness: a cofinal partial theta function}\label{sec:theta}

\begin{proposition}[The cofinal witness]\label{prop:theta}
Let $Q\in\K^\times$ satisfy $\mu=v(Q)>0$. If $\mu$ is an order unit, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:theta}
 \Theta_Q(z)=\sum_{n\ge0}Q^{n(n-1)/2}z^n
\end{equation}
is a nonpolynomial member of $\E$ and satisfies
\begin{align}
 \Theta_Q(z)&=1+z\Theta_Q(Qz),\label{eq:thetafirst}\\
 Qz\Theta_Q(Q^2z)-(1+z)\Theta_Q(Qz)+\Theta_Q(z)&=0.
 \label{eq:thetahom}
\end{align}
If $\mu$ is not an order unit, this same formal series is not entire.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For $x\ne0$ with $\alpha=v(x)$, the $n$th evaluated term has valuation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:thetaval}
 \frac{n(n-1)}2\mu+n\alpha.
\end{equation}
Given a target $\beta\in\Gamma$, choose ordinary nonnegative integers
$M,B$ such that $\alpha\ge-M\mu$ and $\beta\le B\mu$; this is possible
because $\mu$ is an order unit. The expression in \eqref{eq:thetaval} is at
least
\[
 \left(\frac{n(n-1)}2-Mn\right)\mu,
\]
which exceeds $B\mu$ for all sufficiently large ordinary $n$. Thus the
term valuations tend cofinally to $+\infty$. Lemma~\ref{lem:support}(b)
proves strong summability. At $x=0$ evaluation is finite. All coefficients
in \eqref{eq:theta} are nonzero, so the series is not a polynomial.

The coefficient identity
\[
 Q^{n(n-1)/2}=Q^{n-1}Q^{(n-1)(n-2)/2}\qquad(n\ge1)
\]
proves \eqref{eq:thetafirst}. Substitute $Qz$ for $z$ and subtract the two
inhomogeneous identities to obtain \eqref{eq:thetahom}.

Finally, when $\mu$ is not an order unit, choose $H>n\mu$ for every $n$.
At $x=t^{-H}$ the successive differences of the valuations in
\eqref{eq:thetaval} are
\[
 n\mu-H<0.
\]
They therefore form a strictly descending sequence. The family is not
strongly summable, by Lemma~\ref{lem:support}(a).
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{main:q}]
When $v(q)=0$, Corollary~\ref{cor:unitrigid} proves polynomiality. When
$v(q)\ne0$ but its absolute value is not an order unit,
Proposition~\ref{prop:noncofinal} proves polynomiality. This gives the
necessity and the stated uniform obstruction.

For sufficiency, first suppose $v(q)>0$ is an order unit and take $Q=q$ in
Proposition~\ref{prop:theta}. If $v(q)<0$ and $-v(q)$ is an order unit, take
$Q=q^{-1}$. The resulting series is still entire. Replacing $z$ by $q^2z$
in \eqref{eq:thetahom} gives the positive-shift $q$-equation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:inverseq}
 qz\Theta_{q^{-1}}(z)
 -(1+q^2z)\Theta_{q^{-1}}(qz)
 +\Theta_{q^{-1}}(q^2z)=0.
\end{equation}
Its polynomial coefficients are not all zero. This proves the converse.
\end{proof}

The partial theta function is used as an established type of construction,
not advertised as a newly discovered special function. Its role here is
precise: it realizes the positive side of a necessary-and-sufficient
criterion, including nonmonomial $q$ and arbitrary valuation rank.
Only its leading term valuations are used to establish entireness, so
unrestricted higher Hahn tails in $q$ do not invalidate the example.

\subsection{Why finite-order dilations must be excluded}

If $q$ has order $h$, the symbolic expression $\sigma_q^h-1$ acts as zero
on every formal series. Merely requiring a nonzero expression would then
place no restriction at all. The difficulty persists even after excluding
operators with zero action. For $h>1$ and
\[
 f(z)=z^r g(z^h),
\]
one has $f(qz)=q^r f(z)$. Nonpolynomial entire $g$ produces a nonpolynomial
entire $f$, since evaluation is just evaluation of $g$ at $x^h$, followed
by multiplication by $x^r$. The operator $\sigma_q-q^r$ is not zero on all
formal series when $h>1$.

Consequently, finite-order dilations have a genuine periodic-support
exception. They are not a limiting case of Theorem~\ref{main:q}. In
particular, ``the residue is a root of unity'' and ``the dilation is a root
of unity'' are fundamentally different hypotheses.

\section{An extension to mixed differential--dilation equations}
\label{sec:mixed}

For a nonzero noncofinal dilation valuation, derivatives do not repair the
missing-scale problem. The extension is useful when a symbolic system
allows both differentiation and rescaling of the argument.

\begin{lemma}[Polynomial dependence on the index as well as the dilation]
\label{lem:bivariate}
Let $v(q)=\lambda\ne0$ and let $0\ne P(U,X)\in\K[U,X]$. There are
$\beta\in\Gamma$, $k\in\N$, and $N\in\N$ such that
\[
 v(P(n,q^n))=\beta+kn\lambda\qquad(n\ge N).
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Write $P(U,X)=\sum_k C_k(U)X^k$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:integer}, each nonzero
$C_k(n)$ has a fixed finite valuation for sufficiently large $n$.
The finitely many summands then have valuations $\beta_k+kn\lambda$.
The pairwise-order argument in Lemma~\ref{lem:affine} gives a unique
minimizing index eventually, which cannot cancel.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Mixed rigidity at a noncofinal nonzero scale]
\label{thm:mixed}
Suppose $v(q)\ne0$ and $\abs{v(q)}$ is not an order unit. If a strongly
entire series $f$ satisfies a nonzero equation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mixed}
 \sum_{r,\ell}p_{r,\ell}(z)D_z^r\sigma_q^\ell f(z)=0,
 \qquad p_{r,\ell}\in\K[z],
\end{equation}
where the sum is finite and the coefficients are not all zero, then $f$
is a polynomial. Each fixed equation again has a uniform exterior
obstruction and a uniform degree bound.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Expand $p_{r,\ell}(z)=\sum_k c_{r,\ell,k}z^k$. If $s=r-k$, the term of
coefficient index $n+s$ contributes to degree $n$ the quantity
\[
 c_{r,\ell,k}\fall{n+s}{r}q^{\ell(n+s)}a_{n+s}.
\]
Thus the coefficient recurrence has shift polynomials
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mixedshift}
 P_s(U,X)=\sum_{r-k=s}\sum_\ell
       c_{r,\ell,k}q^{\ell s}\fall{U+s}{r}X^\ell.
\end{equation}
For fixed $s$ and $\ell$, distinct $r$ have distinct degrees in $U$.
It follows that a nonempty group of nonzero coefficients cannot cancel
identically, and some $P_s$ is nonzero. Reindex from the smallest active
shift. Replacing $U$ by $U-s_0$ and $X$ by $q^{-s_0}X$ preserves polynomiality
and nonzeroness, so we obtain a recurrence with leftmost coefficient
$A_0(n)=Q_0(n,q^n)$ for a nonzero bivariate polynomial $Q_0$.

Lemma~\ref{lem:bivariate} gives eventual affine valuations for all its
nonzero coefficients. Their differences have the bound
$C+Mn\abs{v(q)}$. As in Proposition~\ref{prop:noncofinal}, this is bounded
by one element of $\Gamma$. The escape lemma proves the result.
\end{proof}

The assumption $v(q)\ne0$ is essential to this proof of the mixed extension.
For $v(q)=0$, the separate pure differential and pure dilation conclusions
have already been proved, but they are not a proof of the mixed conclusion.
No mixed unit-valuation classification is asserted here.

\section{Generic lines and several-variable rigidity}\label{sec:multi}

A single differential equation in several variables is not enough to force
polynomiality: it could ignore most variables. The correct hypothesis is
finite-dimensionality of the span of \emph{all} mixed partial derivatives.
We now prove Theorem~\ref{main:multi} under precisely that hypothesis.

\begin{lemma}[Avoiding countably many polynomial conditions]
\label{lem:generic}
For any countable family of nonzero polynomials in $\K[z_1,\ldots,z_d]$,
there is $c\in\C^d$ at which none of them vanishes.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For one polynomial $H$, take the least Hahn exponent among its finitely
many nonzero coefficients. The coefficient at that exponent is a nonzero
polynomial $\overline H\in\C[z_1,\ldots,z_d]$. If $\overline H(c)\ne0$,
then $H(c)\ne0$.

The coefficients of the countably many polynomials $\overline H$ form a
countable subset of $\C$. The field they generate over $\Q$ is countable.
Choose $c_1,\ldots,c_d$ algebraically independent over that field. This is
possible by successively choosing outside the algebraic closure of a
countable field, which remains countable, whereas $\C$ is uncountable.
No nonzero polynomial from the family can vanish at this tuple.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{main:multi}]
Suppose, for a contradiction, that $F$ is not a polynomial. Write it by total
degree as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:homogeneous}
 F(z)=\sum_{n\ge0}F_n(z),
 \qquad F_n\text{ homogeneous of degree }n.
\end{equation}
Infinitely many $F_n$ are nonzero.

Let $g_1,\ldots,g_s$ be a basis for the finite-dimensional
$\K(z_1,\ldots,z_d)$-span of all mixed derivatives of $F$. Take the $g_i$
themselves to be mixed derivatives and, since $F\ne0$, choose $g_1=F$.
Differentiating this span preserves it, so there are rational matrices
$A_i(z)$ with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pfaffian}
 \partial_i g=A_i(z)g\qquad(1\le i\le d).
\end{equation}
There are finitely many nonzero denominator polynomials in these matrices.
For each such denominator choose one nonzero homogeneous component.

Apply Lemma~\ref{lem:generic} to every nonzero $F_n$, the finitely many
chosen denominator components, and the coordinate polynomials $z_i$.
We obtain $c\in\C^d$ with $F_n(c)\ne0$ whenever $F_n\ne0$, and such that
no denominator becomes the zero polynomial upon substitution $z=wc$.
The series
\[
 h(w)=F(wc)=\sum_{n\ge0}F_n(c)w^n
\]
is therefore nonpolynomial.

For each $x\in\K$, the family
$(a_\alpha c^\alpha x^{\abs{\alpha}})_{\alpha\in\N^d}$ is strongly
summable by entireness of $F$ at $(c_1x,\ldots,c_dx)$. Group it into the
finite blocks of fixed total degree. Lemma~\ref{lem:support}(c) shows that
$\sum_nF_n(c)x^n$ is strongly summable. Hence $h\in\E$.

The formal chain rule applied after clearing the denominators in
\eqref{eq:pfaffian} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:restricted}
 \frac{d}{dw}g(wc)=
 \left(\sum_{i=1}^d c_iA_i(wc)\right)g(wc).
\end{equation}
Every denominator here is a nonzero element of $\K[w]$. It may vanish at
$w=0$, which causes no difficulty: the identity is interpreted in the
Laurent-series field $\K((w))$ and the matrices lie in $M_s(\K(w))$.
The finite $\K(w)$-span of the components of $g(wc)$ is preserved by
$d/dw$. Thus its first component $h$ is D-finite over $\K(w)$.
Theorem~\ref{main:ode} forces $h$ to be a polynomial, contradicting its
construction.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Multivariate algebraic rigidity]\label{cor:multialg}
If a strongly entire $F\in\K[[z_1,\ldots,z_d]]$ is algebraic over
$\K(z_1,\ldots,z_d)$, it is a polynomial.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Each partial derivation extends to the finite separable extension generated
by $F$, by differentiation of its minimal polynomial. Thus all mixed
partial derivatives of $F$ lie in a fixed finite-dimensional space.
Theorem~\ref{main:multi} applies.
\end{proof}

The generic line is an existence device, not a promised effective choice
of ordinary complex constants. In particular, the proof does not say that
one prescribed line detects every nonpolynomial multivariate series.
The line is chosen after the countably many nonzero homogeneous parts and
the finitely many rational connection matrices are known.

\section{Surreal workspaces that separate the theories}\label{sec:surreal}

The results so far are stated entirely in set-sized Hahn fields. This makes
the foundations transparent and allows a literal surreal interpretation
without quantifying over a proper class as though it were a set.

If $\Gamma$ is an additive ordered subgroup of $\No$, the Conway normal-form
interpretation sends
\begin{equation}\label{eq:embedding}
 \sum_\gamma a_\gamma t^\gamma
 \longmapsto \sum_\gamma a_\gamma\omega^{-\gamma}.
\end{equation}
For real coefficients this is an ordered-field embedding into $\No$; with
complex coefficients it gives the corresponding subfield of $\No[i]$.
The normal-form viewpoint is part of the standard connection between
surreals and Hahn fields~\cite{bg}. Here $t^\gamma$ is a formal monomial,
and $\omega^{-\gamma}$ denotes a Conway monomial. The notation does not
silently identify every formal power with a different choice of surreal
exponentiation.

\subsection{An explicit entire function with no differential or dilation equation}

Take
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gammainfty}
 \Gamma_\infty=\bigoplus_{j\ge0}\Q e_j,
 \qquad 0<e_0\ll e_1\ll e_2\ll\cdots,
\end{equation}
ordered so that the largest nonzero coordinate determines the sign. Each
individual group element has only finitely many nonzero coordinates. One
can identify $e_j$ with the surreal $\omega^j$.

The group has no order unit. Indeed, if $\gamma>0$ has largest active index
$j$, then $e_{j+1}>n\gamma$ for every ordinary $n$. Nevertheless, the sequence
$(e_n)$ is cofinal in the whole group. This is a countable cofinality that
cannot be generated by repeatedly adding a single positive element.

\begin{theorem}[An explicit universally nonholonomic entire series]
\label{thm:explicit}
In $K_{\Gamma_\infty}$, the series
\begin{equation}\label{eq:explicitF}
 F(z)=\sum_{n\ge0}t^{e_n}z^n
     =\sum_{n\ge0}\omega^{-\omega^n}z^n
\end{equation}
is strongly entire and nonpolynomial. It satisfies no nonzero linear
differential equation over $K_{\Gamma_\infty}(z)$ and, for every infinite-order
$q\in K_{\Gamma_\infty}^\times$, no nonzero polynomial-coefficient linear
$q$-difference equation. For every $q$ of nonzero valuation, it also
satisfies no nonzero mixed equation of the form \eqref{eq:mixed}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Fix $x\ne0$ and write $\alpha=v(x)\in\Gamma_\infty$. The $n$th term in
\eqref{eq:explicitF} has valuation $e_n+n\alpha$. Given any
$\beta\in\Gamma_\infty$, choose $j$ larger than the active coordinate
indices of both $\alpha$ and $\beta$. For all $n>j$, the largest active
coordinate of $e_n+n\alpha-\beta$ is $n$, with coefficient $1$. Therefore
$e_n+n\alpha>\beta$. The valuations tend cofinally to $+\infty$, and
Lemma~\ref{lem:support}(b) proves entireness. At zero only the constant term
remains. All coefficients are nonzero, so $F$ is nonpolynomial.

The differential conclusion follows from Theorem~\ref{main:ode}. For any
infinite-order $q$, the group has no order unit, so Theorem~\ref{main:q}
excludes a $q$-difference equation. If $v(q)\ne0$, the mixed exclusion follows
from Theorem~\ref{thm:mixed}. Rational coefficient equations can be cleared
of denominators to reduce them to the polynomial-coefficient setting.
\end{proof}

The universal quantifier over $q$ in this theorem is important. The series
has a simple explicit rule for its coefficients, but no choice of one
infinite-order dilation inside its own workspace converts it into a linear
$q$-difference special function. This is a statement about finite linear
annihilating equations, not a statement that the coefficient rule is
uncomputable or lacks a finite description.

\subsection{Why the same formula is not entire on all surcomplex numbers}

Let $H=\omega^\omega$, which dominates every positive element of
$\Gamma_\infty$, and enlarge the group to
$\Delta=\Gamma_\infty+\Q H$. At the surcomplex point
\[
 x=\omega^H=t^{-H},
\]
the evaluated leading exponents in the Hahn convention are
\[
 e_n-nH.
\]
Their successive differences are $e_{n+1}-e_n-H<0$. Hence the defining
family is not strongly summable. This explicit obstruction prevents the
fixed-workspace theorem from being misread as an assertion of a
nonpolynomial power series entire on all of $\No[i]$.

The repository's scalar-extension report already studies this general type
of boundary~\cite{repoentire}. The calculation here is included only to
state the domain of the new example unambiguously; no new universal
scalar-extension theorem is claimed.

\subsection{Two dilations in one rank-two field}

Let $\Gamma=\Q H\oplus\Q\epsilon$, with $H$ dominating every
$n\epsilon$. Then $H$ is an order unit, but $\epsilon$ is not. This gives
a particularly concrete contrast:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}l l X@{}}
\toprule
Dilation $q$ & Its valuation & Nonpolynomial entire $q$-holonomic solution?\\
\midrule
$t^H$ & $H$ & Yes: $\Theta_{t^H}$.\\
$t^{-H}$ & $-H$ & Yes: $\Theta_{t^H}$, using \eqref{eq:inverseq}.\\
$t^\epsilon$ & $\epsilon$ & No: positive, but noncofinal.\\
$1+t^H$ & $0$ & No: an infinite-order unit.\\
$2$ & $0$ & No: complex size does not change Hahn valuation.\\
$-1$ & $0$ & Outside the theorem: periodic-support exceptions exist.\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

Thus finite valuation rank alone is not the criterion. Even within one
rank-two field, changing the scale of $q$ changes the existence question.
The distinction persists for nonmonomial dilations, because the theorem
uses the valuation rather than a requirement that $q$ itself be a monomial.

\begin{corollary}[Detecting order units by functional equations]
\label{cor:detect}
For a nonzero ordered abelian group $\Gamma$, the following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
\item $\Gamma$ has an order unit.
\item Some infinite-order dilation $q\in\K^\times$ admits a nonpolynomial
strongly entire solution of a nonzero linear polynomial-coefficient
$q$-difference equation.
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Use Theorem~\ref{main:q}; for the forward implication take $q=t^\mu$ for an
order unit $\mu$.
\end{proof}

This recovers a property of the ordered group from a global functional
equation existence question. The field in \eqref{eq:gammainfty} shows why
existence of nonpolynomial entire functions by itself is strictly weaker.

\subsection{Real coefficients and degenerate value groups}

The one-variable proofs work over any characteristic-zero coefficient field
with trivial valuation. The root-of-unity argument uses only finitely many
ordinary polynomial roots; it needs no algebraic closedness. All theorems
also hold over $\R((t^\Gamma))$: the generic-line proof uses uncountability
of $\R$ in place of uncountability of $\C$. Thus the results concern surreal
as well as surcomplex workspaces.

We assumed $\Gamma\ne0$ to speak of positive exterior scales. When
$\Gamma=0$, strong evaluation at $x=1$ already permits only finitely many
nonzero coefficients, because every nonzero term has support $\{0\}$.
Entire series are then polynomials for the immediate support reason.

\section{Consequences for symbolic representation and proof engineering}
\label{sec:implementation}

The classification has a direct representational consequence. A system
whose nonpolynomial special functions are represented only by finite linear
differential equations cannot cover the nonpolynomial strong entire
functions of a Hahn workspace. Allowing linear dilation equations enlarges
the class only when a cofinal dilation scale is available. In the
no-order-unit example, an exact coefficient rule such as
\eqref{eq:explicitF} lies outside both pure equation formats.

This does not rule out exact symbolic computation with these functions.
It specifies what a faithful representation needs to keep separate:
the coefficient-generation rule, the exponent group, the strong evaluation
domain, and the type of finite equation being asserted. A field-independent
label such as ``entire theta function'' discards relevant information.
For $\Theta_Q$, the proof certificate is not merely $v(Q)>0$; it includes
that $v(Q)$ is an order unit of the selected group.

\subsection{A finite obstruction certificate}

For a differential operator the mathematical reduction yields a concrete
certificate structure. First compute the finite shift polynomials
$Q_s$ in \eqref{eq:odecoeff}, and shift the least active index to zero.
For each resulting $P_j$, record its least coefficient valuation $\beta_j$
and the corresponding nonzero complex residue polynomial. Record an integer
$N$ beyond their nonnegative integer roots and the recurrence's starting
index. Finally, record a positive $\delta$ satisfying the finitely many
cost inequalities. The escape lemma then certifies that every formal
solution strongly evaluable at $v(x)\le-\delta$ has degree less than $N$.

For a unit dilation, the same certificate uses the finite residue classes
and polynomials $R_r$ in \eqref{eq:residuepoly}. For a nonunit dilation,
it uses the eventual affine winners in Lemma~\ref{lem:affine} and a witness
$\eta$ to noncofinality as in \eqref{eq:dominated}. These are mathematically
finite certificate schemas; they are not a universal algorithm for arbitrary
Hahn inputs. Comparing arbitrary surreal values, extracting arbitrary
supports, or deciding cofinality has not been made computable by the proof.

\subsection{What a Lean implementation would have to establish}

The most reusable formalization target is Lemma~\ref{lem:escape}, separated
from the rest of the analysis. It requires a discrete index sequence, a
valuation into an ordered additive group, and a finite-successor selection.
The contradiction then reduces to the impossibility of a strictly
descending sequence in the support of a strongly summable family.

The next targets are the two valuation stabilization lemmas. The integer
case is finite polynomial reduction. The unit-dilation case is a finite
binomial expansion plus a leading-coefficient polynomial on each residue
class. Only after these are established does one need the elementary
operator-to-recurrence conversions. The several-variable extension adds
countable polynomial avoidance and generic restriction of a finite rational
differential system; it need not be part of the first implementation stage.

No repository files have been modified for this article, and the supplied
Python checks are not a Lean formalization. The proposed decomposition is
a dependency plan, not a claim that these new statements already occur in
the repository's verified code.

\section{Originality assessment and questions left open}
\label{sec:status}

\subsection{What is established background and what is proposed here}

The D-finite/P-recursive correspondence, ordinary properties of Hahn fields,
finite polynomial root bounds, and partial theta-type series are established
material. The repository's cofinality and scalar-extension work is also
explicitly treated as prior project material. We do not claim to originate
any of these ingredients or to have disproved a published theorem about a
different analytic function class.

The proposed research contribution is their combination into the sharp
arbitrary-rank dilation classification, especially the necessity argument
for all noncofinal scales, the unit case with root-of-unity residue, and the
uniform exterior obstruction applying to every solution of a fixed equation.
The mixed noncofinal extension, generic-line rigidity, and the explicit
function excluded from every infinite-order pure dilation equation are
further consequences proved in this manuscript.

The targeted literature check located the standard D-finite framework and
classical complex $q$-difference growth theory, but did not establish whether
an identical arbitrary-rank strong-Hahn formulation appears elsewhere.
Accordingly, this is a proposed original result with proofs, not a certified
claim of bibliographic priority. The paper does not claim a solution of a
named, previously published open conjecture. Independent mathematical review
is still needed, and no machine-checked correctness is asserted.

\subsection{Further mathematical questions}

\begin{question}[Nonlinear differential equations]\label{q:nonlinear}
Can a nonpolynomial $f\in\E$ satisfy a nonzero nonlinear algebraic
differential equation over $\K(z)$? Under which hypotheses on $\Gamma$ can
linear rigidity be strengthened to differential algebraic rigidity?
\end{question}

The escape proof does not answer this. A nonlinear differential relation
produces convolution sums in the coefficients rather than a bounded-step
linear recurrence. Treating it as though it had the form
\eqref{eq:generalrecurrence} would be an invalid extension.

\begin{question}[Mixed operators at unit valuation]\label{q:mixedunit}
For an infinite-order $q$ with $v(q)=0$, must every strongly entire solution
of a nonzero mixed equation \eqref{eq:mixed} be a polynomial?
\end{question}

The unresolved point in the present method is uniform control of
$v(P(n,q^n))$ for general bivariate $P$ in the unit case. The pure
$q$-difference proof controls $P(q^n)$, and the differential proof controls
$P(n)$; neither statement by itself handles their combination. This is a
question suggested by the argument, not asserted to be a previously
catalogued open problem.

\begin{question}[Optimal exterior regions]\label{q:optimal}
For a fixed differential or rigid dilation operator, can one compute the
largest common strong evaluation domain of its formal solution space from
finite valued operator data, rather than only a sufficient exterior
obstruction?
\end{question}

The thresholds supplied here are uniform and explicit once the valuation
certificates are available, but need not be optimal. Distinct solutions may
have cancellations or terminating tails that change their individual
domains. A full answer should retain arbitrary Hahn tails rather than use
only a numerical radius.

\begin{question}[Several independent dilations]\label{q:severaldilations}
What is the corresponding existence criterion for a nonpolynomial strongly
entire solution constrained by equations using several multiplicatively
independent dilations? Is an appropriate cofinality condition on their
joint valuation data sufficient, or do compatibility relations impose
additional obstructions?
\end{question}

The single-dilation theorem identifies the exact obstruction when only one
scale is repeatedly added. With several dilation directions, distinct
monomials can have equal valuation slopes, and their leading coefficients
can themselves produce nontrivial recurrence phenomena. No unproved
multidilation classification is implied by Theorem~\ref{main:q}.

\subsection{Conclusion}

Strong entireness over a surreal Hahn workspace imposes a global scale
requirement that a finite linear differential equation cannot meet unless
its solution terminates. A dilation equation can meet that requirement,
but exactly when its valuation reaches every scale through integer
multiples. The decisive distinction is therefore not simply between
Archimedean and non-Archimedean fields, nor between rank one and higher rank.
It is between \emph{a cofinal dilation scale} and \emph{a scale trapped below
another one}. The descending-support proof makes this distinction without
any hidden completeness or convergence assumption.
