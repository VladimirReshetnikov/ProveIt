\begin{abstract}
\noindent\small
Let $K=\C((t^\Gamma))$ be a Hahn field, with no restriction on the rank
or divisibility of the set-sized ordered abelian group $\Gamma$. For
$q\in K$ of positive valuation $\alpha$, we identify the exact domain on
which the classical bilateral theta and Tate coordinate families are
strongly Hahn-summable: it is the multiplicative subgroup $U_q$ whose
valuations belong to the principal convex subgroup generated by $\alpha$.
Outside this domain, leading exponents themselves contain a strictly
decreasing sequence. Nevertheless, the resulting map gives an isomorphism
$U_q/q^\Z\simeq E_q(K)$ onto \emph{all} rational points of the Tate elliptic
curve. Two independent proofs are given. The first combines a rank-one
coarsening inside the principal convex subgroup with unrestricted formal
evaluation at infinitesimals for a second, coarser valuation. The second
uses no rank-one completion: an integral formal chart at the node, in which
the smoothing becomes $UV=q$, together with a smooth-residue chart and a
chart at infinity, inverts the parametrization explicitly, over any
coefficient field of characteristic zero. Neither proof mistakes Hahn
summation for convergence at every surreal scale.

We also prove compatibility with exponent embeddings, an exact reduction
sequence, a valuation-circle quotient, equality of generated support
monoids in the two coordinate systems of the node chart, exact valuation
identities including a period-sensitivity formula, a kernel obstruction to
extending the map beyond $U_q$, support-preserving recovery of $q$
from $j$, and the formula
$K(E_q[n])=K_{\Gamma+\Z\alpha/n}$ for the full torsion field. Every elliptic
curve over $\No[i]$ with negative valuation of its $j$-invariant is covered,
using set-sized normal-form workspaces. The period-bounded quotient itself
has a precedent in the logarithmic Picard theory of Molcho and Wise.

A second part treats several periods with monomial exponents. Every
admissible lattice in a finite-dimensional higher-rank inner-product space
has a finite positively generating set, which answers affirmatively, and
constructively, Question~11.14 of Amini and Nicolussi as printed in their
2025 article. We characterize exactly when a quadratic lattice family is
strongly Hahn-summable, determine the exact domain of multiscale theta
series and their support order type $\omega^s$, certify theta minima by
finitely many inequalities, and lift smooth initial zeros.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Keywords.} Surcomplex numbers; Hahn series;
Tate curve; convex valuation subgroup; strong summability; formal groups;
ramification; higher-rank lattices; theta series.
\end{abstract}

\section{The problem and the principal results}\label{tate:sec:intro}

The classical multiplicative uniformization of an elliptic curve uses a
parameter $u\in k^\times$ modulo powers of a small element $q$. Over a
complete real-valued non-Archimedean field, Tate's theorem supplies the
isomorphism $k^\times/q^\Z\simeq E_q(k)$ \cite{Tate,Silverman}.
Replacing $k$ by a Hahn field of arbitrary rank is not a harmless change
of coefficients. A positive value $\alpha=v(q)$ need not dominate all
other values by its integer multiples. The same bilateral series can then
have non-well-ordered support.

There are two different questions. Which parameters admit the literal
Hahn formulas? And, after excluding the inadmissible parameters, do those
formulas still reach every elliptic-curve point? The second question does
not follow from a support calculation. It is the central issue addressed
here.

Throughout, $\Gamma$ is a set-sized ordered abelian group, written
additively, and
\[
 K=K_\Gamma:=\K{\Gamma}.
\]
The coefficient field $\C$ has the trivial valuation. Every integer,
degree, and index $n\in\Z$ in a series is an \emph{ordinary finite}
integer, not a surreal integer. We use $v(t^\gamma)=\gamma$ and
$v(0)=+\infty$.

\begin{definition}[The admissible group]\label{tate:def:domain}
For $q\in K^\times$ with $\alpha=v(q)>0$, put
\begin{align}
 H_\alpha&=\{\gamma\in\Gamma: -N\alpha\le\gamma\le N\alpha
                    \text{ for some }N\in\N\},\label{tate:eq:H}\\
 U_q&=v^{-1}(H_\alpha)\subseteq K^\times.\label{tate:eq:U}
\end{align}
Thus $H_\alpha$ is the smallest convex subgroup of $\Gamma$ containing
$\alpha$. We call $U_q$ the \emph{Hahn-admissible multiplicative domain}.
\end{definition}

Define the standard Tate coefficients
\begin{align}
 s_k(q)&=\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{n^kq^n}{1-q^n},\label{tate:eq:sk}\\
 a_4(q)&=-5s_3(q),&
 a_6(q)&=-\frac{5s_3(q)+7s_5(q)}{12},\label{tate:eq:ak}\\
 E_q&:\quad y^2+xy=x^3+a_4(q)x+a_6(q).\label{tate:eq:curve}
\end{align}
The point at infinity is denoted $O$. These definitions are legitimate
strong Hahn sums; the discriminant is nonzero by \cref{tate:prop:discriminant}.

\begin{theorem}[Exact-domain Hahn--Tate uniformization]\label{tate:thm:main}
Let $q\in K_\Gamma$ satisfy $v(q)=\alpha>0$.
The bilateral family
\begin{equation}
 \Theta_q(u)=\sum_{n\in\Z}(-1)^nq^{n(n-1)/2}u^n\label{tate:eq:theta}
\end{equation}
is strongly Hahn-summable if and only if $u\in U_q$.
For $u\in K^\times\setminus q^\Z$, each of the two bilateral families defining
\begin{align}
 X_q(u)&=\sum_{n\in\Z}\frac{q^nu}{(1-q^nu)^2}-2s_1(q),\label{tate:eq:X}\\
 Y_q(u)&=\sum_{n\in\Z}\frac{(q^nu)^2}{(1-q^nu)^3}+s_1(q)\label{tate:eq:Y}
\end{align}
is strongly Hahn-summable if and only if $u\in U_q$.

The map
\begin{equation}
 \Phi_q(u)=
 \begin{cases}(X_q(u),Y_q(u)),&u\notin q^\Z,\\ O,&u\in q^\Z,
 \end{cases}\label{tate:eq:phi}
\end{equation}
is a surjective group homomorphism $U_q\to E_q(K)$ with kernel $q^\Z$.
In particular,
\begin{equation}\boxed{\quad U_q/q^\Z\ \simeq\ E_q(K_\Gamma).\quad}\label{tate:eq:main}
\end{equation}
No divisibility, countability, finite-rank, or real-valuedness assumption
on $\Gamma$ is needed.
\end{theorem}

The summability assertions are proved in \cref{tate:sec:theta,tate:sec:coordinates};
the group and surjectivity assertions are proved in \cref{tate:sec:uniformization}.
The qualification ``the bilateral families'' matters: this is an exact
maximality theorem for specified expressions, not a claim that no different
function can be assigned outside $U_q$. What \emph{is} proved about
extensions beyond $U_q$ is an algebraic obstruction: no homomorphism
$K^\times\to E_q(K)$ extending $\Phi_q$ has kernel $q^\Z$ unless
$H_\alpha=\Gamma$ (\cref{tate:node:thm:obstruction}).

\Cref{tate:thm:main} is proved twice, by independent routes.
The first route (\cref{tate:sec:coarse,tate:sec:germs,tate:sec:uniformization})
runs Tate's complete rank-one theorem in the residue workspace
$K_{H_\alpha}$ and lifts through a coarser valuation. The second route
(\cref{tate:node:sec:second}) uses neither Tate's complete-field theorem nor
any rank-one completion. An integral formal chart at the node, together with
two elementary formal charts, inverts $\Phi_q$ point by point; the group
law comes from Tate's universal cleared secant identities. The second route
works with $\C$ replaced by an arbitrary field of characteristic zero
(\cref{tate:node:thm:main}); in particular it gives the real surreal case
(\cref{tate:node:cor:real}). The first route yields in addition the coarse
reduction diagram of \cref{tate:cor:diagram}, which the second does not.
The generality of the coefficient field is confined to
\cref{tate:thm:main}, to the material of
\cref{tate:node:sec:second,tate:node:sec:metric,tate:node:sec:filtration},
and to \cref{tate:node:cor:real};
the torsion, modular-inversion and classification results below are stated,
and needed, over $\C$.

\begin{theorem}[Exact arithmetic and scalar extension]\label{tate:thm:arithmetic-main}
For an integer $n\ge2$, let
$\Lambda=\Gamma+\Z\alpha/n\subseteq\Gamma\otimes_\Z\Q$.
Inside $K_{\Gamma\otimes\Q}$ one has
\begin{equation}
 K_\Gamma(E_q[n])=K_\Gamma(q^{1/n})=K_\Lambda,\qquad
 [K_\Lambda:K_\Gamma]=\ord(\alpha+n\Gamma)\le n.\label{tate:eq:torsion-main}
\end{equation}
A single point $\Phi_q(q^{1/n})$ generates this field through its
coordinates. The extension is cyclic and totally ramified.

An ordered embedding of exponent groups preserves the Hahn formulas and
$\Phi_q$. In a larger Hahn field the exact parameter domain is again the
inverse image of the principal convex subgroup generated by the image of
$\alpha$; it need not be the whole multiplicative group.
\end{theorem}

The torsion description is classical in its Kummer origin. The point of
\cref{tate:thm:arithmetic-main} is the explicit arbitrary-group ramification
formula and its compatibility with the domain theorem, not a claim to
have discovered Tate torsion.

\begin{corollary}[Surcomplex elliptic curves]\label{tate:cor:surcomplex-main}
Fix the Conway normal-form valuation on $\No[i]$, with
$v(\omega^{-\gamma})=\gamma$. For every nonzero $q$ with $\alpha=v(q)>0$,
\begin{equation}
 \{u\in\No[i]^\times: |v(u)|\le N\alpha\text{ for some }N\in\N\}/q^\Z
       \ \simeq\ E_q(\No[i]).\label{tate:eq:No-main}
\end{equation}
Every elliptic curve $E$ over $\No[i]$ with $v(j(E))<0$ is isomorphic to
an $E_q$ of this kind. The parameter $q$ is uniquely determined by $j(E)$.
These are class statements whose every instance and proof is realized in
a set-sized Hahn workspace.
\end{corollary}

\subsection{Why the result is not an entire-function substitution}

If $H_\alpha=\Gamma$, the parameter domain is all of $K^\times$.
For a nonzero real-valued group this always happens. If
$\Gamma=\R\oplus_{\mathrm{lex}}\R$ and $\alpha=(0,1)$, however,
$H_\alpha=\{0\}\oplus\R$. Elements of valuation $(1,0)$ or $(-1,0)$
are excluded. Nevertheless, \emph{all} $K$-points of $E_q$ are represented.
In particular the restriction does not mean that only points whose
coordinates have $q$-bounded valuations are reached.

The accepted parameter $u=1+h$, where $v(h)$ dominates every $N\alpha$,
already produces points with
\[
 X_q(1+h)\sim h^{-2},\qquad Y_q(1+h)\sim-h^{-3}.
\]
Their coordinates can be vastly larger than every integral power of
$q^{-1}$. Smallness of $u$ and smallness of $u-1$ are different questions.

\subsection{Relation to the repository and to the literature}\label{tate:sec:position}

The repository audit of source~01 was targeted: its document map, the root
description, the spectral report, and the rank-one analytic report were
inspected, and code search was used for elliptic/Tate terminology. The
pinned revision is \sha\ \cite{Repo}; the pins of the other two sources are
recorded in \cref{tate:sec:sources}. The closest existing report works over the fixed
field $\C((t^\R))$ and develops Tate \emph{algebras}, disks, annuli, and a
partial-theta example \cite{RepoRankOne}. A Tate algebra is not a Tate
elliptic curve. The present paper neither reclaims that analytic
infrastructure nor repeats the partial-theta zero calculation.

That report is emphatic that its base is the fixed rank-one field
$\C((t^\R))$, and it does not treat higher rank as a cosmetic variant.
Its \texttt{prop:rankobstruction} proves that the ordered group
$\Q+\Q\omega\subset\No$ admits no order-preserving injective additive map
to $\R$: since $n\cdot1<\omega$ for every positive integer $n$, an
Archimedean target cannot receive both scales faithfully. A higher-rank
valuation therefore cannot be silently scalarized; as that report puts
it, a noninjective order-preserving homomorphism discards an entire
layer of scale, and in its example it must kill $1$. The
present paper is the arbitrary-rank work that \cite{RepoRankOne}
explicitly declines to attempt, carried out on a different object. We
take the obstruction as binding rather than as an obstacle to be argued
away: the real-valued coarsening used below in \cref{tate:lem:rank-one}
is applied only inside $K_{H_\alpha}$, where $\alpha$ is an order unit,
and it is never presented as a faithful image of $\Gamma$ itself. The
valuations discarded by that coarsening are exactly the ones recovered
in \cref{tate:sec:coarse} by formal evaluation, not by a norm.

Tate's original proof explicitly starts with a nontrivial real-valued
absolute value and proves uniformization over any complete field with
that absolute value \cite{Tate}. Modern treatments include
\cite{Silverman,BGR}; the 2026 work of Eterovi\'c--Vermeulen concerns
$p$-adic definability and Tate uniformization \cite{EV}. Higher-rank
analytic geometry already exists, notably the Hahn analytification of
Foster--Ranganathan \cite{FR}. Thus neither ``higher-rank geometry'' nor
``non-Archimedean elliptic functions'' is new here. Tate's own account
already separates universal formal identities from their analytic
specialization, and the second proof below uses exactly those identities
\cite{Tate}. Formal models of the nodal Tate degeneration are standard as
well; Conrad's account places the Tate curve over $\Z[[q]]$ in its modular
context \cite{Conrad}. The Eterovi\'c--Vermeulen result is a definability
theorem on $\C_p$, not a theorem about strong Hahn summability at arbitrary
rank. Neumann's support lemma, Gonshor's normal forms and the summability
conventions of Berarducci--Mantova supply the remaining foundations
\cite{Neumann,Gonshor,BM}.

\paragraph{The bounded-period quotient has a precedent.}
Molcho and Wise impose \emph{bounded monodromy} in the logarithmic and
tropical Picard groups \cite{MW}. Their Definition~2.1.3.1 calls an element
$\beta$ of a partially ordered abelian group \emph{bounded by} $\delta\ge0$
when $m\delta\le\beta\le n\delta$ for some integers $m,n$; Definition~3.5.5
defines bounded monodromy, and Definition~3.6.1 defines the tropical
Jacobian as the bounded part of $\operatorname{Hom}(H_1,M^{\mathrm{gp}})$
modulo $H_1$. In their \S5.1, on the Tate curve, the logarithmic Picard
group $\operatorname{LogPic}^0$ is the subfunctor of $\mathbf G_{\log}$
bounded by the loop length $\delta$, modulo the lattice generated by a lift
of $\delta$, and their tropicalization sequence (5.1.4) exhibits it as an
extension of the bounded tropical quotient by $\mathbf G_m$. For an ordered
value group and the single length $\alpha=v(q)$, the bounded subgroup is
exactly $H_\alpha$ of \cref{tate:def:domain}, the quotient
$\operatorname{LogPic}^0$ is the log-geometric form of $U_q/q^\Z$, and
(5.1.4) has the shape of the value sequence of \cref{tate:thm:tropical}.
Neither the need for boundedness nor the abstract shape of the
period-bounded quotient is therefore claimed as new here. Source~01 did not
know this precedent; source~03 identified it, and it is credited throughout
this report. No functorial comparison between the Hahn map of this report
and the logarithmic Tate quotient is proved; that comparison is the most
important remaining priority check (\cref{tate:sec:limits}).

The potentially new package is more specific: an \emph{if and only if}
Hahn-support domain for the raw bilateral families; a proof that this
restricted domain nevertheless uniformizes every rational point, by
explicit inverse formulas as well as by rank-one lifting; exact
compatibility with coarse reduction, scalar extension, and exponent-group
arithmetic; and the support-monoid and valuation identities of
\cref{tate:node:sec:metric}. The extension obstruction of
\cref{tate:node:thm:obstruction} is a useful consequence, not an
independent deep discovery. No assertion of bibliographic uniqueness
follows from the targeted searches. Related formulations in formal,
logarithmic, adic, or model-theoretic geometry may subsume parts of the
result.

\paragraph{Several periods.}
\Cref{tate:part:theta} (source~02) takes up the multivariable theta series
that a higher-dimensional analogue would need, with monomial periods
$t^{\pi_{ab}}$. Its first theorem is purely lattice-geometric: every
admissible lattice in a finite-dimensional higher-rank inner-product space,
in the sense of Amini--Nicolussi \cite{AN}, has a finite positively
generating set (\cref{tate:theta:thm:positive}). This answers their
Question~11.14 affirmatively and constructively, in the formulation
printed in \cite[\S11.5.3, p.~1181]{AN}. The remaining theorems give the
exact strong-summability criterion for quadratic lattice families
(\cref{tate:theta:thm:flag}), the exact domain of the multiscale theta
series and its unit robustness (\cref{tate:theta:thm:units}), its support
order type (\cref{tate:theta:thm:ordinal}), finite certificates for theta
minima (\cref{tate:theta:thm:certificate,tate:theta:thm:minimizers}), and
the lifting of smooth initial zeros (\cref{tate:theta:thm:zerolift}).
For one variable and a monomial period it recovers the domain of
\cref{tate:thm:theta} in a different normalization
(\cref{tate:theta:cor:rankone}); the hypotheses of the two statements differ
and are kept apart. Rank-one non-Archimedean theta functions and their
tropicalization are due to Foster, Rabinoff, Shokrieh and Soto \cite{FRSS};
convergent higher-rank Hahn subrings are studied by Joswig and Smith
\cite{JS}. Part~II does not construct an abelian variety or a
higher-dimensional uniformization.

\part{One period: Hahn--Tate uniformization}\label{tate:part:uniformization}

\section{Hahn summation and the relevant order structure}\label{tate:sec:hahn}

\subsection{Strong summability}

A Hahn series is a formal expression
\[
 a=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}a_\gamma t^\gamma,
 \qquad a_\gamma\in\C,
\]
whose support $\supp(a)=\{\gamma:a_\gamma\ne0\}$ is well ordered in the
increasing order. For $a\ne0$, its valuation is the least support element; put $v(0)=+\infty$.
A family $(a_i)_{i\in I}$ is \emph{strongly summable} when
$\bigcup_i\supp(a_i)$ is well ordered and each exponent occurs in the
support of only finitely many $a_i$. Its sum is defined coefficientwise.
Neither an order limit nor a limit in the full valuation topology is part
of this definition.

\begin{lemma}[Positive-support calculus]\label{tate:lem:neumann}
Let $S\subseteq\Gamma_{>0}$ be well ordered. Its additive monoid $S^*$,
including $0$, is well ordered. Every element of $\Gamma$ has only
finitely many representations as a finite unordered sum of members of
$S$, counting multiplicities. Consequently, for finitely many Hahn
series $z_1,\dots,z_d$ of positive valuation, any formal power series
$F\in\C[[Z_1,\dots,Z_d]]$ has a well-defined strong evaluation $F(z)$.
Evaluation preserves sums, products, and composition when the inner
formal series have zero constant coefficient.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
We recall the order-theoretic argument behind Neumann's lemma
\cite{Neumann}. The alphabet $S$, with its order, is well quasi-ordered.
Higman's lemma says that finite words over this alphabet are well
quasi-ordered by order-preserving subsequence embedding with each letter
allowed to increase \cite{Higman}. If a word embeds into another, the
sum of its letters does not exceed the sum of the other word. The
inequality is strict if a letter increases or a positive letter is added.
A descending sequence of sums would therefore contradict Higman's lemma.
An infinite collection of distinct nondecreasing words of the same sum
would also contradict it: an embedding between two such words with equal
sum forces the words to coincide. Sorting a word removes the finitely
many permutations of each finite multiset. This proves both assertions.

The union of the finitely many positive supports $\supp(z_i)$ is well
ordered. Expanding all monomials in $F(z)$ produces exponents in $S^*$.
The finiteness assertion bounds the word lengths and the decompositions
at a fixed exponent, and the number of variable labels is finite.
Hence every coefficient is a finite sum. The same argument for the
jointly indexed families proves multiplication and the stated composition
rule. This is the usual Hahn--Neumann calculus, not a new summation
principle.
\end{proof}

In particular, $(1-z)^{-1}=\sum_{n\ge0}z^n$ and
$(1+z)^r=\sum_{n\ge0}\binom rn z^n$ for $r\in\Q$ and $v(z)>0$.
These identities hold even when $n v(z)$ is bounded above in $\Gamma$.
A series with leading term $ct^\gamma$ is inverted by factoring it as
$ct^\gamma(1+\varepsilon)$ and applying this rule to $\varepsilon$.

\subsection{Other coefficient fields and the formal implicit function}

Nothing in \cref{tate:lem:neumann} or its proof uses a property of $\C$:
coefficients enter only through finite sums and products. The lemma holds
verbatim for $k((t^\Gamma))$ and $k[[Z_1,\dots,Z_d]]$ for any field $k$,
which is how source~03 states it (its ``positive-input specialization''
lemma, printed here once). Its proof also gives the support control
\begin{equation}
 \supp F(z)\subseteq\SpanM{\textstyle\bigcup_j\supp z_j},
 \label{tate:node:eq:support-control}
\end{equation}
where $\SpanM S$ is the additive monoid generated by $S$, including $0$;
if $F(0)=0$, the value has positive valuation. The second proof of
\cref{tate:thm:main} rests on the following lemma, taken from source~03.

\begin{lemma}[Formal inverse and implicit function]\label{tate:node:lem:implicit}
Let $k$ be any field, and let $\m_v$ be the
elements of positive valuation in $k((t^\Gamma))$. Suppose
$A(Z)\in k[[Z_1,\ldots,Z_d]]^d$ has zero constant term and invertible
linear part $L_A\in\GL_d(k)$. It has a unique zero-constant formal inverse.
Its evaluation gives a bijection of $\m_v^d$.
If $A$ is defined over $\Z$ with $L_A\in\GL_d(\Z)$, its formal inverse
is defined over $\Z$ as well, before any specialization to $k$.

More generally, if $B(P,Z)$ has zero constant term and the matrix
$\partial B/\partial Z$ at the origin is invertible, the equation
$B(P,Z)=0$ has a unique formal solution $Z=C(P)$ with $C(0)=0$.
Every solution obtained by substituting $P,Z\in\m_v$ equals this solution.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Determine the homogeneous terms of an inverse successively. At total
degree $n$, the unknown correction enters only through multiplication by
$L_A$; all other contributions were determined in earlier degrees. Solving
this finite linear system constructs the inverse, proves uniqueness, and
proves integrality when $L_A^{-1}$ is integral. No division by the
homogeneous degree occurs, so the construction works in every
characteristic. Formal composition identities specialize by
\cref{tate:lem:neumann}. The inverse and the original map both have zero
constant term, hence preserve $\m_v^d$; their two composition identities
give surjectivity and injectivity on that entire domain.

Apply the same inverse construction to the map $(P,Z)\mapsto(P,B(P,Z))$
for the implicit statement. Its inverse, specialized at $(P,0)$, gives
$C(P)$. Specializing the two-sided inverse identity proves uniqueness among
actual infinitesimal inputs, not merely among formal series.
\end{proof}

The characteristic-free formal inverse lemma does not remove the
characteristic-zero hypotheses from the later Tate-curve statements;
those retain their own coefficient assumptions.

The limits of these lemmas should be stated. If $\epsilon>0$ lies in a
proper convex subgroup of $\Gamma$, the sequence $n\epsilon$ need not be
cofinal in $\Gamma$, so a formal remainder of degree $N$ with valuation at
least $N\epsilon$ need not tend to zero in the full valuation topology.
The inverses are constructed as formal series and then specialized by
support arguments; they are not limits of Newton iterates in an assumed
complete metric. \Cref{tate:lem:coarse-eval} below is a different
strengthening, with coefficients of unbounded valuation, and
\cref{tate:theta:lem:implicit} a third, with infinitely many monomial
parameters.

\subsection{Normalization and two convex subgroups}

\begin{lemma}[Finite normalization]\label{tate:lem:normalize}
For $\beta\in\Gamma$, the following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item $\beta\in H_\alpha$;
\item there is an integer $m$ with $0\le\beta+m\alpha<\alpha$.
\end{enumerate}
Such an $m$ is unique. If $\beta\notin H_\alpha$, then either
$\beta>N\alpha$ for every $N\in\N$, or $\beta<-N\alpha$ for every
$N\in\N$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If $|\beta|\le N\alpha$, choose the largest integer
$k\in\{-N,\ldots,N\}$ with $k\alpha\le\beta$; this set is nonempty.
Then $k\alpha\le\beta<(k+1)\alpha$: maximality gives the upper bound
when $k<N$, and $\beta\le N\alpha$ gives it when $k=N$.
Taking $m=-k$ proves existence. If $m'>m$, then
$\beta+m'\alpha\ge\beta+m\alpha+\alpha\ge\alpha$, proving uniqueness.
The converse follows from $|\beta|\le(|m|+1)\alpha$.
Finally, if $\beta\notin H_\alpha$, then $|\beta|>N\alpha$ for every
$N$; its fixed sign gives the stated alternative. No real-valued
floor function or divisibility of $\Gamma$ is used.
\end{proof}

There is another convex subgroup inside $H_\alpha$:
\begin{equation}
 H_\alpha^-=
 \{\gamma\in H_\alpha:n|\gamma|<\alpha\text{ for all }n\ge1\}.
 \label{tate:eq:Hminus}
\end{equation}
It consists of the values infinitesimal relative to $\alpha$.
To check it is a subgroup, take $\gamma,\delta\in H_\alpha^-$.
For every $n\ge1$, the inequalities $2n|\gamma|<\alpha$ and
$2n|\delta|<\alpha$ imply $n|\gamma+\delta|<\alpha$; negation
preserves the defining condition. Convexity follows from
$|\xi|\le|\gamma|$ with $\gamma\in H_\alpha^-$.
It is proper because $\alpha\notin H_\alpha^-$.

The quotient $H_\alpha/H_\alpha^-$ is nonzero and Archimedean.
Indeed, for positive $\gamma\notin H_\alpha^-$ some $n\ge1$
satisfies $n\gamma\ge\alpha$. Given positive $\delta\in H_\alpha$,
choose $N$ with $\delta\le N\alpha$; then
$(N+1)n\gamma\ge(N+1)\alpha>\delta$, with the strict inequality
still true in the quotient since the difference is at least $\alpha$.
This gives the Archimedean comparison there. It also shows that
$H_\alpha^-$ is the largest proper convex subgroup of $H_\alpha$:
a convex subgroup containing such a $\gamma$ contains $\alpha$,
and then every element bounded by an integer multiple of $\alpha$. H\"older's theorem embeds this quotient in $(\R,+)$; normalize
the embedding so that $\alpha$ has image $1$.

This construction is not new in the collection, and we do not present it
as ours. By construction $\alpha$ is an \emph{order unit} of $H_\alpha$:
its positive integer multiples are cofinal there. The companion report on
entire Hahn functions states exactly the resulting passage as its
\texttt{ent:lem:coarsening} \cite{RepoEntire}: for an order unit $h$ of
an exponent group, there is an order-preserving $\pi$ to $\R$ with
$\pi(h)=1$ whose kernel is the convex subgroup of values infinitesimal
relative to $h$, the formula $|a|_\pi=\exp(-\pi(v(a)))$ is a nontrivial
real-valued non-Archimedean absolute value whose topology is the
intrinsic valuation topology, and the Hahn field is complete for it.
Applied with exponent group $H_\alpha$ and $h=\alpha$, that lemma is the
statement we need, with kernel exactly \eqref{tate:eq:Hminus}. We cite it
rather than claim it. One hypothesis must be carried across with it: that
report's standing assumption is that its exponent group is
\emph{divisible}, and \emph{we do not assume $\Gamma$, or $H_\alpha$,
divisible in the one-period uniformization theorem} --- \cref{tate:sec:torsion} depends on
keeping nondivisible groups. Its proof of that lemma uses only the
order-unit property and no divisibility, so the statement does transfer;
because the transfer is ours and not the cited report's, we retain the
short direct argument in \cref{tate:lem:rank-one} below, which uses no
divisibility, rather than rest a step of the main theorem on a hypothesis
we have dropped.

The two quotients serve different purposes. The quotient by
$H_\alpha^-$ produces a rank-one valuation \emph{inside} $K_{H_\alpha}$.
The quotient by $H_\alpha$ produces a coarser valuation on the \emph{whole}
field $K_\Gamma$, whose residue field is $K_{H_\alpha}$.
Interchanging them would destroy the proof.

\section{The exact domain of the bilateral theta series}\label{tate:sec:theta}

\begin{theorem}[Theta domain]\label{tate:thm:theta}
The family in \eqref{tate:eq:theta} is strongly summable exactly for
$u\in U_q$. On that domain,
\begin{equation}
 \Theta_q(qu)=-u^{-1}\Theta_q(u).\label{tate:eq:theta-functional}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Write $\beta=v(u)$. The leading exponent of its $n$th summand is
\begin{equation}
 b_n=\frac{n(n-1)}2\alpha+n\beta.\label{tate:eq:bn}
\end{equation}
Suppose first that $\beta\in H_\alpha$ and choose $N$ with
$|\beta|\le N\alpha$. The differences
$b_{n+1}-b_n=n\alpha+\beta$ show that the positive tail is eventually
strictly increasing. Writing $n=-k$ gives the same property for the tail
as $k\to+\infty$. Each tail eventually exceeds every prescribed element
of $H_\alpha$, by comparison with integer multiples of $\alpha$.
Together with the finitely many remaining indices, the set
$B=\{b_n:n\in\Z\}$ is well ordered. Each value of $b_n$ occurs at most
twice: if $b_n=b_m$ and $n\ne m$, then
$(n+m-1)\alpha+2\beta=0$.

Factor
$q=c_qt^\alpha(1+\varepsilon_q)$ and
$u=c_ut^\beta(1+\varepsilon_u)$ with positive-valuation errors.
Let $M$ be the monoid generated by their two supports. Every summand in
\eqref{tate:eq:theta} has support in $b_n+M$, by \cref{tate:lem:neumann}, including
negative powers of $u$. The sum of two well-ordered subsets of an ordered
abelian group is well ordered, with finitely many pairs giving any fixed
sum. Thus $B+M$ is well ordered. There are finitely many choices of $n$
at a fixed leading value and finitely many decompositions in $M$.
This proves strong summability of the original family.

If $\beta>N\alpha$ for all $N$, put $n=-k$. Then
\[
 b_{-(k+1)}-b_{-k}=(k+1)\alpha-\beta<0.
\]
These are actual nonzero leading coefficients, so their decreasing
exponents already obstruct well-ordering of the union of supports.
If $\beta<-N\alpha$ for all $N$, the sequence $b_n$ decreases for
$n\ge0$. This proves necessity without assuming that possible
cancellations between summands can repair an inadmissible family.

Finally, multiplication by $q$ shifts the index in a strongly summable
family. The coefficient equality
$q^n(-1)^nq^{n(n-1)/2}=-(-1)^{n+1}q^{n(n+1)/2}$ proves
\eqref{tate:eq:theta-functional}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Product and zeros]\label{tate:prop:theta-product}
For $0\le v(u)<\alpha$, the Jacobi product is a Hahn identity:
\begin{equation}
 \Theta_q(u)=(1-u)\prod_{n\ge1}
          (1-q^n)(1-uq^n)(1-u^{-1}q^n).\label{tate:eq:triple}
\end{equation}
The product means the strong sum over its finite subproducts after
expansion. The zeros on $U_q$ are exactly $q^\Z$, and all are simple
in the local external-variable sense.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
All of $q$, $qu$, and $q/u$ have positive valuation. Their support monoid
licenses the expanded product and its regrouping. The classical Jacobi
triple-product identity, in this normalization, is an identity of the
corresponding formal Laurent families \cite{DLMF}; strong evaluation
therefore yields \eqref{tate:eq:triple}. One can also compare coefficients of
$q$ after treating $u$ as a Laurent indeterminate and then use the same
support argument for specialization.

The product after the factor $1-u$ is $1$ plus a positive-valuation
series, hence a unit. Therefore the only zero in the normalized annulus
is $u=1$. The functional equation and \cref{tate:lem:normalize} give all
zeros. At $u=1$ the derivative of the product is
\[
 \Theta_q'(1)=-\prod_{n\ge1}(1-q^n)^3\ne0.
\]
Reindexing transports simplicity to every $q^m$. This is differentiation
of a local power series in a variable; no derivation on the scalar field
is being imposed.
\end{proof}

\section{Tate coordinates, support certificates, and precision}\label{tate:sec:coordinates}

\subsection{A form with positive tails}

Put
\[
 f(z)=\frac{z}{(1-z)^2},\qquad
 g(z)=\frac{z^2}{(1-z)^3},\qquad
 h(z)=\frac{z}{(1-z)^3}.
\]
The elementary identities $f(z^{-1})=f(z)$ and
$g(z^{-1})=-h(z)$ give, after normalizing $0\le\beta=v(u)<\alpha$,
\begin{align}
 X_q(u)&=f(u)+\sum_{n\ge1}\bigl(f(q^nu)+f(q^n/u)\bigr)-2s_1(q),
       \label{tate:eq:Xpositive}\\
 Y_q(u)&=g(u)+\sum_{n\ge1}\bigl(g(q^nu)-h(q^n/u)\bigr)+s_1(q).
       \label{tate:eq:Ypositive}
\end{align}
The minus sign and the unsquared numerator in the last tail of
\eqref{tate:eq:Ypositive} are important. In contrast, the numerator in the
original bilateral formula \eqref{tate:eq:Y} is squared.

At $v(u)=0$ we do \emph{not} expand the isolated expressions $f(u)$ and
$g(u)$ geometrically in $u$. They are finite rational operations, defined
unless $u=1$. Their denominators may be arbitrarily small.

\begin{theorem}[Coordinate domain and support certificate]\label{tate:thm:coordinates}
For $u\in K^\times\setminus q^\Z$, the families in \eqref{tate:eq:X} and \eqref{tate:eq:Y} are
strongly summable exactly when $u\in U_q$.
For $0\le v(u)<\alpha$ and $u\ne1$, let
\begin{equation}
 S=\supp(q)\cup\supp(qu)\cup\supp(q/u)\subseteq\Gamma_{>0}.
 \label{tate:eq:S}
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
 \supp(X_q(u)-f(u)),\ \supp(Y_q(u)-g(u))
        \subseteq S^*\setminus\{0\}.\label{tate:eq:certificate}
\end{equation}
The bilateral and positive-tail formulas agree.
\end{theorem}

The restriction $u\ne0$ is necessary for this exact-domain assertion.
At $u=0$ every term of each bilateral coordinate family is zero, so both
families are strongly summable, although $0\notin U_q$. This does not
extend the multiplicative uniformization domain, and the theta family,
which contains negative powers of $u$, is not defined there.

\begin{proof}
The series $q^nu=q^{n-1}(qu)$ and $q^n/u=q^{n-1}(q/u)$ have positive
valuation for $n\ge1$. Expanding $f,g,h$ in those arguments and
expanding each term of $s_1$ gives exponents in $S^*\setminus\{0\}$.
The full family, including $n$ and every geometric-expansion index, is
strongly summable: at a fixed exponent, the Neumann finiteness theorem
bounds the total word length, hence also $n$ and the geometric index.
This proves \eqref{tate:eq:certificate} and justifies every regrouping in
\eqref{tate:eq:Xpositive}--\eqref{tate:eq:Ypositive}. Multiplication of $u$ by a
power of $q$ merely reindexes the bilateral sums. Finite normalization
therefore proves sufficiency on $U_q$.

For necessity, suppose first $\beta>N\alpha$ for every $N$. All $q^nu$
have positive valuation. The leading exponents in the $X$ family are
$\beta+n\alpha$ and in the $Y$ family are $2\beta+2n\alpha$.
Both decrease strictly as $n\to-\infty$. If $\beta<-N\alpha$ for every
$N$, all $q^nu$ have negative valuation. The rational identities above
show that the leading exponents of both families are
$-\beta-n\alpha$, with a nonzero sign in the $Y$ case. These decrease
as $n\to+\infty$. In either case the original families fail the
well-ordering requirement.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Exact finite-tail bound]\label{tate:prop:tail}
For normalized $u$, let $X^{[N]},Y^{[N]}$ retain the isolated rational
terms and the terms $1\le n\le N$ in the positive-tail formulas, and
replace $s_1$ by $\sum_{n=1}^N nq^n/(1-q^n)$. Then
\begin{equation}
 v(X_q-X^{[N]})\ge(N+1)\alpha-\beta,
 \qquad v(Y_q-Y^{[N]})\ge(N+1)\alpha-\beta.\label{tate:eq:tail}
\end{equation}
Here $v(0)=+\infty$, so cancellations cause no exception.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Each omitted $f(q^n/u)$ or $h(q^n/u)$ has valuation $n\alpha-\beta$;
the other omitted coordinate terms have at least that valuation.
The omitted $s_1$ terms have valuation $n\alpha$. Strong summation and
the valuation inequality give the bounds.
\end{proof}

If a requested precision $\delta$ lies in $H_\alpha$, a finite $N$
certifies \eqref{tate:eq:tail} beyond $\delta$. If $\delta>N\alpha$ for
all $N$, this certificate never reaches it. A valid exact Hahn sum has
therefore not been converted into a convergence theorem at all scales.

\subsection{The elliptic curve and its invariant differential}

\begin{proposition}[Discriminant and modular invariant]\label{tate:prop:discriminant}
The coefficients in \eqref{tate:eq:ak} are Hahn evaluations of integer
power series in $q$. The Weierstrass discriminant and $j$-invariant are
\begin{align}
 \Delta(q)&=q\prod_{n\ge1}(1-q^n)^{24},&v(\Delta(q))&=\alpha,
       \label{tate:eq:Delta}\\
 j(q)&=q^{-1}+744+196884q+21493760q^2+\cdots,&v(j(q))&=-\alpha.
       \label{tate:eq:j}
\end{align}
In particular $E_q$ is a nonsingular elliptic curve over $K_\Gamma$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The formulas for $s_k$ are licensed by positive-support calculus.
The integer divisibility of $(5n^3+7n^5)/12$ follows by checking its
factors modulo $3$ and $4$, or from the standard Tate coefficient
identity. With $a_1=1$, $a_2=a_3=0$, the usual Weierstrass formulas give
\[
 b_2=1,\quad b_4=2a_4,\quad b_6=4a_6,\quad b_8=a_6-a_4^2,
 \qquad c_4=1-48a_4.
\]
The formal identities for $\Delta$ and $c_4^3/\Delta$ are classical
\cite{Tate,Silverman}. They are identities in integer formal power
series (Laurent series for $j$), so positive-support evaluation proves
them here. The product multiplying $q$ is a unit of leading coefficient
$1$. Hence $\Delta\ne0$, and the displayed valuations follow.
\end{proof}

The coordinate functions satisfy the curve equation and the group law;
a proof valid at every rank is given in \cref{tate:sec:uniformization}. The
following local identity can already be checked term by term.

\begin{proposition}[Differential and inversion identities]\label{tate:prop:differential}
On the local external-variable germs in $U_q$, away from poles,
\begin{equation}
 u\frac{dX_q}{du}=X_q+2Y_q,\qquad
 X_q(u^{-1})=X_q(u),\qquad
 Y_q(u^{-1})=-X_q(u)-Y_q(u).\label{tate:eq:diff}
\end{equation}
Consequently the invariant differential pulls back as
\begin{equation}
 \Phi_q^*\left(\frac{dx}{2y+x}\right)=\frac{du}{u}.\label{tate:eq:omega}
\end{equation}
The equation for differentials is interpreted in regular local charts
also where the displayed affine denominator vanishes.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The rational identity $z f'(z)=f(z)+2g(z)$ and the constants
$-2s_1+2s_1=0$ prove the first formula. Differentiating the positive
 tails preserves their support estimates locally, so the interchange is
licensed. The inversion formulas follow from $f(z^{-1})=f(z)$ and
$g(z^{-1})=-f(z)-g(z)$, followed by reindexing. Equation
\eqref{tate:eq:omega} is the first identity rewritten as a differential.
It extends in regular charts since an invariant differential has no
pole on a smooth elliptic curve.
\end{proof}

Nothing in this proposition says that a chosen scalar derivation
$\partial:K\to K$ satisfies these formulas without the chain rule's
$q$-dependence. In particular, it makes no assertion about the
Berarducci--Mantova derivation of surreal scalars.

\section{Coarse infinitesimals admit unrestricted formal evaluation}\label{tate:sec:coarse}

This section supplies the step that a bare appeal to ``completeness of
Hahn fields'' would miss. We shall evaluate formal series whose
coefficients have no uniform bound for the original valuation. A coarser
valuation makes this possible.

\subsection{The coarse residue field}

Let $H\subseteq\Gamma$ be any convex subgroup. The quotient
$\overline\Gamma=\Gamma/H$ is ordered. Define
\[
 w(a)=v(a)+H\quad(a\ne0),\qquad
 \OO_w=\{a:w(a)\ge0\},\qquad
 \m_w=\{a:w(a)>0\}.
\]
For $a\in\OO_w$, retaining exactly the terms with exponents in $H$
gives a residue homomorphism
\begin{equation}
 \res_H:\OO_w\longrightarrow K_H,\qquad
 \sum_\gamma a_\gamma t^\gamma\longmapsto
             \sum_{\gamma\in H}a_\gamma t^\gamma.\label{tate:eq:resH}
\end{equation}
Its kernel is $\m_w$, and the inclusion $K_H\subseteq\OO_w$ is a
coefficient-field section. No section of the ordered group quotient
$\Gamma\to\Gamma/H$ is required.

To verify multiplicativity, observe that the support of an element of
$\OO_w$ contains only nonnegative quotient values. A product exponent
can belong to $H$ only if both summand exponents belong to $H$. Thus its
$H$-part is exactly the product of the $H$-parts.

\begin{lemma}[Unrestricted coarse formal evaluation]\label{tate:lem:coarse-eval}
Let $z_1,\ldots,z_d\in\m_w$. For every formal series
\[
 F(Z)=\sum_{\nu\in\N^d}a_\nu Z^\nu\in K_H[[Z_1,\ldots,Z_d]],
\]
the family $(a_\nu z^\nu)_\nu$ is strongly Hahn-summable in $K_\Gamma$.
There is no boundedness assumption on $v(a_\nu)$ in $H$.
Evaluation is a $K_H$-algebra homomorphism. It commutes with formal
composition when the inner series have zero constant coefficient.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Project the supports of the nonzero $z_i$ into $\Gamma/H$ and let $S$
be their union. A monotone image of a well-ordered set is well ordered:
the least preimage of any nonempty chosen subset maps to its least
image. Since $w(z_i)>0$, every element of $S$ is strictly positive.
Apply \cref{tate:lem:neumann} in the quotient group. The possible quotient
values form the well-ordered monoid $S^*$. At a fixed quotient value
$\bar\gamma$, there are only finitely many decompositions into members
of $S$, including word lengths. In particular, there are only finitely
many multiindices $\nu$ that can contribute there.

For each of these finitely many choices, taking the indicated quotient
fibers of the supports of the $z_i$ and multiplying by $a_\nu$ involves
ordinary finite Hahn products. These are well-defined and have
well-ordered support within the fiber, with finite coefficient
contributions. The finite union over the choices has the same properties.
A subset of an ordered group with well-ordered quotient image and
well-ordered support in every quotient fiber is well ordered: minimize
first the quotient and then the fiber. Hence the union of all supports
is well ordered in $\Gamma$, and each actual exponent receives finitely
many contributions.

For addition and multiplication, use the same argument on the jointly
indexed families. For composition, the zero-constant hypothesis ensures
that at any bounded word length only finitely many formal substitutions
can contribute. The quotient Neumann bound again supplies this word
length bound. Thus the coefficientwise identities survive evaluation.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Why coefficient bounds are unnecessary]\label{tate:ex:unbounded}
Let $\Gamma=\Z\oplus_{\mathrm{lex}}\Z$, $H=\{0\}\oplus\Z$,
and $z=t^{(1,0)}$. Then
\[
 \sum_{n\ge0}t^{(0,-n^2)}z^n
       =\sum_{n\ge0}t^{(n,-n^2)}
\]
is strongly summable, although its coefficient valuations
$(0,-n^2)$ are unbounded below in $H$. The first coordinate separates
the degrees. This is precisely the type of separation used in the
uniformization proof.

The coarse-positive condition cannot be replaced by fine positivity.
In the same field, take $z=t^{(0,1)}$ and
$F(Z)=\sum_{n\ge0}t^{(0,-n)}Z^n\in K_H[[Z]]$.
Now $v(z)>0$ but $w(z)=0$, and every evaluated term is $1$.
Infinitely many terms contribute at exponent zero, so this family is
not strongly summable. A lower bound on the fine valuation of the
input alone cannot compensate for unrestricted coefficient valuations.
\end{example}

\subsection{The rank-one field at the \texorpdfstring{$q$}{q}-scale}

Now set $H=H_\alpha$. Use the embedding
$H/H_\alpha^-\hookrightarrow\R$ from \cref{tate:sec:hahn} to define a
real-valued valuation $v_1$ on $K_H$, with $v_1(t^\alpha)=1$.

\begin{lemma}[Completeness of the residue workspace]\label{tate:lem:rank-one}
The field $K_H$, equipped with the absolute value $|a|_1=e^{-v_1(a)}$,
is complete. Every $q_0\in K_H$ with $v(q_0)=\alpha$ has $|q_0|_1<1$.
\end{lemma}

This is \texttt{ent:lem:coarsening} of \cite{RepoEntire}, instantiated at
the exponent group $H_\alpha$ with the order unit $\alpha$, and is cited
rather than claimed as new; see \cref{tate:sec:hahn}. The proof below is
recorded only because that report states its lemma under a standing
divisibility hypothesis on the exponent group which this one-period
argument does not make, and because the package should read standalone. It is a second
proof of a statement already in the collection, not an additional result.

\begin{proof}
Let $(a_n)$ be a Cauchy sequence. Choose a subsequence $(a_{n_k})$ whose
successive differences $d_k=a_{n_{k+1}}-a_{n_k}$ satisfy $v_1(d_k)>k$.
Then $v(d_k)>k\alpha$. The sequence $k\alpha$ is cofinal in $H$:
for $\gamma\in H$, choose an integer bound for $|\gamma|$ and then a
strictly larger integer.

It follows that $(d_k)$ is strongly summable in $K_H$. Indeed, below any
fixed exponent only finitely many of its supports can occur; any
hypothetical descending sequence of support exponents would therefore
lie in a finite union of well-ordered supports. For each $m$, every term of the tail $\sum_{k\ge m}d_k$ has
fine valuation greater than $m\alpha$, so the strong sum has fine
valuation greater than $m\alpha$ as well, or is zero. Its $v_1$-value
is therefore at least $m$. Thus
$a_{n_1}+\sum_kd_k$ is the $v_1$-limit of the subsequence. The original
Cauchy sequence has the same limit. The assertion about $q_0$ is
$v_1(q_0)=1$.
\end{proof}

The coefficient field of this rank-one valuation need not be $\C$;
it can itself retain infinitesimal scales. Tate's theorem does not
require it to be $\C$ or require the value group to be discrete.
Its complete-field version is exactly the classical input needed here.

\section{Formal germs and the passage from rank one to arbitrary rank}
\label{tate:sec:germs}

We record carefully how classical analytic identities can be used without
illegally specializing a non-summable family.

\subsection{The classical input, stated with its hypotheses}

\begin{theorem}[Tate; external classical input]\label{tate:thm:classical}
Let $F$ be complete for a nontrivial real-valued non-Archimedean absolute
value, and let $q_0\in F$ satisfy $0<|q_0|<1$. The rational bilateral
series \eqref{tate:eq:X}--\eqref{tate:eq:Y}, interpreted by their convergent tails,
define an analytic group homomorphism
\[
 \phi_{q_0}:F^\times\longrightarrow E_{q_0}(F)
\]
which is onto and has kernel $q_0^\Z$. It extends analytically through
those parameters as a map to the projective curve. The same formulas
are locally analytic in the parameter $q_0$ as well as in $u$.
\end{theorem}

The surjective group statement is Tate's Theorem~1
\cite[author manuscript, p.~6]{Tate}, under the complete real-valued
field assumptions stated on its first page. Neither discreteness nor
algebraic closedness is required. Local analyticity in both variables follows directly from
the rational tails: on sufficiently small neighborhoods of any non-pole
pair, the finitely many relevant denominators remain nonzero and the
remaining terms tend uniformly to zero geometrically. At a pole one
uses the projective parameter below. This last observation requires no
new uniformization theorem.

The proof of \cref{tate:thm:main} uses \cref{tate:thm:classical} as an input,
not as a theorem claimed to have been independently reproved here.
All additional all-rank summability and lifting steps are supplied.
The second proof in \cref{tate:node:sec:second} does not use
\cref{tate:thm:classical} at all.

\subsection{The formal neighborhood of the identity}

\begin{lemma}[A universal formal parameter]\label{tate:lem:formal-identity}
With an external variable $h$, the formal expression
\begin{equation}
 B(q,h)=-\frac{X_q(1+h)}{Y_q(1+h)}\label{tate:eq:B}
\end{equation}
after cancellation of its poles belongs to $h\C[[q,h]]$ and has
linear coefficient $1$ in $h$:
\begin{equation}
 B(q,h)=h+h^2 C(q,h),\qquad C\in\C[[q,h]].\label{tate:eq:Blinear}
\end{equation}
It has a unique inverse under composition in the second variable,
$A(q,z)=z+z^2D(q,z)\in z\C[[q,z]]$.
For any $v(q)>0$ and $v(h)>0$, both series evaluate strongly, and
\begin{equation}
 v(B(q,h))=v(h),\qquad \lc(B(q,h))=\lc(h).\label{tate:eq:isometry}
\end{equation}
The germ $h\mapsto\Phi_q(1+h)$ identifies the multiplicative formal
group with the formal group of $E_q$ at $O$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Expand the positive tails with $u=1+h$, treating $q,h$ as formal
variables. They belong to $q\C[[q,h]]$. Hence
\begin{align*}
 h^2X_q(1+h)&=1+h+h^2R_X(q,h),\\
 h^3Y_q(1+h)&=-(1+h)^2+h^3R_Y(q,h),
\end{align*}
where $R_X,R_Y\in q\C[[q,h]]$. The denominator obtained after cancelling
poles has constant coefficient $1$. This proves
\eqref{tate:eq:Blinear}, including the exact coefficient $1$ for every $q$.
Formal coefficient recursion gives the inverse $A$, since this linear
coefficient is a unit.

Positive-support calculus evaluates $B$, $A$, and their composition
identities at the specified arguments. The remainder $h^2 C(q,h)$ has
valuation at least $2v(h)>v(h)$, which proves \eqref{tate:eq:isometry}.

The formal-group identity is a universal identity of formal series in
$q,h_1,h_2$. For ordinary complex $q$ and sufficiently small $h_1,h_2$
it follows from classical multiplicative uniformization. Uniqueness of
Taylor coefficients gives the formal identity; alternatively it is the
standard formal Tate-curve construction \cite{Tate,Conrad}. Strong
evaluation of the jointly indexed positive-support family proves it in
$K_\Gamma$.
\end{proof}

Here is a direct explanation of why this formal germ parametrizes all
points in a coarse identity fiber, rather than just some of them.
Use projective coordinates $[X:Y:Z]$ near $O=[0:1:0]$ and set
\[
 z=-X/Y=-x/y,\qquad r=-Z/Y=-1/y.
\]
The curve equation becomes
\begin{equation}
 r(1-z)=z^3+a_4zr^2+a_6r^3.\label{tate:eq:localcurve}
\end{equation}
It has a unique solution $r\in z^3\C[[q,z]]$ by coefficient recursion.
For $z\in\m_w$, positive-support evaluation gives such a solution.
Uniqueness also holds among actual $r\in\m_w$: subtracting two
solutions factors their difference times
\[
 1-z-a_4z(r_1+r_2)-a_6(r_1^2+r_1r_2+r_2^2),
\]
which is a $w$-unit because its residue is $1$. Thus two points reducing
to $O$ and having the same $z$ coincide. Evaluation of the inverse
$A(q,z)$ supplies the unique corresponding $h\in\m_w$.

\subsection{Taylor prolongation through coarse infinitesimals}

Let $w=v\bmod H_\alpha$, set $F=K_{H_\alpha}$, and put
$q_0=\res_{H_\alpha}(q)$. Then
\[
 q=q_0(1+\rho),\quad \rho\in\m_w,
 \qquad
 u=u_0(1+\sigma),\quad \sigma\in\m_w,
 \quad u_0=\res_{H_\alpha}(u)\in F^\times
\]
for every $u\in U_q$.

\begin{lemma}[Agreement of Taylor and Hahn evaluation]\label{tate:lem:agreement}
The classical local Taylor germ of the projective map
$(Q,U)\mapsto\phi_Q(U)$ at $(q_0,u_0)$, evaluated at
$(\rho,\sigma)$ by \cref{tate:lem:coarse-eval}, is well defined and equals
$\Phi_q(u)$ as defined by the literal Hahn formulas.
This remains true when $u_0\in q_0^\Z$, using a projective local chart.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
First suppose $u_0\notin q_0^\Z$. Replace $(u,u_0)$ by
$(u q^m,u_0 q_0^m)$ so that
$0\le v(u_0)<\alpha$. This is a finite reindexing of the bilateral
formulas, and its relative error is still in $\m_w$.

The isolated rational terms have nonzero constant denominators in $F$.
Their Taylor series in $\rho,\sigma$ evaluate to the actual rational
terms by \cref{tate:lem:coarse-eval}. For the $n$th positive rational tail,
expand first in the relative variables. At every fixed Taylor
multiindex, the coefficient lies in $F$ and its fine valuation tends
to $+\infty$ cofinally in $H_\alpha$ as $n\to\infty$.
More explicitly, after discarding finitely many $n$, the denominators
$1-q_0^nu_0$ and $1-q_0^n/u_0$ are fine units, and a coefficient of
any fixed relative Taylor degree has valuation at least
$n\alpha-v(u_0)$, or a larger positive multiple for the $g$-tail.
Differentiating relative powers only contributes ordinary integer
coefficients and additional factors of these small rational arguments.
The finitely many discarded denominators affect no tail conclusion.

It follows that the sum over $n$ defining every Taylor coefficient is a
strong sum in $F$ and agrees with its rank-one analytic sum, by
\cref{tate:lem:rank-one}. Next fix a quotient exponent for $w$. The quotient
Neumann bound in \cref{tate:lem:coarse-eval} permits only finitely many
Taylor multiindices and quotient-support decompositions at this value.
For each such choice, the remaining sum over $n$ is the cofinally
bounded sum just described. Thus the family jointly indexed by Taylor
degree, tail index, and support decompositions is strongly summable.
Fubini regrouping for this joint family proves that Taylor evaluation
and the original Hahn-tail evaluation coincide. The $s_1$ family has
the same, simpler estimates.

If $u_0=q_0^m$, divide $u$ by $q^m$. The result is $1+h$ with
$h\in\m_w$. Apply \cref{tate:lem:formal-identity} and the regular projective
chart \eqref{tate:eq:localcurve}. The poles of $X,Y$ disappear in this chart,
and the same formal expressions are the Taylor germs of the classical
projective map. This proves the claim, including $h=0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The interchange that must not be omitted]
Formal coefficients in $F$ can have arbitrarily negative fine
valuations, as \cref{tate:ex:unbounded} shows. Ordinary positive-valuation
substitution over $\C$ alone does not justify their evaluation. Also,
a sequence cofinal in $H_\alpha$ need not be cofinal in $\Gamma$.
The proof above uses two separate finiteness statements: quotient
Neumann finiteness controls the coarse Taylor degrees, while cofinality
inside $H_\alpha$ controls the rational tail index. Neither statement
substitutes for the other.
\end{remark}

\section{Proof of arbitrary-rank uniformization}\label{tate:sec:uniformization}

\subsection{The curve equation and the group law}

Take $F=K_{H_\alpha}$, with its complete rank-one coarsening, as in
\cref{tate:lem:rank-one}. Since $v(q_0)=\alpha$, the classical theorem
applies to $q_0$. The projective Taylor germs in
\cref{tate:lem:agreement} satisfy the curve equation: it is an identity of
classical analytic germs, hence an identity of their Taylor series.
Evaluation preserves these polynomial identities. Thus $\Phi_q$ maps
into $E_q(K)$.

The same argument proves
\begin{equation}
 \Phi_q(uv)=\Phi_q(u)+\Phi_q(v)\qquad(u,v\in U_q).\label{tate:eq:grouplaw}
\end{equation}
Here one must use regular local charts on the elliptic curve, not the
chord-slope formula at a vanishing denominator. The group operation on a
smooth elliptic curve is a regular morphism. At the residue points
$\phi_{q_0}(u_0)$, $\phi_{q_0}(v_0)$ and their sum, choose affine
neighborhoods for which it is given by rational expressions with
nonzero residue denominators. Its local Taylor identity with
$\phi_Q(UV)$ follows from \cref{tate:thm:classical}. All denominators remain
$w$-units after coarse evaluation, and \cref{tate:lem:coarse-eval} preserves
the identity. At the identity, use \eqref{tate:eq:localcurve}. This includes
$u_0v_0\in q_0^\Z$, opposite points, equal points, and every other
case where a particular affine addition formula would be undefined.
By \cref{tate:lem:agreement}, these evaluated germs are the Hahn-defined
map, proving \eqref{tate:eq:grouplaw} everywhere on $U_q$.

For clarity, this is not an appeal to a new ``identity principle on
all surcomplex numbers.'' It is an ordinary identity of rank-one
analytic germs, followed by an explicitly proved algebra homomorphism
on their formal Taylor rings.

\subsection{Good reduction for the coarse valuation}

By \eqref{tate:eq:Delta}, $v(\Delta(q))=\alpha\in H_\alpha$, so
$w(\Delta(q))=0$. All coefficients of the projective Weierstrass equation
belong to $\OO_w$, and its discriminant is a unit there. It is therefore
a smooth proper elliptic curve over $\OO_w$, with residue curve
$E_{q_0}$ over $F$. The identification of the residue curve is the
statement $\res_{H_\alpha}(a_k(q))=a_k(q_0)$ for $k=4,6$. It holds
because $\res_{H_\alpha}$ is a ring homomorphism on $\OO_w$ that is
computed coefficientwise, hence commutes with the strong sums
\eqref{tate:eq:sk}: each term $n^kq^n/(1-q^n)$ lies in $\OO_w$, since
$v(q^n)=n\alpha>0$ forces $v(1-q^n)=0$, and
$\res_{H_\alpha}(q)=q_0$.

Every $K$-point has a well-defined reduction to that curve. In the
present projective model, one can see existence directly: scale its
three homogeneous coordinates so that their minimum $w$-value is
zero, and reduce the resulting integral coordinates. The equation
survives reduction, and at least one reduced coordinate is nonzero.
Smooth properness also implies that reduction respects the group law.
We write
\[
 \mathrm{red}:E_q(K)\longrightarrow E_{q_0}(F),
 \qquad E_1(K)=\ker(\mathrm{red}).
\]
The reduction compatibility supplied by \cref{tate:lem:agreement} is
\begin{equation}
 \mathrm{red}(\Phi_q(u))=\phi_{q_0}(u_0).\label{tate:eq:redcompat}
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[The whole coarse identity fiber]\label{tate:prop:identityfiber}
The homomorphism $h\mapsto\Phi_q(1+h)$ induces an isomorphism
\begin{equation}
 1+\m_w\ \simeq\ E_1(K).\label{tate:eq:identityfiber}
\end{equation}
For the local coordinate $z=-x/y$, it has the explicit inverse
$h=A(q,z)$ and satisfies $v(z)=v(h)$ for $h\ne0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
A point reducing to $O=[0:1:0]$ lies in the chart of
\eqref{tate:eq:localcurve}, with $z,r\in\m_w$. The discussion following
\cref{tate:lem:formal-identity} proves that $z$ uniquely determines the
point and that the inverse formal parameter supplies a unique
$h\in\m_w$. Formal composition identities show that these maps are
mutual inverses. The formal-group identity gives the group law; the
valuation assertion is \eqref{tate:eq:isometry}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Surjectivity}

Let $P\in E_q(K)$ be arbitrary, and reduce it to $P_0\in E_{q_0}(F)$.
By the classical Tate theorem there is $u_0\in F^\times$ with
$\phi_{q_0}(u_0)=P_0$. Regard $u_0$ as an element of $K$. Its valuation
belongs to $H_\alpha$, so $\Phi_q(u_0)$ is defined. By
\eqref{tate:eq:redcompat} the point
\[
 P-\Phi_q(u_0)
\]
belongs to $E_1(K)$. By \cref{tate:prop:identityfiber} it equals
$\Phi_q(1+h)$ for a unique $h\in\m_w$. The group law now gives
\[
 P=\Phi_q\bigl(u_0(1+h)\bigr).
\]
The product parameter is in $U_q$. This proves surjectivity, including
points with coordinates outside every scale bounded by powers of $q$.

When $H_\alpha=\Gamma$, the valuation $w$ is trivial and
$\m_w=0$. The proof reduces to the classical theorem on $K_H=K$
with its rank-one coarsening. No separate nontrivial-coarse-valuation
hypothesis was smuggled into the argument.

\subsection{The kernel}

Suppose $\Phi_q(u)=O$. Reducing and using the classical kernel shows
$u_0=q_0^m$ for an integer $m$. Therefore $u/q^m\in1+\m_w$.
Reindexing already gives $\Phi_q(q^m)=O$; the group law and
\cref{tate:prop:identityfiber} imply $u/q^m=1$. Conversely every $q^m$
is mapped to $O$ by definition. Thus $\ker\Phi_q=q^\Z$.
Together with the support theorems and surjectivity, this completes
the proof of \cref{tate:thm:main}.

\subsection{The exact reduction diagram}

The proof gives more than an abstract isomorphism.

\begin{corollary}[Reduction and infinitesimal lifting]\label{tate:cor:diagram}
There is a commutative diagram of exact sequences
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=small,row sep=large]
1\arrow[r]&1+\m_w\arrow[r]\arrow[d,"\sim"']&U_q/q^\Z
 \arrow[r,"\res_H"]\arrow[d,"\sim"']&F^\times/q_0^\Z
 \arrow[r]\arrow[d,"\sim"]&1\\
0\arrow[r]&E_1(K)\arrow[r]&E_q(K)\arrow[r,"\mathrm{red}"]&E_{q_0}(F)
 \arrow[r]&0 .
\end{tikzcd}
\]
The left-hand isomorphism is given by a support-controlled universal
formal series and preserves the original valuation of the local
identity parameter.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Surjectivity of the top residue map follows by taking representatives
in $F$. Its kernel consists of those classes whose residue is a power
of $q_0$; division by the corresponding power of $q$ gives a unique
representative in $1+\m_w$. Uniqueness uses $v(q_0)>0$.
The lower row and all vertical maps were established above.
\end{proof}

\section{A second proof: the integral node chart and three formal charts}
\label{tate:node:sec:second}

This section and the next come from source~03. They prove
\cref{tate:thm:main} again, independently of
\cref{tate:sec:coarse,tate:sec:germs,tate:sec:uniformization}: the
argument uses neither Tate's complete-field theorem
(\cref{tate:thm:classical}), nor the rank-one completion
(\cref{tate:lem:rank-one}), nor unrestricted coarse evaluation
(\cref{tate:lem:coarse-eval}), nor the Taylor/Hahn agreement of
\cref{tate:lem:agreement}. Its only external inputs are Tate's universal
formal identities (the curve equation and the cleared secant identities)
and Tate's elementary group lemma, which is proved below. Those four
results of the first route stay in the report: the reduction compatibility
\eqref{tate:eq:redcompat}, the coarse identity fibre
(\cref{tate:prop:identityfiber}) and the reduction diagram
(\cref{tate:cor:diagram}) are proved only by the first route.

\paragraph{Standing hypotheses and notation.}
Throughout this section and \cref{tate:node:sec:metric}, $k$ is an
arbitrary field of characteristic zero, not necessarily $\C$ and not
necessarily algebraically closed; $\Gamma$ is an arbitrary set-sized
ordered abelian group; and
\[
 \KK=k((t^\Gamma)),
\]
with the valuation $v$ given by the least exponent, $\OO_v=\{a:v(a)\ge0\}$,
its unit group $\OO_v^\times$, and $\m_v=\{a:v(a)>0\}$. When $k=\C$,
$\KK=K_\Gamma$. Fix $q\in\m_v\setminus\{0\}$ and $\alpha=v(q)$, and define
$H_\alpha$ and $U_q=v^{-1}(H_\alpha)\subseteq\KK^\times$ as in
\cref{tate:def:domain}. The Tate sums \eqref{tate:eq:sk}, the coefficients
\eqref{tate:eq:ak}, the curve \eqref{tate:eq:curve}, the functions
$f,g,h$ of \cref{tate:sec:coordinates}, the bilateral families
\eqref{tate:eq:X}--\eqref{tate:eq:Y} and the map \eqref{tate:eq:phi} are
defined over $\KK$ by the same formulas. To avoid collisions with the rest
of the report, source~03's notation is changed as follows: its $\nu$,
$\delta$, $D_q$, $\phi_q$ are $v$, $\alpha$, $U_q$, $\Phi_q$ here; the
Weierstrass polynomial is
\[
 \Fp(Q,x,y)=y^2+xy-x^3-a_4(Q)x-a_6(Q),
\]
which is unrelated to the residue workspace $F=K_{H_\alpha}$ of
\cref{tate:sec:coarse,tate:sec:germs,tate:sec:uniformization}; $Q$ is a
formal period variable, unrelated to the series $Q(J)$ of
\cref{tate:sec:j}; the linear part of the node chart is written $\bL$; the
valuation of a vector is written $\bv$; and the fine filtration of
\cref{tate:node:sec:filtration} is written $E^v_0\supseteq E^v_1$, never
$E_1$, which denotes the coarse kernel of \cref{tate:sec:uniformization}.

\subsection{The Tate cubic over an arbitrary coefficient field}

The coefficients of $a_6$ are integers: for each $m$, $5m^3+7m^5$ is
divisible by $12$, since $m^3\equiv m^5\equiv m$ modulo $3$, and
divisibility by $4$ follows by distinguishing even and odd $m$. Thus $s_k$,
$a_4$ and $a_6$ are evaluations at $q$ of integral power series, and
$a_4(q)=-5q-45q^2-\cdots$, $a_6(q)=-q-23q^2-\cdots$. The discriminant
identity \eqref{tate:eq:Delta} is an identity of integral formal power
series \cite{Tate}, so it holds in $\KK$ and $v(\Delta(q))=\alpha$;
alternatively, nonvanishing alone follows by substituting the coefficients
into the finite discriminant polynomial and reading its linear term. Hence
$E_q$ is an elliptic curve over $\KK$. We also use
\begin{equation}
 s_1(q)=\sum_{n\ge1}f(q^n),\label{tate:node:eq:s1-f}
\end{equation}
since both sides have coefficient $\sigma_1(d)=\sum_{m\mid d}m$ at $q^d$.

The proofs of \cref{tate:thm:theta} and \cref{tate:thm:coordinates} use
only strong summation, \cref{tate:lem:neumann}, the rational identities
$f(z^{-1})=f(z)$, $g(z^{-1})=-h(z)$, and leading exponents with nonzero
leading coefficients; they use no property of $\C$. Both theorems therefore
hold verbatim over $\KK$. For the coordinate families this is also source~03's
own theorem (its ``sharp strong-summability domain''), whose necessity proof
uses exactly the witnesses $\gamma-n\alpha$, $2\gamma-2n\alpha$ and
$-\gamma-n\alpha$ of the proof of \cref{tate:thm:coordinates}; it is printed
once, there. The theorem concerns the specified raw bilateral families. A
procedure that first assigns values to a nonsummable family by a different
regularization is a different definition and is not justified by it;
\cref{tate:node:thm:obstruction} imposes an additional restriction on any
homomorphic extension of the canonical values.

\subsection{Universal identities}

With $Q$ formal and $u$ an indeterminate, the coordinates have expansions
in $R[[Q]]$, where $R=\Z[u,u^{-1},(1-u)^{-1}]$: the degree-zero terms are
$f(u)$ and $g(u)$, and the coefficient of $Q^d$ for $d\ge1$ is given by
\eqref{tate:eq:Xcoeff}--\eqref{tate:eq:Ycoeff}. (Sources~01 and~03 display the
same formulas; they are printed once.) In that ring one has
\begin{equation}
 \Fp(Q,X_Q(u),Y_Q(u))=0.\label{tate:node:eq:universalcurve}
\end{equation}
For three parameters $u,u',uu'$, put $(X_1,Y_1)=(X_Q(u),Y_Q(u))$,
$(X_2,Y_2)=(X_Q(u'),Y_Q(u'))$ and $(X_3,Y_3)=(X_Q(uu'),Y_Q(uu'))$. The
cleared secant identities are
\begin{align}
 (X_1-X_2)^2X_3
  &=(Y_1-Y_2)^2+(Y_1-Y_2)(X_1-X_2)-(X_1-X_2)^2(X_1+X_2),
  \label{tate:node:eq:secant1}\\
 (X_1-X_2)Y_3
  &=-(X_1-X_2)(Y_1+X_3)+(Y_1-Y_2)(X_1-X_3).
 \label{tate:node:eq:secant2}
\end{align}
These are identities in the analogous two-variable coefficient ring, with
$1-u$, $1-u'$ and $1-uu'$ inverted. They are established classical
identities, not inferred from any finite computation: Tate proves them as
universal formal identities by comparison with the complex elliptic
functions \cite[Theorem~1 and its proof]{Tate}. The new obligation is to
justify their \emph{Hahn} specialization.

\subsection{The wide annulus and Hahn specialization}

\begin{proposition}[Summability on the wide annulus]\label{tate:node:prop:annulus}
If $-\alpha<v(u)<\alpha$ and $u\ne1$, both families in
\eqref{tate:eq:X}--\eqref{tate:eq:Y} are strongly summable, with values
\begin{align}
 X_q(u)&=f(u)+\sum_{n\ge1}
       \bigl(f(q^nu)+f(q^n/u)-2f(q^n)\bigr),\label{tate:node:eq:tailX}\\
 Y_q(u)&=g(u)+\sum_{n\ge1}
       \bigl(g(q^nu)-h(q^n/u)+f(q^n)\bigr).\label{tate:node:eq:tailY}
\end{align}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
All three inputs $q$, $a=qu$ and $b=q/u$ have positive valuation.
For example, the fully expanded positive tail of $f$ is
$\sum_{n,m\ge1}ma^mq^{(n-1)m}$. It is a formal series in $a,q$: for a
fixed pair of nonnegative powers there are finitely many contributing
$(n,m)$, and its monomials have positive total degree. The
positive-input form of \cref{tate:lem:neumann} therefore proves joint
summability after substitution. The other tails have the same support
pattern, with different constant coefficients. The negative indices of the
bilateral families are converted by $f(z^{-1})=f(z)$ and $g(z^{-1})=-h(z)$,
and \eqref{tate:node:eq:s1-f} supplies the constants. The central rational
terms $f(u),g(u)$ are single Hahn elements and may be added to the summable
tails; when $u$ is extremely close to $1$, they are not expanded as
geometric series in $u$.
\end{proof}

Formulas \eqref{tate:node:eq:tailX}--\eqref{tate:node:eq:tailY} are
\eqref{tate:eq:Xpositive}--\eqref{tate:eq:Ypositive} with $s_1$ absorbed into
the tails by \eqref{tate:node:eq:s1-f}, extended from the normalized
annulus $0\le v(u)<\alpha$ to the wide annulus $-\alpha<v(u)<\alpha$. The
wider range is what lets the secant identities specialize.

On the wide annulus, expanding \eqref{tate:node:eq:tailX}--\eqref{tate:node:eq:tailY}
and regrouping by $d=nm$ yields the $Q$-expansions above. The preceding
proof establishes joint summability \emph{before} regrouping. Finite
products of these jointly summable families remain jointly summable, by Hahn
multiplication and finite coefficient convolution, so a polynomial identity
among the formal coordinate series can be evaluated either by grouping its
formal $Q$-coefficients first or by summing its expanded Hahn terms first.
The finitely many central denominators, such as $(1-u)^{-1}$, are fixed
Hahn elements and cause no infinite coefficient-fibre issue. Hence
\eqref{tate:node:eq:universalcurve} specializes to an actual point of
$E_q(\KK)$. For an addition identity, normalize one argument to
$0\le v(u)<\alpha$ and the other to $-\alpha<v(u')\le0$; then all three of
$u,u',uu'$ lie in the wide annulus. Excluding the central poles for the
moment, the same joint-summability argument applies to
\eqref{tate:node:eq:secant1}--\eqref{tate:node:eq:secant2}. This closes the
specialization gap that would remain after merely invoking the classical
real-valued convergence theorem.

\subsection{An integral chart for the whole nodal smoothing}

The special fibre of $E_q$ is the nodal cubic $y^2+xy=x^3$. The Tate
parameter has two branches near its node. Instead of choosing one branch by
a square root of $q$, introduce independent variables $U,V$ and set $Q=UV$.

\begin{definition}[The node chart]\label{tate:node:def:node}
In $\Z[[U,V]]$ define
\begin{align}
 \X(U,V)&=f(U)+\sum_{n\ge1}
 \bigl(f(U^{n+1}V^n)+f(U^{n-1}V^n)-2f(U^nV^n)\bigr),\label{tate:node:eq:nodeX}\\
 \Y(U,V)&=g(U)+\sum_{n\ge1}
 \bigl(g(U^{n+1}V^n)-h(U^{n-1}V^n)+f(U^nV^n)\bigr),\label{tate:node:eq:nodeY}
\end{align}
where every $f,g,h$ is expanded at zero, as
$f(z)=\sum_{m\ge1}mz^m$, $g(z)=\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{m(m-1)}2z^m$ and
$h(z)=\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{m(m+1)}2z^m$. We write $T=(\X,\Y)$ for the node
chart.
\end{definition}

The series are well defined in the total-degree formal topology: the
smallest degree in the $n$th bracket is at least $2n-1$, so only finitely
many brackets contribute below any fixed total degree. Their first terms are
\begin{align}
 \X(U,V)&=U+V+2U^2-2UV+2V^2
 +3U^3+U^2V+UV^2+3V^3+O((U,V)^4),\label{tate:node:eq:Xsmall}\\
 \Y(U,V)&=-V+U^2+UV-3V^2
       +3U^3-UV^2-6V^3+O((U,V)^4).\label{tate:node:eq:Ysmall}
\end{align}
The linear part is
\begin{equation}
 \bL=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad \bL^{-1}=\bL.
 \label{tate:node:eq:linear}
\end{equation}
Under $U=u$, $V=q/u$ one has $q^nu=U^{n+1}V^n$, $q^n/u=U^{n-1}V^n$ and
$q^n=U^nV^n$, so $T(u,q/u)=(X_q(u),Y_q(u))$ whenever $0<v(u)<\alpha$, by
\eqref{tate:node:eq:tailX}--\eqref{tate:node:eq:tailY} and
\cref{tate:lem:neumann}.

\begin{theorem}[Explicit formal smoothing coordinates]\label{tate:node:thm:node}
There are unique $\UU,\VV\in\Z[[x,y]]$ with zero constant terms which form
the two-sided formal inverse of $(\X,\Y)$. Put $\QQ(x,y)=\UU(x,y)\VV(x,y)$.
Then
\begin{equation}
 \Fp(UV,\X(U,V),\Y(U,V))=0,
 \qquad \Fp(\QQ(x,y),x,y)=0.\label{tate:node:eq:nodecurve}
\end{equation}
Moreover, there is a formal unit $\mathcal A\in\Z[[Q,x,y]]$ with
$\mathcal A(0,0,0)=1$ such that
\begin{equation}
 \Fp(Q,x,y)=(Q-\QQ(x,y))\,\mathcal A(Q,x,y).\label{tate:node:eq:factorF}
\end{equation}
Thus the formal Tate smoothing is explicitly equivalent to $UV=Q$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The integral inverse follows from \eqref{tate:node:eq:linear} and
\cref{tate:node:lem:implicit}. Substitute $Q=UV$, $u=U$ into the formal
Tate identity \eqref{tate:node:eq:universalcurve}. By
\eqref{tate:eq:Xcoeff}--\eqref{tate:eq:Ycoeff}, a term $Q^du^{-m}$ has
$m\le d$ and becomes $U^{d-m}V^d$, of nonnegative exponents and total degree
at least $d$. The central denominator $1-U$ is a formal unit. Therefore the
substitution is legitimate in $\Z[[U,V]]$ and gives the first identity in
\eqref{tate:node:eq:nodecurve}. Composition with the formal inverse gives
the second.

Finally, $\partial\Fp/\partial Q$ at the origin equals $1$, because
$a_6(Q)=-Q+O(Q^2)$. Subtract the value at $Q=\QQ(x,y)$ from $\Fp(Q,x,y)$ and
use the formal difference quotient in $Q$. This yields
\eqref{tate:node:eq:factorF}; its constant coefficient is that same
derivative, namely $1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[First inverse coefficients]\label{tate:node:prop:coefficients}
The inverse starts as follows:
\begin{align}
 \UU(x,y)&=x+y-3x^2-7xy-3y^2
        +13x^3+46x^2y+47xy^2+16y^3+O((x,y)^4),\label{tate:node:eq:Ucoeff}\\
 \VV(x,y)&=-y+x^2+xy-3y^2
        -2x^3-xy^2-16y^3+O((x,y)^4),\label{tate:node:eq:Vcoeff}\\
 \QQ(x,y)&=-xy-y^2+x^3+5x^2y+5xy^2+O((x,y)^4).\label{tate:node:eq:Qcoeff}
\end{align}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Start with $(U,V)=(x+y,-y)$. At each degree substitute the already known
coefficients into \eqref{tate:node:eq:nodeX}--\eqref{tate:node:eq:nodeY} and
cancel the homogeneous error using $\bL^{-1}=\bL$. This gives the displayed
coefficients by finite integer arithmetic. The program
\file{code/03-tate-uniformization-verify.py} implements this recursion and
checks it through total degree $12$ (\cref{tate:sec:computation}).
\end{proof}

\subsection{Actual nodal points}

\begin{theorem}[Nodal inverse over a Hahn field]\label{tate:node:thm:nodalsurj}
The map
\begin{equation}
 \{u\in\KK^\times:0<v(u)<\alpha\}
  \longrightarrow
 \{(x,y)\in\m_v^2:\Fp(q,x,y)=0\},
 \qquad u\longmapsto(X_q(u),Y_q(u)),\label{tate:node:eq:nodebijection}
\end{equation}
is a bijection. Its inverse is $u=\UU(x,y)$, and $\VV(x,y)=q/u$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For $u$ in the node annulus $0<v(u)<\alpha$, both $u$ and $q/u$ lie in
$\m_v$. \Cref{tate:lem:neumann} evaluates the node chart there, and
\eqref{tate:node:eq:tailX}--\eqref{tate:node:eq:tailY} identify it with the
Tate coordinates. The curve identity is \eqref{tate:node:eq:nodecurve}.

Conversely let $x,y\in\m_v$ satisfy the curve equation. Evaluate the formal
inverse at $(x,y)$ and write $U=\UU(x,y)$, $V=\VV(x,y)$. Both are
infinitesimal. Evaluating \eqref{tate:node:eq:factorF} shows
$(q-UV)\mathcal A(q,x,y)=0$. The second factor has residue $1$ and is a unit,
so $q=UV$. Since $q\ne0$, both factors are nonzero and
$v(U)+v(V)=\alpha$. Hence $0<v(U)<\alpha$, and the two-sided inverse
identity gives $(x,y)=(X_q(U),Y_q(U))$. Uniqueness follows from the same
identity.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Points with nonsingular affine reduction}

\begin{lemma}[Normalization of the special cubic]\label{tate:node:lem:nodalnormalization}
Every $k$-point $(x_0,y_0)\ne(0,0)$ on $y^2+xy=x^3$ is uniquely of the form
\[
 x_0=\frac{c}{(1-c)^2},\qquad
 y_0=\frac{c^2}{(1-c)^3},\qquad c\in k^\times\setminus\{1\}.
\]
The parameter is $c=y_0/(x_0+y_0)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If $x_0=0$, then $y_0=0$. If $x_0+y_0=0$, the equation forces $x_0^3=0$
and again gives the node. Thus both denominators used above are nonzero.
The value of $c$ is neither $0$ nor $1$. Substituting $y_0=cx_0/(1-c)$ into
the equation and cancelling $x_0^2$ yields $x_0=c/(1-c)^2$, and the $y_0$
formula follows.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Smooth-residue inverse]\label{tate:node:prop:smooth}
Let $(x,y)\in E_q(\KK)$ have $x,y\in\OO_v$ and residue pair
$(x_0,y_0)\ne(0,0)$. There is a unique $u\in\OO_v^\times$ with residue
$c=y_0/(x_0+y_0)$ and $(x,y)=(X_q(u),Y_q(u))$. It is obtained by a formal
series in $q$ and $y/(x+y)-c$ with coefficients in $k$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Write $u=c+s$. The formulas for the tails and the fact that both $c$ and
$1-c$ are nonzero make $X_Q(c+s)$ and $Y_Q(c+s)$ formal series in $(Q,s)$
with coefficients in $k$. Their sum is a unit, with constant term
$h(c)\ne0$, so
\[
 R(Q,s)=\frac{Y_Q(c+s)}{X_Q(c+s)+Y_Q(c+s)}\in k[[Q,s]]
\]
is defined. At $Q=0$ the rational functions satisfy $g(u)/(f(u)+g(u))=u$,
whence $R(0,s)=c+s$. The equation $R(Q,s)=c+r_1$ therefore has a unique
formal solution $s=S_c(Q,r_1)$ with zero constant term
(\cref{tate:node:lem:implicit}). Specialize at $r_1=y/(x+y)-c\in\m_v$.

Set $(x',y')=(X_q(c+s),Y_q(c+s))$. This point and $(x,y)$ have the same
ratio $r=y/(x+y)$ and the same residue pair. The ratio satisfies $r\equiv c$,
and $1-r$ is a unit. Any such point has $y=rx/(1-r)$, and its
$x$-coordinate solves
\begin{equation}
 x^3-\frac{r}{(1-r)^2}x^2+a_4(q)x+a_6(q)=0.\label{tate:node:eq:smoothpolynomial}
\end{equation}
At $(q,r,x)=(0,c,x_0)$ the derivative with respect to $x$ is $x_0^2$, which
is nonzero. \Cref{tate:node:lem:implicit}, after translating by these
constants, proves uniqueness of the infinitesimal correction to $x_0$.
Thus $x=x'$ and $y=y'$. Any other $u$ with residue $c$ would solve the same
invertible equation $R(q,u-c)=r$, so it is equal to this one.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Points reducing to infinity}

\begin{lemma}[The valuation pattern at infinity]\label{tate:node:lem:infinityvalues}
If an affine point $(x,y)\in E_q(\KK)$ has either coordinate of negative
valuation, then
\[
 v(x)=-2\epsilon,\qquad v(y)=-3\epsilon
 \quad\text{for some }\epsilon\in\Gamma_{>0}.
\]
In particular $z=-x/y$ and $r=-1/y$ belong to $\m_v$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Put $a=v(x)$ and $b=v(y)$. If $b<0$ and $a\ge0$, the left side $y^2+xy$
has valuation $2b<0$, whereas the right side has nonnegative valuation.
This is impossible. Thus a negative coordinate forces $a<0$. The right side
then has valuation $3a$, because $v(a_4(q)),v(a_6(q))>0$. If $b\ge a$, both
left-side terms have valuation at least $2a>3a$, also impossible. Hence
$b<a$, so $y^2$ strictly dominates $xy$ and $2b=3a$. Set $\epsilon=a-b>0$.
The equality gives $a=-2\epsilon$ and $b=-3\epsilon$, without dividing an
element of $\Gamma$ by $2$ or $3$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Formal inverse at infinity]\label{tate:node:prop:infinity}
Every point of \cref{tate:node:lem:infinityvalues} is $\Phi_q(u)$ for a
unique $u\in1+\m_v$, $u\ne1$. The inverse $s=u-1$ is a zero-constant
formal series in $q$ and $z=-x/y$. The identity $O$ corresponds to $u=1$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
In the coordinates $z=-x/y$, $r=-1/y$ the curve equation is
\eqref{tate:eq:localcurve}. Its derivative with respect to $r$ at the
origin is $1$. It therefore has a unique formal solution $r=r_\infty(Q,z)$
with zero constant term, which specializes uniquely on $\m_v^2$; it begins
with $z^3$.

For $u=1+s$ the central terms of the Tate coordinates give
\[
 X_Q(1+s)=s^{-2}N_X(Q,s),\qquad Y_Q(1+s)=s^{-3}N_Y(Q,s),
\]
where $N_X,N_Y\in k[[Q,s]]$ satisfy $N_X(0,0)=1$ and $N_Y(0,0)=-1$:
the tails are regular in $(Q,s)$, $f(1+s)=(1+s)/s^2$ and
$g(1+s)=-(1+s)^2/s^3$. Thus
\[
 -\frac{X_Q(1+s)}{Y_Q(1+s)}=-s\frac{N_X(Q,s)}{N_Y(Q,s)}
\]
is a formal series vanishing at $s=0$ with derivative $1$ in $s$ at the
origin. It is the series $B(Q,s)$ of \eqref{tate:eq:B}, and its inverse in
the second variable is the series $A(Q,z)$ of \cref{tate:lem:formal-identity};
source~01 evaluates them at the coarse valuation, here they are evaluated
at the fine one. Put $s=A(q,z)$.

The resulting Tate point has the same $z$ and, by \eqref{tate:eq:localcurve},
the same unique $r=r_\infty(q,z)$ as the original point. Since $x=z/r$ and
$y=-1/r$, the points agree. The formal inverse also proves uniqueness. Since
$A(Q,0)=0$ and its linear coefficient in $z$ is $1$, $A$ is $z$ times a
formal unit, so a nonzero $z$ gives $s\ne0$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Constructive surjectivity}

\begin{theorem}[Constructive surjectivity]\label{tate:node:thm:surjectivity}
The map $\Phi_q:U_q\to E_q(\KK)$ is surjective as a map of sets. An inverse
modulo $q^\Z$ can be constructed in the same Hahn field from the input
$q,x,y$ by one of the three formal charts above.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The point $O$ is already in the image. For an affine point, a negative
coordinate puts it in \cref{tate:node:prop:infinity}. Otherwise both
coordinates belong to $\OO_v$, and their residue lies on the special cubic.
If the residue is the node, apply \cref{tate:node:thm:nodalsurj};
otherwise apply \cref{tate:node:prop:smooth}. These cases exhaust all
possibilities. The annular parameter has valuation in $(0,\alpha)$, while
the other two parameters are units, so all belong to $U_q$.
\end{proof}

The argument is constructive in the formal-series sense. It does not
assume that $k$ is algebraically closed or that the value group permits
root extraction; even the valuation pattern at infinity was obtained from
integer linear combinations, without division in $\Gamma$.

\subsection{The group law and the kernel}

Strong reindexing of the bilateral families gives
\begin{equation}
 X_q(qu)=X_q(u),\qquad Y_q(qu)=Y_q(u),\label{tate:node:eq:periodicity}
\end{equation}
and the rational identities $f(z^{-1})=f(z)$, $g(z^{-1})=-f(z)-g(z)$ give
the inversion identities of \eqref{tate:eq:diff},
$X_q(u^{-1})=X_q(u)$ and $Y_q(u^{-1})=-X_q(u)-Y_q(u)$, over $\KK$. This is
negation on the Weierstrass model. For two affine points with distinct
$x$-coordinates the group law is
\[
 \lambda=\frac{y_1-y_2}{x_1-x_2},\qquad
 x_3=\lambda^2+\lambda-x_1-x_2,
 \qquad y_3=-(1+\lambda)x_3-y_1+\lambda x_1,
\]
and the specialized identities
\eqref{tate:node:eq:secant1}--\eqref{tate:node:eq:secant2} say precisely that
the third point is $\Phi_q(uu')$. The cases involving $O$ or a product in
$q^\Z$ follow from \eqref{tate:node:eq:periodicity} and inversion. To avoid a
topological limit argument at equal $x$-coordinates, use Tate's elementary
group lemma \cite[Lemma~1]{Tate}.

\begin{lemma}[Tate's generic-pair lemma]\label{tate:node:lem:generic}
Let $\psi:G\to A'$ map a multiplicative group to an abelian group, with
infinite image. If $\psi(ab)=\psi(a)+\psi(b)$ whenever
$\psi(a)\ne\pm\psi(b)$, then $\psi$ is a homomorphism.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For fixed $a,b$, choose $c$ whose image avoids the finite collection
specified by
\[
 \psi(c)\ne\pm\psi(a),\qquad
 \psi(c)+\psi(a)\ne\pm\psi(b),\qquad
 \psi(c)\ne\pm\psi(ab).
\]
The assumed rule gives
$\psi(c)+\psi(a)+\psi(b)=\psi(ca)+\psi(b)=\psi(cab)=\psi(c)+\psi(ab)$.
Cancel $\psi(c)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Group uniformization]\label{tate:node:thm:group}
The map $\Phi_q:U_q\to E_q(\KK)$ is a group homomorphism, and its kernel is
exactly $q^\Z$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Two affine points have the same $x$-coordinate exactly when they are equal
or negatives, since their $y$-coordinates are the roots of the same
quadratic. Thus the secant argument proves the hypothesis of
\cref{tate:node:lem:generic}. The image is infinite: for $u_n=1+q^n$,
$n\ge1$, the central term of $X_q(u_n)$ has valuation $-2n\alpha$, whereas
its tails have positive valuation, so these values are distinct. The lemma
proves the group law everywhere.

By definition $\Phi_q(q^n)=O$. Every other argument in $U_q$ has
well-defined affine coordinates, so cannot map to $O$. This proves the
kernel assertion.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Theorem~\ref{tate:thm:main} over any coefficient field of characteristic zero]
\label{tate:node:thm:main}
Let $k$ be a field of characteristic zero, $\Gamma$ a set-sized ordered
abelian group, $\KK=k((t^\Gamma))$, and $q\in\KK$ with $v(q)=\alpha>0$.
The theta family \eqref{tate:eq:theta} is strongly summable exactly for
$u\in U_q$; for $u\in\KK^\times\setminus q^\Z$ each of the two bilateral coordinate
families is strongly summable exactly for $u\in U_q$; and $\Phi_q$ is a
surjective group homomorphism $U_q\to E_q(\KK)$ with kernel $q^\Z$, so
\[
 U_q/q^\Z\ \simeq\ E_q(\KK).
\]
Every coset has a unique representative whose valuation lies in
$[0,\alpha)$. No divisibility of $\Gamma$, algebraic closedness of $k$, or
rank-one hypothesis is required. For $k=\C$ this is \cref{tate:thm:main}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Summability: \cref{tate:thm:theta,tate:thm:coordinates}, whose proofs hold
over $\KK$ as noted at the start of this section. Homomorphism and kernel:
\cref{tate:node:thm:group}. Surjectivity: \cref{tate:node:thm:surjectivity}.
Normal forms: \cref{tate:lem:normalize}.
\end{proof}

\section{Support monoids and exact valuation geometry}\label{tate:node:sec:metric}

The standing hypotheses of \cref{tate:node:sec:second} remain in force. For
a vector $a=(a_1,\dots,a_d)$ of elements of $\KK$ put
\[
 \bv(a_1,\ldots,a_d)=\min_jv(a_j).
\]
This records closeness with values in $\Gamma\cup\{\infty\}$; it is not
exponentiated into a real-valued distance.

\subsection{Equality of generated support monoids}

\begin{theorem}[Equality of generated support monoids]\label{tate:node:thm:support}
For any $U,V\in\m_v$ and $(x,y)=(\X(U,V),\Y(U,V))$,
\begin{equation}
 \SpanM{\supp U\cup\supp V}
 =\SpanM{\supp x\cup\supp y}.\label{tate:node:eq:support-equality}
\end{equation}
In particular, for $0<v(u)<\alpha$,
$\SpanM{\supp u\cup\supp(q/u)}=\SpanM{\supp X_q(u)\cup\supp Y_q(u)}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The forward inclusion is \eqref{tate:node:eq:support-control} applied to the
integral zero-constant series $\X,\Y$. Apply it to the zero-constant formal
inverse $\UU,\VV$ for the reverse inclusion. Taking generated monoids on
both sides gives equality.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Exact monoidal descent]\label{tate:node:cor:monoidal}
Let $M\subseteq\Gamma_{\ge0}$ be an additive submonoid, and let $\m_M$ be
the elements of $\m_v$ whose supports lie in $M$. For $0\ne q\in\m_M$, the
node chart restricts to a bijection
\[
 \{(U,V)\in\m_M^2:UV=q\}
   \ \simeq\ \{(x,y)\in\m_M^2:\Fp(q,x,y)=0\}.
\]
No enlargement of the positive exponent monoid is required in either
direction.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Every finite sum of exponents in $M$ remains in $M$, so both formal maps
preserve $\m_M^2$. \Cref{tate:node:thm:nodalsurj} and its proof then
restrict verbatim.
\end{proof}

This retains more information than saying that the answer stays in the
original Hahn \emph{field}: it identifies the positive monoid needed for the
inverse. The individual supports need not be equal; substantial
cancellation can occur in one coordinate, and the theorem is formulated so
that this does not destroy the invariant. It refines, in the node tube, the
one-directional certificate \eqref{tate:eq:certificate} of
\cref{tate:thm:coordinates}, which bounds the supports of $X_q(u)-f(u)$ and
$Y_q(u)-g(u)$ by the monoid of $\supp(q)\cup\supp(qu)\cup\supp(q/u)$. The
analogous statement for the modular parameter, with generated groups instead
of monoids, is \eqref{tate:eq:supportgroup} of \cref{tate:thm:recover}.

\subsection{An isometry principle for formal charts}

\begin{lemma}[Valuation isometry]\label{tate:node:lem:isometry}
Let $A(Z)\in k[[Z_1,\ldots,Z_d]]^d$ have zero constant term and linear part
$L_A\in\GL_d(k)$. Then for $a,b\in\m_v^d$,
\begin{equation}
 \bv(A(a)-A(b))=\bv(a-b).\label{tate:node:eq:isometry}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The assertion is immediate if $a=b$. Otherwise put $\rho_0=\bv(a-b)$ and let
$\epsilon>0$ be the least valuation among all nonzero coordinates of the two
finite vectors $a,b$. Write $A(Z)=L_AZ+N(Z)$, where $N$ has degree at least
two. The difference of any monomial of degree at least two is a sum of terms
containing one coordinate of $a-b$ and at least one additional coordinate of
$a$ or $b$. Formally this gives $N(a)-N(b)=C(a,b)(a-b)$, where every entry of
the formal matrix $C$ has zero constant term. This factorization specializes
by \cref{tate:lem:neumann}; every nonzero entry of $C(a,b)$ has valuation at
least $\epsilon$. Therefore $\bv(N(a)-N(b))\ge\rho_0+\epsilon>\rho_0$.
A constant matrix has entries of nonnegative valuation, and so does
$L_A^{-1}$. Applying both matrices gives $\bv(L_A(a-b))=\bv(a-b)=\rho_0$.
The nonlinear term cannot cancel all coordinates attaining that least
valuation.
\end{proof}

For $d=1$ and linear coefficient $1$ this is the isometry
\eqref{tate:eq:isometry} of \cref{tate:lem:formal-identity}, which
source~01 proves for the coarse identity parameter.

\begin{corollary}[The node chart on the infinitesimal bidisk]\label{tate:node:cor:bidisk}
The map $T=(\X,\Y)$ is a bijection $\m_v^2\to\m_v^2$, and
$\bv\bigl(T(a,b)-T(c,d)\bigr)=\bv(a-c,b-d)$ for all $a,b,c,d\in\m_v$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
\Cref{tate:node:lem:implicit} with \eqref{tate:node:eq:linear} and
\cref{tate:node:lem:isometry}.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Two-period comparison]\label{tate:node:thm:moving}
Let $q,q'\in\m_v\setminus\{0\}$, and choose $u,u'\in\KK^\times$ with
$0<v(u)<v(q)$ and $0<v(u')<v(q')$. Then
\begin{equation}
 \min\{v(X_q(u)-X_{q'}(u')),\,v(Y_q(u)-Y_{q'}(u'))\}
 =\min\{v(u-u'),\,v(q/u-q'/u')\}.\label{tate:node:eq:moving}
\end{equation}
This identity remains valid when one difference is zero, with
$v(0)=\infty$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Both points are values of the \emph{same} formal map $T$, at $(u,q/u)$ and
$(u',q'/u')$. Apply \cref{tate:node:cor:bidisk}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Fixed-period distance and coordinate size]\label{tate:node:cor:fixedmetric}
For $q'=q$ and $u\ne u'$ in the node annulus,
\begin{equation}
 \min\{v(X_q(u)-X_q(u')),\,v(Y_q(u)-Y_q(u'))\}
 =v(u-u')+\min\{0,\alpha-v(u)-v(u')\}.\label{tate:node:eq:fixeddistance}
\end{equation}
Also
\begin{equation}
 \min\{v(X_q(u)),\,v(Y_q(u))\}=\min\{v(u),\,\alpha-v(u)\}.
 \label{tate:node:eq:coordinate-size}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The identity $q/u-q/u'=q(u'-u)/(uu')$ gives the second valuation in
\eqref{tate:node:eq:moving}. For \eqref{tate:node:eq:coordinate-size},
compare $T(u,q/u)$ with $T(0,0)=(0,0)$ using \cref{tate:node:cor:bidisk}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Exact period sensitivity]\label{tate:node:cor:periodsensitivity}
Suppose $q\ne q'$ and $0<v(u)<\min\{v(q),v(q')\}$. Then
\begin{equation}
 \min\{v(X_q(u)-X_{q'}(u)),\,v(Y_q(u)-Y_{q'}(u))\}=v(q-q')-v(u).
 \label{tate:node:eq:sensitivity}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
In \eqref{tate:node:eq:moving} the first difference is zero and the second
is $(q-q')/u$.
\end{proof}

These are equalities, not lower estimates from a triangle inequality: at
least one affine coordinate detects the full change of the branch
coordinates. They include the balanced case $2v(u)=\alpha$, where the
leading terms of $X_q(u)$ may cancel, and smaller scales inside a proper
convex subgroup, which a rank-one quotient of $\Gamma$ would erase. No
noncancellation hypothesis on leading coefficients is imposed.

\subsection{Sharpness and cancellation}

For a balanced example take $k=\C$, $q=t^2$ and $u=it$. Then $q/u=-it$, and
\eqref{tate:node:eq:Xsmall} shows cancellation of the linear $X$-terms:
\[
 X_{t^2}(it)=-6t^2+O(t^4),\qquad Y_{t^2}(it)=it+O(t^2).
\]
The first coordinate alone would suggest depth $2$; the vector depth is $1$,
as \eqref{tate:node:eq:coordinate-size} requires. No general-position
assumption can replace the use of both coordinates.

For period sensitivity take $q=t^3$, $q'=t^3+t^N$ with an integer $N>3$,
and $u=t$. Then \eqref{tate:node:eq:sensitivity} gives exact depth $N-1$:
the formula identifies the valuation of the difference, not merely an upper
bound for its size.

\section{Scalar extension and the valuation-circle quotient}\label{tate:sec:extension}

\subsection{Strong functoriality}

\begin{theorem}[Exponent embeddings preserve uniformization]\label{tate:thm:extension}
Let $\iota:\Gamma\hookrightarrow\Gamma'$ be an ordered group embedding.
The coefficient-preserving map
\[
 \iota_*:K_\Gamma\hookrightarrow K_{\Gamma'},\qquad
 \sum a_\gamma t^\gamma\longmapsto\sum a_\gamma t^{\iota(\gamma)}
\]
is a field embedding preserving strong sums. If $q'=\iota_*(q)$, then
\begin{equation}
 \iota_*(\Phi_q(u))=\Phi_{q'}(\iota_*(u))\qquad(u\in U_q),
 \label{tate:eq:functorial}
\end{equation}
with the corresponding identities for $\Theta,X,Y$ where defined.
Moreover
\begin{equation}
 \iota^{-1}(H_{\iota(\alpha)})=H_\alpha.\label{tate:eq:Hpullback}
\end{equation}
In the larger field the full admissible domain is
$v^{-1}(H_{\iota(\alpha)})$, not in general all of $K_{\Gamma'}^\times$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
An order embedding preserves and reflects well-ordering and preserves
finite coefficient contributions. It therefore preserves the defining
Hahn operations and every strongly summable family. Apply this to the
bilateral formulas, or to their positive-tail forms. The point at
infinity and the powers of $q$ are also preserved. Finally, inequalities
$|\iota(\gamma)|\le N\iota(\alpha)$ are equivalent to
$|\gamma|\le N\alpha$, proving \eqref{tate:eq:Hpullback}. The exact-domain
theorems over $\Gamma'$ give the last assertion.
\end{proof}

This theorem has a useful asymmetric meaning. An old admissible
parameter never becomes inadmissible under an ordered exponent embedding.
But the assertion ``every nonzero parameter is admissible'' may fail
after enlargement: new valuations can lie outside the old $q$-scale.
The curve acquires new points, yet its correct, enlarged admissible
subgroup still reaches all of them.

\begin{example}[A rank-one base and a rank-two extension]\label{tate:ex:extension}
Embed $\R$ as the second summand in
$\Gamma'=\R\oplus_{\mathrm{lex}}\R$, and take $q=t^{(0,1)}$.
Over $\C((t^\R))$, every nonzero parameter is admitted. Over
$K_{\Gamma'}$, a parameter of valuation $(1,0)$ is not admitted.
A parameter $1+t^{(1,0)}$ is admitted and yields a new point reducing
to $O$ for the coarse first-coordinate valuation. Thus excluding a new
multiplicative scale does not exclude its infinitesimal displacement
from a nonzero old parameter.
\end{example}

\subsection{A canonical circularly ordered value quotient}

Let $\OO_v^\times=\{u\in K^\times:v(u)=0\}$ be the units of the
original Hahn valuation ring.

\begin{theorem}[The valuation quotient]\label{tate:thm:tropical}
There is a canonical surjective group homomorphism
\begin{equation}
 \trop_q:E_q(K)\longrightarrow H_\alpha/\Z\alpha,
 \qquad \trop_q(\Phi_q(u))=v(u)+\Z\alpha,\label{tate:eq:tropical}
\end{equation}
and an exact sequence
\begin{equation}
 1\longrightarrow\OO_v^\times\xrightarrow{\ \Phi_q\ }
 E_q(K)\xrightarrow{\ \trop_q\ }H_\alpha/\Z\alpha
 \longrightarrow0.\label{tate:eq:tropical-seq}
\end{equation}
Every value class has a unique representative in $[0,\alpha)$.
The ordering of that interval defines a translation-invariant circular
order on the quotient, with addition followed by reduction modulo
$\alpha$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Two parameters represent the same point exactly when their quotient is
a power of $q$, so their valuations differ by an integer multiple of
$\alpha$. This proves well-definedness. Every $\gamma\in H_\alpha$
is the valuation of $t^\gamma$, proving surjectivity.
If $v(u)=m\alpha$, the same point is represented by $u/q^m$, which is
a valuation-zero unit. Conversely units map to zero in the value
quotient. Their map into $E_q(K)$ is injective, since a power of $q$
has valuation zero only when it is $1$. Finite normalization gives the
unique representative. The usual cyclic order of three representatives
in a fundamental half-open interval is independent of simultaneous
translation, by cutting the interval at the translating point.
\end{proof}

There is a second exact sequence of additive groups
\begin{equation}
 0\longrightarrow H_\alpha^-\longrightarrow H_\alpha/\Z\alpha
 \longrightarrow
 (H_\alpha/H_\alpha^-)/\Z\bar\alpha\longrightarrow0.
 \label{tate:eq:circlelayers}
\end{equation}
Indeed $H_\alpha^-\cap\Z\alpha=0$, and the kernel computation is
immediate. This separates infinitesimal value directions from the
rank-one circular quotient.

When the relevant rank-one value group is all of $\R$, this value
quotient is the familiar real circle of length $v_1(q)$. Over a complete
algebraically closed rank-one field it agrees with the value-circle
description of the Tate curve skeleton \cite{BPR}.
At arbitrary rank, \eqref{tate:eq:tropical-seq} is an \emph{algebraic and
circular-order} assertion. It does not claim the existence of an ordinary
real-metric Berkovich skeleton with all of $H_\alpha$ as its distance
set. Higher-rank analytifications require their own definitions
\cite{FR}. The quotient $H_\alpha/\Z\alpha$ may itself have many proper
convex subgroups, and it is not identified with a classical real circle
unless additional hypotheses provide a real-valued interpretation.

The sequence \eqref{tate:eq:tropical-seq} has a log-geometric precedent.
In the Tate-curve example of Molcho and Wise \cite[\S5.1]{MW}, the
logarithmic Picard group is the part of $\mathbf G_{\log}$ bounded by the
loop length in the sense of their Definition~2.1.3.1, modulo a lift of that
length, and their tropicalization sequence (5.1.4) presents it as an
extension of the bounded tropical quotient by $\mathbf G_m$. For an ordered
value group and one length, the bounded subgroup is $H_\alpha$, and
\eqref{tate:eq:tropical-seq} is the explicit valuative counterpart of that
extension: its kernel is the unit group $\OO_v^\times$ and its quotient is
$H_\alpha/\Z\alpha$. \Cref{tate:thm:tropical} is therefore not claimed as a
new shape of result; what is added is its explicit Hahn realization.
Source~03 proves the same sequence (its ``value exact sequence'', with its
value map $\tau$ in place of $\trop_q$) over any field $k$ of characteristic
zero, by the same argument; by \cref{tate:node:thm:main} the statement and
proof of \cref{tate:thm:tropical} hold verbatim over $k((t^\Gamma))$. It is
printed once, here.

\subsection{The fine filtration and the extension obstruction}
\label{tate:node:sec:filtration}

This subsection comes from source~03, and the standing hypotheses of
\cref{tate:node:sec:second} apply: $k$ is any field of characteristic zero
and $\KK=k((t^\Gamma))$. Define the subgroups
\[
 E^v_0=\Phi_q(\OO_v^\times),\qquad
 E^v_1=\Phi_q(1+\m_v)
\]
of $E_q(\KK)$. The three charts of \cref{tate:node:sec:second} identify
$E^v_0$ with the points whose projective reduction for the fine valuation
$v$, a point of the nodal cubic over $k$, is nonsingular, and $E^v_1$ with
the points reducing to $O$. More explicitly, $E^v_1$ consists of $O$ and the
affine points with the negative coordinate valuations of
\cref{tate:node:lem:infinityvalues}; the other points of $E^v_0$ have
nonsingular affine reduction. The \emph{node tube} is the complement of
$E^v_0$ in $E_q(\KK)$.

The superscript $v$ is essential. The group $E_1(K)$ of
\cref{tate:sec:uniformization} is the kernel of reduction for the
\emph{coarse} valuation $w=v\bmod H_\alpha$; by
\cref{tate:prop:identityfiber} it equals $\Phi_q(1+\m_w)$. Since
$\m_w\subsetneq\m_v$ (for instance $q\in\m_v\setminus\m_w$) and $\Phi_q$ is
injective on $1+\m_v$, one has $E_1(K)\subsetneq E^v_1$ for $k=\C$: the two
filtrations never coincide.

\begin{proposition}[Fine residue sequence]\label{tate:node:prop:residue}
The sequence
\begin{equation}
 0\longrightarrow E^v_1\longrightarrow E^v_0
 \xrightarrow{\ \res\circ\Phi_q^{-1}\ }k^\times\longrightarrow0
 \label{tate:node:eq:residueexact}
\end{equation}
is exact, and $E^v_1\simeq(1+\m_v,\cdot)$. Here $0$ denotes the trivial
group even when a term is written multiplicatively.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$\Phi_q$ is injective on $\OO_v^\times$, because a nontrivial power of $q$
is not a unit, so $\res\circ\Phi_q^{-1}$ is well defined on $E^v_0$.
Residue on $\OO_v^\times$ is onto $k^\times$ with kernel $1+\m_v$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[No full-domain extension with the same kernel]\label{tate:node:thm:obstruction}
Suppose $H_\alpha\ne\Gamma$. There is no group homomorphism
$\Psi:\KK^\times\to E_q(\KK)$ extending $\Phi_q$ and having kernel
$q^\Z$. More precisely, any homomorphic extension $\Psi$ has an exact
sequence
\begin{equation}
 0\longrightarrow q^\Z\longrightarrow\ker\Psi
   \xrightarrow{\ v\bmod H_\alpha\ }\Gamma/H_\alpha
   \longrightarrow0.\label{tate:node:eq:kernel-extra}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Given $a\in\KK^\times$, surjectivity of $\Phi_q$ supplies $d\in U_q$ with
$\Phi_q(d)=\Psi(a)$. Hence $ad^{-1}\in\ker\Psi$, and its valuation modulo
$H_\alpha$ equals that of $a$. This proves surjectivity of the last map in
\eqref{tate:node:eq:kernel-extra}. Its kernel is
$\ker\Psi\cap U_q=\ker\Phi_q=q^\Z$. If $\Gamma/H_\alpha$ is nontrivial,
the extension kernel is strictly larger than $q^\Z$.
\end{proof}

This is not an assertion that every imaginable noncanonical extension is
impossible. It says that an extension cannot preserve both the canonical
values on $U_q$ and the intended period kernel. It is independent of the
failure of the raw sums outside $U_q$: one is a support obstruction, the
other an algebraic one. Source~03 also records the good coarse reduction of
\cref{tate:sec:uniformization} (its ``coarser scales see good reduction''),
with the same proof; it is printed once, there. As source~03 puts it, the
apparent multiplicative degeneration is confined to the scales bounded by
its own period: quotienting away all those scales removes the degeneration
rather than creating a larger parameter domain.

\section{Recovering the Tate parameter without adding scales}\label{tate:sec:j}

\subsection{The inverse modular series}

Write $J=1/j$. The formal series
\[
 J(q)=\frac{\Delta(q)}{c_4(q)^3}\in q\Z[[q]]
\]
has linear coefficient $1$, hence a unique compositional inverse
$Q(J)\in J\Z[[J]]$. Its first terms are
\begin{equation}
 Q(J)=J+744J^2+750420J^3+872769632J^4
       +1102652742882J^5+\cdots.\label{tate:eq:qJ}
\end{equation}
The notation $Q$ here denotes a formal series, not the rational field.

\begin{theorem}[Unique, support-preserving recovery]\label{tate:thm:recover}
Given $j\in K_\Gamma$ with $v(j)<0$, there is a unique $q\in K_\Gamma$
with $v(q)>0$ and $j(q)=j$. It is $q=Q(j^{-1})$ and satisfies
$v(q)=-v(j)$. If $G(a)$ denotes the additive subgroup of $\Gamma$
generated by $\supp(a)$, then
\begin{equation}
 G(q)=G(j).\label{tate:eq:supportgroup}
\end{equation}
More precisely,
\begin{equation}
 \supp(q)\subseteq
 \bigl(\supp(j^{-1})\bigr)^*\setminus\{0\}.\label{tate:eq:qmonoid}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Since $v(j^{-1})>0$, positive-support calculus evaluates $Q$ and both
formal inverse identities. Thus $J(q)=j^{-1}$ and $j(q)=j$.
The leading coefficient of $Q$ is $1$, so $v(q)=v(j^{-1})$.
If another positive-valuation $q'$ gives $j(q')=j$, evaluating the
identity $Q(J(q'))=q'$ proves equality. This argument does not choose
a branch of a transcendental function.

Formula \eqref{tate:eq:qmonoid} follows directly from the positive powers of
$j^{-1}$. Inversion of a Hahn series uses only the additive subgroup of
its support, so $G(j^{-1})\subseteq G(j)$. Therefore $G(q)\subseteq G(j)$.
Conversely $j(q)=q^{-1}$ times a power series in $q$ with nonzero
constant coefficient. Inversion and strong positive evaluation use only
$G(q)$, giving $G(j)\subseteq G(q)$.
\end{proof}

The inverse modular expansion is classical. The support equality is the
useful extra statement in a symbolic or nondivisible Hahn workspace:
one need not pass to the divisible hull merely to recover the elliptic
period parameter.

\subsection{Which elliptic curves this covers}

\begin{corollary}[Negative-$j$ uniformization]\label{tate:cor:negativej}
Assume $\Gamma$ is divisible. Every elliptic curve $E/K_\Gamma$ with
$v(j(E))<0$ is $K_\Gamma$-isomorphic to $E_q$, for the unique parameter
of \cref{tate:thm:recover}. After any such choice of isomorphism,
$E(K_\Gamma)$ is parametrized by $U_q/q^\Z$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
A Hahn field with algebraically closed coefficient field of
characteristic zero and divisible exponent group is algebraically
closed \cite{Poonen}. Elliptic curves over an algebraically closed field
of characteristic zero with the same $j$-invariant are isomorphic
\cite{SilvermanArithmetic}. Apply this to $E$ and $E_q$.
\end{proof}

The parameter $q$ is canonical, but an isomorphism $E\simeq E_q$ need
not be canonical. The hypothesis on $j$ excludes $j=0$ and $1728$, but
does not remove the usual sign ambiguity of an elliptic isomorphism.
For nondivisible $\Gamma$, \cref{tate:thm:recover} still holds, while a
curve with the same $j$ can be a nontrivial twist; we do not silently
identify all such twists with the split Tate curve over the original
field. Curves with $v(j)\ge0$ are outside this positive-$q$ theorem.

\section{Torsion fields and exact exponent ramification}\label{tate:sec:torsion}

This section retains nondivisible exponent groups deliberately. Replacing
$\Gamma$ by its divisible hull at the start would make every degree
computed here equal to one after base extension.

\subsection{Roots of a Hahn parameter}

\begin{lemma}[A single radical extension]\label{tate:lem:radical}
Let $n\ge2$, $q\in K_\Gamma$ with $v(q)=\alpha>0$, and
$\Lambda=\Gamma+\Z\alpha/n$. Then for any choice of $q^{1/n}$ in
$K_{\Gamma\otimes\Q}$,
\begin{equation}
 K_\Gamma(q^{1/n})=K_\Lambda,\qquad
 [K_\Lambda:K_\Gamma]=[\Lambda:\Gamma]
       =\ord(\alpha+n\Gamma).\label{tate:eq:radical}
\end{equation}
The extension is cyclic, with Galois group
$\operatorname{Hom}(\Lambda/\Gamma,\C^\times)$; it has unchanged
residue field $\C$ and value group $\Lambda$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Write $q=c t^\alpha(1+\varepsilon)$, $c\in\C^\times$, $v(\varepsilon)>0$.
Choose $c^{1/n}\in\C$. The binomial series gives
$b=(1+\varepsilon)^{1/n}\in K_\Gamma$, with $b^n=1+\varepsilon$.
Consequently $q^{1/n}=c^{1/n}t^{\alpha/n}b$ up to a root of unity
already in $\C$, so adjoining $q^{1/n}$ is the same as adjoining
$t^{\alpha/n}$.

The group $\Lambda/\Gamma$ is finite cyclic. If its order is $d$,
partition any Hahn support in $\Lambda$ among its $d$ cosets. Factoring
a fixed monomial from each part gives a finite sum of $d$ monomials
with coefficients in $K_\Gamma$. These monomials are linearly independent
over $K_\Gamma$, since their supports lie in disjoint cosets. Thus the
entire Hahn field $K_\Lambda$, not just a smaller subfield with finite
supports, is a degree-$d$ extension generated by $t^{\alpha/n}$.

Multiplying the monomial of exponent $\lambda$ by a character of
$\lambda+\Gamma$ defines a field automorphism, preserving strong sums.
There are $d$ such characters because $\C$ contains all roots of unity,
and they give all automorphisms. The coefficient residue field remains
$\C$ and the ramification index is $d$, equal to the field degree.
Finally $d$ is the least positive integer with $d\alpha\in n\Gamma$,
which is the displayed order in $\Gamma/n\Gamma$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{A primitive torsion point}

\begin{theorem}[The complete torsion field]\label{tate:thm:torsion}
Under the hypotheses of \cref{tate:lem:radical}, the $n^2$ points
\begin{equation}
 \Phi_q(\zeta_n^a q^{b/n}),\qquad 0\le a,b<n,\label{tate:eq:torsionpts}
\end{equation}
are precisely $E_q[n]$. Their field of definition is $K_\Lambda$,
and already
\begin{equation}
 K_\Gamma\bigl(\Phi_q(q^{1/n})\bigr)=K_\Lambda.\label{tate:eq:primitivepoint}
\end{equation}
Here adjoining a point means adjoining its affine coordinates.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Every parameter in \eqref{tate:eq:torsionpts} lies in the admissible group of
$K_\Lambda$, since its valuation is $b\alpha/n$. Its $n$th power is
$q^b$, so the group law makes its image $n$-torsion. If two of the
points coincide, injectivity modulo $q^\Z$ gives
\[
 \zeta_n^{a-a'}q^{(b-b')/n}=q^m
\]
for some integer $m$. Comparing valuations gives $b-b'=nm$; the chosen
ranges force $m=0$ and $b=b'$, and then $a=a'$. Thus there are $n^2$
distinct points. In characteristic zero an elliptic curve has exactly
$n^2$ geometric $n$-torsion points, proving completeness of the list.
They all have coordinates in $K_\Lambda$ by strong evaluation.

To prove the stronger primitive assertion, let
$L=K_\Gamma(q^{1/n})=K_\Lambda$ and let $\sigma\in\Gal(L/K_\Gamma)$
fix the point $P=\Phi_q(q^{1/n})$. The character description in
\cref{tate:lem:radical} implies that $\sigma$ preserves every strong sum,
so it commutes with the coordinate formulas. Therefore
\[
 \Phi_q(\sigma(q^{1/n}))=\Phi_q(q^{1/n}).
\]
The quotient $\sigma(q^{1/n})/q^{1/n}$ is a root of unity. By the
uniformization kernel it must also be a power of $q$. A root of unity
has valuation zero, whereas $v(q^m)=m\alpha$; hence that power is $1$.
Thus $\sigma$ fixes $q^{1/n}$ and is the identity. The stabilizer of
$P$ is trivial, so Galois theory gives \eqref{tate:eq:primitivepoint}.
The full torsion field contains this primitive point field and is
contained in $L$, proving equality.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[A finite-lattice degree formula]\label{tate:cor:lattice}
If $\Gamma\cong\Z^r$ as an abelian group, with any compatible total
order, and $\alpha$ has coordinates $(a_1,\dots,a_r)$, then
\begin{equation}
 [K_\Gamma(E_q[n]):K_\Gamma]
      =\frac{n}{\gcd(n,a_1,\dots,a_r)}.\label{tate:eq:gcddegree}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The order of $(a_1,\ldots,a_r)$ in $(\Z/n\Z)^r$ is the least $d>0$
for which $n$ divides every $da_i$. This is the displayed integer.
\end{proof}

For example, $n=12$ and $\alpha=(6,4)$ give degree $6$; $n=4$ and
$\alpha=(0,6)$ give degree $2$ in the lexicographic group $\Z^2$.
The entire unit part of $q$ can have infinite support and does not
alter these degrees. It is removed by a binomial root already in the
base field. Over $K_\Q$ every $n$th root scale is already available,
so the corresponding degrees are one. This explains why scalar
extension can erase arithmetic information while preserving all
uniformization identities.

\section{Transport to surreal and surcomplex numbers}\label{tate:sec:surcomplex}

\subsection{Set-sized normal-form workspaces}

The Conway normal form writes a surreal as a reverse-well-ordered sum
of real multiples of monomials $\omega^\gamma$. With the convention
$t^\gamma=\omega^{-\gamma}$, increasing well-ordered Hahn supports
correspond to these normal forms. Combining the real and imaginary
normal forms produces complex coefficients and gives the surcomplex
version \cite{Gonshor,BournezGuilmant}.

If a set of surcomplex parameters and point coordinates is specified,
the union of all of its normal-form exponent supports is a set. Its
additive subgroup $\Gamma\subseteq(\No,+)$ is a set as well. The
associated normal-form map embeds $K_\Gamma$ into $\No[i]$ and contains
all the specified elements. Enlarge to the divisible hull only when
algebraic closedness is actually needed.

The proof of \cref{tate:thm:main} works in this set-sized workspace. If a
larger workspace is chosen, \cref{tate:thm:extension} shows that the formulas
and their values agree. There is therefore no choice-dependent global
summation rule and no need to regard a proper class as a set.

\begin{theorem}[Surcomplex uniformization]\label{tate:thm:surcomplex}
For every $q\in\No[i]^\times$ with $\alpha=v(q)>0$, the literal Hahn
Tate map has exactly the admissible domain in \eqref{tate:eq:No-main} and
induces the group isomorphism stated there. This domain is always a
proper subclass of $\No[i]^\times$.
For every elliptic curve $E/\No[i]$ with $v(j(E))<0$, the unique
parameter $q=Q(j(E)^{-1})$ yields an isomorphic Tate curve.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The domain test for one parameter involves only $\alpha$, its valuation,
and ordinary integer inequalities. Its truth is unchanged in any
workspace containing those data. The same holds for all the strong
families in the formulas. For surjectivity, put $q$ and a given point's
coordinates in one workspace and apply \cref{tate:thm:main} there. For the
kernel and the group law, place the finitely many relevant parameters
in a common workspace. These are instances of \cref{tate:thm:extension}.

To see properness, take a positive surreal value
$\gamma=\omega\alpha$. It exceeds $N\alpha$ for every ordinary $N$.
The monomial $u=\omega^{-\gamma}$ lies outside the domain, by the exact
support theorem.

For the final assertion put the Weierstrass coefficients of $E$ in a
workspace and take its divisible hull. The algebraically closed Hahn
field there contains an isomorphism supplied by
\cref{tate:cor:negativej}. Its image in $\No[i]$ proves the assertion.
The parameter obtained by the inverse series is independent of the
workspace, again by strong functoriality.
\end{proof}

The following transfer comes from source~03 and uses the second route. For a
set-sized ordered subgroup $\Gamma\subset\No$ there are coherent Hahn
embeddings $\R((t^\Gamma))\hookrightarrow\No$ and
$\C((t^\Gamma))\hookrightarrow\No[i]$, $t^\gamma\mapsto\omega^{-\gamma}$,
whose values are genuine surreal or surcomplex numbers
\cite{Gonshor,BM}; complex conjugation acts coefficientwise. Enlarging the
exponent workspace preserves coefficient sums, rational operations and every
formal specialization used in
\cref{tate:node:sec:second,tate:node:sec:metric}, by
\cref{tate:lem:neumann}, so these constructions agree on intersections of
workspaces. No single set-sized Hahn field is claimed to contain all
surreals.

\begin{corollary}[Real surreal case and transferred refinements]\label{tate:node:cor:real}
Let $q\in\No[i]$ be nonzero and infinitesimal, with $\alpha=v(q)$. The
inverse charts of \cref{tate:node:sec:second}, the support-monoid equality
\cref{tate:node:thm:support}, the fixed- and moving-period valuation
formulas of \cref{tate:node:sec:metric}, and the kernel obstruction
\cref{tate:node:thm:obstruction} all hold for actual surcomplex numbers.
If $q\in\No$ is real, the same assertions, and the isomorphism
\[
 \{u\in\No^\times:|v(u)|\le N\alpha\text{ for some }N\in\N\}/q^\Z
 \ \simeq\ E_q(\No),
\]
hold over $\No$ with real parameters and real points. The quotient may be
represented by the class of unique normalized parameters with
$0\le v(u)<\alpha$, with multiplication followed by integer normalization;
no collection of all equivalence classes as a set is required.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
For evaluation, choose a common Hahn workspace containing $q$ and $u$ and
apply \cref{tate:node:thm:main} with $k=\C$, or with $k=\R$ when $q$ and
$u$ are real. Non-summability outside the domain is witnessed by a
descending sequence of leading exponents, so it cannot be removed by
enlarging a workspace. For surjectivity, choose a workspace containing $q$
and both coordinates of the desired point; the three inverse charts
construct a parameter in that same workspace, with real coefficients when
the inputs are real. The group law and every finite comparison can be
checked in a common workspace containing the finitely many inputs.
Coherence makes the resulting class function independent of this choice.
All support and valuation assertions transfer literally.
\end{proof}

The real case is not covered by the first route as written, which works
over $\C$ throughout; the classification of curves with $v(j)<0$ in
\cref{tate:thm:surcomplex} still needs $\C$ and a divisible workspace.

\subsection{A forbidden monomial and an admitted fine displacement}

\begin{example}[Two ways to use $\omega^{-\omega}$]\label{tate:ex:omega}
Let $q=\omega^{-1}$, so $\alpha=1$, and put
$\varepsilon=\omega^{-\omega}$. The parameter $u=\varepsilon$ is
inadmissible: $v(u)=\omega>N$ for all ordinary $N$.
For the theta family the exponents along $n=-k$ are
\[
 \frac{k(k+1)}2-k\omega,
\]
a strictly decreasing sequence. For the $X$ family they include
$\omega-k$, again strictly decreasing. No cancellation argument can
make either original family strongly summable.

In contrast $u=1+\varepsilon$ has valuation zero and is admissible.
The point has
\begin{align*}
 X_q(1+\varepsilon)&=\varepsilon^{-2}+\varepsilon^{-1}
                         +\text{a positive-valuation tail},\\
 Y_q(1+\varepsilon)&=-\varepsilon^{-3}-2\varepsilon^{-2}
                         -\varepsilon^{-1}
                         +\text{a positive-valuation tail},\\
 -X_q(1+\varepsilon)/Y_q(1+\varepsilon)
                    &=\varepsilon+O(\varepsilon^2).
\end{align*}
The last $O$ means valuation at least $2\omega$, not an ordinary
real asymptotic estimate. Thus an infinitesimal displacement far finer
than the $q$-scale is retained exactly, and its image has correspondingly
large elliptic coordinates.
\end{example}

Source~03 gives the same pair of tests (with $s=\omega^{-\omega}$, the
coordinates $X_q(1+s)\sim s^{-2}=\omega^{2\omega}$ and
$Y_q(1+s)\sim-s^{-3}=-\omega^{3\omega}$, where $a\sim b$ means that $a/b$
is $1$ plus an infinitesimal); they are printed once, above. It notes that
every negative-index summand for $u=\omega^{-\omega}$ is infinitesimal,
which alone does not make the family summable, and that the domain
restriction is a restriction on the valuation of $u$, not on the full
support of $u$ or on the sizes of the output coordinates. Its rank-two test,
$\Gamma=\Z\oplus\Z$ in lexicographic order with $q=t^{(0,1)}$, where
$t^{(1,0)}$ is inadmissible and $1+t^{(1,0)}$ is admissible and maps near
infinity, is the phenomenon of \cref{tate:ex:extension} and of the
introduction: the distinction is visible in a set-sized rank-two example
and is not an artifact of proper classes. The next example, also from
source~03, is new.

\begin{example}[An internal non-Archimedean scale]\label{tate:node:ex:inner}
Let $q=\omega^{-\omega}$ and $u=\omega^{-1}$. Then
$0<v(u)=1<\alpha=\omega$, and $q/u$ has valuation $\omega-1$. The node
chart gives
\[
 X_q(u)\sim\omega^{-1},\qquad Y_q(u)\sim\omega^{-2}.
\]
All ordinary powers of $u$ have valuations $n$, still below $\omega$; they
nevertheless form admissible Hahn families. The example shows why cofinal
convergence of powers is an inappropriate proof requirement.
\end{example}

\subsection{Why fine-topological convergence is not being used}

For $q=\omega^{-1}$, the powers $q^n$ tend to zero in a rank-one
workspace such as $\C((t^\R))$. They do not tend to zero when neighborhoods
can test against every surreal positive scale: none of the ordinary
$q^n$ is smaller than $\omega^{-\omega}$. The repository already
emphasizes this distinction between its fixed rank-one analysis and
full fine topology \cite{RepoRankOne}.

The present proof uses ordinary convergence only in the named complete
rank-one coarsening of $K_{H_\alpha}$. Everywhere else it uses strong
Hahn summation or finite algebra. Its finite-tail bound is a statement
in the ordered value group; it is not relabeled as an all-scale limit.
Neither a global surcomplex exponential nor a value of $\sin\omega$
is required. Multiplicative periods $q^\Z$ replace those additive
analytic constructions. The second route uses no convergence at all: only
strong Hahn summation, formal inversion and finite algebra.

\paragraph{Which notion of uniformization is asserted.}
The word refers here to a group parametrization by explicit Hahn sums and
formal local charts. It does not assert that $\No[i]$ carries a classical
complex-analytic covering space, that ordinary contours have been lifted,
or that all set-indexed partial sums converge in its fine topology. No
evaluation of an exponential at an arbitrary infinite imaginary argument is
used, and no choice of a global surcomplex logarithm is required: the
construction is multiplicative from the beginning. Nor is every elliptic
curve over $\No[i]$ classified. Source~03 treats the explicit family $E_q$
and curves supplied with an algebraic isomorphism to a member of it;
source~01 adds, through \cref{tate:thm:recover,tate:cor:negativej}, every
curve with $v(j)<0$. A general reduction-type classification, including
$v(j)\ge0$, would be a separate theorem (\cref{tate:sec:limits}).

\part{Several periods: finite positive generation and multiscale theta
series}\label{tate:part:theta}

\section{Setting, hypotheses, and the relation to Part I}\label{tate:theta:sec:setting}

This part comes from source~02. It takes up the first obstacle to a
higher-dimensional analogue of Part~I: deciding exactly when a theta series
in several variables, with several interacting scales, is a Hahn sum. The
gap is not the formal act of writing a familiar theta formula with larger
scalars. It is deciding exactly when that formula represents a Hahn sum,
identifying obstructions that survive every scalar extension, and replacing
infinite lattice comparisons by certified finite ones. These questions
become genuinely different when the value group has more than one
Archimedean level. The first theorem of the part
(\cref{tate:theta:thm:positive}) is a statement of higher-rank lattice
geometry that does not mention Hahn series; it answers a question posed in
print by Amini and Nicolussi. The named question is not originally a
question of surreal analysis; its application here gives the proposed
results about surreal-valued forms and surcomplex theta series.

\paragraph{Hypotheses of Part~II, kept apart from Part~I.}
The coefficient field is $\C$. The exponent group $\Gamma$ is set-sized,
and it is assumed divisible whenever halves of exponents are used
(equivalently, one starts with a divisible $\Gamma$). The periods are
\emph{monomials}: a symmetric matrix $\Pi=(\pi_{ab})$ of \emph{exponents}
in $\Gamma$ is given, and the theta series is
\begin{equation}
 \vartheta_\Pi(z)=\sum_{n\in\Z^g}t^{\pi(n)}z^n,\qquad
 \pi(n)=\tfrac12n^{\mathsf T}\Pi n,\qquad
 \cE_\beta(n)=\pi(n)+\beta\cdot n,\label{tate:theta:eq:energyintro}
\end{equation}
where $z=(z_1,\dots,z_g)$ and $z^n=\prod_jz_j^{n_j}$. Unit tails are allowed
in the arguments $z_j$ (\cref{tate:theta:thm:units}), not in the periods.
Part~I, by contrast, allows any $\Gamma$ and a period $q$ with an arbitrary
Hahn tail; the two sets of hypotheses are not merged anywhere. Source~02
itself makes no claim about Tate uniformization, a global surcomplex
exponential, or class-sized analytic convergence; the uniformization
theorems of this report are those of Part~I, in one variable.

\paragraph{Notation.}
Source~02's notation has been changed where it collided with Part~I:
its exponent matrix $Q$, quadratic form $q(n)$ and theta series
$\Theta_Q$ are $\Pi$, $\pi(n)$ and $\vartheta_\Pi$ here (so neither the Tate
parameter $q$ nor the series $\Theta_q$ of \eqref{tate:eq:theta} is
reused); its real space $H$, higher-rank form $B$ and lattice energy $F$ are
$\cH$, $\cB$ and $\cE$ (not $H_\alpha$, the series $B(q,h)$ of
\eqref{tate:eq:B}, or the residue workspace $F$); its minimum value
$\delta_\beta$ is $\mu_\beta$. The word \emph{admissible} has a fixed
meaning in Part~I (the Hahn-admissible domain $U_q$); here the
Amini--Nicolussi notion is always called \emph{AN-admissible}. The integer
$g$ is the number of variables, unrelated to the function $g(z)$ of
\cref{tate:sec:coordinates}; the integer $s$ is the active depth of
\cref{tate:theta:def:admissible}, unrelated to the Tate sums $s_k(q)$.
Order type is written $\ordtype$; it is support length, never birthday.

\paragraph{Relation to \cref{tate:thm:theta}.}
For $g=1$ and $\Pi=(\alpha)$ with $\alpha>0$, the series is
$\vartheta_{(\alpha)}(z)=\sum_nt^{\alpha n^2/2}z^n$. If the Tate parameter is
the monomial $q=t^\alpha$ and $\Gamma$ is divisible, then
$(-1)^nq^{n(n-1)/2}u^n=t^{\alpha n^2/2}(-t^{-\alpha/2}u)^n$, so
\[
 \Theta_{t^\alpha}(u)=\vartheta_{(\alpha)}\bigl(-t^{-\alpha/2}u\bigr),
\]
and the leading exponent $b_n$ of \eqref{tate:eq:bn} is the energy
$\cE_{\beta'}(n)$ with $\beta'=v(u)-\alpha/2$. Since $\alpha/2\in H_\alpha$,
the domain $\beta'\in H_\alpha$ of \cref{tate:theta:cor:rankone} is exactly
$v(u)\in H_\alpha$: in this case \cref{tate:theta:cor:rankone} recovers the
domain half of \cref{tate:thm:theta}. \Cref{tate:thm:theta} is more
general in two directions that Part~II does not reach: it allows an
arbitrary $q=c_qt^\alpha(1+\varepsilon_q)$, whose leading coefficient and
tail enter the $n$th summand through powers with exponent $n(n-1)/2$, which
are not argument perturbations of the kind covered by
\cref{tate:theta:thm:units}; and it assumes no divisibility. Conversely
Part~II treats $g$ variables. The two results are stated separately and
neither is presented as a special case of the other beyond this
dictionary.

\paragraph{Neighbouring reports.}
The closest relative in the collection is the report on Wick summability
certificates (\file{docs/surcomplex/wick-summability-certificates/}), placed
in the same batch. Both characterize strong summability of a monomial
family indexed by an integer lattice or semigroup by a finite certificate,
both allow Hahn-unit tails and show that such tails cannot repair a failure,
and both assume a divisible $\Gamma$. The mathematics differs: here a
quadratic energy on a lattice and its radical flags; there a linear weight
on an integer semigroup and its Hilbert basis. Neither report uses a theorem
of the other. The word \emph{flag} here always means the chain of radicals
\eqref{tate:theta:eq:flag}; the Markov-generator report
(\file{docs/surreal/markov-generators-at-every-scale/}) uses it for a chain
of plateau idempotents, which is unrelated.

\subsection{Lattices and higher-rank forms}

All vector-space dimensions and matrix sizes are ordinary finite integers.
A lattice in a real vector space is a discrete free abelian subgroup; it is
full in its real span. Initially let $\cH$ have a full lattice $L$, so a
lattice basis identifies $(\cH,L)$ with $(\R^g,\Z^g)$. Let
\[
 \cB=(\cB_1,\ldots,\cB_r):\cH\times\cH\longrightarrow\R^r_{\lex}
\]
be a symmetric real-bilinear map. Larger coordinate indices have lower
priority in the lexicographic order. Set $V_0=\cH$ and, when the indicated
restriction is positive semidefinite, define
\begin{equation}
 V_j=\rad(\cB_j|_{V_{j-1}}).\label{tate:theta:eq:flag}
\end{equation}
The radical of a positive semidefinite form is its nullspace, including all
vectors orthogonal to its entire restricted space.

\begin{definition}[AN-admissible forms]\label{tate:theta:def:admissible}
We call $\cB$ an \emph{AN-admissible higher-rank form on $(\cH,L)$} if, for
every $j$, $\cB_j|_{V_{j-1}}$ is positive semidefinite,
\begin{equation}
 \cB_j(V_j,\cH)=0,\label{tate:theta:eq:globalradical}
\end{equation}
each $V_j$ is rational with respect to $L$, and $V_r=0$. Rationality means
$\spanR(L\cap V_j)=V_j$. The \emph{active depth} is
$s=\#\{j:V_j\ne V_{j-1}\}$. Repeated stages are allowed and do not count
toward $s$.
\end{definition}

Under \eqref{tate:theta:eq:globalradical}, $V_j$ is exactly the common
global radical of $\cB_1,\ldots,\cB_j$. Thus this definition is the
higher-rank inner-product condition of Amini--Nicolussi together with their
full-lattice admissibility condition, written recursively
\cite[Definition~2.5 and Theorem~8.1]{AN}; it is their definition, not a new
one. If $x\ne0$ and $j$ is its first active level, then
\begin{equation}
 \cB_i(x,\cH)=0\ (i<j),\qquad \cB_j(x,x)>0.\label{tate:theta:eq:firstlevel}
\end{equation}
Consequently $\cB(x,x)>0$ lexicographically. The converse to this last
sentence is false: pointwise lexicographic positivity does not force
\eqref{tate:theta:eq:globalradical}.

\subsection{Hahn preliminaries}

Strong summability is the notion of \cref{tate:sec:hahn}. For nonzero
constants $c_i$, the family $(c_it^{\gamma_i})_i$ is strongly summable
precisely when $\{\gamma_i\}$ is well ordered and $i\mapsto\gamma_i$ has
finite fibres; cancellation after grouping is not a licence to omit the
finite-fibre requirement. Finite unions and pairwise sums of well-ordered
subsets of an ordered abelian group are well ordered, and for well-ordered
$A,C$ each exponent has only finitely many representations $a+c$ with
$a\in A$, $c\in C$. Together with \cref{tate:lem:neumann}, these are the
support facts used below. They are algebraic support statements, not
completeness assertions in the fine topology of $\No[i]$. Every
$z_j\in K^\times$ has a unique decomposition
\begin{equation}
 z_j=c_jt^{\beta_j}(1+\varepsilon_j),\qquad c_j\in\C^\times,\quad
 \beta_j\in\Gamma,\quad\varepsilon_j\in\m_v,\label{tate:theta:eq:point}
\end{equation}
using the leading coefficient and exponent, not any surcomplex logarithm;
here $\m_v$ is the set of elements of positive valuation.

\subsection{Why finite-rank coordinates suffice}\label{tate:theta:sec:finiteenvelope}

The entries of $\Pi$ and $\beta$ generate a finite-dimensional rational
vector space inside the divisible hull of $\Gamma$. Such an ordered rational
space has a finite chain of convex subspaces, since every strict inclusion
increases rational dimension. Consecutive quotients are Archimedean, for
otherwise an element infinitesimal relative to another would give an
intermediate convex subspace. Each quotient order-embeds in $\R$ by the
usual rational-cut construction: fix $u_0>0$ and send $x$ to
$\sup\{r\in\Q:ru_0\le x\}$; Archimedeanness makes the set nonempty and
bounded, makes the map additive and rational-linear, and ensures that a
positive element has positive image. Choosing rational-linear sections
yields an order embedding of the original space into some $\R^r_{\lex}$,
the first nonzero quotient coordinate determining the sign.

Thus every particular quadratic lattice family, even in an infinite-rank
value group, can be tested in finitely many real lexicographic coordinates.
For surreal exponents one can instead use the finite-dimensional ordered
\emph{real} span of the entries. We never assert that one fixed coordinate
model contains every possible evaluation vector in a larger value group.
This is an elementary fact about finite-dimensional ordered rational spaces,
not a new result; the Markov-generator report of the collection uses the
same kind of finite rational sign separation.

\section{Finite positive generation}\label{tate:theta:sec:positive}

\begin{definition}[Positive generation]\label{tate:theta:def:posgen}
A finite subset $S\subset L\setminus\{0\}$ \emph{positively generates} $L$
for $\cB$ if every $x\in L$ can be written
\begin{equation}
 x=\sum_{a\in A}m_aa,\qquad
 A\subset S,\quad m_a\in\Z_{>0},\quad \cB(a,b)\ge0\quad(a,b\in A).
 \label{tate:theta:eq:positivegeneration}
\end{equation}
The zero vector has the empty representation.
\end{definition}

This is \cite[Definition~11.10]{AN}, which requires a positive integer
combination of a subset $A\subset S$ whose members have pairwise
nonnegative inner products.

\begin{theorem}[Finite positive generation]\label{tate:theta:thm:positive}
Every admissible lattice in a finite-dimensional higher-rank inner-product
space, in the sense of Amini--Nicolussi, has a finite positively generating
set. In particular every form of \cref{tate:theta:def:admissible} has such a
set. The construction is finite and uses lattice lifts, ordinary acute
cones in graded quotients, and bounded integer shifts. It gives an
affirmative answer to Question~11.14 of \cite{AN} in its published
formulation: ``Does $L$ have a finite, positively generating set $S$ in the
sense of Definition~11.10?'' \cite[\S11.5.3, p.~1181]{AN}.
\end{theorem}

The essential feature is not that a lattice is finitely generated as a
group: arbitrary signed basis decompositions do not preserve the
nonnegativity in \eqref{tate:theta:eq:positivegeneration}. The following
lemmas provide the extra control. The claim concerning Question~11.14 is
pinned to its published formulation, checked in the 2025 article and in the
arXiv preprint; it is not a claim that nobody has answered the question
since.

\subsection{The positive-definite case}

\begin{lemma}[Finite acute generating families]\label{tate:theta:lem:acute}
Let $L'$ be a full lattice in a finite-dimensional real space with a
positive-definite real form $G$. There are finitely many finite sets
$T_1,\ldots,T_h\subset L'\setminus\{0\}$ such that every lattice vector is a
nonnegative integer combination of one $T_\nu$, and $G(a,b)>0$ for every
pair $a,b\in T_\nu$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If $L'=0$, take $T_1=\varnothing$. Otherwise, for each unit vector choose a
narrow simplicial cone with rational rays, containing that vector in its
interior, such that every two nonzero vectors in the cone have positive
$G$-pairing. Such cones exist by continuity of $G$, positive definiteness,
and density of rational directions. Compactness of the ordinary unit sphere
gives a finite covering by these cones.

For one rational simplicial cone, choose independent lattice generators
$v_1,\ldots,v_d$ for its rays. Every lattice point in that cone is
$\sum_ia_iv_i$ with $a_i\ge0$. Subtracting $\sum_i\lfloor a_i\rfloor v_i$
leaves a lattice point in the bounded half-open parallelepiped
$\{\sum_iu_iv_i:0\le u_i<1\}$, which contains finitely many lattice points.
The ray generators and the nonzero parallelepiped points generate the
lattice semigroup in the cone. They remain pairwise strictly acute because
they lie in that cone. Do this for each cone.
\end{proof}

This is the familiar rank-one ingredient, also described by Amini and
Nicolussi immediately after their question (via Gordan's lemma); it is
credited to them. Compactness here is only compactness of an ordinary real
finite-dimensional sphere, not of a surreal sphere.

\subsection{A bounded allocation lemma}

\begin{lemma}[Distributing lower-level coefficients]\label{tate:theta:lem:allocation}
Let $N,M$ be positive integers and $m_a\in\N$ for $a$ in a finite set $A$.
There are integers $c_{ia}\in\{0,\ldots,M\}$ and $r_a\ge0$ such that
\[
 m_a=\sum_{i=1}^Nc_{ia}+r_a,
 \qquad r_a>0\ \Longrightarrow\ c_{ia}=M\quad\text{for every }i.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For a fixed $a$, if $m_a\ge NM$, fill every bin to $M$ and put the remainder
into $r_a$. Otherwise distribute $m_a$ among $N$ bins of capacity $M$ and
set $r_a=0$. The constructions for different $a$ are independent.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[A shift dominates every fixed mixed term]\label{tate:theta:lem:shift}
For an AN-admissible higher-rank form, $a\ne0$ and $x\in\cH$, there is an
ordinary integer $M>0$ such that $\cB(x,a)+M\cB(a,a)>0$. For finite sets of
$x$ and $a$ one $M$ works for every pair.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $j$ be the first level of $a$. By \eqref{tate:theta:eq:firstlevel}, both
terms have zero coordinates before $j$, and $\cB_j(a,a)>0$. Choose an
integer $M>-\cB_j(x,a)/\cB_j(a,a)$. The $j$th coordinate is then positive,
regardless of all later coordinates. Taking a maximum handles finite sets.
\end{proof}

The global radical condition is exactly what excludes an adverse mixed term
at a scale higher than the self-pairing of $a$.

\subsection{Proof by lifting and allocation}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{tate:theta:thm:positive} for a full lattice]
Induct on the number of active levels. The zero-dimensional case is empty,
and the one-level case follows from \cref{tate:theta:lem:acute}. Delete
initial zero stages and call the first form $\cB_1$. Let $V=\rad\cB_1$ and
$L_0=L\cap V$. The quotient $\overline L=L/L_0$ is a full lattice in
$\cH/V$, because $V$ is rational. The induced $\cB_1$ is positive definite
there.

Choose finite acute families in $\overline L$ by
\cref{tate:theta:lem:acute}. Write $\cT$ for their union, and choose once
and for all a lift $\widetilde t\in L$ for every $t\in\cT$. On $V$, the
remaining forms and $L_0$ satisfy the inductive hypotheses. Let $S_0$ be a
finite positively generating set for $L_0$. If $V=0$, the lifted acute sets
suffice, so assume $S_0\ne\varnothing$. By \cref{tate:theta:lem:shift},
choose a positive integer $M$ satisfying
\begin{equation}
 \cB(\widetilde t,a)+M\cB(a,a)>0
 \quad(t\in\cT,\ a\in S_0).\label{tate:theta:eq:uniformshift}
\end{equation}
Define the finite candidate set
\begin{equation}
 S=S_0\ \cup\
 \Bigl\{\widetilde t+\sum_{a\in S_0}c_aa:
          t\in\cT,\ 0\le c_a\le M,\ c_a\in\Z\Bigr\}.
 \label{tate:theta:eq:constructedS}
\end{equation}
Every vector in the second set has nonzero quotient, so none is zero.

Take $x\in L$. If its quotient is zero, use $S_0$. Otherwise represent its
quotient using one acute family, and expand integer multiplicities into a
list $t_1,\ldots,t_N$; every pair $t_i,t_j$ has strictly positive
$\cB_1$-pairing. The residual $w=x-\sum_{i=1}^N\widetilde t_i\in L_0$ has
a representation $w=\sum_{a\in A}m_aa$ for some pairwise $\cB$-nonnegative
$A\subset S_0$. Apply \cref{tate:theta:lem:allocation} to these
coefficients and put $u_i=\widetilde t_i+\sum_{a\in A}c_{ia}a\in S$. Then
$x=\sum_iu_i+\sum_ar_aa$.

It remains to check all pairs of displayed summands. The $u_i$ are mutually
positive at the first level, since adding vectors of $V$ does not change
$\cB_1$. The residual $a$'s remain pairwise nonnegative. Finally, if
$r_a>0$, all $c_{ia}=M$, and
\[
 \cB(u_i,a)=\cB(\widetilde t_i,a)+M\cB(a,a)
       +\sum_{b\in A\setminus\{a\}}c_{ib}\cB(b,a)>0
\]
by \eqref{tate:theta:eq:uniformshift}. Combining duplicate summands gives
the required positive-integer representation. This closes the induction.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Non-full lattices]\label{tate:theta:rem:nonfull}
The theorem also covers the non-full lattices of the published question.
Let $F^j$ be the global radical filtration and $L_j=L\cap F^j$.
Admissibility says that each image of $L_j$ in $F^j/F^{j+1}$ is discrete
\cite[Theorem~8.1]{AN}. Set $W_L=\spanR L$. Inductively,
$W_L\cap F^j=\spanR L_j$: the image of $L_j$ is a lattice, so its rank
equals the dimension of its real span, and rank--nullity identifies the
kernel dimension with $\operatorname{rank}L_{j+1}$; the inclusion of
$\spanR L_{j+1}$ in that kernel is consequently equality. The restricted
form on $W_L$ has filtration $W_L\cap F^j$: a vector outside that
intersection has an earlier positive self-pairing and cannot enter the
restricted radical. Thus $L$ is a full AN-admissible lattice in $W_L$, to
which the proof applies.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Size and dependence}

The construction gives
\begin{equation}
 |S|\le|S_0|+|\cT|(M+1)^{|S_0|},\label{tate:theta:eq:Sbound}
\end{equation}
with possible duplicates removed. This is an existence and termination
bound, not a claim of practical optimality. The set depends only on $\cB$
and $L$, not on any theta evaluation point or linear term. Adjoining its
negative makes it symmetric without invalidating positive generation. The
proof allows irrational coefficients in the forms and irrational lifts of
orthogonal decompositions; only the radical \emph{subspaces} need be
lattice-rational. No algorithm for deciding arbitrary real-number
equalities is asserted. For exact algebraic inputs, the construction uses
ordinary exact arithmetic, sign tests, rational cones, and lattice
semigroup generators.

\section{An exact quadratic Hahn-summability criterion}\label{tate:theta:sec:quadratic}

\subsection{The real one-level obstruction}

\begin{lemma}\label{tate:theta:lem:latticeprojection}
For a real linear map $R:\R^d\to\R^e$, the subgroup $R\Z^d$ is discrete in
its real span if and only if $\ker R$ is rational relative to $\Z^d$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
A rational kernel admits an adapted lattice basis; the remaining basis
vectors give a basis of the quotient real space, and their images generate a
full lattice in $\operatorname{im}R$. Conversely, a discrete subgroup
spanning a $k_0$-dimensional real space is a lattice of rank $k_0$. Hence
$\ker(R|_{\Z^d})$ has rank $d-k_0$, which equals $\dim\ker R$. Its real
span is contained in that kernel and has the same dimension.
\end{proof}

The elementary assertion about discrete subgroups follows, for example, by
choosing a shortest nonzero vector, projecting successively to its
orthogonal complement, and applying discreteness in a bounded fundamental
strip; it is the usual finite-dimensional lattice theorem.

\begin{lemma}[One-level quadratic criterion]\label{tate:theta:lem:onelevel}
Let $A$ be a real symmetric $d\times d$ matrix and $b\in\R^d$. The set
$\{f_A(n)=\tfrac12n^{\mathsf T}An+b\cdot n:n\in\Z^d\}$ is well ordered if
and only if $A$ is positive semidefinite, $V=\ker A$ is rational, and
$b|_V=0$. Under these conditions every bounded sublevel contains finitely
many cosets of $\Z^d\cap V$, and $f_A$ is constant on each such coset.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If $A$ has a negative direction, it has a rational negative direction by
continuity. Multiplying gives an integer $u$ with $u^{\mathsf T}Au<0$, and
$f_A(ku)\to-\infty$, contradicting well-ordering.

Now suppose $A$ is positive semidefinite. If $b(v_0)<0$ for some
$v_0\in V$, choose integer vectors $n_k$ by rounding the coordinates of
$kv_0$. The errors $e_k=n_k-kv_0$ remain bounded. Since $Av_0=0$,
$f_A(n_k)=\tfrac12e_k^{\mathsf T}Ae_k+kb(v_0)+b(e_k)\to-\infty$. Changing
the sign of $v_0$ handles any nonzero restriction of $b$ to $V$. Thus
$b|_V=0$ is necessary.

Factor $A=R^{\mathsf T}R$ with $\ker R=V$. The vanishing of $b$ on $V$ gives
a vector $c$ with $b(n)=c\cdot Rn$. If $V$ is irrational,
\cref{tate:theta:lem:latticeprojection} gives nonzero elements
$\ell_k\in R\Z^d$ with $\ell_k\to0$. Replace each $\ell_k$ by its negative
when necessary so that $c\cdot\ell_k\ge0$. Then
$0<\tfrac12\|\ell_k\|^2+c\cdot\ell_k\to0$, and a strictly decreasing
subsequence exists, so the value set is not well ordered. This proves the
necessity of rationality, including the case $b=0$.

Conversely, rationality makes $R\Z^d$ a lattice. On its real span,
$\tfrac12\|\ell\|^2+c\cdot\ell$ is a positive-definite quadratic polynomial
after completing the square, so its bounded sublevels contain finitely many
lattice points. These correspond precisely to the cosets of $\Z^d\cap V$.
In particular any nonempty subset of the value set has a least member:
start with one value and take the minimum of the finitely many values below
it.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The flag theorem}

Write a quadratic lattice energy in lexicographic coordinates as
\begin{equation}
 \cE(n)=\bigl(\cE_1(n),\ldots,\cE_r(n)\bigr),\qquad
 \cE_j(n)=\tfrac12\cB_j(n,n)+b_j(n),\label{tate:theta:eq:lexenergy}
\end{equation}
with arbitrary real linear forms $b_j$.

\begin{theorem}[Exact flag criterion]\label{tate:theta:thm:flag}
For $g\ge1$, the following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item $\cE(L)$ is well ordered and $\cE:L\to\R^r_{\lex}$ has finite fibres.
\item $\cB$ is AN-admissible (\cref{tate:theta:def:admissible}), and
\begin{equation}
 b_j(V_j)=0\qquad(1\le j\le r).\label{tate:theta:eq:linearcompatibility}
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
Equivalently, any family $(c_nt^{\cE(n)})_{n\in L}$ with all $c_n\ne0$ is
strongly Hahn-summable exactly under these conditions.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Identify $L$ with $\Z^g$. Suppose (i). Projection of a well-ordered
lexicographic set onto its first coordinate is well ordered: a least
preimage gives a least projected value for every nonempty projected subset.
Apply \cref{tate:theta:lem:onelevel} to $\cE_1$. This gives positive
semidefiniteness, the rational kernel $V_1$, and $b_1(V_1)=0$. The global
radical condition at the first level holds automatically.

Assume the conditions have been proved through level $j-1$. The preceding
coordinates are constant on every coset $n_0+L_{j-1}$, where
$L_{j-1}=L\cap V_{j-1}$. Restrict $\cE$ to one such coset, discard its
constant preceding coordinates, and project onto coordinate $j$. The
resulting quadratic polynomial on the full lattice $L_{j-1}$ has quadratic
part $\cB_j|_{V_{j-1}}$ and linear part
\begin{equation}
 x\longmapsto b_j(x)+\cB_j(n_0,x).\label{tate:theta:eq:shiftedlinear}
\end{equation}
The one-level lemma gives positive semidefiniteness and a rational radical
$V_j$. Taking $n_0=0$ gives $b_j(V_j)=0$. Taking every lattice basis vector
as $n_0$ shows $\cB_j(n_0,V_j)=0$ for a real basis of $\cH$, so
$\cB_j(\cH,V_j)=0$; testing only the coset through zero would miss this
essential condition. At the end every coordinate of $\cE$ is constant along
$L\cap V_r$. If $V_r\ne0$, its rationality gives a nonzero integer vector
and hence an infinite fibre. Thus $V_r=0$, proving (ii).

Conversely suppose (ii). The first-coordinate values are well ordered, and
every such value is represented by finitely many cosets of $L_1$, by the
one-level lemma. On each coset the remaining forms on $V_1$ satisfy the
same conditions: the shifted linear terms
\eqref{tate:theta:eq:shiftedlinear} still vanish on every subsequent radical,
because of the global radical conditions. Induct on the number of remaining
coordinates. Within each coset the tail support is well ordered with finite
fibres, and finite unions preserve both properties. Taking the
lexicographic union over the well-ordered first-coordinate values preserves
well-ordering. A full exponent fixes one first-coordinate value and
therefore only finitely many cosets, each with a finite tail fibre. This
proves (i).
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[The exact linear domain]\label{tate:theta:cor:domain}
For an AN-admissible form $\cB$, let
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal D_{\cB}=\{(b_1,\ldots,b_r):b_j\in V_j^\perp\text{ for all }j\}.
 \label{tate:theta:eq:D}
\end{equation}
This is exactly the set of linear terms giving a strongly summable
quadratic lattice family. It is also exactly the set giving an energy with a
global lattice minimum. If $\cB$ is not AN-admissible, no linear term gives
a strongly summable family.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Only the converse concerning minima needs proof. If a compatibility
condition first fails at level $j$, choose $a\in L\cap V_j$ with
$b_j(a)<0$; rationality of $V_j$ ensures that one exists. For every
$n\in L$ the coordinates before $j$ of $\cE(n+a)-\cE(n)$ vanish, and its
$j$th coordinate is $b_j(a)$, because $\cB_j(\cH,V_j)=0$. Hence
$\cE(n+a)<\cE(n)$ for every $n$, so there is no minimum. The other
assertions follow from \cref{tate:theta:thm:flag}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Minimum attainment is stronger than boundedness]\label{tate:theta:rem:bounds}
In a non-Archimedean ordered group, the descending sequence $(0,-k)$ is
bounded below by $(-1,0)$. Thus ``has a global lattice minimum'' in
\cref{tate:theta:cor:domain} cannot be replaced by ``is bounded below''.
The criteria use well-ordering and finite fibres, not an ambient lower
bound.
\end{remark}

\section{The ordinal length of the exponent support}\label{tate:theta:sec:ordinal}

\begin{lemma}[A finite interleaving bound]\label{tate:theta:lem:shuffle}
If $A_1,\ldots,A_N$ are well-ordered subsets of a common linear order, each
of order type at most $\omega^k$ for an ordinary integer $k\ge0$, then their
union has order type at most $\omega^kN$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For a point $x$ in the union, record for each $i$ the order type of
$A_i\cap(-\infty,x)$, and take the Hessenberg natural sum of these $N$
ordinals, meaning addition of coefficients in their finite Cantor normal
forms. This rank strictly increases as $x$ increases, since at least one
component strictly increases and none decreases. Each rank is smaller than
$\omega^kN$: all components are at most $\omega^k$, and at least one is
strictly smaller. The rank map embeds the union in that ordinal.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Exact ordinal support law]\label{tate:theta:thm:ordinal}
Under \cref{tate:theta:thm:flag}, let $s$ be the active depth. Then
\begin{equation}
 \ordtype\cE(L)=\omega^s.\label{tate:theta:eq:ordinal}
\end{equation}
For $g=0$ the value set is a singleton and the formula means $1=\omega^0$.
For $g>0$ one has $1\le s\le g$. The integer $s$ is an intrinsic invariant
of the ordered quadratic exponent family, independent of the chosen
finite-rank lexicographic coordinate model.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Use induction on active depth. Before the first active level every
quadratic and compatible linear coordinate is zero. At the first active
level the energy descends to a positive-definite quadratic polynomial on a
positive-rank quotient lattice. Its distinct values form a locally finite,
bounded-below, unbounded-above subset of $\R$, and hence have order type
$\omega$; each value comes from finitely many cosets of the radical
lattice.

If the radical is zero, each such fibre is finite, and subsequent
coordinates only split it into finitely many points; the whole order type
is $\omega$. Otherwise every radical coset has a tail energy of active
depth $s-1$, with compatible shifted linear terms, whose value set has order
type $\omega^{s-1}$ by induction. A first-coordinate fibre is a finite union
of such sets, so its order type lies between $\omega^{s-1}$ and
$\omega^{s-1}N$ for some positive finite $N$, by
\cref{tate:theta:lem:shuffle}. There are $\omega$ first-coordinate values.
The ordinal sum of their fibre types is exactly $\omega^s$: the lower
estimate gives $\omega^s$, and the upper estimate is a countable sum of
finite multiples of $\omega^{s-1}$, which is again $\omega^s$. Intermediate
inactive coordinates merely order finitely many cosets and are included in
this argument.

Strict drops lower real dimension, so $s\le g$. An order embedding of value
groups preserves the order type of the value set; since distinct finite
powers of $\omega$ are unequal, it also preserves the integer identified by
\eqref{tate:theta:eq:ordinal}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Actual surreal support lengths]\label{tate:theta:cor:surrealordinal}
At monomial evaluation points with positive real residues, the theta sum
has no coefficient cancellation, and its actual Hahn support has order type
$\omega^s$. Under a normal-form embedding into $\No$, this is the ordinal
length of its Conway normal-form support. For $g\ge1$, every $\omega^s$
with $1\le s\le g$ occurs in dimension $g$. The zero-dimensional case has
singleton support $\omega^0=1$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Each exponent receives a finite sum of positive real coefficients, so it
remains in the support. For $1\le s\le g$, split $\Z^g$ into $s$ nonzero
coordinate blocks and take one positive-definite sum of squares in each
successive lexicographic coordinate; the radical flag then has exactly $s$
strict drops.
\end{proof}

The invariant in this corollary is \emph{support length}, not birthday or
sign-sequence length; those are different surreal invariants. At arbitrary
complex residues, cancellations may shorten the support or annihilate the
sum entirely; \cref{tate:theta:thm:ordinal} then describes the exponent
family before cancellation.

\section{Multiscale theta series over arbitrary-rank Hahn fields}\label{tate:theta:sec:theta}

\subsection{The full multiplicative evaluation domain}

Let $\Pi$ be a symmetric $g\times g$ matrix of exponents in the divisible
group $\Gamma$, and write the argument as in \eqref{tate:theta:eq:point}.

\begin{theorem}[Exact domain and unit robustness]\label{tate:theta:thm:units}
The theta family $(t^{\pi(n)}z^n)_{n\in\Z^g}$ is strongly Hahn-summable if
and only if
\begin{equation}
 \{\pi(n)+\beta\cdot n:n\in\Z^g\}
 \quad\text{is well ordered with finite exponent fibres.}
 \label{tate:theta:eq:intrinsicdomain}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, any finite-rank envelope of $\Pi,\beta$ satisfies the flag
and linear compatibility conditions of \cref{tate:theta:thm:flag}. The
condition depends only on $\Pi$ and $\beta$, not on $c$ or the
$\varepsilon_j$. On this domain all polynomially weighted lattice families
and all multiplicative Taylor expansions at the point are strongly
summable.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The leading term of the $n$th summand is $c^nt^{\pi(n)+\beta\cdot n}$,
which is nonzero. Summability of the full family therefore implies
\eqref{tate:theta:eq:intrinsicdomain}: its leading exponents form a
well-ordered subset of the common support, and infinitely many equal
leading exponents would violate pointwise finiteness.

Conversely, let $A$ be the well-ordered leading-exponent set and put
$\Sigma=\bigcup_j\supp(\varepsilon_j)\subset\Gamma_{>0}$, a finite union of
positive well-ordered sets. For every integer $m$,
$(1+\varepsilon_j)^m=\sum_{k\ge0}\binom mk\varepsilon_j^k$ is a valid Hahn
identity by the support lemma, with support in $M=\langle\Sigma\rangle$.
Thus the entire theta family has support in $A+M$, which is well ordered.
A fixed exponent has only finitely many representations $a+\mu$ with
$a\in A$, $\mu\in M$, and each $a$ comes from finitely many $n$. This
proves summability.

Multiplying the $n$th coefficient by a polynomial in $n$ only removes some
terms and changes complex coefficients; it cannot worsen the support. For a
multiplicative perturbation $z_j\mapsto z_j(1+\eta_j)$ with $\eta_j\in\m_v$,
enlarge $\Sigma$ by their finitely many supports. The same monoid argument
justifies the binomial expansions and every interchange of the lattice
indices with Taylor indices; every resulting coefficient at a fixed
exponent receives only finitely many nonzero contributions.
\end{proof}

This is an exact domain for the displayed Hahn family. It does not prohibit
some different analytic continuation or a deliberately chosen
regularization outside that domain; neither is constructed or identified
with the sum here.

\subsection{Extension and non-extension}

\begin{corollary}[No repair by units or ordered scalar extension]\label{tate:theta:cor:norepair}
An order embedding $\Gamma\hookrightarrow\Gamma'$ and an injective
coefficient-field extension preserve summability and non-summability of a
fixed theta family at a fixed monomial valuation point. Infinitesimal unit
changes cannot repair a point outside the domain. New evaluation points in
$\Gamma'$ must, however, be tested anew.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
An order embedding preserves well-ordering, descending subsets, equality of
exponents, and finite fibres. Injectivity preserves nonzero leading
coefficients. Apply \cref{tate:theta:thm:units}.
\end{proof}

This is the multivariable counterpart of the one-variable behaviour recorded
in \cref{tate:thm:extension}.

\begin{corollary}[One-dimensional and diagonal domains]\label{tate:theta:cor:rankone}
For $g=1$ and $\pi(n)=\alpha n^2/2$ with $\alpha>0$, the exact valuation
domain is the principal convex subgroup
\begin{equation}
 H_\alpha=\{\beta\in\Gamma:|\beta|\le N\alpha
                  \text{ for some ordinary }N\in\N\}
 \label{tate:theta:eq:principalconvex}
\end{equation}
of \eqref{tate:eq:H}. For a diagonal $\Pi=\operatorname{diag}(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_g)$
with positive entries, the domain is $\prod_jH_{\alpha_j}$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
In a finite-rank model containing $\alpha$ and $\beta$, the quadratic form
vanishes at every level preceding the first nonzero coordinate of $\alpha$.
The flag criterion says exactly that $\beta$ vanishes at those levels too,
which is equivalent to \eqref{tate:theta:eq:principalconvex}. In the
diagonal case, necessity follows by restricting to each coordinate line.
For sufficiency, the whole exponent family is the finite Cartesian product
of the one-dimensional families, and Hahn multiplication with the
finite-representation support lemma gives summability.
\end{proof}

For the relation with \cref{tate:thm:theta} see
\cref{tate:theta:sec:setting}. A one-dimensional theta family summable at
all valuations in $\R\alpha$ need not be summable at a new valuation
$\beta$ dominating $N\alpha$ for every integer $N$: with the adverse sign,
successive exponents have difference $(n+\tfrac12)\alpha-|\beta|<0$ for
all $n\ge0$. The failure is structural, not a matter of an insufficient
number of computed terms.

\subsection{Quasi-periodicity and formal logarithmic derivatives}

For $m\in\Z^g$ write $t^{\Pi m}$ for the coordinatewise monomial vector.
Reindexing a summable family gives
\begin{equation}
 \vartheta_\Pi(t^{\Pi m}z)=t^{-\pi(m)}z^{-m}\vartheta_\Pi(z),
 \qquad \vartheta_\Pi(z^{-1})=\vartheta_\Pi(z).\label{tate:theta:eq:quasiperiod}
\end{equation}
These are the familiar theta symmetries, not novelty claims; here they are
licensed by the exact support criterion. The domain is stable under
$\beta\mapsto\beta+\Pi m$ and $\beta\mapsto-\beta$; the first statement
follows also from global radical annihilation, the second from
$n\mapsto-n$. For $g=1$ and $\Pi=(\alpha)$ the first identity is the
counterpart of \eqref{tate:eq:theta-functional} in the normalization of
\cref{tate:theta:sec:setting}.

Define the formal logarithmic operator $D_j=z_j\partial/\partial z_j$ on
the lattice characters by $D_jz^n=n_jz^n$. Then
$D_1^{a_1}\cdots D_g^{a_g}\vartheta_\Pi(z)
=\sum_nn_1^{a_1}\cdots n_g^{a_g}t^{\pi(n)}z^n$ is strongly summable
wherever $\vartheta_\Pi$ is. This is a coefficientwise Taylor calculus. It
is not a claim that a fine-topological notion of holomorphy has been
recovered, and it concerns no derivation on the scalars.

\section{Finite certificates and the geometry of theta minima}\label{tate:theta:sec:certificates}

\subsection{A finite test valid for every linear term}

Let $\cB$ be AN-admissible and choose a finite positively generating set
$S$ from \cref{tate:theta:thm:positive}. For a compatible linear term $b$,
put $\cE(n)=\cB(n,n)/2+b(n)$ and $I_m(h)=\cE(m+h)-\cE(m)$.

\begin{theorem}[Finite minimum certificate]\label{tate:theta:thm:certificate}
A lattice vector $m$ is a global minimizer of $\cE$ if and only if
\begin{equation}
 I_m(s)=\tfrac12\cB(s,s)+\cB(m,s)+b(s)\ge0
 \quad\text{for every }s\in S.\label{tate:theta:eq:cert}
\end{equation}
The equivalence of this finite test with global minimality holds even for
an incompatible $b$; in that case no point passes it. For compatible $b$,
repeatedly taking any $s\in S$ with negative difference terminates after
finitely many steps at a global minimum.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Necessity is immediate. For sufficiency, write any displacement as
$h=\sum_{s\in A}a_ss$ with positive integers $a_s$ and pairwise
$\cB$-nonnegative $s$. Direct expansion gives
\begin{equation}
 I_m(h)=\sum_sa_sI_m(s)+\sum_s\binom{a_s}2\cB(s,s)
   +\sum_{s<s'}a_sa_{s'}\cB(s,s').\label{tate:theta:eq:energydecomp}
\end{equation}
Every term is nonnegative under \eqref{tate:theta:eq:cert}; hence
$\cE(m+h)\ge\cE(m)$. No compatibility of $b$ was needed in this
calculation. Compatibility ensures that $\cE(L)$ is well ordered, so an
infinite strictly descending search is impossible. At termination all
finite tests pass.
\end{proof}

Well-ordering proves finite termination for each instance. It supplies
neither a useful uniform bound on the number of descent steps nor an
algorithm for comparing unspecified arbitrary real constants; those are
separate computational questions.

\begin{corollary}[Finite higher-rank Voronoi inequalities]\label{tate:theta:cor:voronoi}
For every $x\in\cH$,
\[
 x\in\Vor_L(0)
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 2\cB(x,s)\le\cB(s,s)\ \text{for every }s\in S.
\]
The inequalities are lexicographic. The conclusion concerns the actual
higher-rank Voronoi cell, not merely its Euclidean closure.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Use $b(n)=-\cB(x,n)$ in \cref{tate:theta:thm:certificate}; it is
compatible by the global radical conditions. The finite-inequality
implication is also the one proved by Amini--Nicolussi conditional on
positive generation, \cite[Lemma~11.11]{AN}; \cref{tate:theta:thm:positive}
supplies the finite generating set that lemma assumes.
\end{proof}

\subsection{All minima and a short zero-cost graph}

Let $\cM=\{n:\cE(n)=\min\cE(L)\}$, which is finite by
\cref{tate:theta:thm:flag}. Join two elements by an edge whenever their
difference or its negative belongs to $S$; use the symmetric enlargement of
$S$ when convenient.

\begin{theorem}[Finite minimizer geometry]\label{tate:theta:thm:minimizers}
The set $\cM$ has at most $2^g$ elements. Its zero-cost $S$-move graph is
connected and has diameter at most $g$. More precisely, for any
$m,n\in\cM$, the displacement $n-m$ is a sum of at most $g$ distinct members
of $S$ that are pairwise $\cB$-orthogonal, and every partial sum, added to
$m$, is a minimizer.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
If distinct $m,n$ were congruent modulo $2L$, their midpoint $u_0$ would be
a lattice vector and
$\cE(u_0)=\tfrac12(\cE(m)+\cE(n))-\tfrac18\cB(m-n,m-n)<\cE(m)$,
contradicting minimality. Thus reduction modulo $2L$ is injective on $\cM$,
giving the bound $|L/2L|=2^g$.

For the stronger assertion, positively decompose $h=n-m$ and apply
\eqref{tate:theta:eq:energydecomp}. Its left side is zero and every summand
on the right is nonnegative, so each is zero. Since $\cB(s,s)>0$, every
multiplicity $a_s$ equals one; moreover $I_m(s)=0$ and $\cB(s,s')=0$ for
distinct selected generators, and expanding any partial sum gives energy
change zero. The selected nonzero pairwise orthogonal vectors are linearly
independent: if $\sum c_ss=0$, pairing with one $s'$ gives
$c_{s'}\cB(s',s')=0$ in $\R^r$, so $c_{s'}=0$. There are at most $g$ of
them, and the path of their partial sums has at most $g$ edges.
\end{proof}

The parity estimate is the familiar midpoint mechanism of lattice Delaunay
geometry, credited as such. The addition here is its combination with one
constructive positively generating set, giving an explicit short zero-cost
move certificate.

\begin{corollary}[Complete finite enumeration after one minimum]\label{tate:theta:cor:BFS}
Starting from one certified minimum, breadth-first search through the
zero-cost $S$-moves lists every minimizer. It visits at most $2^g$ vertices
and requires at most $|S|2^g$ energy-difference tests.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
All discovered vertices are minimizers, and connectivity in
\cref{tate:theta:thm:minimizers} prevents any minimizer from being missed.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The finite initial theta polynomial}

For $\beta$ in the theta domain, define
\begin{equation}
 \mu_\beta=\min_n\cE_\beta(n),\qquad
 \cM_\beta=\{n:\cE_\beta(n)=\mu_\beta\},\qquad
 P_\beta(Z)=\sum_{n\in\cM_\beta}Z^n.\label{tate:theta:eq:initial}
\end{equation}
This is a nonzero Laurent polynomial with at most $2^g$ monomials. For $z$
as in \eqref{tate:theta:eq:point}, direct coefficient extraction gives
\begin{equation}
 \st\bigl(t^{-\mu_\beta}\vartheta_\Pi(z)\bigr)=P_\beta(c).
 \label{tate:theta:eq:initialvalue}
\end{equation}
Consequently $P_\beta(c)\ne0$ implies $v(\vartheta_\Pi(z))=\mu_\beta$. When
$P_\beta(c)=0$, the valuation is strictly larger or the sum is zero; no
equality of valuations is asserted in that case. This is the higher-rank
version of the initial-form mechanism underlying tropical theta geometry
\cite{FRSS}. The finite certificate identifies all its active lattice terms
without an unproved truncation radius. The quasi-periodicity identity gives
$\mu_{\beta+\Pi m}=\mu_\beta-\pi(m)-\beta\cdot m$ and
$\cM_{\beta+\Pi m}=\cM_\beta-m$, so the initial polynomial transforms by the
corresponding Laurent monomial factor.

\section{Lifting smooth initial zeros}\label{tate:theta:sec:zeros}

The formal implicit mechanism is classical, but a topological Newton-limit
argument is insufficient at arbitrary rank. The following support argument
extends \cref{tate:node:lem:implicit} to one unknown with infinitely many
monomial parameters.

\begin{lemma}[Implicit substitution with a Hahn certificate]\label{tate:theta:lem:implicit}
Let $\Sigma\subset\Gamma_{>0}$ be well ordered. For each
$\rho\in\Sigma\cup\{0\}$, let $R_\rho(U,W)$ be an ordinary formal power
series over $\C$ in finitely many variables $U=(U_1,\ldots,U_d)$ and one
variable $W$. Assume $R_0(0,0)=0$ and
$\partial R_0/\partial W(0,0)=a_0\ne0$. For every tuple $u\in\m_v^d$ there
is a unique $w\in\m_v$ satisfying
\begin{equation}
 R_0(u,w)+\sum_{\rho\in\Sigma}t^\rho R_\rho(u,w)=0,\label{tate:theta:eq:implicit}
\end{equation}
the equation being interpreted by strong summation. Moreover,
\begin{equation}
 \supp(w)\subset
 \Bigl\langle\Sigma\cup\bigcup_j\supp(u_j)\Bigr\rangle\setminus\{0\}.
 \label{tate:theta:eq:implicitsupport}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Introduce one independent formal variable $Y_\rho$ for each
$\rho\in\Sigma$, and use the ring of all coefficient families on
finite-support multiindices in the variables $U,Y$. Infinite collections of
monomials of one total degree are allowed; multiplication is still defined
since a fixed multiindex has only finitely many decompositions. The formal
equation $R_0(U,W)+\sum_\rho Y_\rho R_\rho(U,W)=0$ has a unique formal
solution $W=C(U,Y)$ with zero constant term: at each total degree the
coefficient of the new unknown term is $a_0$, while all other contributions
depend on lower-degree terms, and for each multiindex these are finite
coefficient calculations. This is the usual coefficient proof of the formal
implicit theorem, and it works unchanged for the infinite variable set.

Now substitute $Y_\rho=t^\rho$ and $U_j=u_j$. The union of all exponent
supports used is the positive well-ordered set in
\eqref{tate:theta:eq:implicitsupport}. Neumann's lemma makes its additive
monoid well ordered and gives only finitely many representations of any
exponent; since there are only finitely many $U_j$, distributing their
support terms adds only finitely many labels. Therefore the formal
substitution is strongly summable, including all sums used in the
equation, and its value $w$ has positive valuation and satisfies the
support certificate.

For uniqueness even among infinitesimals not known to have this
certificate, let $w,w'\in\m_v$ both satisfy \eqref{tate:theta:eq:implicit}.
Subtract and use
$W^k-(W')^k=(W-W')\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}W^{k-1-\ell}(W')^\ell$. The same
support argument, now including the supports of $w,w'$, factors the
difference as $(w-w')(a_0+\eta)=0$ with $\eta\in\m_v$. The second factor is
a unit, so $w=w'$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Smooth initial zeros lift]\label{tate:theta:thm:zerolift}
Let $\beta$ be in the exact theta domain and $c\in(\C^\times)^g$. Suppose
\[
 P_\beta(c)=0,\qquad
 \Bigl(Z_k\frac{\partial P_\beta}{\partial Z_k}\Bigr)(c)\ne0
\]
for some $k$. Choose arbitrary infinitesimals $\varepsilon_j$ for $j\ne k$.
There is a unique $\varepsilon_k\in\m_v$ such that
\begin{equation}
 \vartheta_\Pi\bigl(c_1t^{\beta_1}(1+\varepsilon_1),\ldots,
                c_gt^{\beta_g}(1+\varepsilon_g)\bigr)=0.
 \label{tate:theta:eq:thetalift}
\end{equation}
With $\Sigma_\beta=\{\cE_\beta(n)-\mu_\beta:n\notin\cM_\beta\}$,
\begin{equation}
 \supp(\varepsilon_k)\subset
 \Bigl\langle\Sigma_\beta\cup\bigcup_{j\ne k}\supp(\varepsilon_j)\Bigr\rangle
 \setminus\{0\}.\label{tate:theta:eq:zerosupport}
\end{equation}
Thus the smooth residue zero gives a genuine Hahn and surcomplex zero with a
specified support monoid, not just a formally suggested leading term.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Normalize the theta series by $t^{-\mu_\beta}$ and group by the exponent
gap; every group is finite. At gap zero one obtains
$R_0(U,W)=P_\beta(c_1(1+U_1),\ldots,c_k(1+W),\ldots,c_g(1+U_g))$, with the
$k$th $U$ omitted. At each positive gap the same construction gives a
finite Laurent polynomial in the $1+U_j$ and $1+W$, hence an ordinary
formal power series at the origin. The set $\Sigma_\beta$ is positive and
well ordered. The assumed logarithmic derivative is precisely
$\partial_WR_0(0,0)$. Apply \cref{tate:theta:lem:implicit}; evaluation of
the original theta family is licensed by \cref{tate:theta:thm:units}.
\end{proof}

No assertion is made that every singular zero of $P_\beta$ has the same
unique lifting behaviour: at singular residue zeros, branching,
ramification or additional obstructions require a separate analysis. The
theorem does not construct an abelian variety or prove a higher-rank
version of analytic uniformization.

\section{Surreal realization and explicit examples}\label{tate:theta:sec:examples}

\subsection{Working inside the surreals without class-sized sums}

For a set-sized additive subgroup $\Gamma\subset\No$, the normal-form map
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{\gamma\in A}c_\gamma t^\gamma\longmapsto
 \sum_{\gamma\in A}c_\gamma\omega^{-\gamma}\label{tate:theta:eq:normalembedding}
\end{equation}
is an ordered Hahn-field realization for real coefficients and a
surcomplex realization for complex coefficients; the sums are normal-form
sums, not limits of partial sums \cite{BournezGuilmant}. Given a finite
surreal exponent matrix and a finite tuple of surcomplex arguments, take the
divisible subgroup generated by the matrix entries, the leading exponents,
and the supports of all unit parts. This subgroup is a set, and all the
constructions above take place inside its Hahn field, so the theorems apply
pointwise throughout $\No[i]$ without a class-sized Hahn sum or a set
containing all surreals. For $\Gamma=\R^r_{\lex}$ a concrete realization
sends $(a_1,\ldots,a_r)\mapsto a_1\omega^{r-1}+\cdots+a_r$ in the exponent
group. In rank two we write
\[
 \tau_1=t^{(1,0)}=\omega^{-\omega},\qquad
 \tau_2=t^{(0,1)}=\omega^{-1};\qquad
 0<\tau_1<\tau_2^N\quad(N\in\N).
\]
These are genuinely distinct surreal scales, not two ordinary small real
parameters taken to zero together.

\subsection{Irrational radicals: positive energies without a Hahn sum}

\begin{example}\label{tate:theta:ex:irrational}
Take $L=\Z^2$ and $\pi(m,n)=\bigl((m-\sqrt2n)^2,\ m^2+n^2\bigr)\in\R^2_{\lex}$.
This is positive for every nonzero real vector, and its leading coordinate
is positive at every nonzero lattice point. Nevertheless
$\sum_{m,n}t^{\pi(m,n)}$ is not Hahn-summable. Define $p_k,q_k\in\Z$ by
$p_k+q_k\sqrt2=(1+\sqrt2)^k$. Then $(p_k-\sqrt2q_k)^2=(3-2\sqrt2)^k$ is
strictly positive and strictly decreasing, so the full lexicographic
exponents along $(p_k,q_k)$ strictly decrease. The first radical is the
irrational line $m=\sqrt2n$, precisely the flag obstruction. A finite
numerical scan can never certify its absence.
\end{example}

\subsection{Mixed drift: rational flags are not enough}

\begin{example}\label{tate:theta:ex:cross}
Consider $\pi(m,n)=(m^2,mn,n^2)\in\R^3_{\lex}$. It is again positive for
every nonzero real vector, and the successive restricted radicals are
rational, but at the second stage the radical is still the $n$-axis and its
mixed pairing with the $m$-axis is nonzero. The exponents
$\pi(1,-k)=(1,-k,k^2)$ strictly decrease. This is why
\cref{tate:theta:thm:flag} requires the \emph{global} annihilation
condition, not merely a sequence of positive semidefinite restrictions.
\end{example}

\subsection{A legitimate sum of support length \texorpdfstring{$\omega^2$}{omega squared}}

\begin{example}\label{tate:theta:ex:omega2}
The sum $\sum_{m,n\in\Z}\tau_1^{m^2}\tau_2^{n^2}$ is strongly summable and
has actual support of order type $\omega^2$. Its first-coordinate values are
$0,1,4,9,\ldots$, and above each is an entire $\omega$-sequence of
second-coordinate values; positive coefficients prevent cancellation. No
enumeration of its lattice terms makes ordinary finite partial sums
converge to it in the rank-two valuation topology: every finite partial sum
omits terms in the row $m=0$, so its error has first valuation coordinate
zero and never has valuation greater than $(1,0)$. The Hahn identity
remains valid. This separates summability from sequential convergence
without appealing to any proper-class topology.
\end{example}

\subsection{Positive generation with an irrational shear}

\begin{example}\label{tate:theta:ex:shear}
Let $\cB(x,y)=\bigl(2x_1y_1,\ 2(x_2-\sqrt2x_1)(y_2-\sqrt2y_1)\bigr)$. The
radical flag is the rational $x_2$-axis followed by zero, although the
lower-level coefficients are irrational. A finite positively generating set
is
\[
 S=\{(0,1),(0,-1)\}\cup\{(\epsilon,k):\epsilon\in\{-1,1\},\ -2\le k\le2\},
\]
with twelve elements. In the construction of
\cref{tate:theta:thm:positive}, take upper lifts $(\pm1,0)$, lower
generators $(0,\pm1)$, and $M=2$. For instance $(3,7)=3(1,2)+(0,1)$ and
$(3,1)=(1,1)+2(1,0)$. In the first representation the mixed lower-level
pairing is $2(2-\sqrt2)>0$; in the second, all pairings are positive at the
first level. The program \file{code/02-multiscale-theta-verify.py}
checks the allocation construction at all $1{,}681$ integer points of
$[-20,20]^2$, using exact signs in $\Q(\sqrt2)$ and no floating point.
\end{example}

\subsection{A coupled two-scale theta zero}\label{tate:theta:sec:coupled}

Take the AN-admissible quadratic energy and linear term
$\pi(m,n)=(m^2,n^2+mn-m^2)$ and $b(m,n)=(-m,m-n)$. Then
\begin{equation}
 \cE(m,n)=\bigl(m(m-1),\ n^2+(m-1)n-m^2+m\bigr).\label{tate:theta:eq:coupledenergy}
\end{equation}
The first coordinate is minimized exactly at $m=0,1$. At $m=0$ the second
coordinate is $n(n-1)$, minimized at $n=0,1$; at $m=1$ it is $n^2$,
minimized only at $n=0$. Hence $\mu=(0,0)$,
$\cM=\{(0,0),(0,1),(1,0)\}$ and $P(Z_1,Z_2)=1+Z_1+Z_2$. The finite
generating set $S=\{(0,\pm1)\}\cup\{(\epsilon,k):\epsilon=\pm1,\ k=-1,0,1\}$
is obtained with $M=1$; it certifies all three minima by eight inequalities
at each point.

Choose the residue $(Z_1,Z_2)=(1,-2)$, a smooth zero since
$Z_2\partial_{Z_2}P=-2\ne0$. Fix the first unit to $1$ and write the second
residue-unit parameter as $\xi\in-2(1+\m_v)$. The theta equation is
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{m,n\in\Z}\tau_1^{m(m-1)}\tau_2^{n^2+(m-1)n-m^2+m}\xi^n=0,
 \label{tate:theta:eq:coupledtheta}
\end{equation}
and \cref{tate:theta:thm:zerolift} gives a unique such $\xi$. The
first-coordinate-zero row of this equation is
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{n\in\Z}\tau_2^{n(n-1)}\xi^n+\sum_{n\in\Z}\tau_2^{n^2}\xi^n=0;
 \label{tate:theta:eq:zerorow}
\end{equation}
terms from other $m$ have first coordinate at least $2$ and cannot affect
any coefficient in that row. Exact coefficient recursion gives
\begin{equation}
 \xi=-2+\tfrac52\tau_2-\tfrac{43}{8}\tau_2^2+\tfrac{247}{16}\tau_2^3
     -\tfrac{5955}{128}\tau_2^4+\tfrac{36493}{256}\tau_2^5+\rho_\xi,
 \qquad v(\rho_\xi)\ge(0,6).\label{tate:theta:eq:rootexpansion}
\end{equation}
The remainder includes the later terms of the zero row and all higher
first-coordinate rows; \eqref{tate:theta:eq:rootexpansion} is not a claim
that the whole solution lies in $\C((\tau_2))$. The program verifies the
zero row through degree eight in exact rational arithmetic; the existence of
all later rows follows from the support-controlled theorem, not from those
finite checks. Under the surreal realization the original arguments of
$\vartheta_\Pi$ are $z_1=\tau_1^{-1}\tau_2$ and $z_2=\tau_2^{-1}\xi$, so the
example is an actual surcomplex zero at separated surreal scales, not a
zero of an unrelated ordinary complex truncation.

\subsection{Why no nontrivial theta family is all-surcomplex entire}

\begin{corollary}\label{tate:theta:cor:notentire}
For $g\ge1$, no fixed symmetric surreal exponent matrix gives a theta
family strongly summable at every monomial point of $(\No[i]^\times)^g$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
A matrix failing the form conditions has no point in its theta domain.
Otherwise $\pi_{11}=\cB(e_1,e_1)>0$. Choose a surreal $\gamma$ larger than
$N\pi_{11}$ for every ordinary positive integer $N$. At the valuation
vector $\beta=(\gamma,0,\ldots,0)$, the exponents along $-ne_1$ have
successive differences $(n+\tfrac12)\pi_{11}-\gamma<0$. They strictly
decrease, contradicting Hahn summability.
\end{proof}

The conclusion concerns the lattice series defining the function. It is the
multivariable counterpart of the properness statement of
\cref{tate:thm:surcomplex}, and it accords with, but does not reprove or
strengthen in full generality, the collection's all-scale entire-function
restrictions. It does not contradict the unilateral example
$\sum_{n\ge0}t^{n^2}z^n$ of the foundations report \cite{RepoFoundations},
which is summable on all of the fixed field $\C((t^\Q))$, not at every
surcomplex point.

\subsection{A theorem checklist}\label{tate:theta:sec:checklist}

Before applying the theta conclusions to a concrete expression, verify
that the indexing lattice is finite-rank and set-sized; that the exponent
matrix is symmetric; that successive restricted forms are positive
semidefinite; that their radicals are lattice-rational and globally
annihilate the corresponding coordinate form; and that the final radical
is zero. For the chosen argument, its leading-exponent linear forms must
annihilate the corresponding radicals. Only then are the Hahn family, its
unit perturbations, and its weighted derivatives licensed. For minimum
computation, retain the positively generating set and the final finite
inequalities. For an asserted zero, record its initial polynomial, its
residue point, and the nonzero logarithmic derivative used for lifting.
For an asserted ordinal support length, record positivity of coefficients
or otherwise account for cancellations. Every one of these distinctions has
a role in a proof or a counterexample above.

\subsection{What an exact symbolic implementation must know}

To represent a concrete instance, a symbolic system needs finite
expressions for the input Hahn series or a reliable lazy coefficient
interface, exact leading exponents and coefficients, and a specified
ordered exponent group. The domain test additionally requires deciding
whether $|\beta|\le N\alpha$ for some ordinary $N$. Comparisons with
individual $N\alpha$ are not, by themselves, a terminating decision
procedure for that existential statement.

For a lexicographically ordered free finite-rank group, the test is
finite. Inspect the first nonzero coordinate of $\alpha$. Values whose
first nonzero coordinate occurs earlier are outside $H_\alpha$;
values supported at that coordinate or later are inside it. In rational
coordinates the finite normalization in \cref{tate:lem:normalize} is then
performed by ordinary floor arithmetic at that coordinate, with the
lower coordinates determining boundary cases. For a general group,
the needed principal-convex-subgroup operation must be part of the
representation contract.

For normalized admissible $u$, an evaluator can return the two isolated
rational terms, a lazy positive-tail object, the support certificate
\eqref{tate:eq:certificate}, and the tail bound \eqref{tate:eq:tail}. That is an
exact expression with an explicit precision policy. A finite truncation
is not an exact elliptic point unless the discarded tail is retained
symbolically. Testing the curve equation numerically after truncation
is not a replacement for the exact identity.

The torsion-degree calculation requires membership in $n\Gamma$ and
finite cyclic order in $\Gamma/n\Gamma$, which is extra group data.
When $\Gamma\cong\Z^r$, \eqref{tate:eq:gcddegree} supplies a direct algorithm.
In a black-box ordered group, it need not be decidable from order
comparisons alone.

For the inverse direction, the second route is explicit: inspect the
coordinate valuations and residues of a point to select the node, smooth
or infinity chart, and compute the formal coefficients of that chart's
inverse by finite homogeneous recursion. There is an important distinction
between such a construction and an algorithm on all surreal numbers.
Arbitrary surreal exponents do not come with a universal effective
comparison or boundedness oracle; even in an effective subgroup, an
unbounded search for an integer $N$ only semidecides $|\gamma|\le N\alpha$
unless a decision procedure for convex-subgroup membership is available,
and the theorems do not promise one. Formal total-degree truncation is
fully effective but must be described honestly: for inputs of least
positive valuation $\epsilon$, omitting terms of degree above $N$ bounds
the omitted valuation below by $(N+1)\epsilon$, and at higher rank this may
never exceed a requested cutoff. The input Hahn series themselves may need
infinitely many coefficients below a cutoff; support finiteness at each
exponent is not a finite algorithm for an arbitrary initial segment.

For Part~II an implementation should keep four objects separate: an
ordered exponent representation, a rational radical flag, a certificate
for positive generation, and a support expression for the resulting Hahn
series. A successful finite numerical sample replaces none of them. For
real-algebraic matrix entries the flag computation can use exact linear
algebra and real-algebraic sign tests; rationality of a subspace is a
distinct step and cannot be inferred from a numerical nullspace. In a fixed
algebraic number field, expand coordinates in a rational basis to solve the
required rational linear equations. For arbitrary symbolic surreal
constants these operations require explicit comparison and identity
certificates from the chosen representation. After the form and valuation
domain are certified, a finite positively generating set makes
global-minimum testing finite: a descent trace plus the final list of
inequalities certifies a minimum; a zero-cost graph trace, the completeness
theorem and the $2^g$ vertex bound certify the initial Laurent polynomial;
and a nonzero initial derivative with the support monoid of
\eqref{tate:theta:eq:zerosupport} certifies a smooth lifted branch. These are
exact logical certificates; the report does not implement a general-purpose
surreal computer algebra system.

\section{Limitations and further mathematical questions}\label{tate:sec:limits}

The present results settle the internal domain and surjectivity questions
posed in \cref{tate:sec:intro}. They leave several broader tasks genuinely
separate.

\paragraph{Beyond negative valuation of $j$.}
The Tate parameter $v(q)>0$ produces $v(j)<0$. Curves with nonnegative
valuation of $j$ require a different treatment, such as formal groups
around a good-reduction model and a residue elliptic curve. The
surcomplex field's algebraic closedness does not by itself provide a
single useful transcendental parametrization of that different regime.
Constant (good-reduction) abelian varieties over $\No[i]$ are treated by
formal groups in Part~VI of the differential-equations report of this
collection (\texttt{diff:aut:prop:abeliansplit},
\texttt{diff:aut:thm:abelianimage}), including their logarithmic-derivative
image for every normalized derivation. That treatment concerns constant
varieties and does not cover Hahn fields with a coarse valuation.

\paragraph{Higher-dimensional totally degenerate abelian varieties.}
A natural extension replaces $q^\Z$ by a finite-rank multiplicative
period lattice and the quadratic theta exponent by a positive
polarization form. One would need an exact ordered-group condition for
all lattice directions and a replacement for the single principal
convex subgroup $H_\alpha$. The support proof in Part~I suggests
the right obstruction, but it is not a proof of a general multivariable
uniformization theorem. Source~03 frames the same gap: for several periods,
support admissibility is likely governed by a boundedness region associated
with their valuation pairing rather than by one principal convex subgroup,
and an adequate theorem must establish finite support fibres for
multidimensional theta sums and prove that the admissible region still
reaches the desired abelian-variety points; the one-node formal inverse of
\cref{tate:node:sec:second} does not by itself prove this.

Part~II (source~02) now supplies the theta-summability half of this
programme, under its own hypotheses: monomial periods $t^{\pi_{ab}}$,
coefficients in $\C$, and a divisible $\Gamma$. For those periods the exact
ordered-group condition for all lattice directions is the radical-flag
criterion of \cref{tate:theta:thm:flag,tate:theta:cor:domain}; it gives the
exact domain of $\vartheta_\Pi$, with finite support fibres and unit
robustness (\cref{tate:theta:thm:units}), and it replaces the single
subgroup $H_\alpha$ by the flag conditions, reducing to $H_\alpha$ for
$g=1$ (\cref{tate:theta:cor:rankone}). Finite certificates for theta minima
and the lifting of smooth initial zeros are also proved. The paragraph
remains open. Not proved are: a multivariable domain theorem for periods with
Hahn tails, which \cref{tate:thm:theta} handles only for $g=1$; any
nondivisible version of Part~II; the analogue of the coordinate maps and the
group structure; surjectivity onto the points of a totally degenerate
abelian variety, or any uniformization theorem at all; the lifting of
singular initial zeros; and geometric constructions of higher-rank theta
line bundles.

\paragraph{Questions about positive generation and minimization.}
Efficient bounds for the size of a positively generating set, beyond the
existence bound \eqref{tate:theta:eq:Sbound}, and the bit complexity of the
minimization algorithm for algebraic inputs remain open; well-ordering
gives termination of each descent but no uniform bound on its length.

\paragraph{Comparison with logarithmic uniformization.}
A precise functorial comparison between the explicit Hahn map here and the
bounded-monodromy logarithmic Tate quotient of Molcho--Wise would identify
exactly which parts of the construction are realizations of existing formal
and logarithmic geometry. The coincidence of the one-loop bounded group
with $H_\alpha$ is already visible in \cite{MW}; a full comparison of
structures is not proved here. This is the most important remaining
priority check for the proposed refinements of Part~I.

\paragraph{Intrinsic higher-rank analytic geometry.}
The algebraic quotient $H_\alpha/\Z\alpha$ retains infinitesimal value
directions discarded by its Archimedean quotient. Relating it precisely
to Hahn analytification, adic spaces, or a suitable skeleton is a
separate geometric theorem. The exact sequence \eqref{tate:eq:circlelayers}
is a starting invariant, not a substitute for that theorem.

\paragraph{Intrinsic invariants beyond the chosen Weierstrass chart.}
The exact formulas of \cref{tate:node:sec:metric} concern the specified
coordinate model. It would be useful to identify which support-monoid
invariants and valuative distances admit a model-independent formulation,
and which necessarily depend on the chosen Tate coordinates. Calling the
current affine distance canonical on every elliptic model would be an
unsupported strengthening, and it is not claimed.

\paragraph{Functions outside the literal Hahn domain.}
We proved that the displayed bilateral families fail strong summability
outside $U_q$. We also proved an algebraic obstruction: when
$H_\alpha\ne\Gamma$, no group homomorphism $K^\times\to E_q(K)$ extending
$\Phi_q$ has kernel $q^\Z$, and for any homomorphic extension $\Psi$ the
excess kernel satisfies $\ker\Psi/q^\Z\simeq\Gamma/H_\alpha$
(\cref{tate:node:thm:obstruction}). We did not prove that every possible
resummation, noncanonical assignment, or change of analytic category is
impossible: an extension that gives up either the canonical values on $U_q$
or the period kernel is not excluded. Any proposed extension must specify
its summation rule and which identities it preserves; it cannot call the
original families strongly Hahn-summable when their leading supports are
decreasing. The same holds for the theta families of Part~II outside their
domain.

\paragraph{Independent verification and priority.}
For the first route, the most important independent proof checks are the
joint-family interchange in \cref{tate:lem:agreement} and the use of regular
projective charts in the group law. The second route proves
\cref{tate:thm:main} without either, but those two steps remain
load-bearing for \cref{tate:cor:diagram}, \eqref{tate:eq:redcompat} and
\cref{tate:prop:identityfiber}. For the second route, the review targets are
the Hahn specialization of the secant identities on the wide annulus and the
exhaustiveness of the three charts; for Part~II, the induction in
\cref{tate:theta:thm:positive} and the global-radical step of
\cref{tate:theta:thm:flag}. None of the three source searches was an
exhaustive search of valuation theory, formal or logarithmic models,
higher-rank lattice geometry, or the model theory of analytic structures.
Finding an earlier theorem with these conclusions would change the priority
assessment, not the support calculations. The report should be treated as
a research draft with explicit proofs, not as an independently certified
publication.

\section{Conclusion}

At arbitrary valuation rank, the correct multiplicative source of the
Hahn Tate map is neither automatically all of $K^\times$ nor merely
one rank-one coefficient subfield. It is
\[
 U_q=\{u:v(u)\in H_{v(q)}\}.
\]
The restriction is forced by the bilateral support geometry, yet it
loses no elliptic-curve points. Classical rank-one uniformization reaches
the coarse residue curve; formal evaluation at coarse infinitesimals
recovers every remaining point. This proves
\[
 U_q/q^\Z\simeq E_q(K)
\]
without claiming convergence at scales where the series do not converge.

The same analysis yields a canonical valuation quotient, exact behavior
under scalar extension, support-preserving modular inversion, and an
explicit torsion ramification law. In particular, the construction gives
a coherent theory of multiplicatively uniformized elliptic curves over
$\No[i]$ while retaining its genuinely non-Archimedean scale structure.

The second proof gives the constructive reason. The integral formal chart
$(U,V)\mapsto(\X(U,V),\Y(U,V))$ with $UV=q$ has unimodular linear part. It
recovers the two branches from any nodal point, preserves the generated
positive support monoid in both directions, and is a valuation isometry on
the whole infinitesimal bidisk. Together with two elementary charts it
gives global surjectivity at every value-group rank and over every
coefficient field of characteristic zero, and its exact period-sensitivity
and pairwise-distance formulas survive arbitrary leading-coefficient
cancellation. The period-bounded quotient itself belongs to an established
landscape, that of bounded monodromy in logarithmic Picard theory; what is
proposed here is its support-explicit, valuation-exact realization in Hahn
and surcomplex arithmetic.

For several monomial periods, Part~II settles the summability side. Finite
positive generation of admissible higher-rank lattices, which answers
Question~11.14 of Amini--Nicolussi in its printed form, turns every theta
minimum into a finite certificate; the radical flag decides exactly which
quadratic lattice families are Hahn sums, with support order type
$\omega^s$; and smooth initial zeros lift with explicit support monoids.
These are support and finite-comparison foundations for a
higher-dimensional theory, not that theory: no abelian variety is
uniformized here, and no rank-one analytic geometry is silently imported
into an arbitrary-rank setting.

\subsection{What is attributed, and what is proved here}

The principal classical inputs of Part~I are Tate's complete rank-one
uniformization theorem, the formal Tate curve and its discriminant,
Jacobi's product, algebraic closedness of divisible Hahn fields over
$\C$, and standard finite torsion and $j$-classification of elliptic
curves. The second route replaces the first input by Tate's universal
integral identities (the curve equation and the cleared secant identities)
and Tate's generic-pair lemma, together with the finite algebraic group law
on a nonsingular Weierstrass cubic. The bounded-period quotient and its
value sequence are credited to the logarithmic Picard theory of
Molcho--Wise as a precedent. Part~II credits the higher-rank inner products,
admissible lattices, Definition~11.10, Lemma~11.11 and the rank-one acute-cone
argument to Amini--Nicolussi; rank-one theta functions and their
tropicalization to Foster--Rabinoff--Shokrieh--Soto; convergent higher-rank
Hahn subrings to Joswig--Smith; the midpoint parity argument to lattice
Delaunay geometry; and initial forms and the formal implicit theorem to the
classical literature. All of these are cited rather than presented as
discoveries.

The proofs supplied here establish the exact strong-summability domains,
the support and precision certificates, unrestricted formal evaluation
across a convex coarsening, its agreement with the literal Tate sums,
the resulting arbitrary-rank surjective quotient (twice), the node-chart
inverse and its support and valuation identities, the fine filtration and
the extension obstruction, the indicated consequences, and the results of
Part~II. The finite computations independently check selected identities
and signs. They neither replace the classical inputs nor certify the
all-rank proofs.

these is a claim that such results are absent from all published or

\subsection{Reproduction}\label{tate:app:reproduction}

The source uses standard TeX Live packages and requires no external
figures, bibliography download, or font installation. From the report
directory, run
\begin{verbatim}
latexmk -pdf -interaction=nonstopmode -halt-on-error article.tex
\end{verbatim}
Recompiling the article does not execute the verification code. The three
programs write their evidence files, and two of them would overwrite each
other's: \file{code/check_identities.py} and
\file{code/02-multiscale-theta-verify.py} both write
\file{data/verification.json} relative to the directory above their own,
and \file{code/03-tate-uniformization-verify.py} writes the file named by
\file{--output} (default \file{verification.json} in the working
directory). Run them on a copy, for example as \file{code/verify.py} in a
scratch directory with its own \file{data/}, and compare the result with
\file{data/verification.json}, \file{data/02-multiscale-theta-verification.json}
and \file{data/03-tate-uniformization-verification.json} respectively. The
first program requires SymPy (\file{data/requirements.txt}); the other two
need only the Python standard library. The recorded outputs are included so
that the finite results remain inspectable without running Python. The
build scripts \file{code/02-multiscale-theta-build.sh} and
\file{code/03-tate-uniformization-build.*} are kept as delivered; they build
their source manuscripts in their original layouts and do not build this
report.
