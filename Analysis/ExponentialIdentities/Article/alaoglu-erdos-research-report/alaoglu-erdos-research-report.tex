\begin{abstract}
The Alaoglu--Erd\H{o}s conjecture asks whether a real $x$ satisfying
$2^x,3^x\in\Z$ must be integral.  It is a particularly concrete unresolved instance of the
four exponentials problem.  This report examines commits
\commit{f492c18}, \commit{5ed4e61}, \commit{a37d97f}, \commit{4a9b03d},
\commit{18a85e6}, and \commit{423ac24} in the \texttt{ProveIt} repository, together with
\texttt{two-base-partial-results.tex}.

The source audit confirms a substantial committed Lean corpus: the three-base theorem for
$2,3,5$; irrationality and transcendence restrictions; verified exclusion below $2^x=256$;
a rank-at-most-two theorem for prime-exponent vectors; logarithmic-square counting;
zero-density and local progression obstructions; affine descent; shrinking-target bounds; and
classification of additional integral bases as $3$-smooth.  It also identifies a reproducibility
discrepancy: at commit \commit{423ac24}, the survey cites
\texttt{FiniteCheck4096}, \texttt{OddCore}, and \texttt{FractionalPartDensity}, but those files
are not present in that commit tree and are not on its import surface.  Results depending on
them are therefore treated here as article-level claims, not as audited committed Lean.

Starting from the committed cross-product identity for odd prime valuations, we prove a new
exact structure theorem.  Conditional on a counterexample, all natural candidates have one
primitive odd core $w$, and a rational-power subgroup argument removes the residual
semigroup ambiguity.  There are canonical integers $a\ge0$ and $d\ge1$ such that
\[
 \beta=a+d\log_2w,\qquad 2^\beta=M=2^aw^d,\qquad 3^\beta=A\in\N,
\]
and every solution has the unique form $n+k\beta$.  Consequently, the candidate monoid is
freely generated by $2$ and $M$, the number of nonintegral solutions in $[N,N+1)$ is exactly
$\lfloor(N+1)/\beta\rfloor$, and their fractional parts in a high unit interval are an initial
segment of the irrational rotation $k\beta\pmod 1$, hence become equidistributed.

We also prove that a progression
$n,n+h,\ldots,n+rh$ of candidate bases is impossible whenever
\[
 \bigl|\thetaq(\thetaq-1)\cdots(\thetaq-r+1)\bigr|h^r<n^{r-\thetaq},
 \qquad \thetaq=\log_2 3,
\]
strictly improving the committed $h^5\le n$ three-term criterion and extending it to arbitrary
length.  Finally, a reproducible 40-decimal interval computation checks every non-power of two
$m<2^{20}$ and certifies $m^{\thetaq}\notin\Z$.  This raises the independently checked finite
barrier from the committed $x\ge8$ to $x\ge20$, subject to the stated software trust boundary.
The remaining obstruction is isolated as a radical four-exponentials statement:
for no odd $u>1$ should $u^{\thetaq}$ lie in $\Z[1/3]$.
\end{abstract}

\section{Executive conclusions}

\subsection{What was and was not proved}

The full conjecture remains unresolved in this investigation.  That conclusion is not a
premature appeal to its historical status: several routes were pushed until their exact
failure point became visible.  In particular, the repository's determinant method was
examined for a two-by-two replacement; the multiplicative-rank statements were converted into
an exact conditional classification; the local convexity argument was sharpened to all higher
finite differences; and the finite search was independently extended.  Each route leaves the
same transcendence-theoretic core rather than an elementary loose end.

The strongest new mathematical conclusion is the following conditional theorem.

\begin{theorem}[Exact primitive-generator theorem; informal executive form]
\label{thm:executive-generator}
Assume there exists a nonintegral real $x$ with $2^x,3^x\in\Z$.  Then there is a unique least
nonintegral such number $\beta>0$.  If $M=2^\beta$ and $A=3^\beta$, then
\[
  \Sol:=\{x\in\R:2^x,3^x\in\Z\}
   =\{n+k\beta:n,k\in\Nzero\},
\]
and the representation is unique.  Equivalently,
\[
 \{(2^x,3^x):x\in\Sol\}
 =\{(2^nM^k,3^nA^k):n,k\in\Nzero\}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{statusbox}{\statusnew}
A complete proof appears in \cref{sec:exact-structure}.  It uses the committed Lean theorem
\lean{odd_factorization_cross_mul} as an input, but the deduction from that theorem to the
exact monoid classification has not been checked by Lean in the audited repository.
\end{statusbox}

This removes a misleading degree of freedom from the survey's proposed ``exponent
semigroup'' direction.  Conditional on failure, the set of odd-core exponents is not an
arbitrary additive submonoid with possible Frobenius gaps.  After primitive normalization it
is exactly $d\Nzero$, and the required power of two is governed by one linear threshold.

\section{The problem and its equivalent forms}
\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Original statement}

\begin{conjecture}[Alaoglu--Erd\H{o}s, two-base integer form]
\label{conj:AE}
For every $x\in\R$,
\[
   2^x\in\Z\quad\text{and}\quad 3^x\in\Z
   \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad x\in\Z.
\]
\end{conjecture}

Since $2^x$ and $3^x$ are positive, the hypotheses actually put them in $\N$.  Moreover,
$2^x\in\N$ forces $x\ge0$: a negative exponent gives $0<2^x<1$.  Thus all solutions live in
$[0,\infty)$.

Alaoglu and Erd\H{o}s formulated the broader expectation that rational powers of two distinct
primes should not simultaneously be rational at a nonintegral exponent, in the course of their
study of highly composite and related numbers \cite{AE1944}.  The modern two-base integer form
is both elementary to state and a direct special case of the four exponentials conjecture.
As of the dated literature check for this report, the four exponentials conjecture remains open
\cite{Waldschmidt2023,TheoremDB2026}.

\subsection{Candidate-base normalization}

Put
\[
  \thetaq:=\frac{\log 3}{\log 2}=\log_2 3
  =1.584962500721156\ldots
\]
and define
\[
 \Cand:=\{m\in\N_{>0}:m^{\thetaq}\in\N\}.
\]

\begin{lemma}[Exponent--candidate bijection]
\label{lem:bijection}
The maps
\[
 x\longmapsto 2^x,
 \qquad
 m\longmapsto \log_2m
\]
are mutually inverse bijections between
\[
 \Sol:=\{x\in\R:2^x,3^x\in\Z\}
 \quad\text{and}\quad
 \Cand.
\]
Under the bijection, $3^x=m^{\thetaq}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If $x\in\Sol$, set $m=2^x\in\N$.  Then
\[
 m^{\thetaq}
 =\exp\!\left(\log m\frac{\log3}{\log2}\right)
 =\exp(x\log3)=3^x\in\N.
\]
Conversely, if $m\in\Cand$ and $x=\log_2m$, then $2^x=m$ and the same identity gives
$3^x=m^{\thetaq}\in\N$.
\end{proof}

Integral solutions correspond exactly to powers of two.  Therefore the conjecture is
\[
  \Cand=\{2^n:n\in\Nzero\}.
\]
The candidate formulation is particularly well suited to unique factorization, finite
certification, finite differences of $t^{\thetaq}$, and multiplicative-rank arguments.

\subsection{The four-exponentials reduction}

Suppose $x$ is nonintegral and $2^x,3^x$ are algebraic.  The rational-exponent argument shows
that $x$ cannot be rational; hence $1,x$ are linearly independent over $\Q$.  The numbers
$\log2,\log3$ are also $\Q$-linearly independent, since $2^a3^b=1$ with integers $a,b$
forces $a=b=0$.  The four exponentials associated to these two pairs are
\[
 e^{1\cdot\log2}=2,
 \quad e^{1\cdot\log3}=3,
 \quad e^{x\log2}=2^x,
 \quad e^{x\log3}=3^x.
\]
All four would be algebraic, contrary to the four exponentials conjecture.  This is exactly why
the two-base statement lies at the first unresolved $2\times2$ transcendence threshold, while
three multiplicatively independent bases can be handled by the six exponentials theorem.

The reduction is not merely historical context.  It is a diagnostic: any proposed elementary
argument must exploit special integrality, positivity, or arithmetic structure beyond the
bare algebraicity of those four exponentials, or else it would prove a substantial case of the
four exponentials conjecture by another name.

\subsection{Rational and algebraic exponents}

A rational exponent is easy.

\begin{lemma}[Rational exponent]
\label{lem:rational}
If $x\in\Q$ and $2^x\in\Z$, then $x\in\Z$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Write $x=a/b$ in lowest terms with $b>0$.  If $2^{a/b}=m\in\N$, then
$2^a=m^b$.  Comparing the exponent of the prime $2$ gives $a=b\,v_2(m)$, so $b\mid a$ and
$b=1$.
\end{proof}

Gelfond--Schneider gives the stronger dichotomy used in the repository: if $2^x$ is algebraic
and $x$ is algebraic irrational, then $2^x$ is transcendental, a contradiction.  Hence a
nonintegral solution must be transcendental.  This does not resolve the conjecture because the
unknown exponent is allowed to be transcendental; indeed, the repository also formalizes that
$\thetaq=\log_2 3$ is transcendental.

\subsection{The determinant architecture}

The three-base proof in \texttt{IntegerExponent.lean} is the conceptual foundation for several
later results.  In outline, for an irrational $x$ it constructs a large square matrix
\[
  \mathcal A_{ij}=\exp(a_i b_j),
\]
where the row arguments encode monomials in $2,3,5$ and the column arguments encode affine
forms in $1,x$.  Irrationality gives injectivity of the column parameters; multiplicative
independence gives injectivity of the row parameters.  Total positivity/nonvanishing of the
exponential determinant gives $\det\mathcal A\ne0$.  The integrality hypotheses make every
entry integral, so the nonzero determinant has absolute value at least one.  An interpolation
remainder estimate, with explicit choices of the matrix size and truncation order, gives the
contradictory upper bound $|\det\mathcal A|<1$.

This is a formal, specialized realization of the six-exponentials mechanism.  The later
\texttt{MultiplicativeRank.lean} abstracts the row bases: three natural candidates with
linearly independent prime-exponent vectors would give three multiplicatively independent
bases and therefore force $\thetaq$ to be rational, contradicting its irrationality.  This
turns transcendence into a rank bound in the free abelian group on the primes.

\subsection{Why the same determinant does not simply shrink to two bases}

The dimension count is critical.  With three independent row directions and two independent
column directions, one can choose enough interpolation conditions to make the analytic upper
bound beat the arithmetic lower bound.  In the two-by-two case the determinant estimate lands
at the classical boundary where the known proof no longer yields decay below one.  Changing
constants, enlarging the finite grid, or optimizing the same asymptotic balance does not remove
this structural deficit.  A successful two-base determinant proof would amount to a new
four-exponentials argument or would have to use the fact that the four algebraic values are
actually positive integers in a way absent from the general theorem.

\label{sec:existing}

Suppose for the rest of this section that $x$ is a counterexample and write
\[
 m=2^x\in\N,
 \qquad n=3^x\in\N.
\]
The existing corpus imposes the following simultaneous restrictions.

\subsection{Transcendence and size}

The exponent $x$ is irrational, indeed transcendental.  The committed finite certificate gives
$x\ge8$; because $x$ is nonintegral, actually $x>8$.  Equivalently, $m\ge256$ and $m$ is not a
power of two.

\subsection{Multiplicative rank}

Every natural candidate $a$ gives a prime-exponent vector
\[
  \mathbf v(a)=(v_p(a))_{p\ \mathrm{prime}}\in\Q^{(\mathbb P)}.
\]
Any three such vectors are linearly dependent, so the rational span of every finite candidate
set has dimension at most two.  Since $2$ is a candidate, all odd parts must lie on at most one
additional rational direction.  This is the doorway to the exact odd-core theorem below.

\subsection{Local gaps and shrinking targets}

For every $k\ge1$, $kx$ is again a solution.  Its fractional part cannot be too close to either
endpoint of its unit interval.  In a simplified form, if $y=kx$ then
\[
  \distZ{y}\gtrsim 2^{-y}.
\]
The repository gives exact logarithmic radii rather than only this asymptotic notation.  Such a
bound is compatible with ordinary Diophantine approximation: Dirichlet guarantees infinitely
many $k$ with $\distZ{kx}<1/k$, and $2^{-kx}$ is incomparably smaller.  Thus density of
fractional parts does not by itself contradict the shrinking target.

\subsection{Affine descent}

If
\[
 m=2^r u^k,\qquad n=3^r v^k
\]
with $r\ge0$ and $k\ge1$, then $(x-r)/k$ is another solution.  Since committed finite checking
excludes nonintegral solutions below $8$, one obtains
\[
 r+8k\le x.
\]
For a general counterexample this is quantitative.  For the least counterexample developed
below, the same descent becomes an exact primitivity statement: no nontrivial such decomposition
can exist at all.

\subsection{Additional bases}

If $a\in\N_{>0}$ and $a^x\in\Z$, then $a=2^u3^v$.  Conversely every such $3$-smooth base has
integral $x$-th power.  This is an exact classification of bases for a \emph{fixed}
hypothetical counterexample.  It should not be confused with classification of the candidate
values $2^x$ as $x$ ranges over all solutions; the latter is the subject of
\cref{sec:exact-structure}.

\section{A primitive common odd core}
\label{sec:odd-core}

This section proves, at paper level, the principal statement attributed in the survey to the
missing \texttt{OddCore.lean}.  The proof starts from the committed cross-valuation theorem and
uses only finite-support unique factorization and B\'ezout.

\subsection{Odd valuation profiles lie on one ray}

\begin{lemma}[Cross-valuation input]
\label{lem:cross-input}
Let $a,b\in\Cand$.  If $p$ is an odd prime with $v_p(a)>0$, then for every odd prime $q$,
\[
 v_p(a)v_q(b)=v_p(b)v_q(a).
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{statusbox}{\statuslean}
This is the mathematical content of
\lean{odd_factorization_cross_mul} in \texttt{CandidateStructure.lean}.
\end{statusbox}

\begin{theorem}[Primitive common odd core]
\label{thm:odd-core}
Assume $\Cand$ contains a non-power of two.  Then there is a unique odd integer $w>1$ that is
power-free in the sense
\[
  \gcd\{v_p(w):p\mid w\}=1,
\]
and every candidate $b\in\Cand$ has a unique representation
\[
  b=2^i w^j,
  \qquad i,j\in\Nzero.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Choose a non-power-of-two candidate $a$.  Its odd valuation vector
$A=(A_q)_{q\ne2}$, $A_q=v_q(a)$, is nonzero and finitely supported.  Let
\[
 g=\gcd\{A_q:A_q>0\},\qquad c_q=A_q/g,
 \qquad w=\prod_{q\ne2}q^{c_q}.
\]
Then $w$ is odd, greater than one, and the positive $c_q$ have gcd one.

Let $b\in\Cand$ and set $B_q=v_q(b)$.  Fix an odd prime $p$ with $A_p>0$.  By
\cref{lem:cross-input},
\[
 A_pB_q=B_pA_q\qquad(q\ne2).
\]
If $A_q=0$, this forces $B_q=0$.  On the support of $A$ it says
\[
 B_q=\frac{B_p}{A_p}A_q
     =\left(\frac{gB_p}{A_p}\right)c_q.
\]
Put $t=gB_p/A_p\in\Q_{\ge0}$.  Every $tc_q$ is an integer.  Since the $c_q$ have gcd one,
there exist integers $r_q$, only finitely many nonzero, with $\sum r_qc_q=1$.  Therefore
\[
 t=\sum_q r_q(tc_q)\in\Z.
\]
Write $j=t\in\Nzero$.  The odd part of $b$ is exactly $w^j$, so
$b=2^iw^j$ with $i=v_2(b)$.

Uniqueness for fixed $w$ follows from odd valuations and then from the $2$-adic valuation.  If
$w'$ is another primitive common core, the odd valuation vectors of $w$ and $w'$ generate the
same rational ray and are both primitive integer vectors; hence they agree.
\end{proof}

\begin{statusbox}{\statusnew}
The theorem is stronger in normalization than a bare common-core statement: taking the gcd of
the odd valuation profile makes $w$ canonical and prevents hidden perfect powers from being
absorbed into its exponent.
\end{statusbox}

\subsection{The logarithmic lattice and its residual ambiguity}

Set
\[
  \alpha:=\log_2w,
  \qquad E:=3^\alpha=w^{\thetaq}.
\]
Then every candidate exponent has the form
\[
 x=\log_2(2^iw^j)=i+j\alpha.
\]
The number $\alpha$ is irrational: if it were rational, the rational-exponent lemma applied to
$2^\alpha=w\in\Z$ would make $\alpha$ integral, forcing the odd $w>1$ to be a power of two.
In fact $\alpha$ is transcendental by the committed Gelfond--Schneider corollary.

At this stage the common-core statement alone does \emph{not} tell us which pairs $(i,j)$ occur.
The candidate condition is
\[
  3^{i+j\alpha}=3^iE^j\in\N.
\]
The survey leaves these pairs in an additive semigroup $B\subseteq\Nzero^2$.  The next section
shows that $B$ has a completely rigid form.

\section{Exact conditional structure of all solutions}
\label{sec:exact-structure}

\subsection{The rational-power index}

Because a non-power-of-two candidate exists, there is some $j>0$ such that $E^j$ is rational:
indeed, $3^iE^j\in\N$.  Let
\[
 d:=\min\{j\in\N_{>0}:E^j\in\Q\}.
\]
The following elementary fact is the key step.

\begin{lemma}[All rational powers are multiples of the least one]
\label{lem:rational-power-index}
For every $j\in\Z$,
\[
 E^j\in\Q\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad d\mid j.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The set
\[
 J:=\{j\in\Z:E^j\in\Q\}
\]
is an additive subgroup of $\Z$: $0\in J$, products add exponents, and reciprocals negate them.
Every nonzero subgroup of $\Z$ is $d\Z$ for its least positive element $d$.
\end{proof}

Write the positive rational $E^d$ in lowest terms.  Its denominator has no prime other than
$3$.  To see this, take any original candidate with odd-core exponent $j=dk>0$ and power-of-two
exponent $i$.  Since
\[
 3^i(E^d)^k\in\N,
\]
any prime in the reduced denominator of $E^d$ would survive in the denominator of its $k$-th
power unless it were $3$.  Hence there are unique integers $a\ge0$ and $A\ge1$ such that
\begin{equation}
  E^d=\frac{A}{3^a},
  \label{eq:Ed}
\end{equation}
with $3\nmid A$ when $a>0$.

\subsection{The primitive generator}

Define
\begin{equation}
  \beta:=a+d\alpha=a+d\log_2w,
  \qquad
  M:=2^\beta=2^aw^d,
  \qquad
  A:=3^\beta.
  \label{eq:betaMA}
\end{equation}
The last notation is consistent with \eqref{eq:Ed}, because
\[
 3^\beta=3^a(E^d)=A.
\]
Thus $\beta$ is itself a solution.  It is irrational because $\alpha$ is irrational.

\begin{theorem}[Exact primitive-generator theorem]
\label{thm:generator}
Assume \cref{conj:AE} is false.  With $w,d,a,\beta,M,A$ as above:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
\item every solution has a unique representation
\[
  x=n+k\beta,\qquad n,k\in\Nzero;
\]
\item every candidate has a unique representation
\[
  m=2^nM^k,\qquad n,k\in\Nzero;
\]
\item every output pair has a unique representation
\[
 (2^x,3^x)=(2^nM^k,3^nA^k),\qquad n,k\in\Nzero;
\]
\item $\beta$ is the unique least nonintegral solution.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $x\in\Sol$.  By \cref{thm:odd-core},
\[
 2^x=2^iw^j
\]
for unique $i,j\in\Nzero$, and therefore $x=i+j\alpha$.  Since
\[
 3^x=3^iE^j\in\N,
\]
we have $E^j\in\Q$, so \cref{lem:rational-power-index} gives $j=dk$ for a unique
$k\in\Nzero$.  Using \eqref{eq:Ed},
\[
 3^x=3^i(E^d)^k=3^{i-ak}A^k.
\]
Because $A/3^a$ was reduced, this is an integer exactly when $i\ge ak$.  Put
$n=i-ak\in\Nzero$.  Then
\[
 x=i+dk\alpha=n+k(a+d\alpha)=n+k\beta.
\]

Conversely, for any $n,k\in\Nzero$,
\[
 2^{n+k\beta}=2^nM^k\in\N,
 \qquad
 3^{n+k\beta}=3^nA^k\in\N,
\]
so $n+k\beta\in\Sol$.

If $n+k\beta=n'+k'\beta$ and $k\ne k'$, then
$\beta=(n'-n)/(k-k')\in\Q$, contradiction.  Hence $k=k'$ and $n=n'$.  This proves (i), and
(ii)--(iii) follow by exponentiation.  A nonintegral solution has $k\ge1$; its least possible
value is obtained at $(n,k)=(0,1)$ and equals $\beta$.  Uniqueness of the least element follows
from uniqueness of the representation.
\end{proof}

\begin{statusbox}{\statusnew}
This theorem is the principal new result of the report.  It sharpens both the committed
rank-two theorem and the survey's article-only odd-core description.  Conditional on failure,
$\Sol$ is not merely contained in a rank-two additive group: it is the free additive monoid on
$1$ and one irrational generator $\beta$.
\end{statusbox}

\subsection{Monoid isomorphisms}

The theorem can be packaged as two exact algebraic isomorphisms:
\[
 (\Nzero^2,+)
   \xrightarrow{\ \sim\ } (\Sol,+),
 \qquad (n,k)\longmapsto n+k\beta,
\]
and
\[
 (\Nzero^2,+)
   \xrightarrow{\ \sim\ } (\Cand,\cdot),
 \qquad (n,k)\longmapsto 2^nM^k.
\]
Similarly the output-pair monoid $\Pair$ is freely generated by $(2,3)$ and $(M,A)$.
Thus every multiplicative relation among candidates is the obvious coordinate relation.  This
is the exact conditional model against which any future density, progression, or descent
argument should be tested.

\subsection{Primitive output-pair constraints}

The leastness of $\beta$ upgrades the repository's inequalities to exact exclusions.

\begin{corollary}[No matched base depth]
\label{cor:no-depth}
For the primitive pair $(M,A)=(2^\beta,3^\beta)$,
\[
  \min\{v_2(M),v_3(A)\}=0.
\]
Equivalently, either $M$ is odd or $3\nmid A$ (and possibly both).
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
If both valuations were positive, $M/2$ and $A/3$ would be integers and
\[
  2^{\beta-1}=M/2,\qquad 3^{\beta-1}=A/3.
\]
Then $\beta-1$ would be a smaller nonintegral solution.
\end{proof}

In the canonical construction, $v_2(M)=a$, and when $a>0$ the reduced numerator condition in
\eqref{eq:Ed} gives $v_3(A)=0$ directly.

\begin{corollary}[No common perfect-power degree]
\label{cor:no-common-power}
There is no $k\ge2$ for which both $M$ and $A$ are perfect $k$-th powers.  Equivalently, the
gcd of all positive prime exponents occurring in the factorizations of $M$ and $A$ is one.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
If $M=U^k$ and $A=V^k$, then
$2^{\beta/k}=U$ and $3^{\beta/k}=V$.  Since $\beta$ is irrational, $\beta/k$ is a
nonintegral solution smaller than $\beta$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Exact affine primitivity]
\label{cor:affine-primitive}
Suppose
\[
 M=2^rU^k,
 \qquad A=3^rV^k.
\]
After choosing $r\in\Nzero$, $k\in\N_{>0}$, and $U,V\in\N$.  Then necessarily
$r=0$ and $k=1$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The affine descent identity gives a solution $(\beta-r)/k$.  It is nonintegral because
$\beta$ is irrational.  If $r>0$ or $k>1$, it is strictly smaller than $\beta$, contradicting
minimality.
\end{proof}

This corollary is the primitive form of
\lean{base_depth_add_eight_mul_degree_le}.  For arbitrary solutions the committed theorem gives
a lower bound; for the canonical generator, leastness removes every nontrivial affine descent.

\section{Equivalent one-number and radical formulations}
\label{sec:radical}

\subsection{Localization at the prime three}

The conjecture can be reduced to a statement about one odd integer and one irrational power.

\begin{theorem}[Localization reformulation]
\label{thm:localization}
The Alaoglu--Erd\H{o}s conjecture is false if and only if there exists an odd integer $u>1$
such that
\[
  u^{\thetaq}\in\Zthree.
\]
More explicitly, failure is equivalent to the existence of $u>1$ odd and $a\in\Nzero$ for
which $3^a u^{\thetaq}\in\N$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
If $x$ is a counterexample, write the integer $2^x$ uniquely as $2^a u$ with $u$ odd.  Since
$2^x$ is not a power of two, $u>1$.  Then
\[
 u^{\thetaq}=\frac{(2^x)^{\thetaq}}{(2^a)^{\thetaq}}
 =\frac{3^x}{3^a}\in\Zthree.
\]
Conversely, if $u^{\thetaq}=B/3^a$ with $B\in\N$, set
$x=a+\log_2u$.  Then
\[
 2^x=2^au\in\N,
 \qquad
 3^x=3^a u^{\thetaq}=B\in\N.
\]
The exponent is not integral, because otherwise the odd $u=2^{x-a}>1$ would be a power of two.
\end{proof}

\begin{statusbox}{\statusnew}
This is a compact target for future work: prove
$u^{\log_2 3}\notin\Z[1/3]$ for every odd $u>1$.  It is stronger than merely proving the
irrationality of $3^{\log_2 3}$ for the special odd core $u=3$, but it exposes exactly the
extra denominator allowed by shifting the exponent by an integer.
\end{statusbox}

\subsection{Canonical radical algebraicity}

In the notation of \cref{thm:generator}, $E=3^{\log_2w}$ satisfies
\[
 E^d=\frac{A}{3^a}\in\Q.
\]
This is a positive rational number.  The minimality of $d$ has an algebraic consequence.

\begin{proposition}[Exact radical degree]
\label{prop:radical-degree}
The binomial
\[
 X^d-\frac{A}{3^a}
\]
is irreducible over $\Q$, and $E$ is algebraic of exact degree $d$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For a positive rational $q$, the standard binomial irreducibility criterion says that
$X^d-q$ can be reducible only if $q$ is a $p$-th power in $\Q$ for some prime $p\mid d$; the
additional $4\mid d$ exceptional case concerns a negative multiple of a fourth power and is
irrelevant for $q>0$.  If $q=r^p$ and $p\mid d$, the positive real number
$E^{d/p}$ is the rational $p$-th root of $q$, contradicting the minimality of $d$.  Therefore
the binomial is irreducible and is the minimal polynomial of $E$.
\end{proof}

A counterexample therefore produces a very special algebraic number
\[
 E=3^{\log_2w}.
\]
It arises after starting from a transcendental exponent $\log_2w$.  Taking logarithms of
$E^d=A/3^a$ yields
\begin{equation}
 d\,\log w\,\log3
   = (\log A-a\log3)\log2.
 \label{eq:quadratic-logs}
\end{equation}
This is a bilinear relation among logarithms of algebraic numbers.  Baker's theorem controls
\emph{linear} forms in logarithms with algebraic coefficients; it does not directly rule out
\eqref{eq:quadratic-logs}.  The four exponentials conjecture does.  The equation is therefore a
precise description of the transcendence-theoretic wall, not a disguised elementary
factorization problem.

\section{Exact counting and equidistribution}
\label{sec:counting}

The exact generator theorem turns several qualitative statements into equalities.

\subsection{One unit interval at a time}

\begin{theorem}[Exact unit-block count]
\label{thm:block-count}
Assume a counterexample exists and let $\beta$ be the primitive generator.  For every
$N\in\Nzero$, the nonintegral solutions in $[N,N+1)$ are precisely
\[
 x_{N,k}=\left\lceil N-k\beta\right\rceil+k\beta,
 \qquad
 1\le k\le \left\lfloor\frac{N+1}{\beta}\right\rfloor.
\]
Consequently,
\[
 \#\bigl((\Sol\setminus\Nzero)\cap[N,N+1)\bigr)
 =\left\lfloor\frac{N+1}{\beta}\right\rfloor,
\]
and
\[
 \#\bigl(\Cand\cap[2^N,2^{N+1})\bigr)
 =1+\left\lfloor\frac{N+1}{\beta}\right\rfloor.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Fix $k\ge1$.  There is at most one integer $n$ with
$N\le n+k\beta<N+1$, because the interval has length one.  Such an $n\ge0$ exists exactly
when $k\beta<N+1$, and then it is $n=\lceil N-k\beta\rceil$.  Since $\beta$ is irrational,
$(N+1)/\beta$ is not an integer, so the admissible positive $k$ are exactly those in the
stated range.  The one integral solution in the block is $N$.  Apply the bijection
$x\mapsto2^x$ for the candidate count.
\end{proof}

The committed bound $\beta>8$ immediately gives
\[
 \#\bigl((\Sol\setminus\Nzero)\cap[N,N+1)\bigr)
 \le\left\lfloor\frac{N}{8}\right\rfloor.
\]
Indeed, $k\beta<N+1$ implies $8k<N+1$, and the integer $8k$ is therefore at most $N$.
Under the independent finite computation of \cref{sec:finite-computation}, $\beta>20$ and the
right side improves to $\lfloor N/20\rfloor$.

\subsection{Cumulative exact count}

For integer $T\ge1$, let
\[
 C(T):=\#\{m\in\Cand:m<2^T\}.
\]
Summing \cref{thm:block-count} gives
\begin{equation}
 C(T)=T+\sum_{r=1}^{T}\left\lfloor\frac r\beta\right\rfloor,
 \label{eq:exact-cumulative}
\end{equation}
and hence
\begin{equation}
 C(T)=\frac{T^2}{2\beta}+O(T).
 \label{eq:asymptotic-count}
\end{equation}
The nonintegral candidates alone have count
$\sum_{r=1}^{T}\lfloor r/\beta\rfloor$.

This is conditionally sharper than the committed universal upper bound
$C(T)\le T^2$ arising from $(\clog_2N)^2$, and sharper than the survey's article-only
$\clog_2N\,\clog_3N$ coefficient.  It also matches the committed conditional quadratic lower
box in its correct leading order.  With $\beta>8$, the leading coefficient is strictly less
than $1/16$; with the independent $\beta>20$ barrier it is strictly less than $1/40$.

\subsection{Equidistribution in high blocks}

The fractional part of $x_{N,k}$ is simply $\fract{k\beta}$.  Thus a high unit interval contains
an initial segment of one irrational rotation orbit.

\begin{theorem}[Blockwise Weyl equidistribution]
\label{thm:block-equidist}
Assume a counterexample exists.  For every interval $I\subseteq[0,1]$ whose boundary has
Lebesgue measure zero,
\[
 \frac{\#\{x\in(\Sol\setminus\Nzero)\cap[N,N+1):\fract{x}\in I\}}
      {\#((\Sol\setminus\Nzero)\cap[N,N+1))}
 \longrightarrow |I|
\]
as $N\to\infty$ through values for which the denominator is nonzero.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By \cref{thm:block-count}, the relevant fractional parts are
\[
 \fract{\beta},\fract{2\beta},\ldots,\fract{K_N\beta},
 \qquad K_N=\left\lfloor\frac{N+1}{\beta}\right\rfloor\to\infty.
\]
Since $\beta$ is irrational, Weyl's criterion applies.  For every nonzero integer $h$,
\[
 \frac1K\sum_{k=1}^{K}e^{2\pi i hk\beta}
 =\frac{e^{2\pi ih\beta}(1-e^{2\pi ihK\beta})}
        {K(1-e^{2\pi ih\beta})}
 \longrightarrow0.
\]
Therefore $\fract{k\beta}$ is uniformly distributed modulo one, which gives the claim.
\end{proof}

\begin{statusbox}{\statusnew}
This is stronger than mere density and gives an exact interpretation of the survey's missing
\texttt{FractionalPartDensity} direction.  No effective discrepancy rate follows without
additional Diophantine information on $\beta$; the geometric-sum proof gives convergence for
each Fourier mode but no uniform control over many modes.
\end{statusbox}

\subsection{Why equidistribution does not finish the conjecture}

For each $k$, the committed shrinking-target theorem gives approximately
\[
  \distZ{k\beta}\gtrsim M^{-k},
  \qquad M=2^\beta.
\]
Equidistribution guarantees visits to any \emph{fixed} neighborhood of an integer and, with
more work, polynomial-scale neighborhoods along subsequences.  It does not force
$\distZ{k\beta}$ below an exponentially shrinking radius $M^{-k}$.  Numbers with bounded
continued-fraction coefficients, for example, satisfy a much larger lower bound of order
$1/k$, and still have equidistributed multiples.  The two statements therefore fit together
without tension.

\section{Sharper finite-difference obstructions}
\label{sec:higher-differences}

The committed local combinatorial theorem forbids three candidate bases in arithmetic
progression under the convenient sufficient condition $h^5\le n$.  The underlying curvature
argument yields a sharper natural exponent and extends to arbitrary progression length.

\subsection{An integral representation for finite differences}

For a function $f$ and step $h>0$, write
\[
 \Delta_hf(t)=f(t+h)-f(t),
 \qquad \Delta_h^r=\underbrace{\Delta_h\cdots\Delta_h}_{r\text{ times}}.
\]
If $f\in C^r$, repeated use of the fundamental theorem of calculus gives
\begin{equation}
 \Delta_h^r f(n)
 =\int_{[0,h]^r}f^{(r)}(n+t_1+\cdots+t_r)\,dt_1\cdots dt_r.
 \label{eq:finite-diff-integral}
\end{equation}

For $f(t)=t^{\thetaq}$ and $r\ge2$,
\[
 f^{(r)}(t)
 =c_r^{\mathrm{sgn}}\,t^{\thetaq-r},
 \qquad
 c_r^{\mathrm{sgn}}=\thetaq(\thetaq-1)\cdots(\thetaq-r+1).
\]
Because $1<\thetaq<2$, this derivative has a fixed nonzero sign on $(0,\infty)$ and its
absolute value decreases with $t$.

\begin{theorem}[Higher-difference progression obstruction]
\label{thm:higher-difference}
Let $r\ge2$ and $n,h\in\N_{>0}$.  Put
\[
 c_r=\left|\thetaq(\thetaq-1)\cdots(\thetaq-r+1)\right|.
\]
If all $r+1$ integers
\[
 n,n+h,n+2h,\ldots,n+rh
\]
are in $\Cand$, then
\[
 1\le c_r h^r n^{\thetaq-r}.
\]
Consequently such a progression is impossible whenever
\begin{equation}
 c_rh^r<n^{r-\thetaq}.
 \label{eq:progression-condition}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
If all $n+jh$ are candidates, then every $(n+jh)^{\thetaq}$ is an integer, and therefore the
integer linear combination $\Delta_h^rf(n)$ is an integer.  Equation
\eqref{eq:finite-diff-integral} and the fixed sign of $f^{(r)}$ show that this finite difference
is nonzero.  Hence its absolute value is at least one.  Since
$t^{\thetaq-r}\le n^{\thetaq-r}$ for $t\ge n$,
\[
 1\le|\Delta_h^rf(n)|
 \le\int_{[0,h]^r}c_r n^{\thetaq-r}\,d\mathbf t
 =c_rh^rn^{\thetaq-r}.
\]
Rearranging gives the contrapositive \eqref{eq:progression-condition}.
\end{proof}

\begin{statusbox}{\statusnew}
The proof is short enough to be an attractive Lean target: it replaces a nested mean-value
construction by the iterated-integral identity and reduces the numerical side to monotonicity
of a negative real power.
\end{statusbox}

\subsection{The sharpened three-term criterion}

For $r=2$, the theorem says that $n,n+h,n+2h$ cannot all be candidates when
\begin{equation}
 \thetaq(\thetaq-1)h^2<n^{2-\thetaq}.
 \label{eq:sharp-3ap}
\end{equation}
Equivalently,
\[
 h<\frac{1}{\sqrt{\thetaq(\thetaq-1)}}
       n^{(2-\thetaq)/2}
 =1.038547802\ldots\,n^{0.2075187496\ldots}.
\]
The committed hypothesis $h^5\le n$ gives $h\le n^{0.2}$ and is therefore a clean but
strictly weaker sufficient condition.

\subsection{Longer progressions}

The first few thresholds from \eqref{eq:progression-condition} are shown below.  The coefficient
$C_r=c_r^{-1/r}$ gives the sufficient exclusion $h<C_rn^{1-\thetaq/r}$.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}rrrr@{}}
\toprule

\section{Independent finite verification through \texorpdfstring{$2^{20}$}{2 to the 20th}}
\label{sec:finite-computation}

\subsection{Statement and trust boundary}

\begin{proposition}[Finite exclusion under interval-software trust]
\label{prop:finite20}
For every integer $m$ with $1\le m<2^{20}$, if $m^{\thetaq}\in\Z$, then $m$ is a power of two.
Equivalently, under the interval computation described below,
\[
 2^x,3^x\in\Z,
 \quad 2^x<2^{20}
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x\in\Z.
\]
Thus a nonintegral solution would satisfy $x>20$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{statusbox}{\statuscomp}
The scan used CPython 3.13.5 and \texttt{mpmath} 1.3.0 interval arithmetic at 40 decimal digits.
It is reproducible and uses outward interval endpoints, but it has not been translated into a
Lean certificate and was not replayed by the Lean kernel.  It should therefore be viewed as a
strong finite computational result, not as an extension of the repository's trusted theorem
\lean{eight_le_of_not_integer_of_two_three_rpow_integer}.
\end{statusbox}

\subsection{Certificate inequality}

For each non-power of two $m$, a floating approximation proposes
$n=\lfloor m^{\thetaq}\rfloor$.  The proposal is accepted only after interval arithmetic proves
both strict inequalities
\begin{align}
 \log m\,\log3-\log n\,\log2&>0,\label{eq:lower-log}\\
 \log(n+1)\,\log2-\log m\,\log3&>0.\label{eq:upper-log}
\end{align}
Because $\log2>0$, these are exactly
\[
 n<m^{\thetaq}<n+1.
\]
Once both interval lower endpoints are positive, the initial floating approximation has no
remaining logical role: even if it had proposed the wrong $n$, the two certified inequalities
could not simultaneously hold.

All $1{,}048{,}555$ non-powers of two below $2^{20}$ passed.  The scan was split into two equal
ranges for reproducibility.

\subsection{Reproducible script}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=pythonstyle,caption={Interval verification for one range.  Set START to 1 or $2^{19}$ and LIMIT to $2^{19}$ or $2^{20}$.},label={lst:interval}]
import math, time, hashlib
import mpmath as mp

START = 1
LIMIT = 1 << 19
DPS = 40

mp.iv.dps = DPS
ln2 = mp.iv.log(2)
ln3 = mp.iv.log(3)
theta_float = math.log(3.0) / math.log(2.0)

checked = 0
failures = []
min_lower = (float("inf"), None, None)
min_upper = (float("inf"), None, None)
manifest = hashlib.sha256()
start_time = time.time()

for m in range(START, LIMIT):
    if (m & (m - 1)) == 0:      # integral exponent: m is a power of two
        continue

    # Only proposes a neighboring integer.  The interval tests below certify it.
    n = math.floor(math.exp(theta_float * math.log(m)))

    lhs = mp.iv.log(m) * ln3
    lower_diff = lhs - mp.iv.log(n) * ln2
    upper_diff = mp.iv.log(n + 1) * ln2 - lhs

    lower_lb = float(lower_diff.a)
    upper_lb = float(upper_diff.a)
    if not (lower_lb > 0.0 and upper_lb > 0.0):
        failures.append((m, n, str(lower_diff), str(upper_diff)))
        break

    min_lower = min(min_lower, (lower_lb, m, n))
    min_upper = min(min_upper, (upper_lb, m, n))
    manifest.update(f"{m}:{n};".encode())
    checked += 1

print("checked_nonpowers=", checked)
print("failures=", len(failures))
print("min_lower_log_margin=", min_lower)
print("min_upper_log_margin=", min_upper)
print("sha256_m_n_manifest=", manifest.hexdigest())
print("elapsed_seconds=", time.time() - start_time)
assert not failures
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{How to turn this into a kernel certificate}

The interval scan is useful reconnaissance, but the repository already demonstrates a stronger
trust architecture: generate rational inequalities externally and replay them with exact
integer arithmetic in Lean.  A scalable $2^{20}$ formal certificate should separate:
\begin{enumerate}
\item a small table identifying, for each $m$, a certified neighboring integer $n$ and a
rational enclosure for $\thetaq$ or the relevant logarithms;
\item exact lemmas converting those enclosures into \eqref{eq:lower-log}--\eqref{eq:upper-log};
\item chunked data modules to keep elaboration and memory bounded;
\item one imported theorem whose trust boundary is only Lean's kernel and the axioms already
used by Mathlib.
\end{enumerate}
A more compact alternative is to use continued-fraction convergents for $\thetaq$ and exact
integer comparisons $m^p$ versus $n^q$, sharing one convergent across large ranges.  The
tradeoff is enormous integers; reflection or verified binary powering is preferable to giant
source literals.

\section{Attempts at a full proof and their exact failure points}
\label{sec:attempts}

\subsection{Attempt 1: collapse rank two to rank one}

The committed determinant theorem gives multiplicative rank at most two, and the always-present
candidate $2$ consumes one direction.  It is natural to hope that integrality of
$m^{\thetaq}$ forces the remaining odd direction to disappear.  The new exact structure theorem
shows precisely what remains: a failure would produce a perfectly coherent rank-two monoid
\[
 \Cand=\langle2,M\rangle_{\mathrm{monoid}}.
\]
Nothing in the rank argument distinguishes this from the allowed rank-one monoid of powers of
two.  Excluding the second generator is exactly the original transcendence problem.

\subsection{Attempt 2: use density of multiples against endpoint gaps}

The orbit $k\beta\pmod1$ is not only dense but equidistributed.  However, the integrality gap is
of exponential scale $M^{-k}$.  Standard equidistribution controls fixed intervals, and even
strong classical discrepancy bounds from continued fractions are polynomial in $k$ unless one
knows very special partial quotients.  There is no contradiction between an irrational rotation
and avoiding exponentially small targets.  To close this route one would need a theorem
specific to the transcendental $\beta$ satisfying $2^\beta,3^\beta\in\N$, not generic metric
equidistribution.

\subsection{Attempt 3: combine exact counts with local progression obstructions}

Conditional failure gives $O(\log X)$ candidates in a multiplicative block $[X,2X)$, with exact
constant $1/\beta$.  Additive-combinatorial theorems such as Roth's theorem become powerful for
sets of positive density, but $O(\log X)$ points are far below that regime.  The new
higher-difference obstruction reaches steps $X^{1-\thetaq/r}$ for fixed progression length
$r+1$, while the average candidate gap is $X/\log X$.  Thus sparse exact structure and local
curvature remain mutually consistent.

A genuinely global theorem for $S$-unit equations among the monomials $2^nM^k$ might still be
useful.  A three-term progression would solve
\[
 2^{n_1}M^{k_1}+2^{n_3}M^{k_3}=2^{n_2+1}M^{k_2}.
\]
General $S$-unit theory gives finiteness in fixed nondegenerate settings, not existence.  The
absence of such progressions is therefore not contradictory.

\subsection{Attempt 4: exploit the primitive output pair}

The pair $(M,A)$ is highly primitive by \cref{cor:no-depth,cor:no-common-power,cor:affine-primitive}.
One might hope that these conditions conflict with
\[
 \frac{\log M}{\log2}=\frac{\log A}{\log3}.
\]
They do rule out all obvious extractions of common integer shifts and roots, but the remaining
equality is still \eqref{eq:quadratic-logs}.  Known lower bounds for linear forms in logarithms
do not apply to a product-of-logs determinant with both coefficient rows transcendental.

\subsection{Attempt 5: force algebraicity of a forbidden auxiliary exponential}

The five exponentials theorem says, in a suitable normalization, that if the four corner
exponentials are algebraic, then an additional exponential $e^{\gamma x}$ is transcendental for
every nonzero algebraic $\gamma$ \cite{WaldschmidtVariations,Roy1992}.  This is a genuine
restriction on a counterexample, but the natural auxiliary quantities needed to reflect prime
factorization, such as $p^x=e^{x\log p}$, have coefficient $\log p$, which is transcendental
rather than algebraic.  The theorem therefore does not directly turn divisors of $M$ or $A$
into a contradiction.

The ordinary six exponentials theorem does imply that if $b$ is a natural base
multiplicatively independent of $2$ and $3$, then $b^x$ must be transcendental: the other five
entries in the $3\times2$ exponential grid are algebraic.  The committed \texttt{ThreeSmooth}
theorem proves the weaker but formalized consequence that $b^x$ cannot be an integer.  Again,
this does not prohibit $b$ from dividing the integer $M=2^x$; it only controls the distinct
quantity $b^x$.

\subsection{The irreducible remainder}

All successful reductions converge on the following statement.

\begin{conjecture}[Radical four-exponentials target]
\label{conj:radical-target}
For every odd integer $u>1$,
\[
 u^{\log_2 3}\notin\Z[1/3].
\]
Equivalently, for every primitive odd $w>1$ and every $d\ge1$,
\[
 \left(3^{\log_2w}\right)^d\notin\Z[1/3].
\]
\end{conjecture}

By \cref{thm:localization}, both formulations are exactly equivalent to
\cref{conj:AE}: write an arbitrary odd $u>1$ as a power of its primitive odd core, or take
$u=w^d$ in the reverse direction.  The restriction to $\Z[1/3]$ is essential; a rational
value with a denominator involving another prime could not be cleared by an integral shift of
the exponent.  The special case $w=3,d=1$ asks whether
$3^{\log_2 3}$ lies outside $\Z[1/3]$; even the stronger assertion that it is irrational is
already outside present Gelfond--Schneider and Baker methods.  Thus a proof that treats this special case by a genuinely new mechanism would be a
meaningful breakthrough even before the full uniform theorem.

\section{Prioritized future research directions}
\label{sec:future}

The following priorities are ordered by a mixture of mathematical leverage and formalization
feasibility.

\subsection{Priority A: formalize the exact primitive-generator theorem}

This should be the immediate next repository milestone.  It converts three article-level
threads---odd core, fractional-part density, and exponent semigroup---into one canonical
structure theorem and gives many corollaries almost for free.

A plausible module split is:
\begin{description}[leftmargin=4.0cm,style=nextline]
\item[\texttt{OddCore.lean}]
Construct the primitive common odd core from the committed cross-valuation theorem
\texttt{odd\_factorization\_cross\_mul}; prove uniqueness and the representation
$m=2^iw^j$.
\item[\texttt{RationalPowerIndex.lean}]
For $E>0$, define the subgroup of integer exponents with rational powers; identify it with
$d\Z$; prove the denominator-support lemma for $E^d$.
\item[\texttt{PrimitiveGenerator.lean}]
Construct $\beta$, $M$, and $A$ and prove
$\Sol=\Nzero+\Nzero\beta$ and $\Cand=\langle2,M\rangle$.
\item[\texttt{ExactCounting.lean}]
Prove the unit-block and cumulative formulas.
\item[\texttt{FractionalPartEquidistribution.lean}]
Prove Weyl's criterion for one irrational rotation via the finite geometric sum, then derive
blockwise equidistribution.
\end{description}

The hardest engineering points are likely to be: a gcd over the finite support of a
\texttt{Finsupp}; a B\'ezout certificate showing a rational scalar is integral; converting the
subgroup of $\Z$ into a least positive generator; and bookkeeping between natural, integer,
rational, and real powers.  None requires new transcendence theory.

\subsection{Priority B: formalize the sharper finite differences}

A reusable theorem for iterated forward differences and iterated integrals would benefit more
than this project.  Once \eqref{eq:finite-diff-integral} is in place, the candidate obstruction
is a short specialization.  The $r=2$ theorem should replace $h^5\le n$ by the sharp natural
condition \eqref{eq:sharp-3ap}; the arbitrary-$r$ theorem can follow afterward.

Potential Lean-facing statement:
\begin{lstlisting}[style=leanstyle,caption={Schematic theorem signatures; not compiled Lean.}]
theorem no_candidate_arithmetic_progression_of_finiteDifference_lt_one
    (r n h : Nat) (hr : 2 <= r) (hn : 0 < n) (hh : 0 < h)
    (hsmall : fallingAbs theta r * h^r < n^(r - theta)) :
    not (forall j : Fin (r + 1), TwoBaseNaturalCandidate (n + j * h))

 theorem no_three_term_candidate_AP_sharp
    (hsmall : theta * (theta - 1) * h^2 < n^(2 - theta)) :
    not (Candidate n and Candidate (n+h) and Candidate (n+2*h))
\end{lstlisting}
The exact syntax will differ because real powers and casts need explicit handling.

\subsection{Priority C: kernel-replay the \texorpdfstring{$2^{20}$}{2 to the 20th} finite region}

The interval computation has already identified a clean target and the worst cases.  The next
step is not a larger untrusted scan; it is a compact proof object.  A generator can choose
continued-fraction enclosures adaptively, emit chunked rational certificates, and have Lean
replay only exact inequalities.  The target theorem would be
\begin{lstlisting}[style=leanstyle]
theorem integer_of_two_three_rpow_integer_of_two_rpow_lt_1048576
    {x : Real}
    (h2 : (2 : Real)^x in Set.range ((up) : Int -> Real))
    (h3 : (3 : Real)^x in Set.range ((up) : Int -> Real))
    (hlt : (2 : Real)^x < 1048576) :
    x in Set.range ((up) : Int -> Real)
\end{lstlisting}
After this, every descent theorem's constant improves from $8$ to $20$ by bookkeeping.

\subsection{Priority D: isolate the radical target in a reusable API}

The equivalence in \cref{thm:localization} should be formalized before attacking it.  Useful
interfaces include:
\begin{itemize}
\item failure of the conjecture $\Longleftrightarrow$ an odd $u>1$ with
$u^{\thetaq}\in\Z[1/3]$;
\item failure $\Longleftrightarrow$ existence of a primitive odd $w$, $d\ge1$, and
$q\in\Z[1/3]_{>0}$ with $(3^{\log_2w})^d=q$;
\item minimal $d$ $\Longrightarrow$ irreducibility of $X^d-q$;
\item a symmetric localization at $2$ obtained by swapping the bases.
\end{itemize}
This creates a small, sharply stated endpoint for importing future transcendence results.

\subsection{Priority E: five and sharp six exponentials}

Formalizing the five exponentials theorem or Roy's sharp six exponentials theorem would add
new restrictions such as transcendence of $e^{\gamma x}$ for nonzero algebraic $\gamma$.
It is not by itself a proof of the conjecture, but it would enlarge the formal transcendence
library and may interact with specially constructed auxiliary functions.  The effort is much
larger than Priorities A--D and should be undertaken with a specific downstream lemma in mind,
not merely for inventory completeness.

\subsection{Priority F: auxiliary-function methods tailored to integrality}

A route beyond four exponentials must exploit more than algebraicity.  Here all four corner
values are positive integers, all their natural powers remain integers, and the exact monoid
theorem provides a canonical primitive pair.  An auxiliary polynomial or determinant could be
designed to vanish to high order on
\[
 \{(2^nM^k,3^nA^k):0\le n<N,\ 0\le k<K\}
\]
while incorporating divisibility or congruence information unavailable in the general
four-exponentials setting.  The danger is that this grid is itself a perfectly regular
rank-two multiplicative group; a successful construction needs a new arithmetic lower bound
stronger than ``nonzero algebraic integer has absolute value at least one.''

Promising variants include:
\begin{itemize}
\item use several non-Archimedean valuations simultaneously, not only the real absolute value;
\item exploit the primitive exclusions in \cref{cor:affine-primitive} to prevent determinant
factors from sharing large perfect powers;
\item work with a resultant or norm in the radical field $\Q(E)$ from
\cref{prop:radical-degree}, combining Archimedean smallness with denominator support only at
$3$;
\item search for a product formula contradiction across all places of $\Q(E)$.
\end{itemize}
The last idea is the most structurally aligned with the new radical formulation: $E$ has a
binomial minimal polynomial and tightly controlled denominator, so all conjugates and local
heights are explicit.

\subsection{Priority G: primitive-pair computation rather than raw candidate scanning}

The exact theorem suggests a more focused experimental search.  A counterexample has a
primitive pair $(M,A)$ satisfying
\[
 M^{\thetaq}=A,
 \qquad \min(v_2(M),v_3(A))=0,
\]
with no common perfect-power degree and no nontrivial matched affine decomposition.  Finite
searches can reject candidate $M$ as soon as one of these conditions fails, and can record the
continued-fraction or logarithmic near miss in a database keyed by the odd core.  Such a search
will not prove the infinite statement, but it can reveal whether the closest approaches cluster
around particular smoothness patterns, denominators, or rational-power indices $d$.

\subsection{Priority H: quantitative irrationality and discrepancy}

Effective irrationality measures for $\thetaq$ improve finite certification of $m^{\thetaq}$,
but they do not directly control the primitive generator $\beta=\log_2M$, whose integer $M$ is
unknown.  Conversely, generic discrepancy for $k\beta$ does not reach the exponentially
shrinking target.  This direction becomes decisive only if one can derive a special effective
measure for logarithms of the hypothetical output pair from the relation
$\log M\log3=\log A\log2$.  Any claimed polynomial measure should be checked carefully against
the fact that this relation lies beyond ordinary linear forms in logarithms.

\section{Lean formalization blueprint}
\label{sec:lean-blueprint}

This section spells out the proof dependencies in a form suitable for implementation planning.
The displayed Lean is schematic; names and coercions must be adapted to the repository.

\subsection{Definitions}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=leanstyle]
def TwoBaseSolution (x : Real) : Prop :=
  (2 : Real)^x in Set.range ((up) : Int -> Real) and
  (3 : Real)^x in Set.range ((up) : Int -> Real)

def TwoBaseNaturalCandidate (m : Nat) : Prop :=
  0 < m and exists n : Nat, (n : Real) = (m : Real)^logThreeDivLogTwo

def IsPrimitiveOddCore (w : Nat) : Prop :=
  Odd w and 1 < w and
  Nat.gcd (w.factorization.support.image w.factorization) = 1
\end{lstlisting}

The actual code already defines the candidate predicate.  A robust primitive-core definition
should avoid taking a gcd of an empty finset by bundling $w>1$ and should probably work directly
with the finite support excluding $2$.

\subsection{Odd-core construction}

The core proof can be decomposed into small lemmas:
\begin{enumerate}
\item choose an anchor candidate with an odd prime factor;
\item define $g$ as the gcd of its positive odd valuations;
\item define $w$ by dividing each odd valuation by $g$ and reconstructing a natural number from
the resulting \texttt{Finsupp};
\item use \lean{odd_factorization_cross_mul} to show every candidate odd valuation vector is a
rational scalar multiple of the primitive vector;
\item use a finite B\'ezout combination of the primitive coordinates to prove the scalar is a
natural number;
\item reconstruct $m=2^iw^j$ by equality of all prime factorizations;
\item prove uniqueness.
\end{enumerate}

Mathlib may not expose exactly the desired ``gcd of a finset has a B\'ezout combination'' lemma.
A practical route is to induct over the finite support while carrying coefficients, or to use
\texttt{Ideal.span} in $\Z$ and identify the ideal generated by the coordinates with the unit
ideal.

\subsection{Rational-power subgroup}

Let $E=3^{\log_2w}>0$.  Define
\[
 J=\{j\in\Z:E^j\in\Q\}.
\]
In Lean, it may be simpler to define an \texttt{AddSubgroup Int} using integer powers in
$\R^\times$ than to mix \texttt{Real.rpow} with negative integer casts.  Positivity makes
ordinary integer powers available.  Then use the classification of additive subgroups of
$\Z$, or prove directly that a nonzero subgroup has a least positive element dividing every
member.

The denominator lemma can be stated purely for rationals:
\begin{lstlisting}[style=leanstyle]
theorem rat_den_prime_support_of_pow_mul_three_pow_integer
    {q : Rat} (hq : 0 < q) {i k : Nat} (hk : 0 < k)
    (h : exists z : Nat, (3 : Rat)^i * q^k = z) :
    exists a A : Nat, q = A / 3^a and (a = 0 or not 3 dvd A)
\end{lstlisting}
A valuation proof over \texttt{Rat.num} and \texttt{Rat.den} is likely shorter than developing
localization machinery.

\subsection{Exact representation theorem}

Once $E^d=A/3^a$ is available, the central calculation is elementary:
\[
 3^iE^{dk}=3^{i-ak}A^k\in\N
 \Longleftrightarrow i\ge ak.
\]
The reverse implication is direct.  The forward implication requires reducedness of the
rational and a divisibility argument.  The rest is normalization and irrational uniqueness.

A useful final signature is:
\begin{lstlisting}[style=leanstyle]
theorem exists_unique_primitive_generator
    (hcounter : exists x : Real, TwoBaseSolution x and not IsInteger x) :
    exists beta M A,
      not IsInteger beta and
      (M : Real) = (2 : Real)^beta and
      (A : Real) = (3 : Real)^beta and
      (forall x, TwoBaseSolution x <->
        exists! nk : Nat x Nat, x = nk.1 + nk.2 * beta) and
      (forall m, TwoBaseNaturalCandidate m <->
        exists! nk : Nat x Nat, m = 2^nk.1 * M^nk.2)
\end{lstlisting}
It may be cleaner to split existence, classification, and uniqueness into separate theorems
rather than force dependent uniqueness into one statement.

\subsection{Equidistribution without a large library dependency}

For the specific sequence $k\beta$, the Weyl sums are finite geometric series.  One can prove:
\begin{lstlisting}[style=leanstyle]
theorem geometric_weyl_sum_tendsto_zero
    (hbeta : Irrational beta) (h : Int) (hh : h != 0) :
    Tendsto (fun K => (1 / K) *
      sum k in Finset.Icc 1 K, Complex.exp (2 * pi * I * h * k * beta))
      atTop (nhds 0)
\end{lstlisting}
The remaining infrastructure is the Weyl criterion or a direct approximation of interval
indicators by trigonometric polynomials.  If Mathlib lacks the full criterion, formalizing it
is a meaningful reusable contribution.  Density alone is much easier, but the exact block
structure deserves the stronger result.

\section{Assessment of the survey's proposed directions}
\label{sec:survey-directions}

The existing survey proposes seven directions.  The new analysis changes their priority as
follows.

\subsection{Equidistribution}

This direction is mathematically complete conditional on failure: \cref{thm:block-equidist}
proves Weyl equidistribution by a one-line geometric-sum estimate.  What remains is formalization
and, separately, effective discrepancy.  The latter still does not confront the exponential
shrinking target without special Diophantine input.

\subsection{Five exponentials}

Still worthwhile as library development and as a source of auxiliary transcendence statements,
but not a direct route to $p^x$ because $\log p$ is not algebraic.  A project should identify a
specific algebraic coefficient $\gamma$ whose exponential $e^{\gamma x}$ can also be forced
algebraic from the primitive pair; otherwise the theorem only adds a restriction that is easy
to state and hard to use.

\subsection{Effective irrationality measures for \texorpdfstring{$\thetaq$}{theta}}

Highly useful for scaling finite candidate checks and replacing enormous per-value
certificates.  Less directly useful for the exact primitive generator, which involves
$\log_2M$ for an unknown integer $M$.  The finite-check application is concrete and should be
pursued.

\subsection{Larger verified regions}

The independent scan shows that $2^{20}$ is computationally routine.  The bottleneck is no
longer discovering the finite truth but expressing it in a compact kernel-replayable form.
Certificate architecture should take priority over simply extending an untrusted endpoint.

\subsection{Sharper local combinatorics}

Partly completed here: \cref{thm:higher-difference} supplies the sharper three-term exponent
and all higher orders.  The remaining open issue is global combinatorial leverage on an
$O(\log X)$-sparse set.

\subsection{Exponent semigroup structure}

Substantially resolved by \cref{thm:generator}.  There are no arbitrary Frobenius-type gaps:
all solutions are $n+k\beta$.  Future work should use this exact form rather than study a
general submonoid $B\subseteq\Nzero^2$.

\subsection{Colossally abundant numbers}

Historically important and suitable for formalization, but logically downstream.  It explains
why Alaoglu and Erd\H{o}s cared about the transcendence statement; it does not currently feed
back into a proof.  It should be pursued as an application after the core structure modules are
stabilized.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The requested commits contain a serious and unusually broad formal attack on the
Alaoglu--Erd\H{o}s conjecture.  The strongest verified themes are complementary:
interpolation determinants settle three independent bases; Gelfond--Schneider excludes
algebraic irrational exponents; finite certificates create a zero-free region; prime
factorization bounds multiplicative rank; finite differences force local additive sparsity;
and affine descent constrains perfect-power structure.

The full conjecture does not fall to a simple combination of those results.  The reason can now
be stated very sharply.  If a counterexample exists, it does not generate a messy exceptional
set that density or semigroup theory is likely to squeeze accidentally.  It generates the
perfectly rigid free monoid
\[
 \Sol=\Nzero+\Nzero\beta,
 \qquad
 \Cand=\{2^nM^k:n,k\in\Nzero\}.
\]
Its block counts, fractional parts, and multiplicative relations are all explicit and internally
consistent with the committed gap and density bounds.  The remaining assertion is that the
second generator $M$ cannot exist, equivalently that no odd $u>1$ has
$u^{\log_2 3}\in\Z[1/3]$.

That reformulation is progress rather than pessimism.  It removes several artificial degrees
of freedom, supplies a canonical primitive object, turns article-level density claims into
exact theorems, and gives a focused interface for future transcendence methods.  The most
productive near-term program is therefore:
\begin{enumerate}
\item formalize the primitive generator and exact counting;
\item kernel-replay the $2^{20}$ finite region;
\item formalize the sharp finite-difference family;
\item investigate product-formula and auxiliary-determinant arguments in the explicit radical
field $\Q(3^{\log_2w})$ under the primitive denominator constraints.
\end{enumerate}
A complete proof still requires a new idea at the four-exponentials boundary, but the target is
now considerably narrower and the surrounding formal infrastructure is strong enough to test
such an idea precisely.

\section{A symmetric formulation}
\label{app:symmetric}

Everything above has a base-swapped version.  Put $\eta=\log_3 2=1/\thetaq$.  Failure is also
equivalent to the existence of an integer $v>1$ with $3\nmid v$ such that
\[
 v^\eta\in\Z[1/2].
\]
For the primitive pair $(M,A)$, factoring $A$ into a power of $3$ times a $3$-coprime part
produces a symmetric primitive core and rational-power index.  The two normalizations give the
same least exponent $\beta$.  The compatibility condition between them is exactly
\[
 \log M\log3=\log A\log2.
\]
This symmetry is useful in formalization because some divisibility arguments may be easier on
one side, and it confirms that \cref{cor:no-depth} is not an artifact of privileging the base
$2$.
