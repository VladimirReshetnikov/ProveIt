\title{\bfseries Lagrange Cardinal Polynomials and the Rvachev Up-Function\\[0.4em]
\Large Closing an Exact Finite/Infinite Representation Loop}
\author{Research report prepared for Vladimir Reshetnikov}
\date{29 August 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Let $u=\RvachevUp$ be Rvachev's compactly supported $C^\infty$ function on
$[-1,1]$, normalized by $\int u=1$, and let $F$ be the Fabius function.
This report gives two explicit representations of every Lagrange cardinal
polynomial on $[-1,1]$ by finitely many affine copies of $u$.
The first is a transparent dyadic scale ladder with two atoms per degree;
the second uses only translates at one common scale and admits the
$d+2$-atom endpoint compression established in the repository's polynomial
synthesis volume.  For distinct nodes $\xi_0,\ldots,\xi_d$, the scale-ladder
coefficients are
\[
 a_{nj}=2^{\binom{n+1}{2}}
 \sum_{r=0}^{d-n}\frac{\gamma_r}{r!}
 L_j^{(n+r)}(-2),
\]
where $L_j$ is the $j$th Lagrange cardinal polynomial and
$\sum\gamma_rz^r/r!=1/M_u(z)$ is the reciprocal moment-generating function
of $u$.  Thus Bernoulli cumulants and complete Bell polynomials enter the
cardinal-to-atom transform explicitly.

Substitution into polynomial approximants closes two loops.  First, every
convergent Lagrange interpolation scheme for $u$ becomes a limit of finite
self-representations by shifted and scaled copies of $u$.  Second, inserting
the formula into the repository's absolutely and uniformly convergent
Fourier--Legendre expansion gives, for every cutoff $N$, an exact finite
identity
\[
 S_N(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{2N}A_{n,N}\left[
 u\!\left(-1+\frac{x+3}{2^{n+1}}\right)+
 u\!\left(-1+\frac{x+1}{2^{n+1}}\right)\right],
\]
where $S_N=\sum_{m=0}^{N}\lambda_{2m}P_{2m}$ and $A_{n,N}$ is an explicit
finite Bernoulli--Bell--Legendre transform.  Since $S_N\to u$ uniformly,
this is a closed Rvachev loop.

Several structural consequences are proved.  The matrix
$[G_n(\xi_i+2)]$ is an Appell--Vandermonde matrix whose inverse columns are
the Lagrange-to-up coefficients; its sample-to-atom determinant is
\[
 \det T_\Xi=
 \frac{2^{\binom{d+2}{3}}\prod_{n=0}^{d}n!}
 {\prod_{i<j}(\xi_j-\xi_i)}.
\]
Consequently no choice of nodes removes the intrinsic quadratic-exponential
coefficient amplification.  A normal, coefficient-independent infinite
series in these one-sided Appell blocks would make $u$ analytic, so any
fixed-coefficient closure must be strongly nonnormal.  Moreover, the top
atom coefficient in the degree-$2N$ Legendre loop has exact logarithmic
limsup $2\log 2$ after division by $N^2$.  Exact symbolic experiments through
degree $32$ verify all finite identities and show a largest amplitude about
$10^{199.7}$ while the corresponding Legendre polynomial already has
pointwise errors near $10^{-4}$ at $0$ and $1$.

The results described as new are new relative to the audited repository
snapshot; no claim of global literature priority is made.
\end{abstract}

\section{Problem, terminology, and corpus audit}

\subsection{The requested loop}
The problem has three logically distinct layers.

\begin{enumerate}
\item Start with a polynomial basis adapted to interpolation: for distinct
nodes $\Xi_d=\{\xi_0,\ldots,\xi_d\}\subset[-1,1]$, write the cardinal
polynomials
\begin{equation}
 L_j(x)=\prod_{\substack{0\le m\le d\\m\ne j}}
 \frac{x-\xi_m}{\xi_j-\xi_m},
 \qquad L_j(\xi_i)=\delta_{ij}.
 \label{eq:lagrange-cardinal}
\end{equation}
\item Represent each $L_j$ exactly on $[-1,1]$ by finitely many affine
copies of $u=\RvachevUp$.
\item Insert those finite representations into an infinite polynomial
representation of $u$ and analyze the resulting self-referential limit.
\end{enumerate}

A terminological distinction matters.  The living primary exposition in the
repository contains a Fourier--\emph{Legendre} series for $u$, whereas the
polynomials in \eqref{eq:lagrange-cardinal} are \emph{Lagrange} cardinal
polynomials.  These words are occasionally interchanged in informal
requests, but they encode different constructions.  This report closes both
natural loops:

\begin{itemize}
\item a genuine Lagrange-interpolation loop, including a nested
Lagrange--Newton series; and
\item the substitution of the Lagrange/up formula into every partial sum of
the existing Fourier--Legendre series.
\end{itemize}

\section{Fabius--Rvachev background}

\subsection{The up-function, the Fabius function, and the sinc product}
Let $u=\RvachevUp$ be the unique normalized compactly supported solution of
\begin{equation}
 u'(x)=2\bigl(u(2x+1)-u(2x-1)\bigr),
 \qquad \SupportOperator u=[-1,1],
 \qquad \int_{\RealNumbers}u(x)\Differential x=1.
 \label{eq:up-fde}
\end{equation}
It is even, nonnegative, and $C^\infty$.  In the angular-frequency Fourier
convention,
\begin{equation}
 \widehat u(\omega)
 =\int_{\RealNumbers}u(x)\EulerE^{-\ImaginaryUnit\omega x}\Differential x
 =\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}
   \frac{\sin(\omega/2^j)}{\omega/2^j}.
 \label{eq:up-fourier-product}
\end{equation}
The Fabius function $F$ is related on the support by
\begin{equation}
 u(x)=
 \begin{cases}
  F(x+1),&-1\le x\le0,\\
  F(1-x),&0\le x\le1,
 \end{cases}
 \qquad
 F(t)=u(t-1),\quad0\le t\le1.
 \label{eq:F-up-relation}
\end{equation}
These normalizations agree with the standard arithmetic treatment of Arias
de Reyna and with Rvachev's survey \cite{Arias2018,Rvachev1990}.

The product \eqref{eq:up-fourier-product} gives the probabilistic model
\begin{equation}
 Z=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}2^{-j}V_j,
 \qquad V_j\stackrel{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\operatorname{Unif}[-1,1],
 \label{eq:random-series}
\end{equation}
whose density is $u$.  This model makes the moment calculus especially
transparent.

\subsection{Moments, Bernoulli cumulants, and Bell polynomials}
Define
\begin{equation}
 M(z)=\Expectation\EulerE^{zZ}
 =\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}
   \frac{\sinh(z/2^j)}{z/2^j}
 =\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\mu_r\frac{z^r}{r!}.
 \label{eq:mgf}
\end{equation}
The symmetry of $Z$ gives $\mu_{2r+1}=0$.  The even cumulants are
\begin{equation}
 \kappa_{2m}
 =\frac{B_{2m}}{2m(1-4^{-m})}
 =\frac{2^{2m-1}B_{2m}}{m(2^{2m}-1)},
 \qquad m\ge1,
 \label{eq:cumulants}
\end{equation}
where $B_{2m}$ are Bernoulli numbers.  Consequently
\begin{equation}
 \mu_n=\Bell_n(\kappa_1,\ldots,\kappa_n),
 \label{eq:mu-bell}
\end{equation}
with $\Bell_n$ the complete exponential Bell polynomial.

The reciprocal moments $\gamma_r$ are defined by
\begin{equation}
 \frac1{M(z)}=\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\gamma_r\frac{z^r}{r!}.
 \label{eq:gamma-def}
\end{equation}
They satisfy
\begin{equation}
 \gamma_n=\Bell_n(-\kappa_1,\ldots,-\kappa_n),
 \qquad \gamma_{2r+1}=0.
 \label{eq:gamma-bell}
\end{equation}
The first values used later are shown in \cref{tab:moments}.

The moment--cumulant recurrence
\begin{equation}
 a_n=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\binom{n-1}{k-1}c_k a_{n-k},
 \qquad a_0=1,
 \label{eq:moment-cumulant-recurrence}
\end{equation}
computes $(\mu_n)$ from $c_k=\kappa_k$ and $(\gamma_n)$ from
$c_k=-\kappa_k$.  This is the exact recurrence used in the accompanying
Python code.

\subsection{The moment Appell family and its inverse}
Define the Appell polynomials
\begin{equation}
 G_n(y)=\Expectation(y-Z)^n
 =M(D_y)y^n
 =\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr\mu_r y^{n-r}.
 \label{eq:G-def}
\end{equation}
Because $Z$ is symmetric, $M(D_y)y^n=\Expectation(y+Z)^n=\Expectation(y-Z)^n$.
The exponential generating function and Appell derivative law are
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}G_n(y)\frac{z^n}{n!}
 =\EulerE^{yz}M(z),
 \qquad
 G_n'(y)=nG_{n-1}(y).
 \label{eq:G-egf}
\end{equation}
The inverse differential operator is
\begin{equation}
 \cD:=M(D_y)^{-1}
 =\sum_{r\ge0}\gamma_r\frac{D_y^r}{r!}.
 \label{eq:deconv-operator}
\end{equation}
On a polynomial of degree $d$, the series terminates at $r=d$ and
\begin{equation}
 M(D_y)\cD p=p.
 \label{eq:finite-inverse}
\end{equation}
Thus the monomials and the $G_n$ are connected by a finite triangular
Bernoulli--Bell transform.

For orientation,
\begin{align}
 G_0(y)&=1,\nonumber\\
 G_1(y)&=y,\nonumber\\
 G_2(y)&=y^2+\frac19,\nonumber\\
 G_3(y)&=y^3+\frac13y,\nonumber\\
 G_4(y)&=y^4+\frac23y^2+\frac{19}{675}.
 \label{eq:first-G}
\end{align}

\section{The dyadic integration ladder and the two-atom blocks}

This section gives a self-contained proof of the exact two-atom identity
that drives the Lagrange synthesis.

\subsection{One causal integration}
Let
\begin{equation}
 (Jf)(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}f(t)\Differential t.
 \label{eq:J-def}
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}[Causal dyadic integration]
For every $x\in\RealNumbers$,
\begin{equation}
 Ju(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}
 u\!\left(\frac{x-(2k+1)}2\right).
 \label{eq:J-one}
\end{equation}
The sum is locally finite.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Set the right-hand side equal to $H(x)$.  Compact support makes the sum
locally finite.  Differentiating and using \eqref{eq:up-fde},
\begin{align*}
 H'(x)
 &=\frac12\sum_{k\ge0}
 u'\!\left(\frac{x-(2k+1)}2\right)\\
 &=\sum_{k\ge0}\bigl(u(x-2k)-u(x-2k-2)\bigr)=u(x),
\end{align*}
by telescoping.  Both $H(x)$ and $Ju(x)$ tend to zero as $x\to-\infty$,
so they are equal.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Repeated integration and binary partitions}
For $m\ge1$, define $p_m(N)$ by
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{N=0}^{\infty}p_m(N)q^N
 =\prod_{\ell=0}^{m-1}\frac1{1-q^{2^\ell}}.
 \label{eq:binary-partition-p}
\end{equation}
Thus $p_m(N)$ counts partitions of $N$ into powers
$1,2,\ldots,2^{m-1}$, with repetitions allowed.  In particular,
$p_m(0)=p_m(1)=1$.

\begin{lemma}[Repeated dyadic integration]
For $m\ge1$,
\begin{equation}
 J^m u(y)
 =2^{\binom m2}\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}p_m(N)
 u\!\left(\frac{y-(2^m-1+2N)}{2^m}\right).
 \label{eq:Jm}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The case $m=1$ is \eqref{eq:J-one}, since $p_1(N)=1$.  Suppose the formula
holds for $m$.  Integrating one scaled summand and applying
\eqref{eq:J-one} gives
\begin{align*}
 &\int_{-\infty}^{y}
 u\!\left(\frac{t-(2^m-1+2N)}{2^m}\right)\Differential t\\
 &\qquad=2^m\sum_{k\ge0}
 u\!\left(
 \frac{y-(2^{m+1}-1+2(N+2^mk))}{2^{m+1}}
 \right).
\end{align*}
Hence the new coefficient of index $R$ is
$\sum_{k\ge0}p_m(R-2^mk)$, whose generating function is the old one divided
by $1-q^{2^m}$.  The prefactor is multiplied by $2^m$, changing
$2^{\binom m2}$ to $2^{\binom{m+1}2}$.
\end{proof}

The reciprocal lacunary product in \eqref{eq:binary-partition-p} is the
integration-side companion of the finite Thue--Morse product
\begin{equation}
 \Theta_m(q)=\prod_{\ell=0}^{m-1}(1-q^{2^\ell})
 =\sum_{r=0}^{2^m-1}(-1)^{s_2(r)}q^r.
 \label{eq:TM-product}
\end{equation}
This is the first direct bridge between the present interpolation problem
and the Thue--Morse/q-product layer of the repository.

\subsection{Tail moments and the two-atom theorem}
For $y\ge1$, the entire support of $u$ lies below $y$, so
\begin{equation}
 J^{n+1}u(y)
 =\frac1{n!}\int_{-1}^{1}(y-t)^n u(t)\Differential t
 =\frac{G_n(y)}{n!}.
 \label{eq:tail-moment}
\end{equation}
Combining this with \eqref{eq:Jm} is decisive.

\begin{theorem}[Two-atom Appell block on $-1\le x\le 1$]
\label{thm:two-atom}
For $n\ge0$ define
\begin{align}
 U_n(x)&=
 u\!\left(-1+\frac{x+3}{2^{n+1}}\right)
 +u\!\left(-1+\frac{x+1}{2^{n+1}}\right),
 \label{eq:Un-def}\\
 D_n&=n!\,2^{\binom{n+1}{2}}.
 \label{eq:Dn-def}
\end{align}
Then, for every $x\in[-1,1]$,
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\qquad D_nU_n(x)=G_n(x+2).\qquad}
 \label{eq:two-atom-G}
\end{equation}
In particular, $D_nU_n$ is a monic polynomial of degree $n$ on the interval.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Put $m=n+1$ and $y=x+2\in[1,3]$ in \eqref{eq:Jm}.  If $N\ge2$, then
\[
 \frac{y-(2^{n+1}-1+2N)}{2^{n+1}}\le-1,
\]
with equality only at $y=3,N=2$, where $u(-1)=0$.  Thus only $N=0$ and
$N=1$ survive, and both have coefficient $1$.  Equation
\eqref{eq:tail-moment} then yields
\[
 \frac{G_n(y)}{n!}
 =2^{\binom{n+1}{2}}
 \left[
 u\!\left(\frac{y-(2^{n+1}-1)}{2^{n+1}}\right)
 +u\!\left(\frac{y-(2^{n+1}+1)}{2^{n+1}}\right)
 \right].
\]
Substituting $y=x+2$ gives \eqref{eq:two-atom-G}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Fabius two-ray transfer identity]
\label{cor:F-two-ray}
For $n\ge1$ and $y\in[1,3]$,
\begin{equation}
 F\!\left(\frac{y+1}{2^{n+1}}\right)
 +F\!\left(\frac{y-1}{2^{n+1}}\right)
 =\frac{G_n(y)}{n!2^{\binom{n+1}{2}}}.
 \label{eq:F-two-ray}
\end{equation}
At $y=1$ this gives the reciprocal-moment identity
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{
 F(2^{-n})=\frac{G_n(1)}{n!2^{\binom{n+1}{2}}}.}
 \label{eq:F-reciprocal-moment}
\end{equation}
Consequently
\begin{equation}
 \frac{
 F((y+1)2^{-n-1})+F((y-1)2^{-n-1})
 }{F(2^{-n})}
 =\frac{G_n(y)}{G_n(1)}.
 \label{eq:F-transfer-ratio}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
For $n\ge1$, both arguments of $u$ in \eqref{eq:Un-def} lie in $[-1,0]$.
Apply \eqref{eq:F-up-relation}.  At $y=1$, the second Fabius argument is
zero and $F(0)=0$.
\end{proof}

For $n=0$, \eqref{eq:two-atom-G} is the partition identity
\begin{equation}
 u\!\left(\frac{x+1}{2}\right)+u\!\left(\frac{x-1}{2}\right)=1,
 \qquad -1\le x\le1.
 \label{eq:partition-pair}
\end{equation}

\section{Exact Lagrange cardinal synthesis}

\subsection{The main finite formula}
Let $\Xi_d=\{\xi_0,\ldots,\xi_d\}\subset[-1,1]$ consist of distinct nodes,
and let $L_j$ be defined by \eqref{eq:lagrange-cardinal}.

\begin{theorem}[Lagrange cardinal as a finite up ladder]
\label{thm:lagrange-up}
For $0\le j\le d$, define
\begin{align}
 b_{nj}
 &=\frac1{n!}\sum_{r=0}^{d-n}
   \frac{\gamma_r}{r!}L_j^{(n+r)}(-2),
 \qquad 0\le n\le d,
 \label{eq:bnj}\\
 a_{nj}
 &=D_nb_{nj}
 =2^{\binom{n+1}{2}}
   \sum_{r=0}^{d-n}\frac{\gamma_r}{r!}L_j^{(n+r)}(-2).
 \label{eq:anj}
\end{align}
Then, exactly on $[-1,1]$,
\begin{align}
 L_j(x)
 &=\sum_{n=0}^{d}b_{nj}G_n(x+2)
 \label{eq:Lj-G}\\
 &=\boxed{\sum_{n=0}^{d}a_{nj}
 \left[
 u\!\left(-1+\frac{x+3}{2^{n+1}}\right)
 +u\!\left(-1+\frac{x+1}{2^{n+1}}\right)
 \right].}
 \label{eq:Lj-up}
\end{align}
Thus a degree-$d$ cardinal polynomial uses at most $2(d+1)$ affine
up-atoms, organized as $d+1$ equal-coefficient pairs.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Set $q_j(y)=L_j(y-2)$.  Apply the finite inverse operator
\eqref{eq:deconv-operator}:
\begin{equation}
 B_j(y):=\cD q_j(y)=M(D_y)^{-1}q_j(y)=\sum_{n=0}^{d}b_{nj}y^n.
 \label{eq:Bj}
\end{equation}
The coefficient of $y^n$ in the $r$th derivative of $q_j$ is
$q_j^{(n+r)}(0)/n!=L_j^{(n+r)}(-2)/n!$, proving \eqref{eq:bnj}.
Now
\[
 q_j(y)=M(D_y)B_j(y)=\sum_{n=0}^{d}b_{nj}G_n(y).
\]
Set $y=x+2$ to obtain \eqref{eq:Lj-G}, then invoke
\cref{thm:two-atom} to obtain \eqref{eq:Lj-up}.
\end{proof}

Because odd $\gamma_r$ vanish, \eqref{eq:anj} can be shortened to
\begin{equation}
 a_{nj}=2^{\binom{n+1}{2}}
 \sum_{s=0}^{\lfloor(d-n)/2\rfloor}
 \frac{\gamma_{2s}}{(2s)!}L_j^{(n+2s)}(-2).
 \label{eq:anj-even}
\end{equation}
Equations \eqref{eq:cumulants}, \eqref{eq:gamma-bell}, and
\eqref{eq:anj-even} are the promised Bernoulli--Bell formula for the
Lagrange-to-Rvachev transform.

\subsection{A nodes-only elementary-symmetric formula}
The derivatives at $-2$ can be computed without first expanding $L_j$.
Define
\begin{equation}
 \rho_{jm}=\frac1{-2-\xi_m},\qquad m\ne j,
 \label{eq:rhojm}
\end{equation}
and let $e_q^{(j)}$ be the elementary symmetric polynomial of degree $q$ in
the $d$ numbers $\{\rho_{jm}:m\ne j\}$.  Since
\begin{equation}
 L_j(-2+z)=L_j(-2)\prod_{m\ne j}(1+\rho_{jm}z),
 \label{eq:Lj-product-anchor}
\end{equation}
we have
\begin{equation}
 L_j^{(q)}(-2)=q!L_j(-2)e_q^{(j)}.
 \label{eq:Lj-derivative-symmetric}
\end{equation}

\begin{corollary}[Explicit nodes-only amplitude]
\label{cor:nodes-only}
The amplitude in \eqref{eq:Lj-up} is
\begin{equation}
 a_{nj}=2^{\binom{n+1}{2}}L_j(-2)
 \sum_{s=0}^{\lfloor(d-n)/2\rfloor}
 \gamma_{2s}\frac{(n+2s)!}{(2s)!}e_{n+2s}^{(j)}.
 \label{eq:anj-nodes-only}
\end{equation}
Here
\begin{equation}
 L_j(-2)=\prod_{m\ne j}\frac{-2-\xi_m}{\xi_j-\xi_m}.
 \label{eq:Lj-minus2}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

This form separates three structures: the interpolation geometry in
$L_j(-2)$ and $e_q^{(j)}$, the Rvachev law in $\gamma_{2s}$, and the dyadic
integration normalization in $2^{\binom{n+1}{2}}$.

\subsection{The Appell--Vandermonde inverse}
Define the $(d+1)\times(d+1)$ matrix
\begin{equation}
 \mathsf A_\Xi=(A_{in})_{0\le i,n\le d},
 \qquad A_{in}=G_n(\xi_i+2).
 \label{eq:Appell-Vandermonde}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Appell--Vandermonde inversion]
\label{thm:Appell-Vandermonde}
Let $\mathsf B=(b_{nj})_{0\le n,j\le d}$ with $b_{nj}$ from
\eqref{eq:bnj}.  Then
\begin{equation}
 \mathsf B=\mathsf A_\Xi^{-1}.
 \label{eq:B=Ainv}
\end{equation}
Moreover,
\begin{equation}
 \det\mathsf A_\Xi=\prod_{0\le i<j\le d}(\xi_j-\xi_i).
 \label{eq:detA}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Evaluating \eqref{eq:Lj-G} at $x=\xi_i$ gives
\[
 \delta_{ij}=\sum_{n=0}^{d}A_{in}b_{nj},
\]
so $\mathsf A_\Xi\mathsf B=I$.  Each $G_n(y)$ is monic of degree $n$.
Changing from the monomial columns $(1,y,\ldots,y^d)$ to
$(G_0(y),\ldots,G_d(y))$ is unit triangular, hence does not change the
Vandermonde determinant.  Replacing $y_i=\xi_i+2$ by $\xi_i$ also leaves
all pairwise differences unchanged.
\end{proof}

For sample data $\bm f=(f(\xi_0),\ldots,f(\xi_d))^{\mathsf T}$, the Appell
coordinates are $\bm c=\mathsf A_\Xi^{-1}\bm f$, while the raw two-atom
amplitudes are
\begin{equation}
 \bm A=\mathsf T_\Xi\bm f,
 \qquad
 \mathsf T_\Xi=\DiagonalOperator(D_0,\ldots,D_d)\mathsf A_\Xi^{-1}.
 \label{eq:sample-to-atom}
\end{equation}

\begin{corollary}[Exact determinant of the sample-to-atom transform]
\label{cor:detT}
For ordered distinct nodes,
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{
 \det\mathsf T_\Xi
 =\frac{2^{\binom{d+2}{3}}\prod_{n=0}^{d}n!}
 {\prod_{0\le i<j\le d}(\xi_j-\xi_i)}.}
 \label{eq:detT}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Multiply \eqref{eq:detA} by $\prod_nD_n$ and use
\[
 \sum_{n=0}^{d}\binom{n+1}{2}=\binom{d+2}{3}.
\]
\end{proof}

\subsection{Algorithm and complexity}

\begin{algorithm}[Exact Lagrange-to-up transform]
\label{alg:lagrange-up}
Given distinct nodes $\xi_0,\ldots,\xi_d$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Compute $\kappa_{2m}$ from \eqref{eq:cumulants} for
$1\le m\le\lfloor d/2\rfloor$.
\item Compute $\gamma_0,\ldots,\gamma_d$ by the recurrence
\eqref{eq:moment-cumulant-recurrence} with cumulants $-\kappa_r$.
\item For each $j$, form $L_j(-2)$ and the elementary symmetric arrays
$e_q^{(j)}$ from \eqref{eq:rhojm}--\eqref{eq:Lj-product-anchor}.
\item Evaluate \eqref{eq:anj-nodes-only} for $0\le n\le d$.
\item Emit the two affine atoms in \eqref{eq:Un-def}, each with amplitude
$a_{nj}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{algorithm}

A straightforward implementation costs $\BigO(d^3)$ arithmetic operations
for all $d+1$ cardinals.  Product trees or prefix/suffix elementary-symmetric
updates reduce the structured part to $\BigO(d^2)$.  For rational nodes all
operations can be exact.  Floating-point computation should use high
precision because the anchor $-2$ lies outside the interpolation interval
and the factors $D_n$ grow superexponentially in $n$.

\section{A common-scale representation using shifts only}

The scale ladder in \cref{thm:lagrange-up} changes the dilation with $n$.
If ``shifted copies'' is interpreted literally as a fixed dilation with only
translations varying, the repository's common-scale theorem gives a second
answer.

\subsection{Global common-scale synthesis}
Let $P\in\Pi_d$ be a polynomial on $[0,1]$, put $H=2^d$, and retain $M(z)$
for the moment-generating function in \eqref{eq:mgf}.  Define
\begin{equation}
 Q:=M(HD_t)^{-1}P
 =\sum_{r=0}^{d}\gamma_rH^r\frac{P^{(r)}}{r!}.
 \label{eq:Q-common}
\end{equation}
The common-scale reproduction theorem from
\cite{UpPolynomialSynthesis} states
\begin{equation}
 P(t)=\frac1H\sum_{k\in\IntegerNumbers}Q(k)
 u\!\left(\frac{t-k}{H}\right).
 \label{eq:global-common}
\end{equation}
On $[0,1]$ only $-H+1\le k\le H$ contribute.

For the cardinal $L_j$ on $[-1,1]$, set
\begin{equation}
 P_j(t)=L_j(2t-1).
 \label{eq:Pj-normalized}
\end{equation}
Then \eqref{eq:global-common} becomes
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{
 L_j(x)=\frac1H\sum_{k=-H+1}^{H}Q_j(k)
 u\!\left(\frac{x+1-2k}{2H}\right),
 \qquad -1\le x\le1,}
 \label{eq:Lj-common-scale}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
 Q_j(t)=\sum_{r=0}^{d}\gamma_rH^r\frac{P_j^{(r)}(t)}{r!}.
 \label{eq:Qj-common}
\end{equation}
All $2H=2^{d+1}$ atoms have the same dilation $2H$ in the $x$ variable.

The scale $H=2^d$ is minimal among positive integer cardinal scales.  In the
angular-frequency convention of \eqref{eq:up-fourier-product}, the minimum
multiplicity of a lattice zero $\widehat u(2\pi H\ell)$ over
$\ell\in\IntegerNumbers\setminus\{0\}$ is $\TwoAdicValuation(H)+1$.  Reproduction of all polynomials
through degree $d$ requires multiplicity at least $d+1$, hence
$\TwoAdicValuation(H)\ge d$ and the smallest integer choice is $H=2^d$.  This is a sharp
Strang--Fix phenomenon, consistent with the classical shift-invariant
framework \cite{deBoorDeVoreRon1994,Zakharov2020}.

\subsection{The minimal d+2 endpoint dictionary}
The common-scale representation has a large alias nullspace.  Define
\begin{equation}
 A_d(z)=\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}
 \bigl(z^{2^{\ell-1}}+1\bigr)^{d-\ell+1}
 =\prod_{m=1}^{d}[2^m]_z,
 \label{eq:Ad}
\end{equation}
where $[N]_z=(1-z^N)/(1-z)$.  Its degree is
$2^{d+1}-d-2$, and the identity
\begin{equation}
 A_d(z)(z-1)^{d+1}
 =(-1)^{d+1}(1-z)\Theta_d(z^2)
 \label{eq:Ad-TM}
\end{equation}
links the alias annihilator directly to the finite Thue--Morse product.

The endpoint-basis theorem of \cite{UpPolynomialSynthesis} compresses
\eqref{eq:Lj-common-scale} to the $d+2$ rightmost centers.  In the present
coordinates there are unique coefficients $\beta_{jk}$, rational whenever
the nodes are rational, for $H-d-1\le k\le H$, such that
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{
 L_j(x)=\frac1H\sum_{k=H-d-1}^{H}\beta_{jk}
 u\!\left(\frac{x+1-2k}{2H}\right),
 \qquad -1\le x\le1.}
 \label{eq:Lj-endpoint}
\end{equation}
They are obtained by extending the early values $Q_j(k)$ through the monic
recurrence $A_d(E)\nu=0$ and subtracting the resulting null sequence on the
last $d+2$ centers.

\begin{remark}[Two exact answers to the finite-synthesis problem]
The ladder \eqref{eq:Lj-up} uses only $2(d+1)$ atoms and has a closed
coefficient formula, but it spans exponentially separated scales.  The
endpoint representation \eqref{eq:Lj-endpoint} uses the optimal fixed
dictionary size $d+2$ and one common scale, but its scale is $2^d$ and its
coefficient extraction is governed by a high-order alias recurrence.  These
are complementary, not competing, normal forms.
\end{remark}

\section{The first closed loop: Lagrange interpolation of the up-function}

\subsection{Finite interpolation and atom synthesis}
For a function $f$ on $[-1,1]$, let
\begin{equation}
 (I_{\Xi_d}f)(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{d}f(\xi_j)L_j(x).
 \label{eq:interpolant}
\end{equation}
Substitute \eqref{eq:Lj-up} and define
\begin{align}
 c_{n,d}(f)&=\sum_{j=0}^{d}f(\xi_j)b_{nj},
 \label{eq:cndf}\\
 A_{n,d}(f)&=D_nc_{n,d}(f)
 =\sum_{j=0}^{d}f(\xi_j)a_{nj}.
 \label{eq:Andf}
\end{align}
Then
\begin{equation}
 I_{\Xi_d}f(x)
 =\sum_{n=0}^{d}c_{n,d}(f)G_n(x+2)
 =\sum_{n=0}^{d}A_{n,d}(f)U_n(x).
 \label{eq:finite-lagrange-loop-general}
\end{equation}
For $f=u$, this is already a finite self-representation: values of $u$ at
the interpolation nodes determine a finite sum of shifted and scaled copies
of $u$ that agrees with the interpolating polynomial on the entire interval.

In matrix form,
\begin{equation}
 \bm c=\mathsf A_\Xi^{-1}\bm f,
 \qquad
 \bm A=\mathsf T_\Xi\bm f.
 \label{eq:matrix-loop}
\end{equation}
Thus the Lagrange basis is an intermediate factorization of the unique
Appell coordinates.

\subsection{Uniform convergence on Chebyshev--Lobatto grids}
Let
\begin{equation}
 \xi_j^{(d)}=-\cos\frac{j\pi}{d},
 \qquad 0\le j\le d,
 \label{eq:cheb-lobatto}
\end{equation}
with the usual interpretation for $d=0$.  Their Lebesgue constants satisfy
$\Lambda_d=\BigO(\log d)$.  If $E_d(f)$ denotes best uniform polynomial
approximation error, then
\begin{equation}
 \|f-I_df\|_\infty\le(1+\Lambda_d)E_d(f).
 \label{eq:Lebesgue-inequality}
\end{equation}
Because $u\in C^\infty[-1,1]$, Jackson estimates give, for every $r$,
\begin{equation}
 E_d(u)=\BigO_r(d^{-r}).
 \label{eq:Jackson-up}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Closed Lagrange interpolation loop]
\label{thm:lagrange-loop}
For Chebyshev--Lobatto nodes, let $A_{n,d}=A_{n,d}(u)$ be given by
\eqref{eq:Andf}.  Then
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{
 u(x)=\lim_{d\to\infty}
 \sum_{n=0}^{d}A_{n,d}
 \left[
 u\!\left(-1+\frac{x+3}{2^{n+1}}\right)
 +u\!\left(-1+\frac{x+1}{2^{n+1}}\right)
 \right]}
 \label{eq:lagrange-closed-loop}
\end{equation}
uniformly for $x\in[-1,1]$.  The convergence is faster than every negative
power of $d$.  For each fixed derivative order $s$, the same conclusion
holds in $C^s[-1,1]$ after taking sufficiently high smoothness in the
Jackson--Markov estimate.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The $d$th finite sum in \eqref{eq:lagrange-closed-loop} is exactly $I_du$ by
\eqref{eq:finite-lagrange-loop-general}.  Equations
\eqref{eq:Lebesgue-inequality} and \eqref{eq:Jackson-up} prove the uniform
claim.  For derivatives, approximate $u$ by a polynomial with sufficiently
small $C^s$ error and apply Markov's inequality to the interpolated remainder;
since $u$ has derivatives of every order, the algebraic power can be chosen
arbitrarily large.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Nested Lagrange--Newton series}
A nested node sequence $\xi_0,\xi_1,\ldots$ gives the normalized Newton
factors
\begin{equation}
 N_0(x)=1,
 \qquad
 N_d(x)=\prod_{m=0}^{d-1}\frac{x-\xi_m}{\xi_d-\xi_m},
 \quad d\ge1.
 \label{eq:Newton-cardinal}
\end{equation}
Let
\begin{equation}
 \Delta_d f=f(\xi_d)-I_{d-1}f(\xi_d),
 \qquad I_{-1}f=0.
 \label{eq:Newton-increment}
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
 I_Df(x)=\sum_{d=0}^{D}\Delta_df\,N_d(x).
 \label{eq:Newton-series-finite}
\end{equation}
Each $N_d$ is a degree-$d$ Lagrange cardinal polynomial for the augmented
node set and therefore has an expansion
\begin{equation}
 N_d(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{d}\alpha_{n,d}U_n(x)
 \label{eq:Nd-up}
\end{equation}
from \cref{thm:lagrange-up}.  Consequently
\begin{equation}
 I_Du(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{D}
 \left(\sum_{d=n}^{D}\Delta_du\,\alpha_{n,d}\right)U_n(x).
 \label{eq:nested-closed-loop}
\end{equation}
For a convergent nested interpolation sequence, such as a suitable Leja
ordering, \eqref{eq:nested-closed-loop} is a genuine triangular Lagrange
series closed through finite up-atom blocks.  The inner coefficient depends
on the cutoff $D$; \cref{sec:analyticity-obstruction} explains why one should
not expect a stable, normally convergent cutoff-independent limit.

\subsection{A compactly supported smooth extension operator}
The right-hand side of \eqref{eq:Lj-up} makes sense globally and is
$C_c^\infty(\RealNumbers)$.  Let $\widetilde L_j$ denote that global atom sum.  Then
\begin{equation}
 \widetilde L_j|_{[-1,1]}=L_j.
 \label{eq:global-extension}
\end{equation}
For nodal data $\bm f=(f_0,\ldots,f_d)^{\mathsf T}$, define
\begin{equation}
 (\widetilde{\cE}_d\bm f)(x)
 =\sum_{j=0}^{d}f_j\widetilde L_j(x).
 \label{eq:extension-operator}
\end{equation}
This is a compactly supported $C^\infty$ cardinal extension.  Equivalently,
define $\cE_d:\Pi_d\to C_c^\infty(\RealNumbers)$ by
$\cE_dp=\widetilde{\cE}_d(p(\xi_0),\ldots,p(\xi_d))^{\mathsf T}$.
If $R$ denotes restriction to $[-1,1]$ and $I_d$ polynomial interpolation,
then
\begin{equation}
 \cP_d=\cE_d I_d R
 \label{eq:projector}
\end{equation}
is idempotent on its natural global function space because
$R\cE_d=\operatorname{Id}_{\Pi_d}$.  The closed loop is therefore a
finite-rank projection conjugated through a smooth atomic extension.

\section{The second closed loop: the Fourier--Legendre expansion}

\subsection{The corpus-known Legendre series}
Let $P_m$ denote the Legendre polynomial normalized by $P_m(1)=1$.  Since
$u$ is even, its Fourier--Legendre expansion has the form
\begin{equation}
 u(x)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{2m}P_{2m}(x),
 \qquad
 \lambda_{2m}=\frac{4m+1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}u(x)P_{2m}(x)\Differential x.
 \label{eq:Legendre-series}
\end{equation}
The primary repository exposition proves absolute and uniform convergence
on $[-1,1]$ and gives the exact dyadic formula
\begin{align}
 \lambda_{2m}
 &=4^{-m}(4m+1)
 \sum_{k=0}^{m}(-1)^{m+k}
 \binom{2m}{m+k}\binom{2m+2k}{2m}
 (2k)!\,2^{\binom{2k+1}{2}}F(2^{-2k-1}).
 \label{eq:lambda-dyadic}
\end{align}
See \cite{ReshetnikovPrimary}.  Write
\begin{equation}
 S_N(x)=\sum_{m=0}^{N}\lambda_{2m}P_{2m}(x).
 \label{eq:SN}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Literal substitution through Lagrange cardinals}
Choose any $2N+1$ distinct nodes
$\Xi_{2N}\subset[-1,1]$.  Since $S_N\in\Pi_{2N}$,
\begin{equation}
 S_N(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{2N}S_N(\xi_j)L_j(x).
 \label{eq:SN-Lagrange}
\end{equation}
Substituting \eqref{eq:Lj-up} gives
\begin{equation}
 S_N(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{2N}
 \left(\sum_{j=0}^{2N}S_N(\xi_j)a_{nj}\right)U_n(x).
 \label{eq:SN-via-Lagrange}
\end{equation}
At first sight the coefficients depend on the arbitrary interpolation grid.
They do not.

\begin{theorem}[Node cancellation]
\label{thm:node-cancellation}
For every polynomial $p\in\Pi_d$ and every distinct node set $\Xi_d$,
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{j=0}^{d}p(\xi_j)b_{nj}
 =[y^n]M(D_y)^{-1}p(y-2),
 \qquad0\le n\le d.
 \label{eq:node-cancellation}
\end{equation}
Hence the up-block coefficients obtained by passing through any Lagrange
grid are independent of that grid.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The sample vector of $p$ is $\bm p=\mathsf A_\Xi\bm c$, where $\bm c$ is
the unique Appell coordinate vector of $p$.  Multiplication by
$\mathsf B=\mathsf A_\Xi^{-1}$ recovers $\bm c$.  The differential formula
for $\bm c$ is exactly \eqref{eq:node-cancellation}.
\end{proof}

Thus Lagrange interpolation is not an extra approximation at a fixed
Legendre cutoff; it is an exact factorization of the same triangular
Appell transform.

\subsection{The explicit Legendre-to-up transform}
Define
\begin{align}
 c_{n,N}
 &=[y^n]M(D_y)^{-1}S_N(y-2)
 \label{eq:cnN-def}\\
 &=\frac1{n!}\sum_{r=0}^{2N-n}
 \frac{\gamma_r}{r!}S_N^{(n+r)}(-2),
 \label{eq:cnN-derivative}\\
 A_{n,N}&=D_nc_{n,N}.
 \label{eq:AnN-def}
\end{align}
Then \cref{thm:two-atom} gives an exact identity for every $N$.

\begin{theorem}[Finite Fourier--Legendre/up closure]
\label{thm:Legendre-finite-loop}
For $-1\le x\le1$,
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{
 S_N(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{2N}A_{n,N}
 \left[
 u\!\left(-1+\frac{x+3}{2^{n+1}}\right)
 +u\!\left(-1+\frac{x+1}{2^{n+1}}\right)
 \right].}
 \label{eq:SN-up-loop}
\end{equation}
The coefficient is the finite transform
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{
 A_{n,N}=2^{\binom{n+1}{2}}
 \sum_{m=\lceil n/2\rceil}^{N}\lambda_{2m}
 \sum_{r=0}^{2m-n}\frac{\gamma_r}{r!}
 P_{2m}^{(n+r)}(-2).}
 \label{eq:AnN-explicit}
\end{equation}
Only even $r$ contribute.  Inserting \eqref{eq:lambda-dyadic} expresses every
$A_{n,N}$ as a finite sum of Bernoulli--Bell coefficients and dyadic Fabius
values.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Apply \eqref{eq:finite-inverse} to the polynomial $S_N(y-2)$, then apply
$M(D_y)$ and the basis identity \eqref{eq:G-def}.  This gives
$S_N(x)=\sum c_{n,N}G_n(x+2)$.  The two-atom theorem gives
\eqref{eq:SN-up-loop}.  Expanding $S_N$ in \eqref{eq:cnN-derivative} gives
\eqref{eq:AnN-explicit}.
\end{proof}

The derivatives in \eqref{eq:AnN-explicit} may be written with Gegenbauer
polynomials:
\begin{equation}
 P_m^{(q)}(z)=2^q\left(\frac12\right)_q
 C_{m-q}^{q+1/2}(z),
 \qquad q\le m.
 \label{eq:Legendre-derivative-Gegenbauer}
\end{equation}
Thus the loop can be made completely explicit in classical special
functions.

\begin{corollary}[Infinite closed Fourier--Legendre loop]
\label{cor:infinite-Legendre-loop}
The limit
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{
 u(x)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{2N}A_{n,N}U_n(x)}
 \label{eq:infinite-Legendre-loop}
\end{equation}
converges absolutely through the Legendre side and uniformly in $x$ on
$[-1,1]$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The finite sum equals $S_N$ identically by
\cref{thm:Legendre-finite-loop}, and $S_N\to u$ uniformly by
\eqref{eq:Legendre-series}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The first three finite loops}
The first Legendre coefficients are
\begin{equation}
 \lambda_0=\frac12,
 \quad \lambda_2=-\frac56,
 \quad \lambda_4=\frac{11}{30},
 \quad \lambda_6=\frac{143}{5670}.
 \label{eq:first-lambdas}
\end{equation}
The first partial sums and their atom closures are
\begin{align}
 S_0(x)&=\frac12U_0(x),
 \label{eq:S0-loop}\\
 S_1(x)&=-\frac{71}{18}U_0(x)+10U_1(x)-20U_2(x),
 \label{eq:S1-loop}\\
 S_2(x)&=\frac{49849}{4050}U_0(x)-\frac{658}{9}U_1(x)
 +\frac{5012}{9}U_2(x)-4928U_3(x)+39424U_4(x).
 \label{eq:S2-loop}
\end{align}
Already at degree four, exact cancellation involves amplitudes of order
$10^4$.

\section{Structural analysis of the closed loop}

\subsection{Finite-rank conjugacy}
Let $\cS_d:\Pi_d\to C_c^\infty(\RealNumbers)$ denote the global ladder extension
\begin{equation}
 (\cS_dp)(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{d}
 D_n[y^n]M(D_y)^{-1}p(y-2)\,U_n(x).
 \label{eq:Sd-extension}
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
 (\cS_dp)|_{[-1,1]}=p.
 \label{eq:right-inverse}
\end{equation}
The Lagrange and Legendre closures are respectively
\begin{equation}
 \cS_dI_{\Xi_d}u,
 \qquad
 \cS_{2N}S_N,
 \label{eq:two-closures-operator}
\end{equation}
restricted back to $[-1,1]$.  The ``loop'' is therefore an exact right
inverse of restriction composed with a sequence of finite-rank polynomial
projectors.

\subsection{Intrinsic sample-to-atom amplification}
Equation \eqref{eq:detT} immediately forces large coefficient maps.

\begin{theorem}[Node-independent lower bound]
\label{thm:node-independent-instability}
For every distinct node set $\Xi_d\subset[-1,1]$,
\begin{equation}
 \|\mathsf T_\Xi\|_2
 \ge
 2^{d(d-1)/6}
 \left(\prod_{n=0}^{d}n!\right)^{1/(d+1)}.
 \label{eq:T-lower}
\end{equation}
More precisely, Stirling--Barnes asymptotics give
\begin{equation}
 \log\|\mathsf T_\Xi\|_2
 \ge \frac{\log2}{6}d^2+\frac12d\log d
 -\left(\frac{\log2}{6}+\frac34\right)d+\BigO(\log d).
 \label{eq:T-lower-asymptotic}
\end{equation}
No choice of interpolation nodes can make the raw ladder transform uniformly
well conditioned as $d\to\infty$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For a square matrix of size $d+1$,
$\|T\|_2^{d+1}\ge|\det T|$.  Since
$|\xi_j-\xi_i|\le2$,
\[
 \left|\prod_{i<j}(\xi_j-\xi_i)\right|
 \le2^{\binom{d+1}{2}}.
\]
Insert this into \eqref{eq:detT} and use
\[
 \binom{d+2}{3}-\binom{d+1}{2}=\binom{d+1}{3}.
\]
Taking the $(d+1)$st root gives \eqref{eq:T-lower}.
\end{proof}

Chebyshev or Fekete nodes improve the Vandermonde factor, but they cannot
remove the dominant $2^{\binom{d+2}{3}}$ volume expansion.  This distinction
is visible in the numerical experiments of \cref{sec:numerics}.

\subsection{Why the anchor minus two is unstable}
The transform \eqref{eq:cnN-derivative} samples $S_N$ and its derivatives at
$-2$, outside the approximation interval.  The Legendre polynomial has the
classical exterior growth
\begin{equation}
 |P_m(-2)|\asymp\frac{(2+\sqrt3)^m}{\sqrt m}.
 \label{eq:Legendre-minus2-growth}
\end{equation}
Let $\rhozero=2+\sqrt3$.

\begin{theorem}[Termwise divergence at the Appell anchor]
\label{thm:Legendre-anchor-divergence}
The terms of the Legendre series for $u$ do not tend to zero at $x=-2$.
More strongly, $|\lambda_{2m}P_{2m}(-2)|$ is unbounded along a subsequence.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
If $|\lambda_{2m}|=\BigO(\rho^{-2m})$ for some $\rho>1$, then the Legendre
series converges normally in every smaller Bernstein ellipse and defines an
analytic continuation of $u$.  Since $u$ is nowhere analytic on its support
\cite{ReshetnikovPrimary}, no such $\rho$ exists.  Fix
$1<\rho<\rhozero$.  Infinitely many $m$ satisfy
$|\lambda_{2m}|>\rho^{-2m}$.  Along that subsequence,
\[
 |\lambda_{2m}P_{2m}(-2)|
 \gtrsim\frac{(\rhozero/\rho)^{2m}}{\sqrt m}\to\infty
\]
by \eqref{eq:Legendre-minus2-growth}.
\end{proof}

This theorem explains why an excellent approximation on $[-1,1]$ can become
a huge cancellation problem after the Appell transform.

\subsection{The top Legendre-loop amplitude}
The coefficient of $x^{2N}$ in $P_{2N}(x)$ is
\begin{equation}
 [x^{2N}]P_{2N}(x)=2^{-2N}\binom{4N}{2N}.
 \label{eq:Legendre-leading}
\end{equation}
The deconvolution operator preserves the leading coefficient.  Therefore
\begin{equation}
 A_{2N,N}
 =(2N)!\,2^{\binom{2N+1}{2}}
 \lambda_{2N}\,2^{-2N}\binom{4N}{2N}.
 \label{eq:top-amplitude}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Exact quadratic logarithmic growth of the top block]
\label{thm:top-growth}
The top amplitudes satisfy
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{
 \limsup_{N\to\infty}
 \frac{\log|A_{2N,N}|}{N^2}=2\log2.}
 \label{eq:top-growth-limsup}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Since $|P_{2N}(x)|\le1$ on $[-1,1]$ and $\int u=1$,
$|\lambda_{2N}|\le(4N+1)/2$.  Thus the Legendre coefficients are at most
polynomially large, while nonanalyticity implies
$\limsup_{N\to\infty}|\lambda_{2N}|^{1/(2N)}=1$; otherwise an exponentially
decaying coefficient bound would give analytic continuation.  Hence
$\log|\lambda_{2N}|=\LittleO(N)$ along the limsup subsequence.  Stirling's
formula gives $\log(2N)!=\BigO(N\log N)$ and
$\log\binom{4N}{2N}=\BigO(N)$.  The sole quadratic term in
\eqref{eq:top-amplitude} is
\[
 \binom{2N+1}{2}\log2=(2N^2+N)\log2.
\]
Divide by $N^2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{conjecture}[Top-block dominance]
\label{conj:top-dominance}
For all sufficiently large $N$---possibly for every $N\ge1$---
\begin{equation}
 \max_{0\le n\le2N}|A_{n,N}|=|A_{2N,N}|.
 \label{eq:top-dominance}
\end{equation}
Consequently the maximum amplitude would also have logarithmic rate
$2\log2$.  Exact computations verify \eqref{eq:top-dominance} for
$1\le N\le16$.
\end{conjecture}

\subsection{Analyticity obstruction to a stable fixed-coefficient loop}
\label{sec:analyticity-obstruction}
The finite loop has a triangular array $A_{n,N}$.  Could there be fixed
coefficients $c_n$ such that $u=\sum c_nG_n(x+2)$ in a stable manner?  The
following theorem rules out every normally convergent version.

\begin{theorem}[No exponentially weighted normal closure]
\label{thm:no-normal-closure}
Suppose $(c_n)$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|c_n|R^n<\infty
 \label{eq:weighted-l1}
\end{equation}
for some $R>4$.  Then
\begin{equation}
 f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_nG_n(z+2)
 \label{eq:fixed-G-series}
\end{equation}
converges normally in a complex neighborhood of $[-1,1]$ and is analytic
there.  Hence it cannot agree with $u$ on a nonempty interval.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Since $|Z|\le1$,
\[
 |G_n(z+2)|=|\Expectation(z+2-Z)^n|
 \le(|z+2|+1)^n.
\]
Choose $\eps>0$ with $4+\eps<R$.  On $|z|\le1+\eps$ the right-hand side is
at most $(4+\eps)^n$.  The Weierstrass test gives normal convergence and
analyticity.  Equality with $u$ would make $u$ real analytic on
$[-1,1]$, contradicting its nowhere-analytic character.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The theorem does not exclude a violently conditional fixed-coefficient
series in a weaker topology.  It says that any such representation must
live outside every natural exponentially weighted coefficient space and
cannot justify interchange of the triangular sums by ordinary normal
convergence.
\end{remark}

\begin{conjecture}[Fixed-index drift]
\label{conj:fixed-index-drift}
For every fixed $n\ge0$, the sequence $A_{n,N}$ is unbounded as
$N\to\infty$, and for many $n$ it changes sign infinitely often.  The exact
data through $N=16$ show strong drift, but a proof appears to require a
quantitative nonanalyticity theorem for the Legendre coefficients rather
than only the qualitative limsup used above.
\end{conjecture}

\subsection{Endpoint asymptotics and a Lambert--W saddle}
Equation \eqref{eq:F-transfer-ratio} turns a two-ray endpoint ratio into a
moment ratio.  Since
\begin{equation}
 G_n(y)=\int_{-1}^{1}(y-t)^n u(t)\Differential t,
 \qquad y\in[1,3],
 \label{eq:G-integral}
\end{equation}
the dominant contribution for large $n$ comes from $t=-1$.  Writing
$t=-1+s$ and $a=y+1$ gives, to first Laplace order,
\begin{equation}
 G_n(y)\approx a^n\int_0^{\delta}
 \exp\!\left(-\frac{n}{a}s\right)F(s)\Differential s.
 \label{eq:G-Laplace-heuristic}
\end{equation}
The small-$s$ logarithm of $F$ has a quadratic logarithmic leading term and
a periodic Lambert correction in the repository's endpoint analysis
\cite{RepresentationFrontiers}.
Balancing the Laplace term with the derivative of the leading endpoint
exponent gives the saddle scale
\begin{equation}
 s_{n,y}\asymp
 \frac{a}{n\log2}
 W_0\!\left(\frac{n\log2}{a}\right).
 \label{eq:Lambert-saddle}
\end{equation}
This complements the lower-branch $W_{-1}$ coordinate used when inverting
$F$ at a small value.  For the next statement write
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal L_F(c)=\int_0^1 \EulerE^{-cs}F(s)\Differential s,
 \qquad c>0.
 \label{eq:local-Laplace-F}
\end{equation}
Only the endpoint $s=0$ contributes to its all-orders large-$c$ expansion.

\begin{conjecture}[All-orders two-ray transfer asymptotics]
\label{conj:two-ray-asymptotic}
For fixed $y\in(1,3]$, the ratio $G_n(y)/G_n(1)$ admits an all-orders
expansion obtained by evaluating the repository's periodic Lambert endpoint
profile at the pair of saddle phases
$W_0(n\log2/(y+1))$ and $W_0(n\log2/2)$.  The leading term has the form
\begin{equation}
 \log\frac{G_n(y)}{G_n(1)}
 =n\log\frac{y+1}{2}
 +\log\frac{\mathcal L_F(n/(y+1))}{\mathcal L_F(n/2)}
 +\LittleO(1),
 \label{eq:transfer-conjectural}
\end{equation}
with explicit Bell-polynomial corrections.  Through
\eqref{eq:F-transfer-ratio}, this would yield a uniform asymptotic theory for
pairs of adjacent dyadic Fabius rays.
\end{conjecture}

\section{Arithmetic and q-structural consequences}

\subsection{Rationality}
If all nodes are rational, then every $L_j^{(q)}(-2)$ is rational and every
$\gamma_{2s}$ is rational, so \eqref{eq:anj-even} gives
\begin{equation}
 a_{nj}\in\RationalNumbers.
 \label{eq:rational-amplitudes}
\end{equation}
This extends the rational coefficient phenomenon of the common-scale
endpoint basis to the explicit multiscale cardinal ladder.

\subsection{Prime support on the equispaced grid}
Take
\begin{equation}
 \xi_j=-1+\frac{2j}{d},
 \qquad 0\le j\le d.
 \label{eq:equispaced-rational}
\end{equation}
Define the finite prime set
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal P_d=
 \{p\text{ prime}:p\le d+1\}
 \cup
 \bigcup_{1\le m\le\lfloor d/2\rfloor}
 \{p:p\mid 2^{2m}-1\}.
 \label{eq:prime-set}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Prime-support bound]
\label{thm:prime-support}
For the equispaced grid \eqref{eq:equispaced-rational}, every denominator of
every reduced amplitude $a_{nj}$ has prime factors only in
$\mathcal P_d$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The equispaced cardinal can be written as an integer polynomial divided by
$2^d j!(d-j)!$, so its coefficients and all derivatives introduce only
primes at most $d$.  By \eqref{eq:cumulants}, the denominator of
$\kappa_{2m}$ can involve primes dividing $m$, primes dividing
$2^{2m}-1$, and primes in the denominator of $B_{2m}$.  The von
Staudt--Clausen theorem says the latter satisfy $p-1\mid2m$, hence
$p\le2m+1\le d+1$.  The recurrence
\eqref{eq:moment-cumulant-recurrence} introduces no new denominator primes,
so the same support bound holds for $\gamma_{2m}$.  Apply
\eqref{eq:anj-even}.
\end{proof}

The accompanying exact computations show, for example, that the least
common denominator at $d=8$ has prime support
$\{2,3,5,7,17\}$; the prime $17$ enters through $2^8-1$.

\begin{problem}[Exact denominator valuations]
Determine $v_p$ of the least common denominator of the entire matrix
$(a_{nj})$ on equispaced, Chebyshev-rational, and dyadic grids.  The theorem
above gives the possible primes, but substantial cancellation occurs and
the exact exponents appear to combine factorial valuations, von
Staudt--Clausen data, and the factors $2^{2m}-1$ in a nontrivial max-plus
fashion.
\end{problem}

\subsection{q-products, aliases, and the two inverse finite products}
The repeated-integration coefficients use
\begin{equation}
 \prod_{\ell=0}^{m-1}(1-q^{2^\ell})^{-1},
 \label{eq:integration-q-product}
\end{equation}
whereas common-scale alias elimination uses the annihilator
$A_d(z)=\prod_{m=1}^{d}[2^m]_z$.  Equation \eqref{eq:Ad-TM} shows that they
are reciprocal sides of the same lacunary Thue--Morse calculus: integration
inverts dyadic differences, while the common-scale recurrence applies them
after removing the polynomial pole at $z=1$.  This gives a concrete
operator-level bridge among the Lagrange coefficients, q-integers,
Thue--Morse signs, and the sinc-product zero multiplicities.

\section{A parity-adapted two-sided ladder}

The one-sided ladder is anchored at $-2$ and has support extending far to the
right.  Even functions such as $u$ and the Legendre partial sums admit a
symmetric alternative.

Define
\begin{equation}
 \Phi_n^{L}(x)=G_n(x+2)=D_nU_n(x),
 \qquad
 \Phi_n^{R}(x)=G_n(2-x)=\Phi_n^{L}(-x).
 \label{eq:left-right-Phi}
\end{equation}
For $r\ge0$, put
\begin{equation}
 E_r(x)=\frac12\left(\Phi_{2r}^{L}(x)+\Phi_{2r}^{R}(x)\right).
 \label{eq:Er-even}
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Even Appell ladder]
\label{prop:even-ladder}
The polynomial $E_r$ is even, monic, and has degree $2r$.  Hence
$E_0,\ldots,E_N$ form a basis of the even polynomials of degree at most
$2N$.  In particular there are unique coefficients $\eta_{r,N}$ such that
\begin{equation}
 S_N(x)=\sum_{r=0}^{N}\eta_{r,N}E_r(x).
 \label{eq:even-SN}
\end{equation}
Each $E_r$ is a four-atom symmetric up block, with the $r=0$ repetitions
combined through the partition identity.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Reflection makes $E_r$ even.  Both summands have leading terms $x^{2r}$, so
the average is monic.  A monic degree-ordered family is triangular with
respect to $1,x^2,\ldots,x^{2N}$ and therefore forms a basis.
\end{proof}

\begin{conjecture}[Symmetric conditioning advantage]
\label{conj:symmetric-conditioning}
After a natural normalization by the leading coefficient, the symmetric
basis \eqref{eq:Er-even} has a smaller coefficient norm for the even
Legendre partial sums than the one-sided basis $G_n(x+2)$.  More strongly,
it is asymptotically optimal among convex combinations of causal and
anticausal dyadic ladders.  This is plausible because it removes odd
coordinates generated solely by the off-center anchor, but a proof requires
sharp singular-value estimates for the two triangular bases.
\end{conjecture}

\section{Further frontier directions}

\subsection{Renormalized atom bases}
The factor $D_n=n!2^{\binom{n+1}{2}}$ is forced by repeated integration, but
it is not the only possible normalization.  Define normalized blocks
\begin{equation}
 \widehat U_n(x)=\frac{G_n(x+2)}{\|G_n(\cdot+2)\|_{[-1,1]}},
 \label{eq:normalized-blocks}
\end{equation}
or use the endpoint value $G_n(1)$ from
\eqref{eq:F-reciprocal-moment}.  This moves the endpoint asymptotics into the
basis and may reveal a more stable coefficient geometry.

\begin{problem}[Optimal diagonal renormalization]
Find diagonal weights $w_n$ minimizing the worst-case condition number of
$\DiagonalOperator(w_n)\mathsf A_\Xi^{-1}$ over node sets or over Chebyshev/Fekete node
families.  Determine whether a Bernoulli--Bell or Lambert-based weight can
reduce the quadratic-exponential growth to ordinary exponential growth.
\end{problem}

\subsection{A topology for a fixed-coefficient closure}
\Cref{thm:no-normal-closure} excludes normal convergence but leaves open
weaker possibilities.

\begin{problem}[Nonnormal Appell expansion]
Does there exist a fixed sequence $(c_n)$ such that
$\sum c_nG_n(x+2)$ converges pointwise, distributionally, or in a
Denjoy--Carleman topology to $u$ on $[-1,1]$?  If so, characterize the weakest
natural coefficient space in which the synthesis map is continuous.  If
not, identify the precise quasi-analytic obstruction.
\end{problem}

A promising route is to regard the degree-dependent vectors
$(c_{0,N},\ldots,c_{2N,N})$ as a projective system in an ultradistribution
space rather than insist on coordinatewise convergence.

\subsection{Sharper Legendre coefficient asymptotics}
The exact dyadic formula \eqref{eq:lambda-dyadic} and the endpoint
Lambert expansion should jointly determine the sign and magnitude of
$\lambda_{2m}$ at a finer scale than the qualitative nonanalyticity argument.
This would attack \cref{conj:fixed-index-drift,conj:top-dominance} and could
produce a periodic modulation in the top amplitudes after removing
$2N^2\log2$ and the Stirling terms.

\begin{conjecture}[Periodic modulation of the top amplitude]
After subtracting the explicit elementary contribution from
\eqref{eq:top-amplitude}, the residual
\begin{equation}
 \log|A_{2N,N}|-
 \log\!\left((2N)!2^{\binom{2N+1}{2}-2N}\binom{4N}{2N}\right)
 =\log|\lambda_{2N}|
 \label{eq:top-residual}
\end{equation}
contains a bounded or slowly varying log-periodic component inherited from
the dyadic endpoint phase.  Establishing this would connect the spectral
coefficients directly to the periodic Lambert correction.
\end{conjecture}

\subsection{Optimal cardinal grids for atomic extension}
Maximizing the ordinary Vandermonde determinant favors Fekete/Chebyshev
nodes, but the actual extension norm depends on the anchor $-2$, the
reciprocal moments, and the flat tails of $u$.

\begin{problem}[Atomic Fekete nodes]
For each $d$, minimize a norm such as
\begin{equation}
 \|\mathsf T_\Xi\|_2,
 \qquad
 \max_j\sum_n|a_{nj}|,
 \quad\text{or}\quad
 \sup_{x\notin[-1,1]}\sum_j|\widetilde L_j(x)|
 \label{eq:atomic-node-objectives}
\end{equation}
over all node sets $\Xi_d\subset[-1,1]$.  Determine the limiting node
distribution and compare it with the arcsine/Fekete law.  The external
anchor suggests a small but systematic asymmetry may appear for the
one-sided ladder, whereas the parity-adapted ladder should restore symmetry.
\end{problem}

\subsection{Formal verification}
The core identities are particularly suitable for Lean or another proof
assistant.  Their ingredients are finite polynomial algebra after importing
four analytic lemmas: the functional-differential equation, compact support,
the repeated-integration identity, and the moment generating function.
A formalization can proceed in the following order:

\begin{enumerate}
\item formalize the recurrence for $p_m(N)$ and prove \eqref{eq:Jm};
\item derive \eqref{eq:two-atom-G};
\item implement truncated formal power series for $M$ and $1/M$;
\item prove \eqref{eq:Lj-G} by polynomial operator inversion;
\item prove the Appell--Vandermonde determinant by a unit-triangular change
of basis; and
\item derive the finite Legendre loop by substitution.
\end{enumerate}

The exact rational examples and Python-generated identities provide a useful
test oracle but are not substitutes for proof.

\section{Conclusion}

The finite synthesis problem has an explicit answer.  Every Lagrange
cardinal polynomial on $[-1,1]$ is a finite sum of dyadically shifted and
scaled Rvachev atoms, with coefficients obtained by applying the reciprocal
moment operator at the exterior anchor $-2$.  Equivalently, it has a
common-scale pure-translate representation and an optimal $d+2$-atom
endpoint compression.

Substitution closes the loop in two complementary senses.  A convergent
Lagrange interpolation scheme for $u$ becomes a uniformly convergent
sequence of finite self-representations.  The repository's
Fourier--Legendre expansion yields an even more explicit closure in which
every coefficient is a finite combination of dyadic Fabius values,
Legendre derivatives, Bernoulli cumulants, and Bell-polynomial reciprocal
moments.

The loop is exact but not numerically benign.  Its sample-to-atom determinant
contains the cubic exponent $\binom{d+2}{3}$, and the top amplitude of the
degree-$2N$ Legendre closure has quadratic logarithmic rate $2\log2$.
This instability is not an accident: the transform extrapolates to $-2$,
where Legendre polynomials grow exponentially, and any normally convergent
fixed-coefficient Appell closure would contradict the nowhere-analytic
nature of $u$.

The resulting picture is therefore a useful duality.  Polynomial
approximation is stable and rapidly convergent on the physical interval,
while its exact Rvachev atomization exposes an extremely delicate
cancellation geometry near the flat dyadic edge.  That geometry is where the
Thue--Morse product, q-integer aliases, Bernoulli--Bell deconvolution, and
Lambert--$W$ endpoint saddle all meet.

\begin{align*}
 U_n(x)&=u\!\left(-1+\frac{x+3}{2^{n+1}}\right)
 +u\!\left(-1+\frac{x+1}{2^{n+1}}\right),\\
 G_n(x+2)&=n!2^{\binom{n+1}{2}}U_n(x).
\end{align*}

\paragraph{Lagrange cardinal coefficients.}
\begin{align*}
 b_{nj}&=\frac1{n!}\sum_{r=0}^{d-n}
 \frac{\gamma_r}{r!}L_j^{(n+r)}(-2),\\
 a_{nj}&=n!2^{\binom{n+1}{2}}b_{nj},\\
 L_j(x)&=\sum_{n=0}^{d}a_{nj}U_n(x).
\end{align*}

\paragraph{Matrix form.}
\begin{align*}
 A_{in}&=G_n(\xi_i+2),\\
 (b_{nj})&=\mathsf A_\Xi^{-1},\\
 \det\mathsf T_\Xi&=
 \frac{2^{\binom{d+2}{3}}\prod_{n=0}^{d}n!}
 {\prod_{i<j}(\xi_j-\xi_i)}.
\end{align*}

\paragraph{Legendre loop.}
\begin{align*}
 S_N&=\sum_{m=0}^{N}\lambda_{2m}P_{2m},\\
 A_{n,N}&=2^{\binom{n+1}{2}}
 \sum_{m=\lceil n/2\rceil}^{N}\lambda_{2m}
 \sum_{r=0}^{2m-n}\frac{\gamma_r}{r!}P_{2m}^{(n+r)}(-2),\\
 S_N(x)&=\sum_{n=0}^{2N}A_{n,N}U_n(x),\\
 u(x)&=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{2N}A_{n,N}U_n(x).
\end{align*}
