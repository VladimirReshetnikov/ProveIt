\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}
The standard theory of basic hypergeometric functions is overwhelmingly a
\emph{forward} theory: products, recurrences, transformations, summations,
limits, and coefficient identities are evaluated from their parameters.
This report develops the complementary \emph{inverse} theory.  Given a level
\(Y\), we solve, branch by branch, equations such as
\[
 (a;q)_n=Y,\qquad (a;q)_\infty=Y,
 \qquad \qbinom nk=Y,
 \qquad \Gamma_q(x)=Y,
\]
not only for the displayed argument but also for the base \(q\), a continuous
order, and the remaining parameters.  The distinction between a reciprocal
and a compositional inverse is maintained throughout.

The main results include: an explicit branch atlas for finite and infinite
\(q\)-Pochhammer symbols in the parameter \(a\); fifth-order reversion
formulae at \(a=0\) and \(q=0\); all-order logarithmic inversion at \(q=1\);
Puiseux laws at critical values, colliding factors, and roots of unity;
Laurent--Puiseux inverses at \(q=\pm\infty\); a radial root-of-unity expansion
for \((a;q)_\infty\), including an explicit \(q\to-1\)
specialization; a log-periodically modulated inverse for
large negative \(a\); inverse maps for complex order \((a;q)_\alpha\);
positive-real global inverses of Gaussian polynomials; explicit expansions
at \(q=0,1,-1,\infty\); two real inverse branches of \(\Gamma_q\) at its
unique minimum; saturating and two-sheeted inverse maps for the continuous
\(q\)-binomial coefficient and \(q\)-beta function; and scaled inverse
\(q\)-exponential expansions converging to the ordinary logarithm.

A branch-aware numerical method is supplied in a commented Python program.
The experiments confirm the local formulae at 80-digit precision.  The last
chapters isolate conjectures on global critical-point geometry,
root-of-unity monodromy, differential transcendence of generic inverse
\(q\)-functions, and uniform double-scaling limits.

\chapter{Orientation, source map, and the inverse viewpoint}

\section{Relation to the existing exponent--\texorpdfstring{$q$}{q}-series corpus}

The source tree reviewed for this report contains two large LaTeX works.  The
first is a proof-oriented monograph on \(q\)-Pochhammer symbols, Gaussian
coefficients, basic hypergeometric series, combinatorics, and arithmetic; the
second is a consolidated frontier report connecting exponent sequences,
\(q\)-series, infinite sinc products, and the Fabius--Rvachev system
\cite{ProveItMonograph,ProveItFrontiers}.  We retain their notation and use
as input their forward identities, especially
\[
 (a;q)_n=\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}(1-aq^j),\qquad
 (a;q)_\infty=\prod_{j\ge0}(1-aq^j),
\]
the finite \(q\)-binomial theorem, reciprocity of Gaussian coefficients,
cyclotomic factorization, complex-order continuation, and the
polylogarithmic expansion as \(q\to1\).

The present report does not reproduce those developments.  Its organizing
question is different:
\begin{quote}
For a fixed level \(Y\), what are all local or global solutions in one chosen
parameter, where do their branches meet, and what asymptotic coordinate makes
the inverse regular?
\end{quote}
In particular, ``inverse \(q\)-Pochhammer'' here never means merely
\(1/(a;q)_n\).  It means a compositional or parameter inverse of a level map.

\section{Four kinds of inverse problem}

Let \(F(z;\theta)\) be a \(q\)-analogue, where \(z\) is the active variable
and \(\theta\) denotes passive parameters.  Four related but distinct
problems occur.

\begin{definition}[Branchwise compositional inverse]
A local inverse branch at \((z_0,Y_0)\), with \(Y_0=F(z_0;\theta)\), is a
germ \(z=\branch(Y;\theta)\) satisfying
\(F(\branch(Y;\theta);\theta)=Y\) and \(\branch(Y_0;\theta)=z_0\).
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Parameter inverse]
If a parameter \(\theta_j\) is declared active, a parameter inverse solves
\(F(z;\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_j,\ldots)=Y\) for \(\theta_j\).  The same
analytic machinery applies, but the singular set changes because
\(F_{\theta_j}\), rather than \(F_z\), controls local invertibility.
\end{definition}

A reciprocal \(F^{-1}=1/F\) is algebraically useful but is not a
compositional inverse.  Finally, a functional equation can possess a
left-inverse or right-inverse operator without producing a pointwise inverse
of a scalar parameter.  Keeping these meanings separate prevents a common
terminological ambiguity in the \(q\)-series literature.

\section{Natural asymptotic coordinates}

The active coordinate should be chosen before series reversion.  The
following table is used repeatedly.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Regime & regular coordinate & typical inverse scale\\
\midrule
\(q\to0\) & \(q\) & Taylor or generalized Puiseux\\
\(q\to1^-\) & \(t=-\log q\) & Taylor in a logarithmic level\\
\(q\to-1\) radially & \(q=-\e^{-t}\) & Taylor in \(1/\log Y\)\\
\(q\to\pm\infty\) & \(z=1/q\) & Laurent--Puiseux in \(Y^{-1/d}\)\\
\(a\to\infty\), finite order & \(1/a\) & Laurent--Puiseux\\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\chapter{Universal branchwise inversion calculus}

\section{Lagrange--B\"urmann reversion}

\begin{theorem}[Analytic local inverse]\label{thm:analytic-inverse}
Let \(F\) be analytic near \(z_0\), put \(Y_0=F(z_0)\), and assume
\(F'(z_0)\ne0\).  Then a unique analytic inverse germ
\(z=\branch(Y)\) exists near \(Y_0\).  Its coefficients are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lagrange-inverse}
 \branch(Y)=z_0+\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{(Y-Y_0)^m}{m!}
 \left[\frac{d^{m-1}}{dz^{m-1}}
 \left(\frac{z-z_0}{F(z)-Y_0}\right)^m\right]_{z=z_0}.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The analytic inverse theorem gives existence and uniqueness.  For the
coefficient of \((Y-Y_0)^m\), write a Cauchy integral around \(z_0\), change
variables from \(Y=F(z)\), and take the residue at \(z_0\).  Integration by
parts \(m-1\) times gives \eqref{eq:lagrange-inverse}; this is the standard
Lagrange--B\"urmann formula \cite{FlajoletSedgewick,Henrici}.
\end{proof}

For repeated use, suppose
\[
 w=c_1z+c_2z^2+c_3z^3+c_4z^4+c_5z^5+O(z^6),\qquad c_1\ne0.
\]
Then direct reversion gives
\begin{align}
 z={}&\frac{w}{c_1}-\frac{c_2}{c_1^3}w^2
 +\frac{2c_2^2-c_1c_3}{c_1^5}w^3\notag\\
 &+\frac{-c_1^2c_4+5c_1c_2c_3-5c_2^3}{c_1^7}w^4\notag\\
 &+\frac{-c_1^3c_5+6c_1^2c_2c_4+3c_1^2c_3^2
 -21c_1c_2^2c_3+14c_2^4}{c_1^9}w^5+O(w^6).
\label{eq:universal-reversion-five}
\end{align}
These coefficients are universal Bell-polynomial expressions.  Formula
\eqref{eq:universal-reversion-five} is the coefficient engine behind most
explicit expansions below.

\section{Implicit differentiation in passive parameters}

\begin{proposition}[Sensitivity of an inverse branch]\label{prop:sensitivity}
Let \(F(z;\theta)=Y\), with \(F_z\ne0\), define a local branch
\(z=\branch(Y;\theta)\).  Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:first-sensitivity}
 \branch_Y=\frac1{F_z},\qquad
 \branch_{\theta_j}=-\frac{F_{\theta_j}}{F_z}.
\end{equation}
Moreover,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:second-sensitivity}
 \branch_{YY}=-\frac{F_{zz}}{F_z^3},
\end{equation}
and all mixed higher derivatives are obtained recursively by differentiating
\(F(\branch(Y;\theta);\theta)=Y\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Differentiate the identity once with respect to \(Y\) or \(\theta_j\).
Differentiating \(F_z\branch_Y=1\) once more in \(Y\) gives
\eqref{eq:second-sensitivity}.  Repetition yields multivariate
Fa\`a di Bruno polynomials.
\end{proof}

For products, it is usually more stable to set \(G=\log F\) and solve
\(G=\log Y\).  Newton's correction then becomes
\[
 \Delta z=-\frac{G(z)-\Log Y}{G_z(z)},
\]
which avoids underflow when \(F\) is exponentially small.

\section{Critical values and Puiseux inverses}

\begin{theorem}[Local critical-point normal form]\label{thm:puiseux-normal}
Suppose
\[
 F(z)=Y_c+c_m(z-z_c)^m+c_{m+1}(z-z_c)^{m+1}+\cdots,
 \qquad c_m\ne0,
\]
with \(m\ge2\).  Then the inverse relation has exactly \(m\) local Puiseux
branches
\begin{equation}\label{eq:puiseux-leading}
 z-z_c=\omega\left(\frac{Y-Y_c}{c_m}\right)^{1/m}
 +O\bigl((Y-Y_c)^{2/m}\bigr),\qquad \omega^m=1.
\end{equation}
A circuit around \(Y_c\) cyclically permutes these branches.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Factor
\(F(z)-Y_c=(z-z_c)^mH(z)\) with \(H(z_c)=c_m\ne0\).  Choose an analytic
branch of \(H(z)^{1/m}\).  Then
\(u=(z-z_c)H(z)^{1/m}\) is a local analytic coordinate with nonzero
derivative and \(F-Y_c=u^m\).  Invert the coordinate and choose one of the
\(m\) roots of \(Y-Y_c\).
\end{proof}

At a simple critical point \(F'(z_c)=0\), \(F''(z_c)\ne0\), the first terms
can be made explicit.  Write
\[
 \delta=F(z)-F(z_c)=a_2s^2+a_3s^3+a_4s^4+a_5s^5+\cdots,
 \qquad s=z-z_c.
\]
Then the two branches are
\begin{align}
 s={}&\pm\frac{\delta^{1/2}}{\sqrt{a_2}}
 -\frac{a_3}{2a_2^2}\delta
 \pm\frac{5a_3^2-4a_2a_4}{8a_2^{7/2}}\delta^{3/2}\notag\\
 &-\frac{a_2^2a_5-3a_2a_3a_4+2a_3^3}{2a_2^5}\delta^2
 +O(\delta^{5/2}).
\label{eq:simple-critical-puiseux}
\end{align}

\section{The discriminant and the branch atlas}

For a polynomial level map \(F(z;\theta)-Y\), its discriminant in \(z\)
vanishes exactly when a root is multiple.  Analytically, the corresponding
critical-value set is
\[
 \Disc_F=\{(Y,\theta):\exists z,\ F(z;\theta)=Y,\ F_z(z;\theta)=0\}.
\]
The complement is a covering space on which inverse branches continue
analytically.  A \emph{branch atlas} records: regular basepoints, critical
values, local ramification indices, real monotonicity intervals, and branch
continuation paths.  This global object is more informative than one Taylor
series.

\begin{principle}[Endpoint saturation]\label{pr:endpoint-saturation}
If a monotone level map approaches a finite limit exponentially,
\(F(z)=L(1-c\rho^z+O(\rho^{2z}))\) with \(0<\rho<1\), then its inverse has a
logarithmic singularity:
\[
 z=\frac{\log(1-F/L)-\log c}{\log\rho}+O(1-F/L).
\]
If instead \(\log F(z)\sim Cz^2\), then the inverse scale is
\(z\sim\sqrt{\log Y/C}\).  Both mechanisms occur repeatedly below.
\end{principle}

\chapter{Finite \texorpdfstring{$q$}{q}-Pochhammer inverse in the parameter \texorpdfstring{$a$}{a}}

Throughout this chapter
\[
 P_n(a;q)\defeq(a;q)_n=\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}(1-aq^j),\qquad n\ge1.
\]
Unless explicitly specialized, \(q\ne0\).

\section{Low orders and exact radicals}

For \(n=1\), the inverse is \(a=1-Y\).  For \(n=2\),
\[
 Y=(1-a)(1-aq)=1-(1+q)a+qa^2,
\]
so, for \(q\ne0\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:n2-a-inverse}
 a=\frac{1+q\pm\sqrt{(1-q)^2+4qY}}{2q}.
\end{equation}
The discriminant identifies the unique finite critical value
\(Y_c=-(1-q)^2/(4q)\).  Formula \eqref{eq:n2-a-inverse} is the simplest
model of the general two-sheeted branching at a real critical point.

\section{The principal branch at \texorpdfstring{$a=0$}{a=0}}

The finite \(q\)-binomial theorem gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:finite-qbinomial-a}
 (a;q)_n=\sum_{r=0}^{n}(-1)^r q^{\binom r2}\qbinom nr a^r.
\end{equation}
Set
\[
 A=[n]_q,\quad B=q\qbinom n2,\quad C=q^3\qbinom n3,
 \quad D=q^6\qbinom n4,\quad E=q^{10}\qbinom n5.
\]

\begin{theorem}[Fifth-order inverse at \(a=0\)]\label{thm:finite-a-zero-inverse}
Let \(\delta=1-Y\) and assume \(A\ne0\).  The inverse branch satisfying
\(a(1)=0\) is
\begin{align}
 a={}&\frac{\delta}{A}+\frac{B}{A^3}\delta^2
 +\left(\frac{2B^2}{A^5}-\frac{C}{A^4}\right)\delta^3\notag\\
 &+\left(\frac{5B^3}{A^7}-\frac{5BC}{A^6}+\frac{D}{A^5}\right)\delta^4\notag\\
 &+\left(\frac{14B^4}{A^9}-\frac{21B^2C}{A^8}
 +\frac{6BD+3C^2}{A^7}-\frac{E}{A^6}\right)\delta^5
 +O(\delta^6).
\label{eq:finite-a-zero-inverse}
\end{align}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Equation \eqref{eq:finite-qbinomial-a} gives
\(\delta=Aa-Ba^2+Ca^3-Da^4+Ea^5+O(a^6)\).  Substitute
\(c_1=A,c_2=-B,c_3=C,c_4=-D,c_5=E\) in
\eqref{eq:universal-reversion-five}.
\end{proof}

The failure condition \(A=[n]_q=0\) is itself informative.  It occurs when
\(q\ne1\) is an \(n\)-th root of unity.  The regular Taylor inverse then
changes to a Puiseux inverse, analyzed in \cref{sec:root-unity-a}.

\section{Inverse branches at the simple zeros}

For generic \(q\), the zeros are
\[
 a_j=q^{-j},\qquad 0\le j<n.
\]

\begin{proposition}[Derivative at a zero]\label{prop:finite-root-derivative}
If the numbers \(q^{-j}\) are distinct, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:finite-root-derivative}
 P_n'(q^{-j};q)=(-1)^{j+1}q^{-j(j-1)/2}(q;q)_j(q;q)_{n-1-j}.
\end{equation}
Consequently, the inverse branch through \((Y,a)=(0,q^{-j})\) begins
\begin{align}
 a={}&q^{-j}
 +\frac{(-1)^{j+1}q^{j(j-1)/2}}{(q;q)_j(q;q)_{n-1-j}}Y\notag\\
 &+\frac{L_j}{P_n'(q^{-j};q)^2}Y^2+O(Y^3),
\label{eq:finite-root-inverse}
\end{align}
where
\[
 L_j=\sum_{\substack{0\le r<n\\r\ne j}}
 \frac{q^r}{1-q^{r-j}}.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Differentiate the vanishing factor \(1-aq^j\) and evaluate all remaining
factors at \(a=q^{-j}\).  For \(r<j\), use
\(1-q^{r-j}=-q^{r-j}(1-q^{j-r})\); the product of these powers is
\(q^{-j(j+1)/2}\).  This gives \eqref{eq:finite-root-derivative}.
Writing \(P=p_1h+p_2h^2/2+\cdots\), \(h=a-q^{-j}\), gives
\(h=Y/p_1-p_2Y^2/(2p_1^3)+\cdots\).  If the vanishing factor is removed,
logarithmic differentiation shows \(p_2/p_1=-2L_j\).
\end{proof}

\section{Complete real branch geometry}\label{sec:real-a-geometry}

\begin{theorem}[Real branch atlas]\label{thm:real-a-atlas}
Assume \(q>0\), \(q\ne1\), and order the positive zeros increasingly as
\(r_0<r_1<\cdots<r_{n-1}\).  Then:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
\item \(P_n\) is strictly decreasing and positive on \(( -\infty,r_0)\),
      mapping that interval bijectively onto \((0,\infty)\).
\item Every gap \((r_j,r_{j+1})\) contains exactly one critical point
      \(c_j\), and it is simple: \(P_n''(c_j)\ne0\).
\item There is no critical point in \((r_{n-1},\infty)\).
\item Every finite real branch collision occurs at one of the values
      \(P_n(c_j)\) and has square-root form.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Away from the roots,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:log-derivative-a-roots}
 \frac{P_n'(a)}{P_n(a)}=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac1{a-r_j},
 \qquad
 \frac{d}{da}\frac{P_n'}{P_n}=-\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac1{(a-r_j)^2}<0.
\end{equation}
On \(( -\infty,r_0)\), every summand in the first expression is negative,
so \(P_n'<0\); the endpoint limits are \(+\infty\) and \(0\).  In a gap,
the logarithmic derivative decreases continuously from \(+\infty\) to
\(-\infty\), giving one zero.  At that zero,
\(P_n''/P_n=(P_n'/P_n)'<0\), so the critical point is simple.  To the right
of the last root all summands are positive.  The final assertion follows
from \cref{thm:puiseux-normal}.
\end{proof}

\section{Roots of unity and exact ramification}\label{sec:root-unity-a}

\begin{theorem}[Root-of-unity factorization]\label{thm:root-unity-factorization}
Let \(\zeta\) be a primitive \(d\)-th root of unity and write
\(n=md+r\), \(0\le r<d\).  Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:root-unity-factorization}
 (a;\zeta)_n=(1-a^d)^m(a;\zeta)_r.
\end{equation}
If \(r=0\), the inverse at \(a=0,Y=1\) is exactly
\begin{equation}\label{eq:root-unity-exact-inverse}
 a=\omega\bigl(1-Y^{1/m}\bigr)^{1/d},\qquad \omega^d=1.
\end{equation}
If \(r>0\), the branch at \(a=0\) is regular because
\[
 \partial_a(a;\zeta)_n\big|_{a=0}
 =-\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\zeta^j=-\frac{1-\zeta^r}{1-\zeta}\ne0.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Group the product into \(m\) complete residue blocks modulo \(d\), using
\(\prod_{j=0}^{d-1}(1-a\zeta^j)=1-a^d\), followed by the residual block.
When \(r=0\), solve \((1-a^d)^m=Y\) directly.  The derivative formula for
\(r>0\) follows by differentiating at the origin.
\end{proof}

At \(q=-1\), this specializes to
\[
 (a;-1)_{2m}=(1-a^2)^m,
 \qquad
 (a;-1)_{2m+1}=(1-a)(1-a^2)^m.
\]
Thus even order produces an exact square-root ramification at \(a=0\), while
odd order remains locally univalent there.

\section{Inverse at \texorpdfstring{$a=\infty$}{a=infinity}}

Let \(N=\binom n2\),
\(A_n=(-1)^nq^N\), and define
\[
 e_1=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}q^{-j},\qquad
 e_2=\sum_{0\le r<s<n}q^{-r-s}.
\]
Factoring every term gives
\[
 P_n(a;q)=A_na^n\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}(1-q^{-j}a^{-1}).
\]

\begin{proposition}[Laurent--Puiseux inverse at infinity]
Choose a branch
\(Z=(Y/A_n)^{1/n}\) such that \(Z/a\to1\).  Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:a-infinity-inverse}
 a=Z+\frac{e_1}{n}
 +\left(\frac{(n-1)e_1^2}{2n^2}-\frac{e_2}{n}\right)Z^{-1}
 +O(Z^{-2}).
\end{equation}
There are \(n\) branches at infinity, one for each \(n\)-th root.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Raise
\(\prod_j(1-q^{-j}/a)\) to the power \(1/n\), expand to second order in
\(1/a\), and revert \(Z=a+s_1+s_2/a+\cdots\).
\end{proof}

\section{Cross-expansions of \texorpdfstring{$a(Y;q)$}{a(Y;q)} in the base}

Fix a level \(Y\) and a branch \(a_0=1-Y\) at \(q=0\).  Direct implicit
expansion yields, for \(n\ge5\),
\begin{align}
 a(Y;q)={}&a_0+a_0(a_0-1)q+2a_0^2(a_0-1)q^2\notag\\
 &+a_0^2(a_0-1)(5a_0-2)q^3\notag\\
 &+a_0^2(a_0-1)(2a_0-1)(7a_0-2)q^4+O(q^5).
\label{eq:a-cross-q0}
\end{align}
For smaller \(n\), terms are retained only until a missing factor
\((1-aq^j)\) first contributes.

Near \(q=1\), put \(q=\e^{-t}\) and choose an \(n\)-th root
\(r=Y^{1/n}\), so \(a_0=1-r\).  If \(r\ne0\), then
\begin{align}
 a(Y;\e^{-t})={}&a_0+\frac{n-1}{2}a_0t\notag\\
 &+\frac{a_0(n-1)\{2n-4-3a_0(n-1)\}}
 {24(1-a_0)}t^2+O(t^3).
\label{eq:a-cross-q1}
\end{align}
This follows either from \cref{prop:sensitivity} or from the logarithmic
expansion in the next chapter.

\chapter{Finite \texorpdfstring{$q$}{q}-Pochhammer inverse in the base \texorpdfstring{$q$}{q}}

Fix \(a\) and \(n\).  As a function of \(q\), \(P_n(a;q)\) is a polynomial
of degree \(N=\binom n2\) when \(a(1-a)\ne0\).  The generic level equation
therefore has \(N\) complex branches.  The purpose of this chapter is to
choose the correct coordinates at \(0,1,\pm\infty\), roots, and critical
points.

\section{Derivatives, regular points, and the discriminant}

For a nonzero product,
\begin{align}
 \partial_q\log P_n(a;q)
 &=-\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{ajq^{j-1}}{1-aq^j},
 \label{eq:q-log-derivative}\\
 \partial_q^2\log P_n(a;q)
 &=-\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}
 \left(\frac{aj(j-1)q^{j-2}}{1-aq^j}
 +\frac{a^2j^2q^{2j-2}}{(1-aq^j)^2}\right).
 \label{eq:q-log-second}
\end{align}
At any base point \(q_0\) where \(P_q(a;q_0)\ne0\),
\cref{thm:analytic-inverse} gives an ordinary Taylor inverse.  The branch
points are the critical values
\[
 Y_c=P_n(a;q_c),\qquad P_q(a;q_c)=0,
\]
and the multiplicity is the order of the first nonzero \(q\)-derivative.
Unlike the \(a\)-geometry in \cref{thm:real-a-atlas}, the real critical-point
pattern in \(q\) need not be interlacing or uniform because the exponents
\(j\) enter asymmetrically.

\section{Expansion and inversion at \texorpdfstring{$q=0$}{q=0}}

The factor independent of \(q\) may be removed:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qzero-factor}
 \frac{P_n(a;q)}{1-a}=(aq;q)_{n-1}
 =\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}(1-aq^j).
\end{equation}
Define
\[
 \eta=1-\frac{Y}{1-a}.
\]
The coefficients of \(\eta(q)\) count subsets of
\(\{1,\ldots,n-1\}\) by their sum, weighted by powers of \(a\).  In
particular, for \(n\ge6\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eta-qzero}
 \eta=aq+aq^2+(a-a^2)q^3+(a-a^2)q^4+(a-2a^2)q^5+O(q^6).
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Fifth-order inverse at \(q=0\)]\label{thm:finite-qzero-inverse}
Assume \(a\ne0,1\) and \(n\ge6\).  Then the branch satisfying \(q=0\) at
\(Y=1-a\) is
\begin{align}
 q={}&\frac{\eta}{a}-\frac{\eta^2}{a^2}
 +\frac{a+1}{a^3}\eta^3-\frac{4a+1}{a^4}\eta^4\notag\\
 &+\frac{3a^2+11a+1}{a^5}\eta^5+O(\eta^6).
\label{eq:finite-qzero-inverse}
\end{align}
The same formula holds for \((a;q)_\infty\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Expand \eqref{eq:qzero-factor}.  Through degree five, all contributing
indices are at most five, so the coefficients stabilize once \(n-1\ge5\).
Revert \eqref{eq:eta-qzero} with
\eqref{eq:universal-reversion-five}.  The infinite product has the same
finite jet because factors with \(j\ge6\) begin beyond the displayed order.
\end{proof}

For arbitrary \(n\), the exact coefficient engine is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qzero-subset-coeff}
 [q^m]\frac{P_n(a;q)}{1-a}
 =\sum_{r\ge0}(-a)^r
 \#\{S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n-1\}:\abs S=r,\ \sum_{s\in S}s=m\}.
\end{equation}
Inserting these coefficients into \eqref{eq:universal-reversion-five}
produces the short-order corrections automatically.

\section{All-order logarithmic expansion and inverse at \texorpdfstring{$q=1$}{q=1}}

Put \(q=\e^{-t}\) and choose a branch of the logarithm.  For fixed \(a\)
with \(a\notin[1,\infty)\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:single-factor-taylor}
 \log(1-a\e^{-x})
 =\log(1-a)+\sum_{r\ge1}\frac{(-1)^{r+1}}{r!}
 \Li_{1-r}(a)x^r.
\end{equation}
Let
\[
 S_r(n)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}j^r,
 \qquad
 K_r(a,n)=(-1)^{r+1}\Li_{1-r}(a)S_r(n).
\]

\begin{theorem}[Logarithmic \(q=1\) inverse]\label{thm:finite-qone-inverse}
Let
\[
 u=\log\frac{Y}{(1-a)^n}.
\]
If \(K_1\ne0\), then the branch \(q\to1\) is obtained from
\begin{equation}\label{eq:t-u-allorders}
 u=\sum_{r\ge1}\frac{K_r}{r!}t^r,
 \qquad q=\e^{-t},
\end{equation}
and begins
\begin{align}
 t={}&\frac{u}{K_1}-\frac{K_2}{2K_1^3}u^2
 +\frac{3K_2^2-K_1K_3}{6K_1^5}u^3\notag\\
 &+\left(-\frac{K_4}{24K_1^5}
 +\frac{5K_2K_3}{12K_1^6}
 -\frac{5K_2^3}{8K_1^7}\right)u^4+O(u^5).
\label{eq:finite-qone-t-inverse}
\end{align}
For \(n\ge2\) and \(a\ne0,1\),
\[
 K_1=\frac{a}{1-a}\binom n2\ne0.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Substitute \(x=jt\) in \eqref{eq:single-factor-taylor} and sum over
\(0\le j<n\), proving \eqref{eq:t-u-allorders}.  Reversion gives
\eqref{eq:finite-qone-t-inverse}.  The expression for \(K_1\) uses
\(\Li_0(a)=a/(1-a)\) and \(S_1(n)=\binom n2\).
\end{proof}

The use of \(t=-\log q\), rather than \(q-1\), is not cosmetic.  It makes
the coefficients polylogarithmic power sums, separates the base geometry
from exponent combinatorics, and extends directly to the infinite product.

\section{A critical scaled classical limit}

The forward classical limit
\[
 G_n(x,t)=\frac{(\e^{-xt};\e^{-t})_n}{(1-\e^{-t})^n}\longrightarrow(x)_n
\]
contains a previously hidden inverse transition.  The elementary expansion
\[
 \log\frac{1-\e^{-z t}}{zt}
 =-\frac{zt}{2}+\sum_{m\ge1}
 \frac{B_{2m}}{2m(2m)!}z^{2m}t^{2m}
\]
gives
\begin{align}
 \log\frac{G_n(x,t)}{(x)_n}
 ={}&-\frac t2\left(\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}(x+j)-n\right)\notag\\
 &+\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{B_{2m}t^{2m}}{2m(2m)!}
 \left(\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}(x+j)^{2m}-n\right).
\label{eq:scaled-poch-log}
\end{align}

\begin{proposition}[Square-root inverse on the critical locus]\label{prop:scaled-critical-locus}
Assume \(n\ge2\), \((x)_n\ne0\), and
\[
 x=\frac{3-n}{2}.
\]
Then the coefficient of \(t\) in \eqref{eq:scaled-poch-log} vanishes, while
that of \(t^2\) equals
\[
 \frac{n(n-1)(n+1)}{288}>0.
\]
Therefore inversion of \(G_n(x,t)=Y\) at \(t=0\) has two square-root
branches in \(\log(Y/(x)_n)\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The linear coefficient is zero exactly when
\(nx+n(n-1)/2-n=0\).  On this locus,
\[
 \sum_{j=0}^{n-1}(x+j)^2-n=\frac{n(n-1)(n+1)}{12},
\]
and multiplication by \(B_2/(2\cdot2!)=1/24\) gives the stated number.
Apply \cref{thm:puiseux-normal}.
\end{proof}

For odd \(n\ge3\), the critical value of \(x\) is a nonpositive integer and
one factor vanishes identically; the nontrivial normalized transition is
therefore the even-\(n\) case.  This is an example of a codimension-one
parameter locus where a regular \(q=1\) inverse changes universality class.

\section{Laurent--Puiseux inversion at \texorpdfstring{$q=\pm\infty$}{q=+-infinity}}

Assume \(n\ge2\), \(a(1-a)\ne0\), and set
\[
 N=\binom n2,\qquad C=(1-a)(-a)^{n-1}.
\]
Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:q-infinity-factor}
 P_n(a;q)=Cq^N H(q^{-1}),\qquad
 H(z)=\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}(1-a^{-1}z^j).
\end{equation}
Write \(h_1=-a^{-1}\), and
\[
 h_2=\begin{cases}-a^{-1},&n\ge3,\\0,&n=2.\end{cases}
\]

\begin{proposition}[Inverse at either infinite end]\label{prop:finite-q-infinity}
Choose a branch
\[
 Z=\left(\frac YC\right)^{1/N}
\]
for which \(Z/q\to1\).  Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:finite-q-infinity-inverse}
 q=Z+\frac1{Na}
 -\left(\frac{h_2}{N}+\frac1{2N}\left(\frac1N-1\right)h_1^2\right)Z^{-1}
 +O(Z^{-2}).
\end{equation}
The same formula describes \(q\to+\infty\), \(q\to-\infty\), and complex
sectors; only the chosen \(N\)-th root changes.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
From \eqref{eq:q-infinity-factor},
\(Z=qH(q^{-1})^{1/N}=q+s_1+s_2/q+\cdots\), where
\[
 s_1=\frac{h_1}{N},\qquad
 s_2=\frac{h_2}{N}+\frac1{2N}\left(\frac1N-1\right)h_1^2.
\]
Reversion gives \(q=Z-s_1-s_2/Z+\cdots\), and
\(-s_1=1/(Na)\).
\end{proof}

On the real line, parity determines the endpoints.  If \(N\) is even, the
same sign of \(Y/C\) is reached at both \(+\infty\) and \(-\infty\); if
\(N\) is odd, the signs are opposite.  Thus a large real level can have one
or two real asymptotic branches depending on \(C\), \(N\), and the sign of
\(Y\).

\section{Zeros in the base and colliding factors}

Suppose \(a q_0^j=1\) for an index \(1\le j<n\), and no other factor
vanishes.  Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:q-root-derivative}
 \partial_qP_n(a;q_0)
 =-j q_0^{-1}\prod_{\substack{0\le r<n\\r\ne j}}(1-q_0^{r-j}).
\end{equation}
Therefore
\[
 q=q_0+\frac{Y}{\partial_qP_n(a;q_0)}+O(Y^2).
\]
If exactly \(m\) indices satisfy \(a q_0^j=1\), then the zero has
multiplicity \(m\) and the inverse has \(m\)-th-root ramification.  Such
collisions occur precisely when ratios of relevant exponents are compatible
with a root of unity.  They are the base-variable analogue of
\cref{thm:root-unity-factorization}.

At a nonzero value \(P_n(a;-1)\), the point \(q=-1\) is ordinarily regular;
its first inverse coefficient is obtained by substituting \(-1\) in
\eqref{eq:q-log-derivative}.  Exceptional values of \(a\) for which this
logarithmic derivative vanishes create square-root branches.  The Gaussian
coefficient has a much more rigid \(q=-1\) theory, developed in
\cref{chap:gaussian-inverse}.

\chapter{The infinite \texorpdfstring{$q$}{q}-Pochhammer inverse}

For \(0<\abs q<1\), define
\[
 P_\infty(a;q)=(a;q)_\infty=\prod_{j=0}^\infty(1-aq^j).
\]
The forward product is entire in \(a\), while its behavior in \(q\) becomes
singular on the unit circle.  Both facts are reflected sharply in its
inverse branches.

\section{Inverse in \texorpdfstring{$a$}{a}: origin, zeros, and real branches}

The \(q\)-binomial theorem gives
\[
 (a;q)_\infty=\sum_{r\ge0}\frac{(-1)^r q^{\binom r2}}{(q;q)_r}a^r.
\]
Taking the limit \(n\to\infty\) in \cref{thm:finite-a-zero-inverse} gives a
convergent local inverse at \(Y=1\).  Its first four terms simplify to
\begin{align}
 a={}&(1-q)\delta
 +\frac{q(1-q)}{1+q}\delta^2\notag\\
 &+\frac{q^2(1-q)(q^2+q+2)}{(1+q)^2(1+q+q^2)}\delta^3\notag\\
 &+\frac{q^3(1-q)(q^4+q^3+5q^2+5)}
 {(1+q)^3(1+q^2)(1+q+q^2)}\delta^4+O(\delta^5),
\label{eq:infinite-a-origin-inverse}
\end{align}
where \(\delta=1-Y\).

The zeros are again \(a_j=q^{-j}\), now for all \(j\ge0\).  They are simple
when \(q\) is not a root of unity, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:infinite-root-derivative}
 \partial_a(a;q)_\infty\big|_{a=q^{-j}}
 =(-1)^{j+1}q^{-j(j-1)/2}(q;q)_j(q;q)_\infty.
\end{equation}
Thus the zero-branch expansion in \cref{prop:finite-root-derivative} remains
valid after replacing \((q;q)_{n-1-j}\) by \((q;q)_\infty\).

\begin{theorem}[Real branch atlas for the entire product]\label{thm:infinite-a-real-atlas}
Let \(0<q<1\).  On \(( -\infty,1)\), \((a;q)_\infty\) is positive and
strictly decreasing from \(+\infty\) to \(0\), so it has a unique positive
level inverse there.  Between every pair \(q^{-j}<q^{-(j+1)}\) there is
exactly one simple critical point, and the associated inverse branches meet
with square-root ramification.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The normally convergent logarithmic derivative is
\[
 \partial_a\log(a;q)_\infty
 =\sum_{j\ge0}\frac1{a-q^{-j}},
\]
and its derivative is the strictly negative sum of squares.  The proof of
\cref{thm:real-a-atlas} passes to the infinite product on every compact
interval avoiding the zeros.  As \(a=-x\to-\infty\), infinitely many factors
exceed one and the product diverges; a quantitative form follows next.
\end{proof}

\section{Large negative \texorpdfstring{$a$}{a} and log-periodic inversion}

Set \(q=\e^{-\tau}\), \(\tau>0\), and \(a=-x\), \(x>0\).  Write uniquely
\[
 x=q^{-m}r,\qquad m\in\N_0,\qquad 1\le r<q^{-1},
\]
or equivalently \(L=\log x=m\tau+\rho\), \(0\le\rho<\tau\).  Splitting the
product at \(j=m\) gives the exact identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:negative-a-exact-split}
 (-x;q)_\infty
 =r^m q^{-m(m+1)/2}(-q/r;q)_m(-r;q)_\infty.
\end{equation}
Define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Psi-definition}
 \Psi_q(\rho)=
 \log\!\left[(-\e^\rho;q)_\infty(-q\e^{-\rho};q)_\infty\right]
 -\frac\rho2-\frac{\rho^2}{2\tau}.
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Theta-modulated growth]\label{thm:log-periodic-growth}
The function \(\Psi_q\) extends to a smooth \(\tau\)-periodic function, and
as \(L\to+\infty\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:negative-a-log-growth}
 \log(-\e^L;q)_\infty
 =\frac{L^2}{2\tau}+\frac L2+\Psi_q(L\bmod\tau)+O(\e^{-L}).
\end{equation}
Consequently, if \(Y=(-x;q)_\infty\to\infty\), put
\[
 R=\sqrt{2\tau\log Y},\qquad
 \rho_0=(R-\tau/2)\bmod\tau.
\]
Then the inverse satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:negative-a-inverse}
 \log x
 =R-\frac\tau2
 +\frac{\tau\bigl(\tau/8-\Psi_q(\rho_0)\bigr)}{R}
 +O\bigl((\log Y)^{-1}\bigr).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
In \eqref{eq:negative-a-exact-split}, replace the finite product
\((-q/r;q)_m\) by \((-q/r;q)_\infty\); the omitted tail is
\(1+O(q^m)=1+O(\e^{-L})\).  Since \(m=(L-\rho)/\tau\), elementary algebra
gives
\[
 m\rho+\frac{\tau m(m+1)}2
 =\frac{L^2}{2\tau}+\frac L2-\frac{\rho^2}{2\tau}-\frac\rho2.
\]
This proves \eqref{eq:negative-a-log-growth}.  Under
\(\rho\mapsto\rho+\tau\), the product inside the logarithm changes by the
factor \(\e^{\rho+\tau}\); the two subtracted quadratic terms change by the
same amount, proving periodicity.  Complete the square in
\eqref{eq:negative-a-log-growth} and expand the positive square root.  The
phase correction is only \(O(R^{-1})\), so replacing the exact phase by
\(\rho_0\) changes the displayed term by \(O(R^{-2})\).
\end{proof}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{log_periodic_correction.pdf}
\caption{The bounded theta-type function \(\Psi_q\) produces a genuine
log-periodic modulation in the inverse of \((-x;q)_\infty\).  It is invisible
in a purely smooth approximation in powers of \(1/\log x\).}
\label{fig:log-periodic}
\end{figure}

This phenomenon is structurally close to periodic fluctuations in digital
and divide-and-conquer asymptotics.  It also supplies a new bridge to the
exponent-sequence themes in the Fabius--Rvachev corpus: a geometric scale
induces periodicity after taking a logarithm.

\section{Radial approach to a root of unity}

The Lambert-series identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lambert-log-poch}
 \log(a;q)_\infty=-\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{a^m}{m(1-q^m)}
\end{equation}
permits a uniform treatment of \(q=1,-1\), and every root of unity.  Let
\(\zeta\) be a primitive \(d\)-th root and put
\(q=\zeta\e^{-t}\), \(t\to0^+\).  Assume \(\abs a<1\), so all rearrangements
are absolutely convergent.

\begin{theorem}[Radial root-of-unity expansion]\label{thm:radial-root-unity}
Define
\begin{align}
 A_d(a)&=\frac1{d^2}\Li_2(a^d),\label{eq:Ad}\\
 C_{d,\zeta}(a)
 &=\frac1{2d}\log(1-a^d)
 -\sum_{\substack{m\ge1\\d\nmid m}}
 \frac{a^m}{m(1-\zeta^m)},\label{eq:Cd}\\
 T_{d,\zeta}(a)
 &=-\frac{a^d}{12(1-a^d)}
 +\sum_{\substack{m\ge1\\d\nmid m}}
 \frac{a^m\zeta^m}{(1-\zeta^m)^2}.
\label{eq:Td}
\end{align}
Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:root-unity-radial-expansion}
 \log(a;\zeta\e^{-t})_\infty
 =-\frac{A_d(a)}{t}+C_{d,\zeta}(a)
 +T_{d,\zeta}(a)t+O(t^2).
\end{equation}
If \(A_d(a)\ne0\), set
\(X=-\Log Y+C_{d,\zeta}(a)\) and
\(c_{d,\zeta}=-T_{d,\zeta}\).  The radial inverse begins
\begin{equation}\label{eq:root-unity-radial-inverse}
 t=\frac{A_d}{X}+\frac{A_d^2c_{d,\zeta}}{X^3}+O(X^{-4}).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Split \eqref{eq:lambert-log-poch} into multiples \(m=d\ell\) and
nonmultiples.  For the multiples, use
\[
 \frac1{1-\e^{-d\ell t}}
 =\frac1{d\ell t}+\frac12+\frac{d\ell t}{12}+O(t^3).
\]
The three resulting sums are respectively
\(-\Li_2(a^d)/(d^2t)\), \((2d)^{-1}\log(1-a^d)\), and
\(-a^dt/[12(1-a^d)]\).  For \(d\nmid m\), expand the denominator
analytically at \(t=0\); its first derivative is
\(-m\zeta^m/(1-\zeta^m)^2\), producing the second sums in
\eqref{eq:Cd}--\eqref{eq:Td}.  Reversion of
\(X=A_d/t+c_{d,\zeta}t+O(t^2)\) proves
\eqref{eq:root-unity-radial-inverse}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The specialization \texorpdfstring{$q\to1^-$}{q to 1}}

For \(d=1\), \(\zeta=1\), the theorem gives the familiar
polylogarithmic expansion, here extended far enough to invert:
\begin{align}
 \log(a;\e^{-t})_\infty
 ={}&-\frac{\Li_2(a)}t+\frac12\log(1-a)
 -\frac{a}{12(1-a)}t\notag\\
 &+\frac{a(1+a)}{720(1-a)^3}t^3+O(t^5).
\label{eq:qone-infinite-forward}
\end{align}
Let
\[
 A=\Li_2(a),\quad c=\frac{a}{12(1-a)},\quad
 d=\frac{a(1+a)}{720(1-a)^3},\quad
 X=-\log Y+\frac12\log(1-a).
\]
Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qone-infinite-inverse}
 t=\frac AX+\frac{A^2c}{X^3}
 +\frac{2A^3c^2-A^4d}{X^5}+O(X^{-7}),
 \qquad q=\e^{-t}.
\end{equation}
For \(0<a<1\), this is a real inverse as \(Y\downarrow0\).

\subsection{The specialization \texorpdfstring{$q\to-1$}{q to -1}}

For \(d=2\), \(\zeta=-1\), the constants simplify:
\begin{align}
 \log(a;-\e^{-t})_\infty
 ={}&-\frac{\Li_2(a^2)}{4t}+\frac12\log(1-a)
 -\frac{a(a+3)}{12(1-a^2)}t\notag\\
 &+\frac{a(15a^4+4a^3+90a^2+4a+15)}
 {720(1-a^2)^3}t^3+O(t^5).
\label{eq:qminusone-forward}
\end{align}
The odd powers arise from separating even and odd Lambert indices.  With
\[
 A_-=\frac14\Li_2(a^2),\quad
 c_-=\frac{a(a+3)}{12(1-a^2)},\quad
 d_-=\frac{a(15a^4+4a^3+90a^2+4a+15)}{720(1-a^2)^3},
\]
and the same \(X=-\log Y+\tfrac12\log(1-a)\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qminusone-inverse}
 t=\frac{A_-}{X}+\frac{A_-^2c_-}{X^3}
 +\frac{2A_-^3c_-^2-A_-^4d_-}{X^5}+O(X^{-7}),
 \qquad q=-\e^{-t}.
\end{equation}
This is the first nontrivial real root-of-unity inverse treated beyond
\(q=1\) in the present atlas.

\section{Expansion at \texorpdfstring{$q=0$}{q=0}}

The local expansion in \cref{thm:finite-qzero-inverse} applies without
change.  It is often preferable to use the exact identity
\[
 \frac{(a;q)_\infty}{1-a}=(aq;q)_\infty
\]
and then evaluate the inverse series in the bounded variable
\(\eta=1-Y/(1-a)\).  This avoids cancellation when \(a\) is close to one.

\section{Complex branch remarks}

For a complex target, \(X=-\Log Y+C\) in
\eqref{eq:root-unity-radial-inverse} depends on the logarithm branch.  Each
addition of \(2\pi\ii\) to \(\Log Y\) produces a different asymptotic sheet.
The root-of-unity point is therefore an essential accumulation point of
inverse branches, not an isolated algebraic branch point.  Sectorial error
bounds can be obtained by keeping \(t\) in a cone where
\(\Re t>0\) and using the same Lambert-series splitting.

\chapter{Inverse with respect to continuous order and geometric step}

\section{Complex order}

Fix a branch of \(\Log q\), with \(0<\abs q<1\), and define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:complex-order-definition}
 (a;q)_\alpha=\frac{(a;q)_\infty}{(aq^\alpha;q)_\infty},
 \qquad q^\alpha=\e^{\alpha\Log q}.
\end{equation}
This agrees with the finite product when \(\alpha\in\N_0\).  On a
simply connected logarithmic domain satisfying
\(\abs a<1\) and \(\abs{aq^\alpha}<1\), the convergent Lambert
representation is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:complex-order-lambert}
 \log(a;q)_\alpha
 =-\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{a^m(1-q^{m\alpha})}{m(1-q^m)}.
\end{equation}
Beyond that domain, the identity and the inverse formulae below are understood
by meromorphic continuation on any simply connected region avoiding zeros and
poles and carrying compatible logarithm branches.

\begin{theorem}[Local inverse in the order]\label{thm:order-local-inverse}
Fix \(\alpha_0\) away from the poles of \((a;q)_\alpha\), assume
\(Y_0=(a;q)_{\alpha_0}\ne0\) and \(\abs{a q^{\alpha_0}}<1\) so that the
Lambert cumulants below converge, put \(s=\alpha-\alpha_0\), and
\[
 u=\Log(Y/Y_0).
\]
Define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lambda-order}
 \lambda_r=(\Log q)^r\sum_{m\ge1}
 \frac{m^{r-1}a^m q^{m\alpha_0}}{1-q^m},\qquad r\ge1.
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:order-forward-local}
 u=\sum_{r\ge1}\frac{\lambda_r}{r!}s^r.
\end{equation}
If \(\lambda_1\ne0\), the inverse branch is
\begin{align}
 s={}&\frac{u}{\lambda_1}
 -\frac{\lambda_2}{2\lambda_1^3}u^2
 +\frac{3\lambda_2^2-\lambda_1\lambda_3}{6\lambda_1^5}u^3\notag\\
 &+\left(-\frac{\lambda_4}{24\lambda_1^5}
 +\frac{5\lambda_2\lambda_3}{12\lambda_1^6}
 -\frac{5\lambda_2^3}{8\lambda_1^7}\right)u^4+O(u^5).
\label{eq:order-inverse-local}
\end{align}
If the first nonzero \(\lambda_r\) has index \(r=m>1\), the order inverse
has \(m\)-th-root Puiseux ramification.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Use the local quotient
\[
 \frac{(a;q)_{\alpha_0+s}}{(a;q)_{\alpha_0}}
 =\frac{(a q^{\alpha_0};q)_\infty}
 {(a q^{\alpha_0+s};q)_\infty}.
\]
Its logarithm is a normally convergent Lambert series under the stated
hypothesis.  Expanding \(q^{ms}=\exp(ms\Log q)\) and interchanging the
sums gives \eqref{eq:order-forward-local}.  Apply
\eqref{eq:universal-reversion-five}, or
\cref{thm:puiseux-normal} in the degenerate case.
\end{proof}

At \(\alpha_0=0\), provided \(\abs a<1\), \(Y_0=1\) and
\[
 \lambda_r=(\Log q)^r\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{m^{r-1}a^m}{1-q^m}.
\]
Thus the inverse order is governed by a hierarchy of Lambert series.  At a
positive integer \(N\), the same formula simply inserts \(q^{mN}\); the
series then converges rapidly whenever \(\abs{a q^N}<1\).

\section{The endpoint \texorpdfstring{$\alpha\to+\infty$}{alpha to infinity}}

Assume \(0<q<1\), \(a\ne0\), \((a;q)_\infty\ne0\), and let
\(\alpha\to+\infty\).  Put
\[
 b=aq^\alpha,
 \qquad R=\frac{Y}{(a;q)_\infty},
 \qquad r=R-1.
\]
Then
\[
 R=\frac1{(b;q)_\infty}=e_q(b)
 =1+\frac{b}{1-q}+\frac{b^2}{(1-q)(1-q^2)}+\cdots.
\]

\begin{proposition}[Logarithmic endpoint inverse]\label{prop:order-endpoint}
As \(R\to1\),
\begin{align}
 b={}&(1-q)r-\frac{1-q}{1+q}r^2\notag\\
 &+\frac{(1-q)(2q^2+q+1)}{(1+q)^2(1+q+q^2)}r^3+O(r^4),
\label{eq:b-from-r}
\end{align}
and therefore
\begin{align}
 \alpha=\frac1{\Log q}\bigg[&\Log\frac{(1-q)r}{a}
 -\frac{r}{1+q}\notag\\
 &+\frac{3q^2+q+1}{2(1+q)^2(1+q+q^2)}r^2+O(r^3)\bigg].
\label{eq:alpha-endpoint-inverse}
\end{align}
In particular, the inverse order diverges logarithmically as the level
approaches \((a;q)_\infty\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Revert the Taylor series of \(e_q(b)-1\), then use
\(\alpha=\Log(b/a)/\Log q\).  Expanding the logarithm of
\eqref{eq:b-from-r} gives \eqref{eq:alpha-endpoint-inverse}.
\end{proof}

The endpoint is saturating: a finite change in \(\alpha\) multiplies the
small parameter \(b\) by \(q\).  This is precisely the mechanism in
\cref{pr:endpoint-saturation}.

\section{Poles in the order variable}

The denominator in \eqref{eq:complex-order-definition} vanishes when
\[
 aq^{\alpha_0+j}=1,
 \qquad j\in\N_0.
\]
Suppose only one factor vanishes and the numerator does not.  If
\[
 D_j=\left.\frac{d}{d\alpha}(aq^\alpha;q)_\infty\right|_{\alpha=\alpha_0},
\]
then
\[
 (a;q)_\alpha=\frac{(a;q)_\infty}{D_j(\alpha-\alpha_0)}+O(1).
\]
Hence the inverse at large level is an ordinary reciprocal expansion,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:order-pole-inverse}
 \alpha=\alpha_0+\frac{(a;q)_\infty}{D_jY}+O(Y^{-2}).
\end{equation}
For a fixed base \(0<\abs q<1\), distinct denominator factors cannot
collide.  Under continuation of the base toward a root of unity, however,
several factors may coalesce; the pole order then increases and the inverse
scale becomes \(Y^{-1/m}\).  Thus the order variable exhibits the same
root-of-unity collision mechanism as the base variable, but at poles rather
than zeros.

\section{Inversion in the geometric step}

Consider the two-base symbol
\[
 F(\beta)=(a;q^\beta)_\alpha.
\]
Fixing a branch of \(\Log q\), set \(Q=q^\beta\).  Every inverse result in
the base \(Q\) immediately gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:beta-reduction}
 \beta=\frac{\Log Q}{\Log q}.
\end{equation}
This simple reduction is preferable to differentiating a nested exponential.
For direct sensitivity calculations,
\[
 \frac{d}{d\beta}\log(a;q^\beta)_n
 =-(\Log q)\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}
 \frac{ajq^{\beta j}}{1-aq^{\beta j}},
\]
which is the \(q\)-derivative formula
\eqref{eq:q-log-derivative} multiplied by the Jacobian
\(Q\Log q\).

More generally, for a signed product
\[
 \mathcal P(q)=\prod_{\nu=1}^{r}(a_\nu;q)_{\alpha_\nu}^{\sigma_\nu},
 \qquad \sigma_\nu\in\Z,
\]
all logarithmic cumulants add.  Thus the local inverse coefficients are
obtained by replacing every \(K_r\), \(\lambda_r\), or Lambert coefficient
by the corresponding signed sum.  This observation covers individual terms
of basic hypergeometric series, \(q\)-beta ratios, and many partition
products without a new inversion proof.

\chapter{Gaussian coefficients: inverse in the base}\label{chap:gaussian-inverse}

Let \(0<k<n\) and
\[
 G_{n,k}(q)=\qbinom nk
 =\prod_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1-q^{n-k+j}}{1-q^j}.
\]
It is a palindromic polynomial of degree
\[
 d=k(n-k),
\]
with nonnegative integral coefficients.  The coefficient of \(q^m\) counts
partitions of \(m\) inside a \(k\times(n-k)\) rectangle
\cite{AndrewsPartitions,StanleyEC1}.

\section{The positive real inverse is global}

\begin{theorem}[Unique positive-real Gaussian inverse]\label{thm:qbin-positive-global}
For \(0<k<n\), \(G_{n,k}(q)\) is strictly increasing on \((0,\infty)\),
with
\[
 G_{n,k}(0)=1,
 \qquad
 G_{n,k}(q)\to\infty\quad(q\to\infty).
\]
Consequently, every level \(Y>1\) has a unique positive real inverse
\(q=Q_{n,k}(Y)\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The polynomial has nonnegative coefficients and its coefficient of \(q\) is
one.  Hence its derivative is positive for every \(q>0\).  Its leading
coefficient is one and its degree is \(d>0\).
\end{proof}

This theorem supplies an unusually clean global branch.  Negative and
complex branches are organized by cyclotomic zeros and critical values.

\section{The partition reversion at \texorpdfstring{$q=0$}{q=0}}

Write
\[
 G_{n,k}(q)=1+q+c_2q^2+c_3q^3+c_4q^4+c_5q^5+\cdots,
\]
where \(c_j\) is the number of partitions of \(j\) fitting in the rectangle.

\begin{theorem}[Fifth-order Gaussian inverse at zero]\label{thm:qbin-qzero}
Let \(\delta=Y-1\).  Then the positive branch has
\begin{align}
 q={}&\delta-c_2\delta^2+(2c_2^2-c_3)\delta^3\notag\\
 &+(-5c_2^3+5c_2c_3-c_4)\delta^4\notag\\
 &+(14c_2^4-21c_2^2c_3+6c_2c_4+3c_3^2-c_5)\delta^5
 +O(\delta^6).
\label{eq:qbin-qzero-inverse}
\end{align}
If \(\min(k,n-k)\ge5\), then
\((c_2,c_3,c_4,c_5)=(2,3,5,7)\), and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qbin-qzero-stable}
 q=\delta-2\delta^2+5\delta^3-15\delta^4+52\delta^5+O(\delta^6).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Apply \eqref{eq:universal-reversion-five} with \(c_1=1\).  If both rectangle
dimensions are at least five, every partition of an integer at most five
fits, so the coefficients are the ordinary partition numbers
\(p(2),\ldots,p(5)\).
\end{proof}

The coefficients in \eqref{eq:qbin-qzero-stable} are themselves a natural
inverse-partition sequence.  Determining their asymptotics when the rectangle
is unbounded is a well-posed combinatorial problem discussed in
\cref{prob:inverse-partition-coefficients}.

\section{Expansion at \texorpdfstring{$q=1$}{q=1}}

Put \(q=\e^t\), and let
\[
 u=\log\frac{Y}{\binom nk}.
\]
From
\[
 \log\frac{\e^{zt}-1}{zt}
 =\frac{zt}{2}+\sum_{r\ge1}
 \frac{B_{2r}}{2r(2r)!}z^{2r}t^{2r},
\]
one obtains
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qbin-cumulant}
 u=\frac d2t+\sum_{r\ge1}\frac{B_{2r}t^{2r}}{2r(2r)!}
 \sum_{j=1}^{k}\left((n-k+j)^{2r}-j^{2r}\right).
\end{equation}
The absence of odd centered powers is the analytic form of palindromicity.

\begin{theorem}[Gaussian inverse at one]\label{thm:qbin-qone}
As \(Y\to\binom nk\),
\begin{align}
 t={}&\frac{2u}{d}-\frac{n+1}{3d^2}u^2
 +\frac{(n+1)^2}{9d^3}u^3+O(u^4),
\label{eq:qbin-t-one}\\
 q={}&1+\frac{2u}{d}+\frac{5-n}{3d^2}u^2
 +\frac{n^2-4n+7}{9d^3}u^3+O(u^4).
\label{eq:qbin-q-one}
\end{align}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The quadratic coefficient in \eqref{eq:qbin-cumulant} is
\[
 \frac1{24}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\bigl((n-k+j)^2-j^2\bigr)
 =\frac{d(n+1)}{24}.
\]
There is no cubic term.  Revert
\(u=(d/2)t+d(n+1)t^2/24+O(t^4)\), then expand
\(q=\e^t\).
\end{proof}

For \(0<q<1\), the theorem's variable \(t=\log q\) is negative.
If one prefers \(\tau=-\log q>0\), substitute \(t=-\tau\) in both
the forward and inverse series.  The formula itself is analytic across
\(q=1\).

\section{Reciprocity and the inverse at infinity}

Palindromicity is the exact identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qbin-reciprocity}
 G_{n,k}(q)=q^dG_{n,k}(q^{-1}).
\end{equation}
Let \(Z=Y^{1/d}\) be chosen so that \(Z/q\to1\).  If \(c_2=[q^2]G_{n,k}\),
then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qbin-infinity-inverse}
 q=Z-\frac1d
 -\left(\frac{c_2}{d}-\frac{d-1}{2d^2}\right)Z^{-1}
 +O(Z^{-2}).
\end{equation}
This is the Gaussian specialization of
\cref{prop:finite-q-infinity}.  The same branch formula works at
\(q=-\infty\) after choosing \(Z\sim q\).  If \(d\) is even, a large
positive level has both positive and negative asymptotic candidates; if
\(d\) is odd, the negative branch corresponds to a large negative level.

\section{The point \texorpdfstring{$q=-1$}{q=-1}}

The \(q\)-Lucas theorem or cyclotomic factorization gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qbin-minus-one-value}
 G_{n,k}(-1)=
 \begin{cases}
 0,&n=2m,\ k=2r+1,\\[2mm]
 \displaystyle\binom{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor},&\text{otherwise}.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Inverse branches at \(q=-1\)]\label{thm:qbin-minus-one-inverse}
Let \(d=k(n-k)\).
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
\item If \(n=2m\), \(k=2r+1\), then \(q=-1\) is a simple zero and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qbin-minus-one-zero-slope}
 G_{2m,2r+1}'(-1)=(m-r)\binom mr.
\end{equation}
Hence
\[
 q=-1+\frac{Y}{(m-r)\binom mr}+O(Y^2).
\]
\item Otherwise let \(Y_0=G_{n,k}(-1)\ne0\).  Then \(d\) is even and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qbin-minus-one-nonzero-slope}
 G_{n,k}'(-1)=-\frac d2Y_0.
\end{equation}
Therefore
\[
 q=-1-\frac{2(Y-Y_0)}{dY_0}+O((Y-Y_0)^2).
\]
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
In case (i), the factor \(\Phi_2(q)=q+1\) occurs exactly once.  Dividing the
product formula by \(q+1\) and taking the limit, or applying \(q\)-Lucas to
the quotient, gives \eqref{eq:qbin-minus-one-zero-slope}.  In case (ii),
differentiate reciprocity \eqref{eq:qbin-reciprocity}:
\[
 G'(q)=dq^{d-1}G(q^{-1})-q^{d-2}G'(q^{-1}).
\]
At \(q=-1\) and even \(d\), this becomes
\(G'=-dG-G'\), proving \eqref{eq:qbin-minus-one-nonzero-slope}.
\end{proof}

\section{General roots of unity}

The cyclotomic factorization is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qbin-cyclotomic}
 G_{n,k}(q)=\prod_{s\ge1}\Phi_s(q)^{e_s},
 \qquad
 e_s=\left\lfloor\frac ns\right\rfloor
 -\left\lfloor\frac ks\right\rfloor
 -\left\lfloor\frac{n-k}s\right\rfloor\in\{0,1\}.
\end{equation}
The exponent \(e_s\) is a carry indicator: it equals one precisely when
adding the residues of \(k\) and \(n-k\) modulo \(s\) crosses \(s\).

\begin{corollary}[Cyclotomic inverse atlas]\label{cor:cyclotomic-atlas}
Let \(\zeta\) be a primitive \(s\)-th root of unity.
If \(e_s=1\), then \(G_{n,k}\) has a simple zero at \(\zeta\), and the
inverse at \(Y=0\) is analytic with slope
\[
 \frac1{G_{n,k}'(\zeta)}
 =\frac1{\Phi_s'(\zeta)
 \prod_{r\ne s}\Phi_r(\zeta)^{e_r}}.
\]
If \(e_s=0\), the value is nonzero and the inverse is regular unless
\(G_{n,k}'(\zeta)=0\).  Any such derivative cancellation creates a
higher ramification not explained by a cyclotomic zero.
\end{corollary}

This clean separation between \emph{zero ramification} and
\emph{critical-value ramification} is useful computationally: cyclotomic
points can be preclassified arithmetically before any numerical root search.

\section{Exact low-degree examples}

For \(k=1\), the Gaussian coefficient is the \(q\)-integer
\[
 G_{n,1}(q)=[n]_q=1+q+\cdots+q^{n-1}.
\]
Thus \(G_{2,1}^{-1}(Y)=Y-1\), while
\[
 G_{3,1}^{-1}(Y)=\frac{-1\pm\sqrt{4Y-3}}2.
\]
These examples already display the general principles: one global positive
branch, square-root collisions at critical values, and multiple complex
branches determined by polynomial degree.

\chapter{Continuous Gaussian coefficients and inversion in both parameters}

Integer upper and lower indices cannot be varied analytically.  The natural
interpolation for \(0<q<1\) is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:continuous-qbinom}
 \mathcal C_q(x,y)
 =\frac{\Gamma_q(x+1)}{\Gamma_q(y+1)\Gamma_q(x-y+1)},
 \qquad x>y>0,
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qgamma-definition}
 \Gamma_q(z)=(1-q)^{1-z}\frac{(q;q)_\infty}{(q^z;q)_\infty}.
\end{equation}
At integral \(x=n,y=k\), \(\mathcal C_q(n,k)=\qbinom nk\).

\section{The \texorpdfstring{$q\to1$}{q to 1} bridge}

A standard fixed-argument expansion, obtainable from Euler--Maclaurin or the
asymptotics of Moak and McIntosh \cite{Moak1984,McIntosh1999}, is
\begin{align}
 \log\Gamma_{\e^{-t}}(z)
 ={}&\log\Gamma(z)
 -\frac{(z-1)(z-2)}4t\notag\\
 &+\frac{(z-2)(z-1)(2z+3)}{144}t^2+O(t^3),
\label{eq:qgamma-qone-expansion}
\end{align}
locally uniformly for \(z\) in compact subsets avoiding nonpositive
integers.  Substitution in \eqref{eq:continuous-qbinom} gives the exact same
first cumulants as the integer Gaussian polynomial.

\begin{proposition}[Continuous Gaussian expansion at one]\label{prop:continuous-qbin-qone}
Let
\[
 d=y(x-y),\qquad
 u=\log\frac{\mathcal C_{\e^{-t}}(x,y)}{\binom{x}{y}},
 \qquad
 \binom{x}{y}=\frac{\Gamma(x+1)}{\Gamma(y+1)\Gamma(x-y+1)}.
\]
Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:continuous-qbin-forward-one}
 u=-\frac d2t+\frac{d(x+1)}{24}t^2+O(t^3).
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\begin{align}
 t&=-\frac{2u}{d}+\frac{x+1}{3d^2}u^2+O(u^3),\label{eq:continuous-t-inverse-one}\\
 q&=1+\frac{2u}{d}+\frac{5-x}{3d^2}u^2+O(u^3).
\label{eq:continuous-q-inverse-one}
\end{align}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Insert \(z=x+1,y+1,x-y+1\) in
\eqref{eq:qgamma-qone-expansion}.  The linear combination of first
coefficients is \(-y(x-y)/2\), and the quadratic combination is
\(y(x-y)(x+1)/24\).  Reversion gives the inverse.
\end{proof}

\section{Inverse in the upper parameter}

Let \(y>0\) be fixed and regard \(x\ge y\) as active.  Write
\(\psi_q=(\log\Gamma_q)'\).  From the Lambert series,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qpsi}
 \psi_q(x)=-\log(1-q)+(\log q)
 \sum_{m\ge0}\frac{q^{m+x}}{1-q^{m+x}},
\end{equation}
and \(\psi_q'(x)>0\).

\begin{theorem}[Saturating upper-parameter inverse]\label{thm:continuous-upper-inverse}
For fixed \(y>0\), \(x\mapsto\mathcal C_q(x,y)\) is strictly increasing on
\([y,\infty)\), with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:upper-range}
 \mathcal C_q(y,y)=1,
 \qquad
 \lim_{x\to\infty}\mathcal C_q(x,y)
 =L_q(y)\defeq\frac{(q^{y+1};q)_\infty}{(q;q)_\infty}
 =\frac1{(1-q)^y\Gamma_q(y+1)}.
\end{equation}
Thus it has a unique real inverse for \(1<Y<L_q(y)\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Differentiate:
\[
 \partial_x\log\mathcal C_q(x,y)
 =\psi_q(x+1)-\psi_q(x-y+1)>0.
\]
At \(x=y\), the value is one.  Substituting
\eqref{eq:qgamma-definition} and letting \(x\to\infty\) gives
\eqref{eq:upper-range}.
\end{proof}

Near the lower endpoint, write \(x=y+s\) and
\(u=\log\mathcal C_q(y+s,y)\).  Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:upper-near-y-forward}
 u=\lambda_1s+\frac{\lambda_2}{2}s^2+\cdots,
 \quad
 \lambda_r=\psi_q^{(r-1)}(y+1)-\psi_q^{(r-1)}(1),
\end{equation}
and \(s(u)\) follows from \eqref{eq:order-inverse-local} with
\(\lambda_r\) in place of the order cumulants.

The upper endpoint has a logarithmic inverse.  Put
\[
 r=q^{x-y+1},\qquad R=\frac{\mathcal C_q(x,y)}{L_q(y)}.
\]
Then the exact expansion
\begin{equation}\label{eq:continuous-upper-ratio}
 \log R=-\sum_{m\ge1}
 \frac{1-q^{ym}}{m(1-q^m)}r^m
\end{equation}
shows, with \(\Delta=1-R\) and
\(A=[y]_q=(1-q^y)/(1-q)\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:r-from-delta-upper}
 r=\frac{\Delta}{A}
 +\frac{A^2/2-(1-q^{2y})/[2(1-q^2)]}{A^3}\Delta^2
 +O(\Delta^3).
\end{equation}
Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:x-upper-endpoint}
 x=y-1+\frac{\log r}{\log q},
\end{equation}
which diverges logarithmically as \(Y\uparrow L_q(y)\).

\section{Inverse in the lower parameter}

Fix \(x>0\) and let \(0<y<x\).  Symmetry gives
\(\mathcal C_q(x,y)=\mathcal C_q(x,x-y)\).  Set
\(g(y)=\log\mathcal C_q(x,y)\).  Then
\begin{align}
 g'(y)&=-\psi_q(y+1)+\psi_q(x-y+1),\label{eq:continuous-y-first}\\
 g''(y)&=-\psi_q'(y+1)-\psi_q'(x-y+1)<0.
\label{eq:continuous-y-second}
\end{align}

\begin{theorem}[Two lower-parameter branches]\label{thm:continuous-lower-branches}
The function \(y\mapsto\mathcal C_q(x,y)\) is strictly increasing on
\((0,x/2)\), strictly decreasing on \((x/2,x)\), and has a unique maximum
at \(y=x/2\).  Let
\[
 u_0=\frac x2+1,
 \quad M=\mathcal C_q(x,x/2),
 \quad \delta=\log(M/Y).
\]
For \(Y\uparrow M\), the two inverse branches are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:continuous-lower-puiseux}
 y=\frac x2\pm\sqrt{\frac{\delta}{\psi_q'(u_0)}}
 \left(1-\frac{\psi_q'''(u_0)}{24\psi_q'(u_0)^2}\delta
 +O(\delta^2)\right).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The sign of \eqref{eq:continuous-y-first} follows from strict increase of
\(\psi_q\).  Around \(y=x/2+s\), symmetry removes odd powers and
\[
 \log M-g(x/2+s)
 =\psi_q'(u_0)s^2+\frac{\psi_q'''(u_0)}{12}s^4+O(s^6).
\]
Revert this even series and take the two square roots.
\end{proof}

At the endpoint \(y=0\), \(\mathcal C_q(x,0)=1\) and
\[
 \lambda_1=\psi_q(x+1)-\psi_q(1)>0,
 \quad
 \lambda_2=-\psi_q'(1)-\psi_q'(x+1)<0.
\]
Thus, with \(u=\log Y\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:continuous-y-endpoint-inverse}
 y=\frac{u}{\lambda_1}-\frac{\lambda_2}{2\lambda_1^3}u^2+O(u^3),
\end{equation}
and the branch near \(y=x\) follows by reflection.

\section{Small and large base}

The product form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:continuous-qbin-product}
 \mathcal C_q(x,y)=
 \frac{(q^{x-y+1};q)_\infty(q^{y+1};q)_\infty}
 {(q^{x+1};q)_\infty(q;q)_\infty}
\end{equation}
shows that \(\mathcal C_q(x,y)\to1\) as \(q\to0^+\).  Its expansion is a
generalized power series whose exponent semigroup is generated by
\(1,y+1,x-y+1,x+1\).  The universal leading term is
\[
 \mathcal C_q(x,y)=1+q+o(q),
\]
because all three numerator/extra denominator exponents exceed one.  Thus
the positive inverse begins \(q=Y-1+o(Y-1)\), but fractional corrections
appear when \(x\) or \(y\) is nonintegral.

For \(q>1\), define the interpolation by the standard reciprocity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:continuous-qbin-reciprocity}
 \mathcal C_q(x,y)=q^{y(x-y)}\mathcal C_{1/q}(x,y).
\end{equation}
It follows that every small-\(q\) generalized expansion has a corresponding
large-\(q\) Laurent--Puiseux inverse with leading scale
\[
 q\sim Y^{1/[y(x-y)]}.
\]

\begin{conjecture}[Continuous positive-base monotonicity]\label{conj:continuous-qbin-monotone}
For every real \(x>y>0\), the map
\(q\mapsto\mathcal C_q(x,y)\) is strictly increasing on \((0,\infty)\)
under the reciprocal continuation \eqref{eq:continuous-qbin-reciprocity}.
This is automatic for integral \((x,y)\) by coefficient positivity, and the
expansions at \(0,1,\infty\) have the predicted sign, but a global proof for
all real parameters appears to require a new positivity representation for
\(\partial_q\log\mathcal C_q\).
\end{conjecture}

\chapter{Inverse \texorpdfstring{$q$}{q}-gamma, \texorpdfstring{$q$}{q}-factorial, and \texorpdfstring{$q$}{q}-beta functions}

\section{The inverse \texorpdfstring{$q$}{q}-gamma function in its argument}

For \(0<q<1\), \(\log\Gamma_q(x)\) is strictly convex on \((0,\infty)\)
\cite{DLMFqGamma,AndrewsAskeyRoy}.  The series \eqref{eq:qpsi} makes the
geometry explicit.

\begin{theorem}[Two real branches of \(\Gamma_q^{-1}\)]\label{thm:qgamma-two-branches}
There exists a unique \(x_q>0\) satisfying \(\psi_q(x_q)=0\).  The number
\[
 M_q=\Gamma_q(x_q)
\]
is the unique global minimum.  For every \(Y>M_q\), the equation
\(\Gamma_q(x)=Y\) has exactly two positive real solutions, one in
\((0,x_q)\) and one in \((x_q,\infty)\); for \(Y=M_q\) they coalesce.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
As \(x\downarrow0\), the term \(m=0\) in \eqref{eq:qpsi} gives
\(\psi_q(x)=-1/x+O(1)\), so \(\psi_q\to-\infty\).  As \(x\to\infty\), the
sum tends to zero and \(\psi_q(x)\to-\log(1-q)>0\).  Strict convexity means
\(\psi_q' >0\), so there is one zero.  Also
\(\Gamma_q(x)\to\infty\) at both endpoints: near zero it has a simple pole,
and at infinity the factor \((1-q)^{1-x}\) grows exponentially.  The branch
count follows.
\end{proof}

Let
\[
 p_j=\psi_q^{(j)}(x_q),\qquad j\ge1,
 \quad
 \delta=\log(Y/M_q).
\]

\begin{proposition}[Puiseux expansion at the minimum]\label{prop:qgamma-min-puiseux}
The two inverse branches have
\begin{align}
 x={}&x_q\pm\sqrt{\frac{2\delta}{p_1}}
 -\frac{p_2}{3p_1^2}\delta\notag\\
 &\pm\frac{\sqrt2(5p_2^2-3p_1p_3)}{36p_1^{7/2}}\delta^{3/2}
 +O(\delta^2).
\label{eq:qgamma-min-puiseux}
\end{align}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Taylor expand
\[
 \log\Gamma_q(x_q+s)-\log M_q
 =\frac{p_1}{2}s^2+\frac{p_2}{6}s^3
 +\frac{p_3}{24}s^4+\cdots
\]
and substitute \(a_2=p_1/2,a_3=p_2/6,a_4=p_3/24\) in
\eqref{eq:simple-critical-puiseux}.
\end{proof}

\section{Endpoint expansions of the two branches}

Put \(\ell=\log q<0\) and
\begin{align}
 A_q&=\frac{1-q}{-\ell},\label{eq:Aq}\\
 c_q&=-\log(1-q)-\frac12\ell
 +\ell\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{q^m}{1-q^m}.
\label{eq:cq}
\end{align}
The Laurent expansion of the left branch is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qgamma-zero-forward}
 \Gamma_q(x)=\frac{A_q}{x}+A_qc_q+O(x),\qquad x\downarrow0.
\end{equation}
Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qgamma-left-inverse}
 x=\frac{A_q}{Y}+\frac{A_q^2c_q}{Y^2}+O(Y^{-3})
 \qquad(Y\to\infty)
\end{equation}
for the left branch.

For the right branch, let
\[
 h=-\log(1-q)>0,
 \qquad
 X_0=1+\frac{\log Y-\log(q;q)_\infty}{h}.
\]
Since
\[
 -\log(q^x;q)_\infty=\frac{q^x}{1-q}+O(q^{2x}),
\]
one obtains
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qgamma-right-inverse}
 x=X_0-\frac{q^{X_0}}{h(1-q)}+O(q^{2X_0}).
\end{equation}
Thus the two branches have qualitatively different endpoint scales:
reciprocal on the left and affine-logarithmic with exponentially small
correction on the right.

Since \([x]_q!=\Gamma_q(x+1)\), all results transfer immediately to the
continuous \(q\)-factorial after shifting the active variable by one.

\section{Inverse in the base of \texorpdfstring{$\Gamma_q(x)$}{Gamma q(x)}}

A classical monotonicity theorem states that for \(0<q<r<1\),
\(\Gamma_q(x)<\Gamma_r(x)\) when \(0<x<1\) or \(x>2\), while the inequality
is reversed for \(1<x<2\); \(\Gamma_q(1)=\Gamma_q(2)=1\)
\cite{DLMFqGamma,Kocic1990}.  Consequently, for fixed
\(x\notin\{1,2\}\), the equation \(\Gamma_q(x)=Y\) has a unique
\(q\in(0,1)\) whenever \(Y\) lies strictly between \(1\) and \(\Gamma(x)\).

The exact small-base logarithm is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qgamma-small-q-log}
 \log\Gamma_q(x)
 =(1-x)\log(1-q)
 -\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{q^m-q^{mx}}{m(1-q^m)}.
\end{equation}
It is a generalized power series in the semigroup generated by \(1\) and
\(x\).  The leading inverse laws are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qgamma-small-q-inverses}
 q\sim
 \begin{cases}
  (Y-1)^{1/x},&0<x<1,\\[1mm]
  \displaystyle\frac{Y-1}{x-2},&1<x<2\text{ or }x>2,
 \end{cases}
 \qquad Y\to1,
\end{equation}
with the real side chosen according to the sign of \(Y-1\).  The transition
at \(x=1,2\) is exact degeneracy, since \(\Gamma_q(1)=\Gamma_q(2)=1\) for
all \(q\).

For \(q>1\), the standard continuation satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qgamma-reciprocity}
 \Gamma_q(x)=q^{(x-1)(x-2)/2}\Gamma_{1/q}(x).
\end{equation}
If \((x-1)(x-2)\ne0\), inversion at \(q=\infty\) therefore begins
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qgamma-qinfty-inverse}
 q\sim Y^{2/[(x-1)(x-2)]},
\end{equation}
followed by generalized corrections inherited from
\eqref{eq:qgamma-small-q-log}.  For negative or complex large \(q\), a
branch of \(q^x\) and \(\Log q\) must be fixed, and the inverse is sectorial.

\section{Inverse \texorpdfstring{$q$}{q}-beta functions}

Define
\[
 B_q(a,b)=\frac{\Gamma_q(a)\Gamma_q(b)}{\Gamma_q(a+b)},
 \qquad a,b>0.
\]
For fixed \(b\),
\[
 \partial_a\log B_q(a,b)=\psi_q(a)-\psi_q(a+b)<0,
\]
so the active-\(a\) map is strictly decreasing.

\begin{theorem}[One-parameter \(q\)-beta inverse]\label{thm:qbeta-one-param}
For fixed \(b>0\), \(a\mapsto B_q(a,b)\) maps \((0,\infty)\) bijectively
onto
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qbeta-range}
 \bigl((1-q)^b\Gamma_q(b),\infty\bigr).
\end{equation}
As \(Y\to\infty\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qbeta-left-inverse}
 a=\frac{A_q}{Y}+O(Y^{-2}),
\end{equation}
where \(A_q\) is in \eqref{eq:Aq}.  At the finite lower endpoint
\(L=(1-q)^b\Gamma_q(b)\), put \(R=Y/L\) and \(r=q^a\).  Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qbeta-endpoint-ratio}
 \log R=\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{1-q^{bm}}{m(1-q^m)}r^m,
\end{equation}
so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qbeta-endpoint-inverse}
 a=\frac{\log\{(R-1)/[b]_q\}}{\log q}+O(R-1).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Monotonicity was shown above.  Near zero, the pole of \(\Gamma_q(a)\) gives
\eqref{eq:qbeta-left-inverse}.  At infinity, substitute the product formula
for the ratio \(\Gamma_q(a)/\Gamma_q(a+b)\), obtaining the limit and
\eqref{eq:qbeta-endpoint-ratio}; apply endpoint saturation.
\end{proof}

A second geometry appears if the sum \(s=a+b\) is fixed.  Then
\[
 H_s(a)=B_q(a,s-a),\qquad0<a<s,
\]
is symmetric and strictly log-convex because
\[
 (\log H_s)''=\psi_q'(a)+\psi_q'(s-a)>0.
\]
It has a unique minimum at \(a=s/2\), diverges at both endpoints, and its
inverse has two square-root branches.  If
\(\delta=\log(Y/H_s(s/2))\), then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qbeta-fixed-sum-inverse}
 a=\frac s2\pm\sqrt{\frac{\delta}{\psi_q'(s/2)}}
 \left(1-\frac{\psi_q'''(s/2)}{24\psi_q'(s/2)^2}\delta+O(\delta^2)\right).
\end{equation}
This is the convex dual of the concave lower-parameter geometry in
\cref{thm:continuous-lower-branches}.

\chapter{Elementary \texorpdfstring{$q$}{q}-analogues: exact and asymptotic inverses}

The simplest examples are valuable because they expose, without the masking
of a large special-function apparatus, almost every singular mechanism that
recurs later: exact logarithmic inversion, algebraic base inversion, endpoint
saturation, parity effects at \(q=-1\), and a change from Taylor to Puiseux
scales when an exponent crosses an integer threshold.

\section{The \texorpdfstring{$q$}{q}-number and its exact inverse in the exponent}

For a fixed branch of \(q^x=\exp(x\Log q)\), define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:q-number-definition}
 [x]_q=\frac{1-q^x}{1-q},\qquad q\ne1,
 \qquad [x]_1=x.
\end{equation}
The inverse with respect to \(x\) is elementary but multivalued.

\begin{theorem}[Exact exponent inverse of the \(q\)-number]\label{thm:q-number-x-inverse}
Fix \(q\in\C\setminus\{0,1\}\), a branch of \(\Log q\), and a branch of the
logarithm at \(1-(1-q)Y\).  Every compatible local inverse branch is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:q-number-exact-x-inverse}
 x=\frac{\Log\bigl(1-(1-q)Y\bigr)+2\pi \ii \ell}{\Log q},
 \qquad \ell\in\Z.
\end{equation}
For positive \(q\ne1\) and real levels for which the logarithm is real, the
real branch is obtained by \(\ell=0\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Equation \([x]_q=Y\) is equivalent to
\(q^x=1-(1-q)Y\).  Taking logarithms gives
\eqref{eq:q-number-exact-x-inverse}; conversely exponentiation recovers the
level equation.
\end{proof}

This formula is a useful model for parameterwise branch accounting.  Changing
\(\ell\) changes the inverse sheet, while changing the branch of \(\Log q\)
changes the ambient definition of the forward function itself.

Put \(\eps=1-q\).  Expansion of the exact inverse gives, uniformly for \(Y\)
in compact subsets avoiding the logarithmic cut,
\begin{align}
 x={}&Y+\frac{Y(Y-1)}2\eps
 +\frac{Y(Y-1)(4Y+1)}{12}\eps^2 \notag\\
 &+\frac{Y(Y-1)(6Y^2+2Y+1)}{24}\eps^3+O(\eps^4).
 \label{eq:q-number-x-qone}
\end{align}
Thus the inverse tends to the identity as \(q\to1\), but its first correction
is already nonlinear in the prescribed level.

The other endpoint regimes are singular.  For fixed \(Y\ne1\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:q-number-x-qzero}
 x=\frac{\Log(1-Y)}{\Log q}
   +O\!\left(\frac{q}{\Log q}\right),\qquad q\to0,
\end{equation}
so all finite nonunit levels are compressed toward \(x=0\).  As
\(q\to\infty\) in a fixed sector,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:q-number-x-qinfty}
 x=1+\frac{\Log Y}{\Log q}
   +O\!\left(\frac1{q\Log q}\right).
\end{equation}
The points \(x=0\) and \(x=1\) are therefore the two endpoint condensers for
base degeneration.

\section{Inverse in the base for integral exponent}

For \(n\in\N\), \(n\ge2\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:q-integer-polynomial}
 [n]_q=1+q+\cdots+q^{n-1}
\end{equation}
is a polynomial of degree \(n-1\).  Its inverse is algebraic, with branch
points at the critical values of this polynomial.

\subsection{The branch at \texorpdfstring{$q=0$}{q=0}}

Write \(\delta=Y-1\).  For \(n\ge6\), the inverse through fifth order is
stable with respect to \(n\):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:q-integer-qzero-inverse}
 q=\delta-\delta^2+\delta^3-\delta^4+\delta^5+O(\delta^6).
\end{equation}
More generally, the coefficients agree with the expansion of
\(\delta/(1+\delta)\) through degree \(n-1\).  This follows from
\(
[n]_q=(1-q^n)/(1-q)
\): the term \(q^n\) cannot influence reversion below order \(n\).

For nonintegral \(x\), the same base inverse becomes a generalized Puiseux
problem.  If \(0<\Re x<1\), then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:q-number-base-small-subunit}
 [x]_q=1-q^x+q+\cdots,
 \qquad
 q\sim (1-Y)^{1/x}.
\end{equation}
If \(\Re x>1\), the ordinary term \(q\) leads and \(q\sim Y-1\), but the
correction \(q^x\) creates a nonintegral transseries whenever \(x\notin\N\).
This elementary example explains why a uniform ``Taylor series in \(q\)'' is
not available for all continuous parameters.

\subsection{The branch at \texorpdfstring{$q=1$}{q=1}}

Let \(h=q-1\), \(\delta=Y-n\), and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:q-integer-ABC}
 A=\binom n2,
 \qquad B=\binom n3,
 \qquad C=\binom n4,
 \qquad D=\binom n5.
\end{equation}
Since
\([n]_{1+h}=n+Ah+Bh^2+Ch^3+Dh^4+\cdots\), reversion yields
\begin{align}
 q={}&1+\frac{\delta}{A}-\frac{B}{A^3}\delta^2
 +\frac{2B^2-AC}{A^5}\delta^3 \notag\\
 &+\frac{-5B^3+5ABC-A^2D}{A^7}\delta^4+O(\delta^5).
 \label{eq:q-integer-qone-inverse}
\end{align}
This is the elementary prototype of the Gaussian-coefficient formula in
\cref{chap:gaussian-inverse}.

\subsection{The parity point \texorpdfstring{$q=-1$}{q=-1}}

For \(n=2m\) and \(n=2m+1\), respectively,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:q-integer-minus-one-values}
 [2m]_{-1}=0,
 \quad [2m]'_{-1}=m;
 \qquad
 [2m+1]_{-1}=1,
 \quad [2m+1]'_{-1}=-m.
\end{equation}
Consequently the local inverses are regular for \(m\ge1\):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:q-integer-minus-one-inverse}
 q=-1+\begin{cases}
 Y/m+O(Y^2),&n=2m,\\
 -(Y-1)/m+O((Y-1)^2),&n=2m+1.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
The analogous point for Gaussian coefficients is subtler because cyclotomic
multiplicity can make the derivative vanish.

\subsection{The branch at infinity}

Put \(d=n-1\) and \(Z=Y^{1/d}\).  For \(n\ge3\), on each chosen \(d\)-th
root branch,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:q-integer-infinity-inverse}
 q=Z-\frac1d-\frac{d+1}{2d^2}Z^{-1}+O(Z^{-2}).
\end{equation}
For \(n=2\) the exact answer is \(q=Y-1\).  Negative infinity is obtained by
the appropriate root branch; when \(d\) is even, a large positive level has
two real tails, whereas when \(d\) is odd it has one.

\section{Jackson's two \texorpdfstring{$q$}{q}-exponentials and their inverses}

For \(\abs q<1\), let
\begin{equation}\label{eq:q-exponentials}
 e_q(z)=\frac1{(z;q)_\infty},
 \qquad
 E_q(z)=(-z;q)_\infty.
\end{equation}
Their compositional inverses in \(z\) are not new transcendental objects
independent of the Pochhammer inverse; exactly,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:q-exponential-poinverse}
 e_q^{-1}(Y)=\branch_{a,q}(Y^{-1}),
 \qquad
 E_q^{-1}(Y)=-\branch_{a,q}(Y),
\end{equation}
where \(\branch_{a,q}\) denotes a chosen inverse branch of
\(a\mapsto(a;q)_\infty\).

At \(q=0\) the inverses collapse to elementary functions:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:q-exponential-qzero}
 e_0(z)=\frac1{1-z},\quad e_0^{-1}(Y)=1-Y^{-1};
 \qquad
 E_0(z)=1+z,\quad E_0^{-1}(Y)=Y-1.
\end{equation}
At fixed \(q\), it is often better to invert the logarithmic level
\(L=\Log Y\).  One has
\begin{align}
 \Log e_q(z)&=\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{z^m}{m(1-q^m)},\label{eq:log-eq}\\
 \Log E_q(z)&=\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{(-1)^{m+1}z^m}{m(1-q^m)}.
 \label{eq:log-Eq}
\end{align}
The universal formula \eqref{eq:lagrange-inverse} therefore gives all local
coefficients at \(z=0\).

The classical limit is regular only after scaling the argument.  Set
\(q=1-\eps\), and define \(z_e\) and \(z_E\) by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:scaled-qexp-inverse-def}
 e_q(\eps z_e)=Y,
 \qquad E_q(\eps z_E)=Y,
 \qquad L=\Log Y.
\end{equation}
Direct inversion of \eqref{eq:log-eq}--\eqref{eq:log-Eq} gives
\begin{align}
 z_e={}&L-\frac{L^2}{4}\eps
 +\frac{L^2(L-9)}{72}\eps^2
 -\frac{L^2(L^2-8L+36)}{576}\eps^3+O(\eps^4),
 \label{eq:scaled-eq-inverse}\\
 z_E={}&L+\frac{L^2}{4}\eps
 +\frac{L^2(L+9)}{72}\eps^2
 +\frac{L^2(L^2+8L+36)}{576}\eps^3+O(\eps^4).
 \label{eq:scaled-Eq-inverse}
\end{align}
Both tend to the ordinary logarithm.  Their odd corrections have opposite
sign, reflecting the identity \(e_q(z)E_q(-z)=1\).

\begin{remark}[Base inversion]
Solving \(e_q(z)=Y\) or \(E_q(z)=Y\) for \(q\) is precisely the base inverse
of the infinite Pochhammer symbol.  Hence the radial root-of-unity expansion,
including \(q\to-1\), transfers without further work after replacing the
level by \(Y^{-1}\) or replacing \(a\) by \(-z\).  No canonical
\(q\to\infty\) statement exists for the product definitions
\eqref{eq:q-exponentials} until a continuation outside the unit disk has been
chosen.
\end{remark}

\section{A normalized \texorpdfstring{$q$}{q}-polylogarithm family}

The inverse theory of a whole class of Lambert-series analogues can be
presented at once.  For \(0<q<1\), \(s\in\C\), and \(\abs z<1\), define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:q-polylog-normalized}
 \mathcal L_{s,q}(z)=\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{z^m}{[m]_q^s}.
\end{equation}
At \(q=1\) this is \(\Li_s(z)\); at \(q=0\) it is \(z/(1-z)\) for every
\(s\).  The case \(s=1\) is a normalized Jackson-type logarithm.

\begin{proposition}[Local inverse in \(z\)]\label{prop:qpolylog-z-inverse}
Put \(A_j=[j]_q^{-s}\).  The branch satisfying \(z(0)=0\) is
\begin{align}
 z={}&Y-A_2Y^2+(2A_2^2-A_3)Y^3
 +(-5A_2^3+5A_2A_3-A_4)Y^4 \notag\\
 &+(14A_2^4-21A_2^2A_3+6A_2A_4+3A_3^2-A_5)Y^5
 +O(Y^6).
 \label{eq:qpolylog-z-inverse}
\end{align}
At the exact endpoint \(q=0\), this sums to \(z=Y/(1+Y)\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Apply fifth-order reversion to
\(Y=z+A_2z^2+A_3z^3+\cdots\).  At \(q=0\), all \(A_j=1\), so the forward
series is geometric.
\end{proof}

The perturbation from the classical polylogarithm is especially simple.
Since
\begin{equation}\label{eq:q-number-near-one-m}
 [m]_{1-\eps}=m-\binom m2\eps+O(\eps^2),
\end{equation}
we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qpolylog-near-one}
 \mathcal L_{s,1-\eps}(z)
 =\Li_s(z)+\frac{s\eps}{2}\bigl(\Li_{s-1}(z)-\Li_s(z)\bigr)
 +O(\eps^2).
\end{equation}
If \(z_0\) is a regular branch of \(\Li_s^{-1}(Y)\), then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qpolylog-inverse-near-one}
 z=z_0-\frac{s\eps z_0}{2}
 \left(1-\frac{Y}{\Li_{s-1}(z_0)}\right)+O(\eps^2).
\end{equation}
For \(s=1\), \(z_0=1-\e^{-Y}\), giving a fully elementary classical
anchor.

There are also useful global parameter inverses.

\begin{theorem}[Inverse in \(q\) and in \(s\)]\label{thm:qpolylog-parameter-inverses}
Let \(0<z<1\).
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
\item If \(s>0\), then \(q\mapsto\mathcal L_{s,q}(z)\) is strictly decreasing
on \((0,1)\), from \(z/(1-z)\) to \(\Li_s(z)\).  Hence each intermediate
level has a unique base inverse.
\item If \(0<q<1\), then \(s\mapsto\mathcal L_{s,q}(z)\) is strictly
decreasing on \(\R\), and tends to \(z\) as \(s\to+\infty\).  Its upper
endpoint inverse satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qpolylog-s-inverse}
 s=\frac{\log\{z^2/(Y-z)\}}{\log[2]_q}+o(1),
 \qquad Y\downarrow z.
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For \(m\ge2\), \([m]_q\) increases strictly with \(q\), proving (i)
termwise.  Differentiating in \(s\) gives
\[
 \partial_s\mathcal L_{s,q}(z)
 =-\sum_{m\ge2}\frac{z^m\log[m]_q}{[m]_q^s}<0.
\]
As \(s\to\infty\), the \(m=1\) term survives and the \(m=2\) term is the
first correction:
\(Y-z=z^2[2]_q^{-s}(1+o(1))\).  Taking logarithms proves
\eqref{eq:qpolylog-s-inverse}.
\end{proof}

Near the base endpoint \(q=0\), for fixed \(s\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qpolylog-qzero}
 \mathcal L_{s,q}(z)
 =\frac{z}{1-z}-\frac{s z^2}{1-z}q+O(q^2),
\end{equation}
locally uniformly for \(s\) in compact sets.  Hence, for \(s\ne0\), the
local real inverse has leading term
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qpolylog-qzero-inverse}
 q=\frac{z-(1-z)Y}{s z^2}+o\!\left(\frac{z}{1-z}-Y\right).
\end{equation}
For fixed \(s\), this is an ordinary analytic germ at \(q=0\).
Fractional powers can arise only in a double scaling in which \(s\) itself
depends singularly on \(q\), not in the fixed-parameter expansion.

\chapter{Basic hypergeometric functions and parameterwise inversion}

The Pochhammer and Gaussian cases are the atoms of basic hypergeometric
series.  Once their logarithmic derivatives and local inverses are known, a
large class of parameter inversions becomes algorithmic.  We use the standard
normalization of \cite{GasperRahman,AndrewsAskeyRoy}.

\section{The inverse in the series variable}

Let
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rphis-definition}
 {}_r\phi_s\!\left(
 \begin{matrix}a_1,\ldots,a_r\\ b_1,\ldots,b_s\end{matrix};q,z\right)
 =\sum_{m\ge0}c_m z^m,
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rphis-coefficients}
 c_m=
 \frac{(a_1,\ldots,a_r;q)_m}
      {(q,b_1,\ldots,b_s;q)_m}
 \left((-1)^m q^{\binom m2}\right)^{1+s-r}.
\end{equation}
Assume no denominator factor vanishes and \(c_1\ne0\).

\begin{theorem}[Universal local inverse of a basic hypergeometric series]
\label{thm:rphis-z-inverse}
For \(\delta=Y-1\), the branch through \(z=0\) begins
\begin{align}
 z={}&\frac{\delta}{c_1}
 -\frac{c_2}{c_1^3}\delta^2
 +\frac{2c_2^2-c_1c_3}{c_1^5}\delta^3 \notag\\
 &+\frac{-5c_2^3+5c_1c_2c_3-c_1^2c_4}{c_1^7}\delta^4
 +O(\delta^5).
 \label{eq:rphis-z-inverse}
\end{align}
The complete coefficient sequence is given by \eqref{eq:lagrange-inverse}.
If \(c_1=\cdots=c_{d-1}=0\) and \(c_d\ne0\), the inverse is instead a
\(d\)-sheeted Puiseux germ \(z\sim(\delta/c_d)^{1/d}\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
This is ordinary series reversion applied to \(Y-1=\sum_{m\ge1}c_mz^m\).
The degenerate statement follows from \cref{thm:puiseux-normal}.
\end{proof}

The theorem includes terminating \(q\)-orthogonal polynomials after an
affine normalization of the dependent variable.  In a terminating case
\(a_i=q^{-N}\), the forward map is a polynomial of degree at most \(N\) in
\(z\), and the complete inverse surface is algebraic.  Its branch values are
the zeros of the discriminant of the level polynomial.

\section{Derivatives with respect to every continuous parameter}

For a finite Pochhammer factor away from its zeros,
\begin{align}
 \partial_a\log(a;q)_m
 &=-\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\frac{q^j}{1-aq^j},
 \label{eq:finite-poch-a-logder}\\
 \partial_q\log(a;q)_m
 &=-\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}\frac{ajq^{j-1}}{1-aq^j}.
 \label{eq:finite-poch-q-logder}
\end{align}
Consequently, termwise differentiation of \eqref{eq:rphis-definition}
gives, for an upper parameter \(a_i\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rphis-upper-derivative}
 \partial_{a_i}F
 =-\sum_{m\ge1}c_mz^m
   \sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\frac{q^j}{1-a_iq^j},
\end{equation}
and for a lower parameter \(b_i\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rphis-lower-derivative}
 \partial_{b_i}F
 =\sum_{m\ge1}c_mz^m
   \sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\frac{q^j}{1-b_iq^j}.
\end{equation}
The base derivative is obtained by combining
\eqref{eq:finite-poch-q-logder} for every factor with the derivative of the
explicit power of \(q\) in \eqref{eq:rphis-coefficients}.

\begin{corollary}[Parameter sensitivity of the inverse]\label{cor:rphis-sensitivity}
Let \(F(z;\theta)=Y\), with \(F_z\ne0\).  For every continuous upper or lower
parameter, and for the base away from its singular set,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rphis-parameter-sensitivity}
 \frac{\partial z}{\partial\theta}=-\frac{F_\theta}{F_z}.
\end{equation}
If the level also varies,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rphis-total-sensitivity}
 dz=\frac{dY-\sum_jF_{\theta_j}\,d\theta_j}{F_z}.
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

Equation \eqref{eq:rphis-total-sensitivity} is the differential equation for
continuing a chosen inverse sheet through parameter space.  It also identifies
the numerical condition number: every inverse becomes ill-conditioned when
\(F_z\) approaches zero.

\section{Terminating parameters and singular lower parameters}

An upper parameter \(a_i=q^{-N}\) truncates the series because
\((q^{-N};q)_m=0\) for \(m>N\).  Varying \(a_i\) away from this value
reactivates the tail.  Inside any compact parameter subdomain on which the
nonterminating series converges normally and no denominator factor vanishes,
the variation is analytic.  Thus a terminating point is not automatically a branch
point of the parameter inverse.  It becomes one only if the corresponding
parameter derivative \eqref{eq:rphis-upper-derivative} vanishes.

A lower parameter \(b_i=q^{-N}\), by contrast, lies on the polar divisor of
the generic nonterminating function.  Near a simple polar factor the forward
map has the form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rphis-lower-pole-model}
 F(z;b_i)=\frac{A(z)}{1-b_iq^N}+B(z)+O(1-b_iq^N),
\end{equation}
with \(A(z)\ne0\) generically.  Inverting a large level gives the reciprocal
scale
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rphis-lower-pole-inverse}
 b_i=q^{-N}\left(1-\frac{A(z)}{Y}+O(Y^{-2})\right).
\end{equation}
When numerator and denominator zeros cancel, one must first remove the
singularity; the inverse type is determined by the first nonvanishing term of
the reduced germ, not by the unreduced quotient.

\section{Perturbative inversion near \texorpdfstring{$q=1$}{q=1}}

A broad class of scaled basic hypergeometric functions has an expansion
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qto1-general-perturbation}
 F_q(z)=F_0(z)+\eps F_1(z)+\eps^2F_2(z)+\cdots,
 \qquad q=1-\eps,
\end{equation}
where \(F_0\) is an ordinary hypergeometric or confluent hypergeometric
function.  The correct scaling of \(z\) depends on \(r-s\), so no single
unscaled formula covers all \({}_r\phi_s\).

\begin{proposition}[Inverse perturbation about the classical limit]
\label{prop:inverse-perturbation-qone}
Let \(z_0\) satisfy \(F_0(z_0)=Y\) and \(F_0'(z_0)\ne0\).  The corresponding
inverse branch of \eqref{eq:qto1-general-perturbation} is
\begin{align}
 z={}&z_0-\eps\frac{F_1(z_0)}{F_0'(z_0)} \notag\\
 &+\eps^2\left[
 -\frac{F_2(z_0)}{F_0'(z_0)}
 +\frac{F_1(z_0)F_1'(z_0)}{F_0'(z_0)^2}
 -\frac{F_0''(z_0)F_1(z_0)^2}{2F_0'(z_0)^3}
 \right]+O(\eps^3).
 \label{eq:inverse-perturbation-qone}
\end{align}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Substitute \(z=z_0+\eps z_1+\eps^2z_2+\cdots\) into
\eqref{eq:qto1-general-perturbation}, Taylor expand, and equate powers of
\(\eps\).
\end{proof}

This proposition turns any known forward \(q\to1\) expansion into an inverse
expansion.  The true obstruction is not reversion but uniformity: if
\(F_0'(z_0)=0\), the expansion must be rescaled and replaced by the Puiseux
normal form of \cref{thm:puiseux-normal}.

\section{Roots of unity and base singularities}

At a root of unity, denominator factors \((q;q)_m\) and
\((b_i;q)_m\) can vanish periodically.  There are three distinct cases.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item A terminating numerator kills every potentially singular term.  The
function has a finite removable limit, and ordinary or Puiseux inversion
applies to the reduced polynomial.
\item A finite number of uncancelled terms is singular.  The parameter inverse
has an algebraic reciprocal scale such as \eqref{eq:rphis-lower-pole-inverse}.
\item Infinitely many terms are coherently singular.  The radial asymptotic is
usually exponential in \(1/t\), \(q=\zeta e^{-t}\), and inversion takes the
logarithmic form \(t\asymp A/\Log Y\).  Cyclic quantum dilogarithms and
root-of-unity Pochhammer asymptotics provide the forward coefficients in this
case; see \cite{Kopp2025,Fantini2025} for related modern contexts.
\end{enumerate}

Thus a root of unity is not one universal singularity.  Cancellation data and
the order of termination decide the inverse scale.

\section{Orthogonal-polynomial and \texorpdfstring{$q$}{q}-Appell specializations}

Many families in the \(q\)-Askey scheme are terminating
\({}_r\phi_s\) functions \cite{Ismail2005}.  For a fixed degree \(n\),
inversion in the polynomial variable is therefore algebraic.  A useful
uniform statement is the following.

\begin{proposition}[Fixed-degree inverse atlas]
Let \(P_n(x;q,\theta)\) be a degree-\(n\) polynomial in \(x\), analytic in
continuous parameters on a domain \(U\), with nonzero leading coefficient.
Then the multivalued inverse of \(P_n(x;q,\theta)=Y\) is an \(n\)-sheeted
branched covering of the \((Y,q,\theta)\)-space.  Its finite branch divisor is
contained in
\begin{equation}\label{eq:polynomial-inverse-discriminant}
 \Disc_x(P_n(x;q,\theta)-Y)=0,
\end{equation}
and its local expansions are analytic at simple points and Puiseux at points
of higher ramification.
\end{proposition}

For \(q\to1\), the branches may be continued from the inverse branches of the
corresponding classical orthogonal polynomial, except where classical
critical values collide.  For \(n\to\infty\), however, the fixed-degree
algebraic atlas is not uniform; turning points and edge scalings are expected
to generate Airy-type inverse transitions.

\chapter{Simultaneous inversion, condition numbers, and branch geometry}

A scalar equation cannot determine several unknown parameters without
additional constraints.  The phrase ``inverse with respect to all
parameters'' therefore means, first, inverting one parameter at a time while
holding the others fixed; and, second, solving a square system of independent
level equations when several parameters are to be recovered simultaneously.

\section{Iso-level hypersurfaces}

For one scalar observable \(F(\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_p)=Y\), a regular level
is a hypersurface.  If \(F_{\theta_p}\ne0\), the local graph
\(\theta_p=\branch(Y,\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_{p-1})\) satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:isolevel-gradient}
 \partial_Y\branch=\frac1{F_{\theta_p}},
 \qquad
 \partial_{\theta_j}\branch=-\frac{F_{\theta_j}}{F_{\theta_p}}.
\end{equation}
For \((a;q)_\alpha\), this gives a three-directional inverse calculus in
\(a,q,\alpha\).  The vanishing of one chosen denominator derivative does not
make the level hypersurface singular; it means only that the selected graph
coordinate has failed.  Another parameter may remain a valid local graph.

\section{Several observables and the inverse Jacobian}

Let \(\bm F=(F_1,\ldots,F_p)\) and \(\bm\theta=(\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_p)\).
If
\begin{equation}\label{eq:inverse-jacobian-condition}
 J(\bm\theta)=\det\left(\frac{\partial F_i}{\partial\theta_j}\right)_{i,j=1}^p
 \ne0,
\end{equation}
then \(\bm\theta\) is locally an analytic function of the level vector
\(\bm Y\), and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:inverse-jacobian-differential}
 d\bm\theta=(D\bm F)^{-1}d\bm Y.
\end{equation}
For example, two Pochhammer observations
\begin{equation}\label{eq:two-observation-pair}
 (a;q)_{n_1}=Y_1,
 \qquad
 (a;q)_{n_2}=Y_2
\end{equation}
can locally recover both \(a\) and \(q\) whenever the two logarithmic-gradient
rows are independent.  This is a natural inverse-identification problem for
experimental or symbolic data.

\section{Relative condition numbers}

For a scalar inverse \(F(z)=Y\), define the local relative condition number
\begin{equation}\label{eq:relative-condition-number}
 \kappa_{z\leftarrow Y}
 =\left|\frac{Y}{zF'(z)}\right|,
\end{equation}
when \(zY\ne0\).  At a critical point of multiplicity \(m-1\),
\(z-z_c\asymp(Y-Y_c)^{1/m}\), so the absolute derivative of the inverse
diverges like \(\abs{Y-Y_c}^{1/m-1}\).  Near an endpoint saturation
\(Y-L\asymp q^\alpha\), the inverse in \(\alpha\) has derivative
\begin{equation}\label{eq:endpoint-condition}
 \frac{d\alpha}{dY}\asymp\frac1{(Y-L)\log q},
\end{equation}
which is logarithmically severe.  These estimates dictate both working
precision and continuation step size.

\section{Monodromy as the global inverse invariant}

For a finite polynomial level equation, analytic continuation of inverse
branches around critical values permutes its sheets.  The resulting monodromy
group is a finer invariant than the discriminant alone.  In the finite
Pochhammer case, the generic polynomial in \(a\) has prescribed geometrically
spaced zeros, but its critical values are not geometrically spaced.  In the
base variable the exponents form a sparse subset-sum pattern, and the
monodromy can change on algebraic loci in \(a\).

For infinite products, branch points accumulate through zero sets and radial
boundary singularities.  The appropriate global object is no longer a finite
branched covering but a Riemann surface with logarithmic ends.  The
root-of-unity expansions in \cref{sec:root-unity-a} and the radial formulas
for \((a;q)_\infty\) are local coordinate charts on this surface.

\chapter{Branch-aware numerical inversion and experiments}

\section{Why direct Newton iteration is not enough}

A black-box solve of \(F(z)=Y\) can converge to the wrong sheet, cross a
product zero, or lose all significant digits near a critical level.  The
analytic work above supplies three pieces of information a robust solver must
use:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
\item a local coordinate and asymptotic seed;
\item a branch label, usually inherited by continuation from a known point;
\item a representation of \(F\), \(F'\), and often \(F''\) that remains
stable in the selected regime.
\end{enumerate}

For products with very large or very small magnitude, solve the logarithmic
equation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:logarithmic-residual}
 G(z)=\Log F(z)-\Log Y=0
\end{equation}
instead of subtracting two nearly equal huge numbers.  The branch of the
logarithm must be continued consistently.

\section{Newton, Halley, and continuation equations}

Newton's step for \eqref{eq:logarithmic-residual} is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:log-newton}
 z_{r+1}=z_r-\frac{G(z_r)}{G'(z_r)}.
\end{equation}
When a stable second derivative is available, Halley's step is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:log-halley}
 z_{r+1}=z_r-
 \frac{2G(z_r)G'(z_r)}{2G'(z_r)^2-G(z_r)G''(z_r)}.
\end{equation}
Near a simple solution this is cubically convergent.  Near a ramification
point, neither method should be applied in the original coordinate; use
\(w=(Y-Y_c)^{1/m}\) or factor out the known multiplicity first.

To track a sheet as a passive parameter \(s\) changes, integrate
\begin{equation}\label{eq:continuation-ode}
 \frac{dz}{ds}=-\frac{F_s(z;s)}{F_z(z;s)}.
\end{equation}
Predictor--corrector continuation combines an ODE step with one or two Newton
corrections.  A small loop in parameter space can be used to compute the
monodromy permutation numerically.

\begin{algorithm}[Branch-aware inverse solver]\label{alg:branch-aware-solver}
Given a level \(Y\), parameters, and a desired branch:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
\item Identify the nearest regular point, zero, critical point, endpoint, root
of unity, or infinite regime.
\item Select the corresponding coordinate: \(Y-Y_0\), a fractional power,
\(-\Log q\), \(1/q\), \(q^\alpha\), or \(1/\Log Y\).
\item Evaluate the derived asymptotic expansion to obtain a branch-labelled
seed.
\item Evaluate products through logarithmic sums and Gaussian polynomials
through recurrence when quotient formulas contain removable singularities.
\item Correct with safeguarded Newton or Halley iteration.  On a real monotone
interval, retain a bracketing interval.
\item Verify the residual and, when possible, a posteriori certify uniqueness
with an interval bound on \(F'\).
\end{enumerate}
\end{algorithm}

\chapter{Conjectures and frontier problems}

The following statements are intentionally separated from the proved
results.  They are designed to be testable through exact computation,
validated numerics, or a sharply defined analytic program.

\section{Generic critical geometry of finite Pochhammer maps}

\begin{conjecture}[Generic simplicity in the base]\label{conj:generic-simple-qcritical}
Fix \(n\ge3\).  Outside a proper algebraic subset of \(a\in\C\), every finite
critical point of \(q\mapsto(a;q)_n\) is simple and distinct critical points
have distinct critical values.  Equivalently, the level polynomial has only
simple ramification away from the explicit collision loci forced by
\(a=0,1\) and roots of unity.
\end{conjecture}

The conjecture can be attacked by computing two resultants: one detecting a
multiple zero of \(\partial_q(a;q)_n\), and one detecting equality of two
critical values.  It is enough to exhibit a single parameter value outside
each resultant locus to prove that the exceptional set is proper for every
fixed \(n\).  A uniform proof in \(n\) would likely exploit the sparse
subset-sum support of the polynomial.

\begin{problem}[Monodromy groups]
Determine the generic monodromy group of the inverse in \(a\) and in \(q\)
for \((a;q)_n=Y\).  Decide when the geometric progression of roots forces a
proper subgroup of the full symmetric group.
\end{problem}

\section{Real and sectorial univalence}

The real critical points of \(a\mapsto(a;q)_n\) for \(0<q<1\) interlace the
positive zeros.  A complex analogue should control inverse branches in
sectors.

\begin{conjecture}[Complex thickening of real inverse sheets]
For fixed \(0<q<1\), each real monotonicity interval \(I\) determined by the
zeros and critical points of \(P_n(a)=(a;q)_n\) is contained in a maximal
simply connected domain \(D_I\), symmetric about the real axis, on which
\(P_n\) is univalent.  The boundary of \(D_I\) is assembled from critical
trajectories issuing from zeros and critical points.  As \(n\to\infty\),
domains whose real cores remain in a fixed compact set converge in the
Carath\'eodory sense to univalence domains of \((a;q)_\infty\).
\end{conjecture}

A practical first step is to seek domains on which a rotation of \(P_n'\)
has positive real part, so that the Noshiro--Warschawski criterion applies,
or to establish starlikeness through bounds for \(aP_n'(a)/P_n(a)\).
Numerical critical trajectories can be used to formulate the correct boundary
arcs before attempting a uniform proof.

\section{Root-of-unity transseries and monodromy}

\begin{conjecture}[All-order radial inverse at a root of unity]
Let \(\zeta\) be a primitive \(d\)-th root of unity and let \(a\) avoid the
radial singular set.  The inverse of
\((a;\zeta e^{-t})_\infty=Y\) admits, in each non-Stokes sector, a complete
transseries in odd and even powers of \(X^{-1}\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rootunity-inverse-conjectural}
 t\sim\frac{A_d(a)}{X}
 \left(1+\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{P_m(a,\zeta)}{X^m}\right),
 \qquad X=-\Log Y+C_d(a,\zeta),
\end{equation}
whose coefficients lie in the differential field generated by polylogarithms
of \(a^d\) and finite cyclic dilogarithmic sums.  Stokes jumps act by explicit
translations of \(X\) and generate the local inverse monodromy.
\end{conjecture}

Recent work on root-of-unity asymptotics, quantum dilogarithms, and modular
resurgence shows that the forward expansions carry nontrivial exponentially
small data \cite{Kopp2025,Fantini2025}.  The frontier question is how that data
transforms under compositional inversion.

\section{Large-argument log-periodic inversion}

\begin{conjecture}[Full log-periodic inverse expansion]
Let \(0<q<1\), \(\tau=\abs{\log q}\), \(a=-\e^L\), and
\(R=\sqrt{2\tau\,\Log Y}\).  The asymptotic inverse of
\((a;q)_\infty=Y\) has a complete expansion
\begin{equation}\label{eq:logperiodic-full-conjecture}
 L=R-\frac{\tau}{2}
 +\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{C_m(\theta)}{R^m},
 \qquad
 \theta=\left\{\frac{R-\tau/2}{\tau}\right\},
\end{equation}
where the \(C_m\) are periodic and smooth away from a finite set of matching
phases.  The first coefficient is predicted by the proved split-product
formula to be
\[
 C_1(\theta)=\tau\left(\frac{\tau}{8}-\Psi_q(\tau\theta)\right).
\]
Exponentially small corrections should complete the algebraic expansion to a
sectorial transseries.
\end{conjecture}

A Mellin--Poisson treatment should generate the higher periodic coefficients
and determine whether phase matching produces exponentially small Stokes
switches.

\section{Inverse Gaussian coefficients and partition coefficients}

\begin{problem}[Asymptotics of inverse partition coefficients]
\label{prob:inverse-partition-coefficients}
In a stable rectangle, write
\begin{equation}\label{eq:inverse-gaussian-coeffs}
 q=\sum_{m\ge1}d_m\left(\qbinom nk-1\right)^m.
\end{equation}
Determine the sign pattern, integrality properties, exact combinatorial model,
and asymptotic growth of \(d_m\).  In the unrestricted stable range the first
terms are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:inverse-gaussian-first}
 1,-2,5,-15,52,\ldots.
\end{equation}
Is there a sign-reversing model on rooted partition structures that explains
these coefficients directly?
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}[Global proof of continuous Gaussian monotonicity]
Prove or disprove \cref{conj:continuous-qbin-monotone}.  A proof would turn
the local base inverses of \(\mathcal C_q(x,y)\) into global real branches.
The logarithmic derivative is an explicit Lambert series, but its sign
involves a nontrivial cancellation among the three \(q\)-gamma terms.
\end{problem}

\section{Differential and \texorpdfstring{$q$}{q}-difference transcendence}

\begin{conjecture}[Generic nonholonomicity of compositional inverses]
Apart from algebraic and explicitly logarithmic degeneracies, a generic local
compositional inverse of \((a;q)_\infty\), \(\Gamma_q(x)\), or a
nonterminating \({}_r\phi_s\) is neither D-finite in its level nor
\(q\)-holonomic with respect to that level.
\end{conjecture}

The forward functions satisfy rich \(q\)-difference identities, but
compositionally inverting a nonlinear function rarely preserves linear
differential or recurrence structure.  A possible route is to combine
singularity proliferation of the inverse surface with closure theorems for
D-finite functions.  Exceptional parameter values should be classified rather
than excluded silently.

\section{Uniform double scaling at coalescing branches}

The local formulas in this report assume one distinguished singular
mechanism at a time.  Several important limits violate that separation:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
\item \(q\to1\) while the target approaches a critical value;
\item \(q\to\zeta\) while an active parameter approaches a colliding zero;
\item degree or order tends to infinity while the inverse approaches an edge;
\item a \(q\)-gamma level approaches the minimum while \(q\to1\).
\end{enumerate}

\begin{problem}[Canonical inverse double scalings]
Classify the normal forms obtained when two or more derivatives vanish at the
same asymptotic rate.  Determine when the inverse transition is governed by
a fold, cusp, Airy, Pearcey, or Lambert--\(W\) coordinate, and derive uniform
error bounds across the transition.
\end{problem}

For the last item, if \(x_q\) is the minimum point of \(\Gamma_q\) and
\(Y-\Gamma_q(x_q)\) shrinks together with \(1-q\), the appropriate expansion
must combine the \(q\)-Stirling correction with a square-root fold.  This is a
tractable first model.

\section{Formalization roadmap}

The theory separates naturally into layers suitable for mechanized proof.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
\item Formal power series: compositional inverse, Bell-polynomial coefficient
formulae, and the Puiseux normal form for a finite leading order.
\item Finite algebra: Pochhammer polynomial degrees, roots, derivatives,
reciprocity, cyclotomic valuations, and Gaussian positivity.
\item Real analysis: monotonicity intervals, convexity of \(\log\Gamma_q\),
and uniqueness of real inverse branches.
\item Complex analysis: uniform convergence of logarithmic products,
parameter differentiation, and radial root-of-unity asymptotics.
\item Certified numerics: interval enclosures for critical points and branch
continuation, producing machine-checkable evidence for the finite-degree
conjectures.
\end{enumerate}

The first two layers are largely independent of delicate asymptotics and could
be incorporated into the existing semi-formalized corpus early.  The inverse
Jacobian identities then provide a common API for all parameter inversions.

\section{A parameter-by-parameter checklist}

Given a new \(q\)-analogue \(F\), a systematic inverse study should answer the
following questions in order.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
\item What is the natural domain and which branches of \(q^x\), logarithms,
and infinite products define the forward function?
\item Which parameter is active?  If several are active, are there enough
independent observables for a square inverse system?
\item Where does the corresponding derivative vanish or blow up?  Remove all
removable singularities before classifying the germ.
\item Which endpoint or singular coordinate regularizes the forward
asymptotic: \(q\), \(-\log q\), \(1/q\), \(q^\alpha\), a logarithmic level, or
a fractional power?
\item What are the critical values and how do their cuts label inverse sheets?
\item Is there a real monotonicity or convexity theorem that upgrades a local
inverse to a global one?
\item Can a recurrence, logarithmic sum, or scaled representation evaluate the
forward function stably on that branch?
\item Which coefficients are proved, which are numerically inferred, and which
uniformity assumptions are needed when parameters coalesce?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Main conclusions}

The inverse theory of \(q\)-analogues is not a collection of unrelated
special-function inverses.  It is organized by a small number of normal forms:
regular Taylor reversion, algebraic Puiseux ramification, reciprocal inversion
at poles, logarithmic endpoint saturation, Laurent--Puiseux inversion at
infinity, and \(1/\Log Y\) inversion at radial essential singularities.  The
finite Pochhammer symbol realizes all algebraic forms; the infinite symbol
adds logarithmic ends and log-periodicity; Gaussian coefficients add
positivity and cyclotomic cancellations; \(q\)-gamma and \(q\)-beta add
convex two-sheeted real geometry; and basic hypergeometric functions assemble
these atoms into a broad parameterwise calculus.

The most promising next step is a computer-assisted branch atlas.  Exact
resultants can locate finite algebraic degeneracies, interval methods can
certify real branches, and asymptotic coordinates can seed continuation toward
\(q=0,1,-1,\infty\) and arbitrary roots of unity.  Such an atlas would turn
the present local theory into a searchable global resource and provide a
precise interface with the Fabius--Rvachev and exponent-sequence questions in
the surrounding ProveIt corpus.

\chapter{Universal reversion formulae through fifth order}

Let
\begin{equation}\label{eq:appendix-forward-reversion}
 y=A_1x+A_2x^2+A_3x^3+A_4x^4+A_5x^5+O(x^6),
 \qquad A_1\ne0.
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{align}
 x={}&\frac{y}{A_1}
 -\frac{A_2}{A_1^3}y^2
 +\frac{2A_2^2-A_1A_3}{A_1^5}y^3 \notag\\
 &+\frac{-5A_2^3+5A_1A_2A_3-A_1^2A_4}{A_1^7}y^4 \notag\\
 &+\frac{14A_2^4-21A_1A_2^2A_3+6A_1^2A_2A_4
 +3A_1^2A_3^2-A_1^3A_5}{A_1^9}y^5
 +O(y^6).
 \label{eq:appendix-inverse-reversion}
\end{align}
This single identity produces the origin expansions for finite Pochhammer
symbols, Gaussian coefficients, \(q\)-polylogarithms, and basic
hypergeometric series after the appropriate coefficients are substituted.

For logarithmic forward series
\begin{equation}\label{eq:appendix-log-forward}
 u=K_1t+\frac{K_2}{2!}t^2+\frac{K_3}{3!}t^3
 +\frac{K_4}{4!}t^4+O(t^5),
\end{equation}
substitution into \eqref{eq:appendix-inverse-reversion} gives
\begin{align}
 t={}&\frac{u}{K_1}-\frac{K_2}{2K_1^3}u^2
 +\frac{3K_2^2-K_1K_3}{6K_1^5}u^3 \notag\\
 &+\left(-\frac{K_4}{24K_1^5}
 +\frac{5K_2K_3}{12K_1^6}
 -\frac{5K_2^3}{8K_1^7}\right)u^4+O(u^5).
 \label{eq:appendix-log-inverse}
\end{align}
