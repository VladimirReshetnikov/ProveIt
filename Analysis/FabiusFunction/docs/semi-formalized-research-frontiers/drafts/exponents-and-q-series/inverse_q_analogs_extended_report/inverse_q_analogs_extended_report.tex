\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}
This report develops a parameter-wise inverse theory for finite and infinite
\(q\)-Pochhammer symbols, Gaussian and multinomial coefficients, \(q\)-numbers,
\(q\)-factorials, \(q\)-gamma, \(q\)-digamma, \(q\)-beta, Euler
\(q\)-exponentials, basic hypergeometric series, theta functions, and a
geometrically weighted family containing the Rvachev up-function and Fabius
function.  Every inverse is indexed by the parameter being recovered, a real
interval or complex sheet, and a normalization path.  This avoids the common
but serious ambiguity of speaking about ``the inverse'' of a multiparameter
special function.

The analysis is organized around a universal reversion calculus.  Regular
branches are computed to arbitrary order by Bell-polynomial recurrences,
logarithmic coordinates, and multivariate Lagrange--Good inversion.  Critical
branches are treated by Newton--Puiseux series.  Endpoint inverses are obtained
by asymptotic reversion, including fractional powers, logarithms, Lambert
\(W\), periodic corrections, and root-of-unity radial transseries.

Several concrete results sharpen the earlier ProveIt inverse report.  For the
finite product \(F_n(a,q)=(a;q)_n\), the complete nonconstant critical locus of
the \(q\)-slice at \(q=-1\) is
\[
 a_*(2m)=\frac1{2m-1},
 \qquad
 a_*(2m+1)=-\frac1{2m+1}.
\]
All these slices have an ordinary quadratic fold except
\[
 (a;q)_3\big|_{a=-1/3}=\frac{32}{27}+\frac4{27}(q+1)^3,
\]
which has an exact cubic inverse.  In the full \((a,q)\)-plane this exceptional
point is nevertheless a nondegenerate Morse saddle; the cubic occurs because
the fixed-\(a\) line is tangent to a null direction of the quadratic form.  At
a primitive \(m\)-th root \(\zeta\), colliding zeros in the argument have
multiplicity equal to the number of indices in one congruence class modulo
\(m\), giving explicit Puiseux exponents.

For the infinite product on a radial path \(q=\zeta e^{-t}\), \(\zeta^m=1\),
\(|a|<1\), we derive
\[
 \log(a;\zeta e^{-t})_\infty
 =-\frac{\Polylogarithm_2(a^m)}{m^2t}+C_\zeta(a)-\Dcal_\zeta(a)t+O(t^2),
\]
with explicit convergent series for \(C_\zeta\) and \(\Dcal_\zeta\), and revert
it to
\[
 t=\frac{A}{X}+\frac{\Dcal_\zeta A^2}{X^3}+O(X^{-4}),
 \qquad A=\frac{\Polylogarithm_2(a^m)}{m^2},\quad X=-\log y+C_\zeta(a).
\]
The cases \(q\to1\) and \(q\to-1\) receive higher-order real formulas.  A
second, nonuniform regime \(a=q^x\) produces logarithmic corrections and a
Lambert-\(W\) preconditioner.

For Gaussian coefficients, coefficient positivity gives the global positive
base inverse, reciprocal symmetry gives the \(q=\infty\) inverse, and
palindromicity turns the expansion at \(q=1\) into cumulant reversion for the
partition-area distribution.  At roots of unity, the cyclotomic valuation is
exactly a carry indicator and therefore every forced zero is simple.  The
continuous lower-parameter inverse has two sheets meeting in a square-root fold
at the symmetry center.  Similar formulas are given for \(q\)-multinomials and
for every positive palindromic \(q\)-polynomial.

For \(\Gamma_q(x)\), the report separates the fractional-power Newton polygon
at \(q=0\), the ordinary reversion at \(q=1\), and the reciprocal \(q=\infty\)
regime.  In particular,
\[
 \log\frac{\Gamma_{e^{-t}}(x)}{\Gamma(x)}
 =-\frac{(x-1)(x-2)}4t
 +\frac{(x-1)(x-2)(2x+3)}{144}t^2+O(t^3).
\]
The corresponding \(q\)-beta expansion has a critical curve \(xy=1\), on
which base inversion changes from an ordinary Taylor branch to a square-root
branch.  The \(q\)-digamma base inverse has an analogous critical value
\(x=3/2\).

Finally, the random series
\[
 X_q=(1-q)\sum_{j\ge0}q^jU_j,
 \qquad U_j\sim\operatorname{Unif}[-1,1],
\]
contains the Rvachev law at \(q=1/2\).  Its variance recovers the deformation
parameter exactly:
\[
 \Variance X_q=\frac{1-q}{3(1+q)},
 \qquad q=\frac{1-3\Variance X_q}{1+3\Variance X_q}.
\]
All even cumulants give independent monotone inverse observables.  The
accompanying Python program regenerates the symbolic tables, numerical checks,
and figures at high precision.

\chapter*{Notation and branch conventions}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Notation and branch conventions}
We use
\[
 (a;q)_n=\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}(1-aq^j),
 \qquad
 (a;q)_\infty=\prod_{j=0}^{\infty}(1-aq^j),\quad |q|<1,
\]
\[
 \GaussianBinomial{n}{k}{q}=\frac{(q;q)_n}{(q;q)_k(q;q)_{n-k}},
 \qquad
 [x]_q=\frac{1-q^x}{1-q}.
\]
Unless stated otherwise, \(n,k\) are integers with \(0\le k\le n\), while
\(a,q,x,y\) may be complex.  A symbol such as \(F^{-1}_{q,\gamma}\) means the
inverse with respect to \(q\) on the sheet \(\gamma\); it never means a
simultaneous inverse in all arguments.  Complex powers and logarithms carry an
explicit or contextually fixed branch.  Radial limits use
\[
 q=\zeta e^{-t},\qquad \Re t>0,
\]
and continuation from positive real \(t\).  For a primitive \(m\)-th root
\(\zeta\), this convention fixes the branches in \(C_\zeta(a)\) and in the
inverse transseries.

\part{A general calculus of parameter-wise inversion}

\chapter{Inverse problems, sheets, and identifiability}

\section{One scalar equation does not recover every parameter}
Let
\[
 F(p_1,\ldots,p_d)=y.
\]
For \(d>1\), this is generically a hypersurface of dimension \(d-1\), not a
single point.  A well-posed inverse problem must therefore do at least one of
the following:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item fix all parameters except one;
\item provide \(d\) independent observables \(F_1,\ldots,F_d\);
\item impose a normalization, variational principle, or statistical prior;
\item select a discrete parameter by an external integrality condition.
\end{enumerate}
This elementary point is especially important for \(q\)-special functions,
where ``argument'', ``base'', ``order'', and ``shape parameter'' are all
plausible inverse variables.

\begin{definition}[Partial inverse branch]
Fix auxiliary parameters \(\lambda\), write \(F(p;\lambda)\), and choose a
regular point \((p_0,\lambda_0)\).  A partial inverse branch is a function
\(p=G(y;\lambda)\) satisfying
\[
 F(G(y;\lambda);\lambda)=y,
 \qquad G(F(p_0;\lambda_0);\lambda_0)=p_0,
\]
together with a specified real interval or complex continuation path.
\end{definition}

\section{Real branches and complex coverings}
If \(F\colon I\to\RealNumbers\) is continuous and strictly monotone, it has one ordinary
real inverse on \(F(I)\).  In the complex plane the natural object is instead
\[
 \mathscr R_F=\{(p,y)\in\ComplexNumbers^2:F(p)=y\},
\]
projected to the \(y\)-plane.  At a regular point \(F_p\ne0\), the projection
is locally biholomorphic.  A point with \(F_p=0\) creates a branch value; a loop
around it permutes inverse sheets.  Finite \(q\)-Pochhammer products and
Gaussian coefficients give algebraic coverings.  Infinite products and basic
hypergeometric functions give infinite-sheeted coverings, often with
accumulating singular structure near \(|q|=1\).

\begin{principle}[Branch data are part of the answer]
A numerical value called ``the inverse'' is incomplete unless it records at
least one of: a real interval, a base point and continuation path, an asymptotic
condition, a logarithm index, or a root label transported continuously from a
regular parameter value.
\end{principle}

\section{Jacobian identifiability}
Suppose \(d\) observables are available:
\[
 \bm F(\bm p)=\bm y,
 \qquad
 \bm F=(F_1,\ldots,F_d).
\]

\begin{theorem}[\statusproved\ Local identifiability]
If the Jacobian
\[
 J(\bm p_0)=\left(\frac{\partial F_i}{\partial p_j}(\bm p_0)\right)_{i,j=1}^d
\]
is nonsingular, then \(\bm p\) is a locally analytic function of \(\bm y\).
The first-order sensitivity is
\[
 \Differential\bm p=J(\bm p)^{-1}\Differential\bm y.
\]
If \(J\) is singular, either identifiability fails or a fractional-power
inverse must be used after resolving the singularity.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
This is the analytic inverse-function theorem.  Differentiating
\(\bm F(\bm p(\bm y))=\bm y\) gives the sensitivity formula.
\end{proof}

For noisy data with covariance matrix \(\Sigma_y\), the linearized covariance
is
\[
 \Sigma_p\approx J^{-1}\Sigma_y(J^{-1})^*.
\]
Thus the smallest singular value of a suitably scaled Jacobian measures local
recoverability.  Near a fold it tends to zero like the square root of the
distance to the critical value; near a cusp it vanishes faster.

\section{Dimensionless condition numbers}
For a scalar inverse \(p=G(y)\), define the absolute and relative condition
numbers
\[
 \kappa_{\rm abs}=\AbsoluteValue{G'(y)}=\frac1{\AbsoluteValue{F'(p)}},
 \qquad
 \kappa_{\rm rel}=\AbsoluteValue{\frac{y}{pF'(p)}}.
\]
A large condition number is not a defect in an algorithm; it is an intrinsic
property of the inverse problem.  The right numerical response is to change
coordinates, add an observable, or return a validated set rather than a
misleading decimal.

\chapter{Regular analytic reversion}

\section{The scalar inverse jet}
Let \(y_0=f(x_0)\), put \(\Delta=y-y_0\), and write
\[
 f(x_0+u)=y_0+f_1u+\frac{f_2}{2!}u^2+\frac{f_3}{3!}u^3+\cdots,
 \qquad f_r=f^{(r)}(x_0).
\]
When \(f_1\ne0\), the inverse has a convergent series
\[
 u=c_1\Delta+c_2\Delta^2+c_3\Delta^3+\cdots.
\]

\begin{theorem}[\statusproved\ Bell-polynomial recursion]
The coefficients are determined by
\[
 c_1=\frac1{f_1},
 \qquad
 c_n=-\frac1{n!f_1}\sum_{r=2}^{n}f_r
 B_{n,r}(1!c_1,2!c_2,\ldots,(n-r+1)!c_{n-r+1}),
\]
where \(B_{n,r}\) is the exponential partial Bell polynomial.  In particular,
\begin{align*}
 c_2&=-\frac{f_2}{2f_1^3},\\
 c_3&=\frac{3f_2^2-f_1f_3}{6f_1^5},\\
 c_4&=\frac{-15f_2^3+10f_1f_2f_3-f_1^2f_4}{24f_1^7},\\
 c_5&=\frac{105f_2^4-105f_1f_2^2f_3+10f_1^2f_3^2
 +15f_1^2f_2f_4-f_1^3f_5}{120f_1^9}.
\end{align*}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Substitute \(u(\Delta)=\sum_{n\ge1}c_n\Delta^n\) into the Taylor series for
\(f\).  The coefficient of \(\Delta^n\) in \(u^r\) is
\[
 \frac{1}{n!}B_{n,r}(1!c_1,2!c_2,\ldots).
\]
The coefficient of \(\Delta\) gives \(c_1\); for \(n\ge2\), the term
\(f_1c_n\) is the only occurrence of \(c_n\), so it can be isolated
recursively.
\end{proof}

\section{Lagrange--Bürmann form}
If \(y-y_0=u/\phi(u)\), with \(\phi(0)\ne0\), then
\[
 u=\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{(y-y_0)^n}{n!}
 \left[\frac{\Differential^{n-1}}{\Differential u^{n-1}}\phi(u)^n\right]_{u=0}.
\]
More generally, for analytic \(H\),
\[
 H(u(y))=H(0)+\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{(y-y_0)^n}{n!}
 \left[\frac{\Differential^{n-1}}{\Differential u^{n-1}}
 H'(u)\phi(u)^n\right]_{u=0}.
\]
This is often the cleanest route when a \(q\)-product factors into a simple
zero times a regular unit.

\section{Logarithmic reversion}
Products should normally be inverted after taking a logarithm.  If
\[
 \log\frac{F(p)}{F(p_0)}=A_1u+A_2u^2+A_3u^3+\cdots,
 \qquad u=p-p_0,
\]
and \(s=\log(y/F(p_0))\), then the same recursion applies to
\(s=A_1u+A_2u^2+\cdots\).  This has three advantages:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item products become sums and derivatives become Lambert series;
\item relative error, rather than absolute scale, is controlled;
\item reciprocal or palindromic symmetry frequently eliminates all odd or all
even terms after centering.
\end{enumerate}

For the common form
\[
 s=A u+B u^2+O(u^4),
\]
with no cubic term, one obtains
\[
 u=\frac{s}{A}-\frac{B}{A^3}s^2+\frac{2B^2}{A^5}s^3+O(s^4).
\]

\section{Auxiliary-parameter derivatives}
Let \(F(G(y,\lambda),\lambda)=y\).  Subscripts denote total derivatives after
substitution of \(p=G(y,\lambda)\).

\begin{proposition}[\statusproved\ First and second inverse sensitivities]
At a regular point,
\begin{align*}
 G_y&=\frac1{F_p},&
 G_{\lambda_i}&=-\frac{F_{\lambda_i}}{F_p},\\
 G_{yy}&=-\frac{F_{pp}}{F_p^3},&
 G_{y\lambda_i}&=\frac{F_{pp}F_{\lambda_i}-F_pF_{p\lambda_i}}{F_p^3},\\
 G_{\lambda_i\lambda_j}
 &=-\frac{F_{pp}G_{\lambda_i}G_{\lambda_j}
 +F_{p\lambda_i}G_{\lambda_j}+F_{p\lambda_j}G_{\lambda_i}
 +F_{\lambda_i\lambda_j}}{F_p}.
\end{align*}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Differentiate the defining identity once and twice.  No independent inverse
formula is needed; higher jets follow recursively by multivariate
Fa\`a di Bruno.
\end{proof}

\section{Multivariate Lagrange--Good inversion}
A convenient canonical system is
\[
 w_i=z_i\phi_i(\bm w),\qquad i=1,\ldots,d,
\]
with \(\phi_i(\bm0)\ne0\).  Good's formula \cite{Good1960} states that for a
formal power series \(H\),
\[
 H(\bm w(\bm z))
 =\sum_{\bm n\in\NaturalNumbers^d}\bm z^{\bm n}
 [\bm x^{\bm n}]
 H(\bm x)\prod_{i=1}^d\phi_i(\bm x)^{n_i}
 \det\left(\delta_{ij}-\frac{x_j}{\phi_i(\bm x)}
 \frac{\partial\phi_i}{\partial x_j}(\bm x)\right).
\]
A general locally invertible vector equation can be brought to this form after
a linear change of variables.  In practice, however, a coefficient-by-
coefficient Jacobian recursion is often simpler to implement and easier to
certify.

\chapter{Singular inversion and asymptotic reversion}

\section{Simple folds and Puiseux coefficients}
Assume
\[
 f'(x_*)=0,\qquad f''(x_*)=f_2\ne0,
 \qquad y_*=f(x_*).
\]
Then the inverse is not analytic in \(y-y_*\), but it is analytic in a square
root.

\begin{theorem}[\statusproved\ Fold normal form]
With \(\Delta=y-y_*\), the two local sheets are
\begin{align*}
 x-x_*
 &=a_1\Delta^{1/2}-\frac{f_3}{3f_2^2}\Delta
 +a_1\frac{5f_3^2-3f_2f_4}{36f_2^3}\Delta^{3/2}\\
 &\quad+
 \frac{-9f_2^2f_5+45f_2f_3f_4-40f_3^3}{270f_2^5}\Delta^2
 +O(\Delta^{5/2}),
\end{align*}
where \(a_1=\pm\sqrt{2/f_2}\), with compatible branches of the square root.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Put \(x-x_*=a_1s+a_2s^2+a_3s^3+\cdots\), \(s^2=\Delta\), substitute into
the Taylor series, and solve successively.  The coefficient of \(s^2\) gives
\(a_1^2f_2/2=1\); the remaining displayed coefficients follow by triangular
elimination.
\end{proof}

More generally, if
\[
 f'(x_*)=\cdots=f^{(r-1)}(x_*)=0,
 \qquad f^{(r)}(x_*)\ne0,
\]
then \(r\) sheets meet and
\[
 x-x_*\sim\left(\frac{r!(y-y_*)}{f^{(r)}(x_*)}\right)^{1/r}.
\]
Continuation once around \(y_*\) cycles these sheets.

\section{Newton polygons for mixed powers}\label{sec:newton-polygon-placeholder}
Near \(q=0\), continuous parameters often produce generalized expansions such
as
\[
 s=c_1q^\alpha+c_2q+c_3q^{2\alpha}+\cdots,
 \qquad 0<\alpha<1.
\]
The next correction is not determined by the visual order in which the terms
were written; it is determined by the lower convex hull of exponent pairs.
The resulting inverse is a Hahn or Puiseux series whose exponents lie in the
additive semigroup generated by \(1\) and \(\alpha\).  Rational \(\alpha\)
produces an ordinary Puiseux series after a finite ramification.  Irrational
\(\alpha\) produces a well-ordered generalized series but no finite
ramification.

\section{Large-log inversion}
A recurring endpoint form is
\[
 \log y=-\frac{A}{t}+C-Dt+Et^3-Ft^5+\cdots,
 \qquad A\ne0,
\]
with \(t\to0\).  Put \(X=-\log y+C\).  Formal reversion gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:large-log-reversion}
 t=\frac{A}{X}+\frac{DA^2}{X^3}
 +\frac{A^3(2D^2-AE)}{X^5}
 +\frac{A^4(A^2F-6ADE+5D^3)}{X^7}+\cdots.
\end{equation}
This formula is the main engine for radial inverses of infinite
\(q\)-products.

\section{Logarithmic corrections and Lambert \texorpdfstring{\(W\)}{W}}
A second canonical form is
\[
 \Lambda=\frac{A}{t}+B\log t+C+O(t).
\]
Ignoring \(O(t)\) but retaining the logarithm already gives a much better
preconditioner than \(A/\Lambda\).  If \(B\ne0\), set
\[
 R=\frac{\Lambda-C}{B}-\log\frac{A}{B}.
\]
Then the truncated equation is solved exactly by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lambert-preconditioner}
 t=-\frac{A}{B\,W_k(-e^{-R})},
\end{equation}
where the branch \(k\) is selected to make \(t\) approach the prescribed ray.
This does not replace the full asymptotic series; it supplies an excellent
starting value for Newton or interval Newton iteration.

\section{Exponentially small and periodic corrections}
Euler--Maclaurin expansions may miss terms of size \(e^{-c/t}\), while splitting
a geometric product at its largest term can produce a bounded periodic
function of \(\log x/\log(1/q)\).  Both effects survive inversion.  A complete
inverse asymptotic may therefore have the transseries form
\[
 p(y)\sim p_{\rm alg}(y)+\Omega(\log y)+
 \sum_{r\ge1}e^{-rS(p_{\rm alg})}\,p_r(y),
\]
not a single power series.  The archive's numerical algorithms preserve these
corrections by evaluating the original product rather than only its formal
jet.

\chapter{A universal theory for positive and palindromic \texorpdfstring{\(q\)}{q}-polynomials}

\section{Positive-base inversion}
Let
\[
 P(q)=\sum_{j=0}^{D}c_jq^j,
 \qquad c_j\ge0,
\]
with at least one \(c_j>0\) for \(j>0\).

\begin{theorem}[\statusproved\ Global positive inverse]
The restriction \(P:(0,\infty)\to(c_0,\infty)\) is strictly increasing and
therefore has exactly one inverse for every \(y>c_0\).
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For \(q>0\),
\[
 P'(q)=\sum_{j=1}^{D}jc_jq^{j-1}>0.
\]
Also \(P(q)\to c_0\) as \(q\downarrow0\) and \(P(q)\to\infty\) as
\(q\to\infty\).
\end{proof}

If \(c_1>0\), the small-base inverse is
\[
 q=\frac{y-c_0}{c_1}-\frac{c_2}{c_1^3}(y-c_0)^2
 +\frac{2c_2^2-c_1c_3}{c_1^5}(y-c_0)^3+O((y-c_0)^4).
\]
If \(c_1=\cdots=c_{r-1}=0\), \(c_r>0\), the inverse begins with the
\(r\)-th root \(((y-c_0)/c_r)^{1/r}\).

\section{Cumulant coordinates at \texorpdfstring{\(q=1\)}{q=1}}
Assume \(P(1)>0\), and define a random variable \(J\) by
\[
 \Pr(J=j)=\frac{c_j}{P(1)}.
\]
Then
\[
 \frac{P(e^t)}{P(1)}=\mathbb E(e^{tJ}),
 \qquad
 \log\frac{P(e^t)}{P(1)}
 =\sum_{r\ge1}\frac{\kappa_r(J)}{r!}t^r.
\]
Thus inversion of \(P\) at \(q=1\) is exactly cumulant reversion.  This
probabilistic language is not metaphorical: the coefficients of a Gaussian
polynomial enumerate partition areas, and those areas are the random variable
\(J\).

\begin{theorem}[\statusproved\ Palindromic reduction]
If \(c_j=c_{D-j}\), then
\[
 \kappa_1(J)=D/2,
 \qquad
 \kappa_{2r+1}(J)=0\quad(r\ge1).
\]
Consequently, with \(s=\log(y/P(1))\),
\[
 s=\frac D2t+\frac{\kappa_2}{2}t^2+\frac{\kappa_4}{24}t^4+\cdots,
\]
and the positive local inverse is obtained by ordinary reversion in \(t=\log q\).
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Palindromicity makes \(J-D/2\) symmetric in distribution.  Its odd moments and
odd cumulants vanish.
\end{proof}

\section{Reciprocity and the inverse at infinity}
If
\[
 P(q)=q^DP(q^{-1}),
 \qquad P(q)=1+c_1q+c_2q^2+\cdots,
\]
set \(R=y^{-1/D}\) on a chosen branch.  Reversion at \(Q=q^{-1}=0\) yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:palindromic-large-inverse}
 q=R^{-1}-\frac{c_1}{D}
 +\left(\frac{(D-1)c_1^2}{2D^2}-\frac{c_2}{D}\right)R+O(R^2).
\end{equation}
The formula simultaneously describes \(q\to+\infty\), \(q\to-\infty\), and
complex rays; the branch of \(R=y^{-1/D}\) labels the sheet.

\section{Cyclotomic zeros}
For a polynomial product, a root of unity may force factors to vanish.  If
\[
 P(q)=\Phi_m(q)^\nu U(q),\qquad U(\zeta)\ne0,
\]
near a primitive \(m\)-th root \(\zeta\), then
\[
 P(q)=\bigl(\Phi_m'(\zeta)\bigr)^\nu U(\zeta)(q-\zeta)^\nu
 (1+O(q-\zeta)).
\]
Hence the inverse at target zero has \(\nu\) Puiseux sheets,
\[
 q-\zeta\sim
 \left(\frac{y}{(\Phi_m'(\zeta))^\nu U(\zeta)}\right)^{1/\nu}.
\]
This simple observation controls all root-of-unity inverses of
\(q\)-factorials and many of those of Gaussian coefficients.

\part{Finite and infinite \texorpdfstring{\(q\)}{q}-Pochhammer inverses}

\chapter{Finite \texorpdfstring{\(q\)}{q}-Pochhammer inversion in the argument}

\section{The polynomial covering in \texorpdfstring{\(a\)}{a}}
Fix \(q\ne0\) and write
\[
 F_n(a;q)=(a;q)_n=\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}(1-aq^j).
\]
If the numbers \(q^{-j}\) are distinct, \(F_n\) is a degree-\(n\) polynomial
with simple zeros
\[
 a_j=q^{-j},\qquad 0\le j<n.
\]
The inverse equation \(F_n(a;q)=y\) therefore has \(n\) complex sheets.  Their
branch points are the critical values \(F_n(a_*;q)\) with
\[
 \frac{\partial}{\partial a}\log F_n(a;q)
 =-\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{q^j}{1-aq^j}=0.
\]
The discriminant
\[
 \Disc_a(F_n(a;q)-y)
\]
is a polynomial in \(q\) and \(y\); its zero set is the branch curve.  For
large \(n\), it is usually better to track critical points numerically than to
expand this discriminant.

\section{The inverse jet at \texorpdfstring{\(a=0\)}{a=0}}
The finite \(q\)-binomial theorem gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:finite-qpoch-qbinom}
 (a;q)_n=\sum_{r=0}^{n}(-a)^rq^{\binom r2}\GaussianBinomial{n}{r}{q}.
\end{equation}
Let \(u=1-y\), and assume \([n]_q\ne0\).  Reversion of
\[
 u=[n]_qa-q\GaussianBinomial{n}{2}{q}a^2+q^3\GaussianBinomial{n}{3}{q}a^3-\cdots
\]
gives the explicit connection between inverse Pochhammer coefficients and
Gaussian coefficients:
\begin{align}
 a={}&\frac{u}{[n]_q}
 +\frac{q\GaussianBinomial{n}{2}{q}}{[n]_q^3}u^2 \notag\\
 &+\left(
 \frac{2q^2\GaussianBinomial{n}{2}{q}^2}{[n]_q^5}
 -\frac{q^3\GaussianBinomial{n}{3}{q}}{[n]_q^4}
 \right)u^3+O(u^4).
 \label{eq:argument-inverse-at-zero}
\end{align}
All higher terms are obtained from the Bell recursion in
\Cref{thm:argument-all-order}, below.

\begin{theorem}[\statusproved\ All-order argument inverse at one]
\label{thm:argument-all-order}
Let
\[
 b_r=(-1)^{r+1}q^{\binom r2}\GaussianBinomial{n}{r}{q},
 \qquad
 u=\sum_{r\ge1}b_ra^r.
\]
If \(b_1=[n]_q\ne0\), the coefficient of \(u^N\) in the inverse \(a(u)\) is
\[
 [u^N]a(u)=\frac1N[z^{N-1}]
 \left(\frac{z}{\sum_{r\ge1}b_rz^r}\right)^N.
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
This is Lagrange--Bürmann applied to \(u=a/\phi(a)\), with
\(\phi(a)=a/u(a)\).
\end{proof}

At a cyclotomic base with \([n]_q=0\), the branch at \(a=0\) is singular.  If
the first nonzero coefficient in \eqref{eq:finite-qpoch-qbinom} has index
\(r\), then \(a\asymp(1-y)^{1/r}\).  Thus the same root-of-unity geometry
appears whether one inverts in \(a\) or in \(q\).

\section{Local inverse at a simple zero}
Assume \(q^r\ne q^s\) for \(0\le r<s<n\).  At \(a_r=q^{-r}\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:simple-zero-derivative}
 F_n'(a_r;q)=(-1)^{r+1}q^{-r(r-1)/2}(q;q)_r(q;q)_{n-1-r}.
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[\statusproved\ Zero-sheet expansion]
For each \(r\), the inverse branch satisfying \(a(0)=q^{-r}\) is
\[
 a(y)=q^{-r}+\frac{y}{F_n'(q^{-r};q)}
 -\frac{F_n''(q^{-r};q)}{2F_n'(q^{-r};q)^3}y^2+O(y^3).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The generic inverse coefficients apply because
\eqref{eq:simple-zero-derivative} is nonzero.  To derive that derivative,
remove the vanishing factor \(1-aq^r\).  For \(j<r\), use
\(1-q^{j-r}=-q^{j-r}(1-q^{r-j})\), and multiply the resulting powers of
\(q\).
\end{proof}

\section{Colliding zeros at roots of unity}
Let \(\zeta\) be primitive of order \(m\).  At \(q=\zeta\), the zero
\(a=\zeta^{-r}\) is repeated once for every index \(j\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}\)
congruent to \(r\pmod m\).  Its multiplicity is
\[
 \nu_r(n,m)=
 \begin{cases}
 1+\Floor{(n-1-r)/m},&0\le r<\min(m,n),\\
 0,&r\ge n.
 \end{cases}
\]

\begin{theorem}[\statusproved\ Cyclotomic argument ramification]
At \(q=\zeta\), write
\[
 (a;\zeta)_n=(1-a\zeta^r)^{\nu_r}U_r(a),
 \qquad U_r(\zeta^{-r})\ne0.
\]
Then the inverse branches at target \(y=0\) satisfy
\[
 a-\zeta^{-r}
 \sim-\zeta^{-r}
 \left(\frac{y}{U_r(\zeta^{-r})}\right)^{1/\nu_r}.
\]
Thus the ramification index is exactly the occupancy of one congruence class
of exponents modulo \(m\).
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
All factors with \(j\equiv r\pmod m\) coincide with
\(1-a\zeta^r\); all others are nonzero at \(a=\zeta^{-r}\).  Apply the
multiplicity form of the Puiseux theorem.
\end{proof}

\section{Exact inverses at \texorpdfstring{\(q=1\)}{q=1} and
\texorpdfstring{\(q=-1\)}{q=-1}}
At \(q=1\),
\[
 (a;1)_n=(1-a)^n,
 \qquad
 a=1-\omega_n^j y^{1/n},\quad 0\le j<n.
\]
At \(q=-1\), put
\[
 E=\Ceiling{n/2},\qquad O=\Floor{n/2}.
\]
Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qminus1-argument}
 (a;-1)_n=(1-a)^E(1+a)^O.
\end{equation}
For even \(n=2m\), this is \((1-a^2)^m\), so every inverse sheet is explicit:
\[
 a=\pm\sqrt{1-\omega_m^j y^{1/m}},
 \qquad 0\le j<m.
\]
At \(y=1\), all sheets pair at \(a=0\), and the real inverse has the expected
square-root singularity.

For odd \(n=2m+1\), the derivative of
\((1-a)^{m+1}(1+a)^m\) vanishes at
\[
 a=-\frac1{2m+1}.
\]
The corresponding critical value is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:odd-argument-critical-value}
 Y_m=\left(\frac{2m+2}{2m+1}\right)^{m+1}
 \left(\frac{2m}{2m+1}\right)^m.
\end{equation}
The two real argument branches merge there in a quadratic fold.  This same
parameter will reappear as the critical \emph{base} parameter at \(q=-1\), a
nonaccidental intersection studied in \Cref{sec:minus-one-complete}.

\section{Large-base clustering of argument sheets}
For fixed \(n\), the zeros are \(1,q^{-1},\ldots,q^{1-n}\).  As
\(|q|\to\infty\), one zero remains near \(a=1\), while the other \(n-1\) zeros
collapse toward the origin on distinct scales.  The expansion
\eqref{eq:finite-qpoch-qbinom} shows that a branch with bounded target and
\(a\to0\) typically has
\[
 a\sim\frac{1-y}{[n]_q}\sim(1-y)q^{1-n}.
\]
Branches attached to the zero \(q^{-r}\) should instead be rescaled by
\(a=q^{-r}(1+u)\).  The resulting Newton polygon separates the root cluster
into \(n-1\) geometric layers.  This is a useful example where a single
unscaled expansion in \(a\) cannot be uniform across all inverse sheets.

\chapter{Finite \texorpdfstring{\(q\)}{q}-Pochhammer inversion in the base}

\section{Logarithmic derivatives and the critical set}
For fixed \(a\),
\begin{align}
 \partial_q\log(a;q)_n
 &=-a\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{jq^{j-1}}{1-aq^j},
 \label{eq:qpoch-q-logderivative}\\
 \partial_a^r\log(a;q)_n
 &=-(r-1)!\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{q^{jr}}{(1-aq^j)^r}.
 \label{eq:qpoch-a-higher}
\end{align}
A base inverse branch is regular precisely when the first expression is finite
and nonzero.  Multiplying it by the product denominator produces an explicit
polynomial equation for the critical set in \((a,q)\).  Eliminating \(q\) from
that equation and \((a;q)_n-y=0\) gives the branch-value discriminant.

An alternative expansion, valid analytically where the sums converge and
formally elsewhere, is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:finite-lambert}
 \log(a;q)_n=-\sum_{r\ge1}\frac{a^r}{r}
 \frac{1-q^{nr}}{1-q^r}.
\end{equation}
It is often the most efficient source of high derivatives with respect to
\(q\).

\section{All-order inversion at \texorpdfstring{\(q=0\)}{q=0}}
Assume \(n\ge2\) and \(a\notin\{0,1\}\).  Factor the constant term:
\[
 \frac{(a;q)_n}{1-a}=\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}(1-aq^j)
 =\exp\left(\sum_{r\ge1}\ell_r^{(n)}(a)q^r\right),
\]
where
\begin{equation}\label{eq:q0-logcoeff}
 \ell_r^{(n)}(a)
 =-\sum_{\substack{d\mid r\\1\le d\le n-1}}
 \frac{a^{r/d}}{r/d}.
\end{equation}
The forward coefficients are complete Bell polynomials in the \(\ell_r\), and
the inverse coefficients follow from the scalar recursion.  This is an
all-order arithmetic description: divisors of the exponent control each jet.

For \(n\ge4\), define
\[
 \eta=\frac{(1-a)-y}{a(1-a)}.
\]
Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:q0-finite-inverse}
 q=\eta-\eta^2+(1+a)\eta^3+O(\eta^4).
\end{equation}
The cubic coefficient changes to \(2+a\) for \(n=3\), while for \(n=2\) the
relation is exactly \(q=\eta\).  For the infinite product, the stable
all-order coefficients begin
\begin{equation}\label{eq:q0-infinite-inverse-preview}
 q=\eta-\eta^2+(1+a)\eta^3-(1+4a)\eta^4
 +(1+11a+3a^2)\eta^5+O(\eta^6).
\end{equation}

\section{All-order inversion at \texorpdfstring{\(q=1\)}{q=1}}
Use the exponentially natural coordinate \(q=e^{-t}\).  Put
\[
 S_r(n)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}j^r.
\]
From
\[
 \log(1-ae^{-x})
 =\log(1-a)+\sum_{r\ge1}
 \frac{(-1)^{r+1}}{r!}\Polylogarithm_{1-r}(a)x^r,
\]
we obtain the exact formal jet
\begin{equation}\label{eq:finite-q1-forward}
 \log\frac{(a;e^{-t})_n}{(1-a)^n}
 =\sum_{r\ge1}A_rt^r,
 \qquad
 A_r=\frac{(-1)^{r+1}}{r!}\Polylogarithm_{1-r}(a)S_r(n).
\end{equation}
When \(A_1\ne0\), let
\[
 s=\log\frac{y}{(1-a)^n}.
\]
Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:finite-q1-inverse}
 t=\frac{s}{A_1}-\frac{A_2}{A_1^3}s^2
 +\frac{2A_2^2-A_1A_3}{A_1^5}s^3+O(s^4),
 \qquad q=e^{-t}.
\end{equation}
This formula applies to complex \(a\), provided the logarithms and
polylogarithms are continued consistently.  The degeneracies \(a=0\) and
\(n=1\) correspond to a product independent of \(q\).  The value \(a=1\)
puts the target at an identically vanishing factor and requires a joint scaling
of \(a-1\) and \(q-1\).

\section{The complete \texorpdfstring{\(q=-1\)}{q=-1} critical classification}
\label{sec:minus-one-complete}
At \(q=-1\), recall
\[
 F_n(a,-1)=(1-a)^E(1+a)^O,
 \quad E=\Ceiling{n/2},\quad O=\Floor{n/2}.
\]
Evaluating \eqref{eq:qpoch-q-logderivative} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qminus1-logderivative}
 \left.\partial_q\log F_n(a,q)\right|_{q=-1}
 =\frac{aE(E-1)}{1-a}-\frac{aO^2}{1+a}.
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[\statusproved\ Exact nonconstant critical parameter at
\(q=-1\)]
Let \(n\ge3\), and exclude \(a=0,\pm1\) when they make the slice constant or
singular.  The fixed-\(a\) function \(q\mapsto(a;q)_n\) is critical at
\(q=-1\) if and only if
\[
 a=a_*(n)=
 \begin{cases}
 \dfrac1{n-1},&n\text{ even},\\[0.6em]
 -\dfrac1n,&n\text{ odd}.
 \end{cases}
\]
For even \(n=2m\), \(m\ge2\),
\begin{align}
 F_*&=\left(\frac{4m(m-1)}{(2m-1)^2}\right)^m,\label{eq:even-critical-value}\\
 \left.\partial_q^2\log F_n\right|_*
 &=-\frac{(m+1)(2m-1)^2}{12m(m-1)}\ne0.
 \label{eq:even-critical-second}
\end{align}
For odd \(n=2m+1\), \(m\ge2\), the critical value is \(Y_m\) from
\eqref{eq:odd-argument-critical-value}, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:odd-critical-second}
 \left.\partial_q^2\log F_n\right|_*
 =-\frac{(m-1)(2m+1)^2}{12m(m+1)}\ne0.
\end{equation}
Thus all these inverse branches are quadratic folds.  The sole nonconstant
higher degeneracy is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:exact-cubic}
 (a;q)_3\big|_{a=-1/3}
 =\frac{32}{27}+\frac4{27}(q+1)^3.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Equation \eqref{eq:qminus1-logderivative} is zero, apart from the constant
case \(a=0\), precisely when
\[
 E(E-1)(1+a)=O^2(1-a).
\]
For \(n=2m\), this gives \(a=1/(2m-1)\); for \(n=2m+1\), it gives
\(a=-1/(2m+1)\).  Substitution into \((1-a)^E(1+a)^O\) gives the critical
values.  Differentiating \eqref{eq:qpoch-q-logderivative} once more and summing
the even and odd arithmetic progressions gives
\eqref{eq:even-critical-second} and \eqref{eq:odd-critical-second}.  The odd
formula vanishes only at \(m=1\).  Direct factorization at \(n=3\) gives
\eqref{eq:exact-cubic}.  No other solution exists because the critical
equation is linear in \(a\) after removing the constant factor \(a\).
\end{proof}

The fold inverse follows immediately.  Since \(F_q=0\) at the critical point,
\(F_{qq}=F_*\,\partial_q^2\log F\), and therefore
\[
 q+1=\pm\sqrt{\frac{2(y-F_*)}{F_{qq}(a_*,-1)}}+O(y-F_*).
\]
For \(n=3\), the inverse is exact:
\[
 q=-1+\left(\frac{27}{4}\left(y-\frac{32}{27}\right)\right)^{1/3}.
\]

\section{The exceptional cubic is a Morse saddle in two parameters}
For odd \(n=2m+1\), the same point
\[
 (a,q)=\left(-\frac1{2m+1},-1\right)
\]
also satisfies \(F_a=0\), as follows from
\eqref{eq:qminus1-argument}.  At a point where both first derivatives vanish,
the Hessian of \(F\) is \(F_*\) times the Hessian of \(\log F\).  Its entries
are
\begin{align*}
 L_{aa}&=-\frac{(2m+1)^3}{4m(m+1)},\\
 L_{aq}&=-\frac{(2m+1)^2}{4(m+1)},\\
 L_{qq}&=-\frac{(m-1)(2m+1)^2}{12m(m+1)},
\end{align*}
and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:odd-hessian-det}
 \det\nabla^2L
 =-\frac{(2m+1)^4(m^2+m+1)}{48m^2(m+1)^2}<0.
\end{equation}

\begin{corollary}[\statusproved\ Morse interpretation]
Every odd critical point above, including \(n=3\), is a nondegenerate saddle
of the two-variable map \((a,q)\mapsto(a;q)_n\).  The cubic inverse in
\eqref{eq:exact-cubic} is a nongeneric one-dimensional slice tangent to a null
direction of the indefinite quadratic form; it is not a cusp of the full
parameter surface.
\end{corollary}

\section{Other roots of unity}
Let \(\zeta\) be primitive of order \(m\), and write \(n=mN+r\),
\(0\le r<m\).  Periodicity gives the exact block formula
\begin{equation}\label{eq:finite-root-block}
 (a;\zeta)_n=(1-a^m)^N(a;\zeta)_r.
\end{equation}
A regular base inverse has
\[
 q=\zeta+\frac{y-(a;\zeta)_n}{\partial_q(a;q)_n|_{q=\zeta}}+O((y-y_0)^2).
\]
The critical locus is the rational equation
\[
 \sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{ja\zeta^{j-1}}{1-a\zeta^j}=0,
\]
after cancellation of any factors vanishing at \(a^m=1\).  Multiplication by
\(\prod_j(1-a\zeta^j)\) shows that it is algebraic over the cyclotomic field
\(\RationalNumbers(\zeta)\).

\begin{researchproblem}
Classify the critical parameters \(a\) and their degeneracy at every primitive
root of unity.  The \(m=2\) theorem above suggests that the generic critical
points are folds and that higher degeneracies arise only from special
low-dimensional tangencies or colliding product zeros.
\end{researchproblem}

\section{The inverse at \texorpdfstring{\(q=\pm\infty\)}{q=+-infinity}}
Let \(N=\binom n2\), assume \(n\ge3\), and put
\[
 C=(1-a)(-a)^{n-1},\qquad Q=q^{-1}.
\]
Then
\[
 (a;q)_n=Cq^N\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}(1-a^{-1}Q^j)
 =Cq^N\left(1-a^{-1}Q-a^{-1}Q^2+O(Q^3)\right).
\]
For a selected branch
\[
 R=\left(\frac{C}{y}\right)^{1/N},
\]
reversion gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:finite-p-infinity-inverse}
 q=R^{-1}+\frac{a^{-1}}{N}+O(R).
\end{equation}
Further terms follow from the divisor/Bell machinery at \(Q=0\).  Real
\(q\to-\infty\) is possible only on target sign sectors compatible with
\(C(-1)^N\); in the complex plane all \(N\) branches are present.

\chapter{Order inversion and simultaneous recovery for finite products}

\section{Discrete order}
For integer order,
\[
 (a;q)_{n+1}=(a;q)_n(1-aq^n).
\]
A discrete inverse in \(n\) is therefore a threshold or search problem, not an
analytic inverse, unless an interpolation is chosen.  On a real sign domain
where all factors lie in \((0,1)\), the sequence decreases strictly and the
minimal \(n\) satisfying \((a;q)_n\le y\) is unambiguous.  Outside such a
domain, oscillation and repeated values require a branch label even at the
discrete level.

\section{Complex order and exact reduction to argument inversion}
For \(|q|<1\), define
\[
 (a;q)_\alpha=\frac{(a;q)_\infty}{(aq^\alpha;q)_\infty},
\]
with a chosen branch of \(q^\alpha\).  Let \(\Aq(z)\) denote a selected
argument inverse of \((b;q)_\infty=z\).  Then
\begin{theorem}[\statusproved\ Exact order inverse]
Every order branch satisfying \((a;q)_\alpha=y\) is of the form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:order-inverse-exact}
 \alpha=\frac{\PrincipalLogarithm(\Aq((a;q)_\infty/y)/a)+2\pi\ImaginaryUnit k}{\PrincipalLogarithm q},
 \qquad k\in\IntegerNumbers,
\end{equation}
with compatible logarithm and \(\Aq\) sheets.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The equation is equivalent to
\((aq^\alpha;q)_\infty=(a;q)_\infty/y\).  Invert in the argument, then take a
logarithm of \(q^\alpha=\Aq(\cdot)/a\).
\end{proof}

The order derivative is the Lambert series
\begin{equation}\label{eq:order-derivative}
 \partial_\alpha\log(a;q)_\alpha
 =(\log q)\sum_{j\ge0}\frac{aq^{\alpha+j}}{1-aq^{\alpha+j}}.
\end{equation}
It supplies local inverse jets and quantifies ill-conditioning when the
argument inverse approaches a critical point.

\section{Exact recovery from consecutive products}
Suppose \(P_n=(a;q)_n\), \(P_{n+1}\), and \(P_{n+2}\) are observed exactly,
with no zero denominator.  Define
\[
 r_n=1-\frac{P_{n+1}}{P_n},
 \qquad
 r_{n+1}=1-\frac{P_{n+2}}{P_{n+1}}.
\]
Then \(r_n=aq^n\) and \(r_{n+1}=aq^{n+1}\).

\begin{theorem}[\statusproved\ Two-parameter reconstruction]
If \(r_n\ne0\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:exact-aq-recovery}
 q=\frac{r_{n+1}}{r_n},
 \qquad
 a=r_nq^{-n}.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
This exact formula is preferable to solving one high-degree equation.  With
noise, the ratios should be fitted jointly across many consecutive orders;
the logarithms
\[
 \log r_j=\log a+j\log q
\]
turn the problem into a linear regression after branch unwrapping.

\section{General two-observable inversion}
If only two nonconsecutive measurements
\[
 F_{n_1}(a,q)=y_1,
 \qquad F_{n_2}(a,q)=y_2
\]
are available, local identifiability is controlled by
\[
 J=\det\begin{pmatrix}
 \partial_aF_{n_1}&\partial_qF_{n_1}\\
 \partial_aF_{n_2}&\partial_qF_{n_2}
 \end{pmatrix}.
\]
The locus \(J=0\) is an experimental-design discriminant: measurements there
cannot separate changes in \(a\) from changes in \(q\) to first order.  One
should choose orders whose logarithmic derivative vectors are as far from
parallel as possible.

\chapter{The infinite \texorpdfstring{\(q\)}{q}-Pochhammer inverse in its argument}

\section{Principal real branch}
Fix \(0<q<1\).  On \(a<1\),
\[
 P_q(a)=(a;q)_\infty>0,
 \qquad
 \partial_a\log P_q(a)=-\sum_{j\ge0}\frac{q^j}{1-aq^j}<0.
\]
Moreover, \(P_q(a)\to0\) as \(a\uparrow1\), while
\(P_q(a)\to\infty\) as \(a\to-\infty\).  Hence there is a unique decreasing
bijection
\[
 P_q:(-\infty,1)\longrightarrow(0,\infty).
\]
We denote its inverse by
\[
 a=\Aq(y).
\]
Other real branches lie between successive positive zeros \(q^{-j}\), and the
complex inverse has infinitely many sheets.

\section{Near target one: argument close to zero}
The logarithmic series
\[
 \log(a;q)_\infty=-\sum_{r\ge1}\frac{a^r}{r(1-q^r)}
\]
gives
\[
 (a;q)_\infty
 =1-\frac{a}{1-q}
 +\frac{q}{(1-q)^2(1+q)}a^2+O(a^3).
\]
With \(u=1-y\), the principal inverse is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:infinite-argument-near-one}
 \Aq(y)=(1-q)u+\frac{q(1-q)}{1+q}u^2+O(u^3).
\end{equation}
The full series is obtained by Lagrange inversion of the Lambert exponential.
Its radius of convergence is the distance in the \(y\)-plane to the nearest
critical value of the entire function \(a\mapsto(a;q)_\infty\).

\section{Near target zero: argument close to one}
Write \(a=1-\eps\).  Then
\[
 (a;q)_\infty=\eps(aq;q)_\infty.
\]
Define Lambert sums
\[
 L_1(q)=\sum_{j\ge1}\frac{q^j}{1-q^j},
 \qquad
 L_2(q)=\sum_{j\ge1}\frac{q^{2j}}{(1-q^j)^2}.
\]
Taylor expansion at \(a=1\) gives
\[
 (1-\eps;q)_\infty
 =(q;q)_\infty\eps
 \left(1+L_1(q)\eps+\frac{L_1(q)^2-L_2(q)}2\eps^2+\cdots\right).
\]
Therefore, with \(v=y/(q;q)_\infty\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:infinite-argument-near-zero-target}
 \Aq(y)=1-v+L_1(q)v^2+O(v^3).
\end{equation}
This expansion is especially effective for inverse Euler
\(q\)-exponentials.

\section{Large positive target and the negative argument sheet}
Set \(a=-x\), \(x>0\), and \(L=\log(1/q)\).  Splitting the product near the
index \(j\approx\log x/L\), or using a theta-function completion, yields an
asymptotic of the form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:large-negative-argument}
 \log(-x;q)_\infty
 =\frac{(\log x)^2}{2L}+\frac12\log x+C_q
 +\Omega_q\!\left(\frac{\log x}{L}\right)+O(x^{-1}),
\end{equation}
where \(\Omega_q\) is bounded and one-periodic.  Hence
\[
 \log x=\sqrt{2L\log y}-\frac L2+O(1),
 \qquad
 \Aq(y)=-\exp\!\left(\sqrt{2L\log y}-\frac L2+O(1)\right).
\]
The bounded \(O(1)\) term contains a log-periodic correction; dropping it can
produce a relative error that does not tend monotonically to zero.  The prior
inverse report develops this branch in greater numerical detail
\cite{ReshetnikovInverse}; here it serves as a prototype of periodic inverse
asymptotics.

\section{Nonprincipal real branch points}
Between consecutive zeros \(q^{-j}\) and \(q^{-j-1}\), the real product has at
least one critical point.  For large \(j\), scale
\[
 a=q^{-j}b.
\]
The local product separates into a finite sign-changing block and a stable
tail, producing an asymptotic lattice of branch values.  A systematic
description uses the same splitting that produces \(\Omega_q\).  The branch
index becomes a discrete phase variable coupled to the continuous target.
This is an instance of an inverse covering with asymptotically self-similar
sheets.

\chapter{The infinite product inverted in the base}

\section{Near \texorpdfstring{\(q=0\)}{q=0}}
For the infinite product, all coefficients in
\eqref{eq:q0-infinite-inverse-preview} are stable because only finitely many
factors contribute to each power of \(q\).  Explicitly, with
\[
 \eta=\frac{(1-a)-y}{a(1-a)},
\]
\begin{align}
 q={}&\eta-\eta^2+(1+a)\eta^3-(1+4a)\eta^4\notag\\
 &+(1+11a+3a^2)\eta^5-(1+26a+22a^2)\eta^6+O(\eta^7).
 \label{eq:infinite-q0-inverse}
\end{align}
The divisor formula \eqref{eq:q0-logcoeff}, now with no upper restriction on
\(d\), and Bell reversion generate arbitrary order.  Singular cases occur at
\(a=0\) and \(a=1\), where the normalization by \(a(1-a)\) fails because the
forward map degenerates.

\section{Fixed argument and \texorpdfstring{\(q\to1^-\)}{q to 1}}
Let \(q=e^{-t}\), \(t\downarrow0\), and keep \(|a|<1\) fixed.  Expanding
\(1/(1-e^{-rt})\) in the Lambert series gives the classical McIntosh-type
expansion \cite{McIntosh1999,BanerjeeWilkerson}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fixed-a-q1}
 \log(a;e^{-t})_\infty
 =-\frac{\Polylogarithm_2(a)}{t}+\frac12\log(1-a)
 -\sum_{r\ge1}\frac{B_{2r}}{(2r)!}\Polylogarithm_{2-2r}(a)t^{2r-1}.
\end{equation}
Thus
\[
 A=\Polylogarithm_2(a),\quad
 D=\frac{\Polylogarithm_0(a)}{12},\quad
 E=\frac{\Polylogarithm_{-2}(a)}{720},\quad
 F=\frac{\Polylogarithm_{-4}(a)}{30240}
\]
in \eqref{eq:large-log-reversion}.  With
\[
 X=-\log y+\frac12\log(1-a),
\]
the base inverse is
\begin{align}
 t={}&\frac{A}{X}+\frac{DA^2}{X^3}
 +\frac{A^3(2D^2-AE)}{X^5}\notag\\
 &+\frac{A^4(A^2F-6ADE+5D^3)}{X^7}+O(X^{-9}),
 \qquad q=e^{-t}.
 \label{eq:fixed-a-q1-inverse}
\end{align}
For real \(0<a<1\), \(y\downarrow0\) and all displayed quantities are real.
For complex \(a\), this formula labels branches through the choices of
\(\Polylogarithm_2\), \(\log(1-a)\), and \(\log y\).

\section{The nonuniform scaling \texorpdfstring{\(a=q^x\)}{a=q^x}}
When \(a\to1\) together with \(q\to1\), the fixed-\(a\) expansion is not
uniform.  Put \(a=q^x=e^{-xt}\), with fixed \(x\notin\{0,-1,-2,\ldots\}\).
A Bernoulli-polynomial expansion is
\begin{align}
 \log(q^x;q)_\infty
 ={}&-\frac{\pi^2}{6t}+\left(\frac12-x\right)\log t
 +\frac12\log(2\pi)-\log\Gamma(x)\notag\\
 &-\sum_{r\ge1}\frac{B_rB_{r+1}(x)}{r(r+1)!}t^r.
 \label{eq:qpower-scaling}
\end{align}
The first correction is \(B_2(x)t/4\).

Let \(\Lambda=-\log y\), \(A=\pi^2/6\),
\[
 B=x-\frac12,
 \qquad
 C=-\frac12\log(2\pi)+\log\Gamma(x).
\]
Then
\[
 \Lambda=\frac{A}{t}+B\log t+C-\frac{B_2(x)}4t+\cdots.
\]
A first explicit approximation is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qpower-first-inverse}
 t\sim\frac{A}{\Lambda+B\log\Lambda-B\log A-C}.
\end{equation}
Retaining the logarithm exactly gives the Lambert-\(W\) preconditioner
\eqref{eq:lambert-preconditioner}.  At \(x=1/2\), the logarithm disappears and
the leading inverse is simply \(t=A/(\Lambda-C)\).

The 2026 gamma-product formula of Arabi Ardehali and Rosengren
\cite{ArdehaliRosengren} is a promising basis for making this inverse uniform
when \(x\) itself varies with \(t\), including transition scales not covered by
a fixed Bernoulli jet.

\section{Radial inversion at \texorpdfstring{\(q=-1\)}{q=-1}}
Put \(q=-e^{-t}\), \(t\downarrow0\).  Pairing even and odd factors gives the
exact identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:minus-one-pairing}
 (a;-e^{-t})_\infty
 =(a;e^{-2t})_\infty(-ae^{-t};e^{-2t})_\infty.
\end{equation}
Alternatively, split the Lambert series into even and odd indices.  For
\(|a|<1\), one obtains
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qminus1-infinite-forward}
 \log(a;-e^{-t})_\infty
 =-\frac{\Polylogarithm_2(a^2)}{4t}+\frac12\log(1-a)
 -\frac{a(a+3)}{12(1-a^2)}t+O(t^3).
\end{equation}
The constant term simplifies nontrivially: the even-index contribution is
\(\frac14\log(1-a^2)\), the odd-index contribution is
\(-\frac14\log((1+a)/(1-a))\), and their sum is
\(\frac12\log(1-a)\).

\begin{theorem}[\statusproved\ Real radial inverse at \(-1\)]
For real \(|a|<1\), let \(y=(a;-e^{-t})_\infty\), and set
\[
 A=\frac{\Polylogarithm_2(a^2)}4,
 \quad C=\frac12\log(1-a),
 \quad D=\frac{a(a+3)}{12(1-a^2)},
 \quad X=-\log y+C.
\]
Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qminus1-infinite-inverse}
 t=\frac{A}{X}+\frac{DA^2}{X^3}+O(X^{-5}),
 \qquad q=-e^{-t}.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Equation \eqref{eq:qminus1-infinite-forward} has the canonical form of
\Cref{eq:large-log-reversion}.  Formal reversion gives the result; standard
remainder estimates for the Lambert series justify the asymptotic substitution
on compact subsets of \(|a|<1\).
\end{proof}

\section{A primitive-root inverse normal form}
Let \(\zeta\) be primitive of order \(m\), and approach radially through
\(q=\zeta e^{-t}\), \(\Re t>0\).  In
\[
 \log(a;q)_\infty=-\sum_{r\ge1}\frac{a^r}{r(1-q^r)},
\]
only indices divisible by \(m\) produce the \(1/t\) singularity.  Define
\begin{align}
 C_\zeta(a)
 &=\frac1{2m}\log(1-a^m)
 -\sum_{\substack{r\ge1\\m\nmid r}}
 \frac{a^r}{r(1-\zeta^r)},
 \label{eq:cyclotomic-C}\\
 \Dcal_\zeta(a)
 &=\frac{a^m}{12(1-a^m)}
 -\sum_{\substack{r\ge1\\m\nmid r}}
 \frac{a^r\zeta^r}{(1-\zeta^r)^2}.
 \label{eq:cyclotomic-D}
\end{align}
Both series converge absolutely for \(|a|<1\).

\begin{theorem}[\statusproved\ Radial cyclotomic inversion]
For \(|a|<1\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cyclotomic-forward}
 \log(a;\zeta e^{-t})_\infty
 =-\frac{\Polylogarithm_2(a^m)}{m^2t}+C_\zeta(a)-\Dcal_\zeta(a)t+O(t^2).
\end{equation}
Let
\[
 A=\frac{\Polylogarithm_2(a^m)}{m^2},
 \qquad X=-\log y+C_\zeta(a).
\]
On a branch for which \(X\to\infty\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cyclotomic-inverse}
 t=\frac{A}{X}+\frac{\Dcal_\zeta(a)A^2}{X^3}+O(X^{-4}),
 \qquad q=\zeta e^{-t}.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For \(r=ms\), expand
\[
 \frac1{1-e^{-rt}}=\frac1{rt}+\frac12+\frac{rt}{12}+O(t^3).
\]
The singular sum is
\(-\Polylogarithm_2(a^m)/(m^2t)\); its constant and linear pieces give the first terms in
\eqref{eq:cyclotomic-C} and \eqref{eq:cyclotomic-D}.  For \(m\nmid r\),
\[
 \frac1{1-\zeta^re^{-rt}}
 =\frac1{1-\zeta^r}-\frac{r\zeta^r}{(1-\zeta^r)^2}t+O(t^2).
\]
Absolute convergence permits termwise summation.  Reversion then follows as in
\eqref{eq:large-log-reversion}.
\end{proof}

For \(m=1\), \(C_1(a)=\frac12\log(1-a)\) and
\(\Dcal_1(a)=a/[12(1-a)]\).  For \(m=2\), the formulas reduce exactly to
\eqref{eq:qminus1-infinite-forward}.  For \(m>2\), the constants are generally
complex and encode a cyclic quantum-dilogarithm factor.  Higher coefficients
are convergent cyclotomic Lambert sums.

\section{Exterior continuation and \texorpdfstring{\(q=\pm\infty\)}{q=+-infinity}}
The defining infinite product does not converge for \(|q|>1\).  One must first
choose an exterior normalization.  Two common choices are
\[
 P^{\rm ext}(a,q)=(a;q^{-1})_\infty,
 \qquad |q|>1,
\]
or a \(q\)-gamma normalization in which explicit powers of \(q\) remove the
divergent finite-product growth.  For the first choice, \(Q=q^{-1}\to0\), and
\eqref{eq:infinite-q0-inverse} applies directly after replacing \(q\) by
\(Q\).  It is mathematically incorrect to discuss ``the'' value of
\((a;q)_\infty\) at \(q=\infty\) without specifying such a normalization.

\section{Stokes data and continuation paths}
The leading action \(A=\Polylogarithm_2(a^m)/m^2\) can rotate across a Stokes line, and
\(C_\zeta(a)\) changes by logarithmic monodromy when \(a\) crosses the branch
set of \(\Polylogarithm_2(a^m)\).  Consequently, two radial inverse expansions with the
same formal powers may represent different analytic sheets.  A robust complex
algorithm stores the branch-state tuple
\[
 (\zeta,m,\arg t,\PrincipalLogarithm y,\PrincipalLogarithm(1-a^m))
\]
as part of every continuation record.

\begin{conjecture}[\statusconjectural\ Cyclotomic resurgent inverse]
After removal of the action \(\Polylogarithm_2(a^m)/(m^2t)\), every radial branch of
\((a;q)_\infty\) admits a resurgent transseries whose perturbative coefficients
belong to the differential algebra generated by cyclotomic Lambert sums,
polylogarithms, and cyclic quantum dilogarithms.  Its inverse transseries is
closed under the same algebra, with Stokes automorphisms transported by formal
composition.
\end{conjecture}

\part{Gaussian, multinomial, and parameter inverses}

\chapter{Gaussian coefficients inverted in the base}

\section{The global positive branch}
For \(0<k<n\), the Gaussian coefficient
\[
 G_{n,k}(q)=\GaussianBinomial{n}{k}{q}
\]
is a polynomial with nonnegative integer coefficients, constant term one, and
degree
\[
 D=k(n-k).
\]
It is palindromic:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gaussian-reciprocity}
 G_{n,k}(q)=q^DG_{n,k}(q^{-1}).
\end{equation}
The global positive-inverse theorem therefore applies.

\begin{theorem}[\statusproved\ Unique positive Gaussian base]
For every \(0<k<n\) and every \(y>1\), there exists exactly one \(q>0\) such
that \(\GaussianBinomial{n}{k}{q}=y\).  The solution depends analytically on \(y\) away from
critical values in the complex extension.
\end{theorem}

Although this theorem is elementary, it is algorithmically decisive: bisection
on \(q>0\) is globally safe, and a local asymptotic can be used only as an
accelerator, never as an unverified branch selector.

\section{The inverse at \texorpdfstring{\(q=0\)}{q=0}}
The coefficient of \(q^j\) counts partitions of \(j\) contained in a
\(k\times(n-k)\) rectangle.  Write
\[
 G_{n,k}(q)=1+c_1q+c_2q^2+c_3q^3+\cdots,
 \qquad c_1=1.
\]
Then, with \(u=y-1\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gaussian-q0-inverse}
 q=u-c_2u^2+(2c_2^2-c_3)u^3+O(u^4).
\end{equation}
The second coefficient has the transparent form
\[
 c_2=\mathbf 1_{k\ge2}+\mathbf 1_{n-k\ge2}.
\]
More generally, any partition generator or dynamic program for the rectangle
coefficients immediately becomes an all-order inverse generator through Bell
reversion.

The inverse is badly conditioned for recovering \(n\) or \(k\) when
\(q\to0\), because every nontrivial Gaussian coefficient tends to one.  Base
recovery remains well conditioned because the first coefficient is always
one.

\section{Bernoulli and cumulant reversion at \texorpdfstring{\(q=1\)}{q=1}}
Set \(q=e^t\), and define
\[
 P_r(n,k)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\bigl((n-k+j)^r-j^r\bigr).
\]
The elementary expansion
\[
 \log\frac{e^x-1}{x}
 =\frac x2+\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{B_{2m}}{2m(2m)!}x^{2m}
\]
gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gaussian-q1-forward}
 \log\frac{G_{n,k}(e^t)}{\binom nk}
 =\frac D2t+
 \sum_{m\ge1}\frac{B_{2m}P_{2m}(n,k)}{2m(2m)!}t^{2m}.
\end{equation}
This is an all-order expansion with no odd powers beyond the linear term.  In
probabilistic language, if \(J\) is the area of a uniformly selected partition
in the rectangle, then
\[
 \mathbb E J=\frac D2,
 \qquad
 \kappa_{2m}(J)=\frac{B_{2m}}{2m}P_{2m}(n,k),
 \qquad
 \kappa_{2m+1}(J)=0\quad(m\ge1).
\]
In particular,
\[
 P_2=D(n+1),
 \qquad
 \Variance J=\frac{D(n+1)}{12}.
\]

Let
\[
 s=\log\frac{y}{\binom nk}.
\]
Reversion of the first two nonzero terms gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gaussian-q1-inverse}
 t=\frac{2s}{D}-\frac{n+1}{3D^2}s^2
 +\frac{(n+1)^2}{9D^3}s^3+O(s^4),
 \qquad q=e^t.
\end{equation}
The coefficient of \(s^4\) receives the first contribution from the fourth
cumulant; higher terms are generated mechanically.

\begin{remark}
Using \(t=\log q\), rather than \(q-1\), exposes reciprocal symmetry and
substantially simplifies the inverse.  Conversion to a series in \(q-1\) is
best delayed until the final step.
\end{remark}

\section{The exact first inverse jets at \texorpdfstring{\(q=-1\)}{q=-1}}
Write
\[
 n=2a+r,
 \qquad k=2b+s,
 \qquad r,s\in\{0,1\}.
\]
The \(q\)-Lucas theorem gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:q-lucas-minus-one}
 G_{n,k}(-1)=\binom ab\binom rs.
\end{equation}
Thus the value vanishes exactly when \(n\) is even and \(k\) is odd.

\begin{theorem}[\statusproved\ Gaussian inverse at \(-1\)]
If \((r,s)\ne(0,1)\), put \(C=\binom ab\).  Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gaussian-minus-one-nonzero}
 G_{n,k}(q)=C-\frac{D}{2}C(q+1)+O((q+1)^2),
\end{equation}
and
\[
 q=-1-\frac{2(y-C)}{DC}+O((y-C)^2).
\]
If \(n=2a\) and \(k=2b+1\), then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gaussian-minus-one-zero}
 G_{2a,2b+1}(q)
 =(a-b)\binom ab(q+1)+O((q+1)^2),
\end{equation}
and
\[
 q=-1+\frac{y}{(a-b)\binom ab}+O(y^2).
\]
Every nontrivial Gaussian base inverse is therefore regular at \(q=-1\), even
when the forward value is zero.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The value formula is the order-two case of \(q\)-Lucas.  In the nonzero case,
\(D\) is even.  Differentiate the reciprocal identity
\(G(q)=q^DG(q^{-1})\) at \(q=-1\); the two derivative terms combine to give
\(G'(-1)=-DG(-1)/2\).  In the zero case, \(q+1=\Phi_2(q)\) occurs with
cyclotomic valuation one.  Cancelling that factor in the product formula and
taking the limit gives the coefficient
\((a-b)\binom ab\).  Ordinary reversion completes the proof.
\end{proof}

\section{Primitive roots of unity and carry geometry}
Let \(\zeta\) be primitive of order \(m\).  The exponent of \(\Phi_m(q)\) in a
Gaussian coefficient is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gaussian-cyclotomic-valuation}
 \nu_m(n,k)=\Floor{n/m}-\Floor{k/m}-\Floor{(n-k)/m}.
\end{equation}
This number is either zero or one.  It equals one precisely when adding the
least residues of \(k\) and \(n-k\) modulo \(m\) produces a carry.  Equivalently,
if
\[
 n=am+r,
 \qquad k=bm+s,
 \qquad 0\le r,s<m,
\]
then a forced zero occurs exactly when \(s>r\).  The value in the no-carry case
is
\[
 \GaussianBinomial{n}{k}{\zeta}=\binom ab\GaussianBinomial{r}{s}{\zeta}.
\]

\begin{corollary}[\statusproved\ Every forced cyclotomic zero is simple]
If \(\nu_m(n,k)=1\), then at each primitive \(m\)-th root \(\zeta\),
\[
 G_{n,k}(q)=(q-\zeta)G'_{n,k}(\zeta)+O((q-\zeta)^2),
 \qquad G'_{n,k}(\zeta)\ne0.
\]
Hence the inverse near target zero is an ordinary Taylor branch, not a
fractional-power branch.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
In \((q;q)_r\), the exponent of \(\Phi_m\) is \(\Floor{r/m}\).  Taking the
difference in the Gaussian quotient gives \eqref{eq:gaussian-cyclotomic-valuation}.
The floor identity restricts it to zero or one.  Since cyclotomic polynomials
have simple roots over characteristic zero, valuation one gives a nonzero
first derivative.
\end{proof}

In the no-carry case, \(G(\zeta)\ne0\), but the derivative can in principle
vanish.  Such points are genuine complex critical points rather than forced
cyclotomic zeros; they belong to the discriminant of
\(G(q)-y\).

\section{The inverse at \texorpdfstring{\(q=\pm\infty\)}{q=+-infinity}}
Since \(c_1=1\), \eqref{eq:palindromic-large-inverse} becomes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gaussian-large-inverse}
 q=R^{-1}-\frac1D+
 \left(\frac{D-1}{2D^2}-\frac{c_2}{D}\right)R+O(R^2),
 \qquad R=y^{-1/D}.
\end{equation}
On the positive branch, \(R>0\) and \(q\to+\infty\).  For real
\(q\to-\infty\), the sign of the target is \((-1)^D\) to leading order.  In
the complex plane the \(D\) choices of \(R\) label the asymptotic inverse
sheets.

\section{Complex critical values and monodromy}
The positive real branch has no critical points, but the complex polynomial
usually has many.  They are roots of \(G'_{n,k}(q)\), and their images under
\(G\) are the finite branch values.  A computationally stable procedure is:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
\item compute the polynomial by the Gaussian recurrence over exact integers;
\item isolate roots of \(G'\) with certified disks;
\item evaluate critical values with ball arithmetic;
\item connect roots of \(G(q)=y\) by homotopy while avoiding those disks.
\end{enumerate}
A resultant gives the exact discriminant, but its coefficient growth makes it
less useful than certified root tracking beyond modest degrees.

\begin{conjecture}[\statusconjectural\ Generic Gaussian monodromy]
After removing the reciprocal and exceptional cyclotomic symmetries, the
monodromy group of a generic Gaussian polynomial \(G_{n,k}(q)-y\) is as large
as allowed by those symmetries.  Equivalently, almost all critical points are
simple and the associated transpositions generate the full permitted
permutation group of inverse sheets.
\end{conjecture}

\chapter{Continuous upper and lower parameter inverses}

\section{The \texorpdfstring{\(q\)}{q}-gamma interpolation}
For \(0<q<1\), define
\[
 \Gamma_q(x)=(1-q)^{1-x}\frac{(q;q)_\infty}{(q^x;q)_\infty}.
\]
The continuous Gaussian coefficient is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:continuous-gaussian}
 \Bcal_q(z,w)=
 \frac{\Gamma_q(z+1)}{\Gamma_q(w+1)\Gamma_q(z-w+1)}.
\end{equation}
It agrees with \(\GaussianBinomial{n}{k}{q}\) at integers.  Its logarithmic derivatives are
\begin{align}
 \partial_z\log\Bcal_q(z,w)
 &=\psi_q(z+1)-\psi_q(z-w+1),
 \label{eq:continuous-upper-derivative}\\
 \partial_w\log\Bcal_q(z,w)
 &=-\psi_q(w+1)+\psi_q(z-w+1).
 \label{eq:continuous-lower-derivative}
\end{align}
Here \(\psi_q=\Gamma_q'/\Gamma_q\), and
\[
 \psi_q'(x)=(\log q)^2\sum_{j\ge0}
 \frac{q^{x+j}}{(1-q^{x+j})^2}>0.
\]

\section{Upper-parameter inversion}
For fixed \(w>0\), \eqref{eq:continuous-upper-derivative} is positive when
\(z>w\).  Hence \(z\mapsto\Bcal_q(z,w)\) is strictly increasing.  At the lower
endpoint \(z=w\), the value is one.  As \(z\to\infty\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:continuous-upper-saturation}
 \Bcal_q(z,w)\longrightarrow
 L_q(w)=\frac{(q^{w+1};q)_\infty}{(q;q)_\infty}.
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[\statusproved\ Upper inverse for \(0<q<1\)]
For fixed \(w>0\) and every \(1<y<L_q(w)\), there is exactly one real
\(z>w\) satisfying \(\Bcal_q(z,w)=y\).  No finite real solution exists for
\(y\ge L_q(w)\).
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Strict monotonicity follows from the increasing \(q\)-digamma function.
The endpoint values follow from \eqref{eq:continuous-gaussian} and the
asymptotic cancellation of \((q^z;q)_\infty\).
\end{proof}

At \(z=w\), put
\[
 A=\psi_q(w+1)-\psi_q(1)>0.
\]
Then
\[
 z-w=\frac{y-1}{A}+O((y-1)^2).
\]
At the upper saturation, let \(z\to\infty\).  Expanding the ratio of tail
products gives, for integer \(w=k\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fixed-k-upper-inverse}
 L_q(k)-\Bcal_q(z,k)
 \sim L_q(k)[k]_q q^{z-k+1}.
\end{equation}
Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fixed-k-n-inverse}
 z\sim k-1+\log_q
 \left(\frac{L_q(k)-y}{L_q(k)[k]_q}\right).
\end{equation}
The logarithmic divergence explains why upper-parameter recovery becomes
ill-conditioned near saturation.

For \(q>1\), reciprocal gamma normalization gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:continuous-gaussian-reciprocity}
 \Bcal_q(z,w)=q^{w(z-w)}\Bcal_{1/q}(z,w).
\end{equation}
Thus, for fixed \(w\), the upper inverse grows logarithmically with the target:
\[
 z=w+\frac{\log y+\log(q^{-1};q^{-1})_w}{w\log q}+o(1)
\]
for integer \(w\), with the obvious gamma-product replacement for continuous
\(w\).

\section{Lower-parameter inversion and the central fold}
Equation \eqref{eq:continuous-lower-derivative} is positive for \(w<z/2\), zero
at \(w=z/2\), and negative for \(w>z/2\).  Hence
\(w\mapsto\Bcal_q(z,w)\) is symmetric and has a unique maximum
\[
 M_q(z)=\Bcal_q(z,z/2).
\]

\begin{theorem}[\statusproved\ Two lower-parameter sheets]
For fixed \(z>0\), every target \(1<y<M_q(z)\) has exactly two real solutions
\(w_-<z/2<w_+\), related by \(w_+=z-w_-\).  Near the maximum,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lower-center-fold}
 w_\pm=\frac z2\pm
 \sqrt{\frac{M_q(z)-y}{M_q(z)\psi_q'(z/2+1)}}
 +O(M_q(z)-y).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Monotonicity and symmetry give the global branch count.  At the center,
\[
 \partial_w^2\log\Bcal_q(z,w)|_{w=z/2}
 =-2\psi_q'(z/2+1),
\]
so
\(\Bcal_q''(z,z/2)=-2M_q(z)\psi_q'(z/2+1)\).  The fold formula gives
\eqref{eq:lower-center-fold}.
\end{proof}

Near \(w=0\), let
\[
 A_z=\psi_q(z+1)-\psi_q(1).
\]
Then
\[
 w=\frac{y-1}{A_z}
 -\frac{A_z^2-\psi_q'(1)-\psi_q'(z+1)}{2A_z^3}(y-1)^2
 +O((y-1)^3).
\]
This branch is useful when a Gaussian coefficient is continued from a sparse
lower index.

\section{Discrete aliasing at special bases}
At \(q=0\), every nontrivial integer Gaussian coefficient equals one, so
integer upper/lower parameters cannot be recovered from a single value.  At
\(q=-1\), \(q\)-Lucas reduces the value to a smaller ordinary binomial plus a
parity bit; many distinct \((n,k)\) pairs alias.  At a primitive \(m\)-th root,
the residues modulo \(m\) and the quotient-level binomial are the recoverable
data.  Parameter inversion at roots of unity is therefore intrinsically
arithmetic and set-valued.

\section{A dilogarithmic double-scaling inverse}
Let
\[
 q=e^{-\tau/n},
 \qquad k=\rho n,
 \qquad 0<\rho<1,
\]
with \(n\to\infty\).  Euler--Maclaurin applied to the product gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gaussian-double-scaling}
 \frac1n\log\GaussianBinomial{n}{\rho n}{q}
 \longrightarrow
 \Phi(\rho,\tau),
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dilog-rate}
 \Phi(\rho,\tau)=\frac1\tau\left[
 \frac{\pi^2}{6}+\Polylogarithm_2(e^{-\tau})
 -\Polylogarithm_2(e^{-\rho\tau})
 -\Polylogarithm_2(e^{-(1-\rho)\tau})
 \right].
\end{equation}
The rate is symmetric under \(\rho\leftrightarrow1-\rho\) and has a unique
maximum at \(\rho=1/2\).  Consequently, inversion in \(\rho\) has two branches
and a central square-root transition, while inversion in \(\tau\) is governed
by derivatives of dilogarithms.  Finite-size corrections can be reverted
jointly with \eqref{eq:dilog-rate} to recover \((n,\rho)\) from several
observables.

\begin{researchproblem}
Develop a uniform inverse expansion through the three simultaneous limits
\(n\to\infty\), \(q\to1\), and \(\rho\to1/2\).  The expected normal form
combines an error-function central scaling with dilogarithmic large deviations
and should provide a \(q\)-analogue of uniform inverse-binomial asymptotics.
\end{researchproblem}

\chapter{Multinomials and broader positive \texorpdfstring{\(q\)}{q}-analogs}

\section{Gaussian multinomials}
For nonnegative integers \(k_1+\cdots+k_r=n\), define
\[
 \qmultinom{n}{k_1,\ldots,k_r}
 =\frac{(q;q)_n}{\prod_{i=1}^r(q;q)_{k_i}}.
\]
It is a positive palindromic polynomial of degree
\[
 D=\sum_{1\le i<j\le r}k_ik_j.
\]
Thus it has a unique positive base inverse for every \(y>1\), the universal
small-\(q\) inverse, and the reciprocal large-\(q\) inverse.

At \(q=e^t\),
\begin{align}
 \log\frac{\qmultinom{n}{k_1,\ldots,k_r}}
 {\binom{n}{k_1,\ldots,k_r}}
 ={}&\frac D2t\\
 &+\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{B_{2m}}{2m(2m)!}
 \left(\sum_{j=1}^n j^{2m}
 -\sum_{i=1}^r\sum_{j=1}^{k_i}j^{2m}\right)t^{2m}.
 \label{eq:multinomial-q1}
\end{align}
Hence all base-inverse coefficients are explicit polynomials in power sums and
Bernoulli numbers.

\section{A reusable inversion template}
Many \(q\)-Catalan, \(q\)-Narayana, rank-generating, Hilbert, and Poincar\'e
polynomials satisfy
\[
 P(q)=\sum c_jq^j,
 \qquad c_j\ge0,
 \qquad P(q)=q^DP(q^{-1}).
\]
For every such object:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item positive-base inversion is globally unique;
\item the \(q=0\) inverse is governed by the first combinatorial coefficients;
\item the \(q=1\) inverse is cumulant reversion for the graded statistic;
\item the \(q=\infty\) inverse is determined by reciprocity;
\item roots of unity are controlled by cyclotomic valuations and cancellations.
\end{enumerate}
This turns a large class of apparently unrelated inverse problems into one
standard pipeline.

\section{Coefficient distributions and asymptotic inverse universality}
Suppose a sequence \(P_N(q)\) has exponent variables \(J_N\) whose centered
cumulants, after scaling, approach those of a Gaussian.  Then near \(q=1\),
\[
 \log\frac{P_N(e^t)}{P_N(1)}
 =\mu_Nt+\frac{\sigma_N^2t^2}{2}+o(\sigma_N^2t^2)
\]
uniformly on a shrinking window.  The inverse is then universally
\[
 t=\frac{s}{\mu_N}-\frac{\sigma_N^2}{2\mu_N^3}s^2+\cdots.
\]
Non-Gaussian limiting cumulants produce systematic corrections.  This
probabilistic organization is particularly effective for Gaussian
coefficients, plane partitions, and graded representation dimensions.

\begin{conjecture}[\statusconjectural\ Inverse cumulant universality]
For broad mod-Gaussian families of positive palindromic \(q\)-polynomials, the
base inverse admits a uniform expansion in standardized cumulants whose domain
extends up to the nearest complex saddle of the limiting cumulant-generating
function.  The first failure of the expansion is governed by a universal
Airy-type coalescence of inverse saddles.
\end{conjecture}

\part{Other \texorpdfstring{\(q\)}{q}-analogs and their parameter inverses}

\chapter{Inverse \texorpdfstring{\(q\)}{q}-numbers and \texorpdfstring{\(q\)}{q}-factorials}

\section{The \texorpdfstring{\(q\)}{q}-number inverted in \texorpdfstring{\(x\)}{x}}
For fixed \(q\notin\{0,1\}\),
\[
 [x]_q=\frac{1-q^x}{1-q}=y
\]
is elementary to invert, but multivalued over \(\ComplexNumbers\):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qnumber-x-inverse}
 x=\frac{\PrincipalLogarithm(1-(1-q)y)+2\pi\ImaginaryUnit k}{\PrincipalLogarithm q},
 \qquad k\in\IntegerNumbers.
\end{equation}
The denominator also depends on the chosen logarithm of \(q\).  For real
\(q>0\), \(q\ne1\), and a positive logarithm branch, the real inverse is
unique on every interval where the target remains in range.

At \(q=1\), \([x]_1=x\), so inversion is the identity.  At \(q=0\), the
continuation depends sharply on \(\Re x\): for \(\Re x>0\), \([x]_0=1\), and
information about \(x\) is lost; for \(\Re x<0\), the term \(q^x\) diverges.

\section{The base inverse of \texorpdfstring{\([x]_q\)}{[x]q}}
For fixed \(x\), the base equation is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qnumber-base-equation}
 q^x-yq+(y-1)=0.
\end{equation}
It is a polynomial when \(x\) is a nonnegative integer and a transcendental,
branch-dependent equation otherwise.

At \(q=e^t\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qnumber-hyperbolic}
 \frac{[x]_{e^t}}{x}
 =e^{(x-1)t/2}
 \frac{\sinh(xt/2)/(xt/2)}{\sinh(t/2)/(t/2)}.
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qnumber-q1-forward}
 \log\frac{[x]_{e^t}}x
 =\frac{x-1}{2}t+
 \sum_{m\ge1}\frac{B_{2m}(x^{2m}-1)}{2m(2m)!}t^{2m}.
\end{equation}
For \(x\ne0,1\), set \(s=\log(y/x)\).  Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qnumber-q1-inverse}
 t=\frac{2s}{x-1}-\frac{x+1}{3(x-1)^2}s^2
 +\frac{(x+1)^2}{9(x-1)^3}s^3+O(s^4),
 \qquad q=e^t.
\end{equation}
The values \(x=0\) and \(x=1\) are constant maps and have no base inverse.

\section{Fractional Newton polygons at \texorpdfstring{\(q=0\)}{q=0}}
For noninteger \(x>0\),
\[
 [x]_q=\frac{1-q^x}{1-q}=1+q+q^2+\cdots-q^x(1+q+\cdots).
\]
There are three basic inverse regimes.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item If \(0<x<1\), then \(1-y\sim q^x\), so
\[
 q\sim(1-y)^{1/x}.
\]
The next exponent is selected by comparing \(2x\) and \(1\).
\item If \(x>1\), then \(y-1\sim q\), but a fractional correction
\(q^x\) enters before \(q^2\) when \(1<x<2\).
\item If \(x\) is a positive integer, the expansion is an ordinary polynomial
inverse and coefficient positivity gives the unique positive branch for
\(y>1\).
\end{enumerate}
These are elementary examples of the generalized-series mechanism developed in
\Cref{sec:newton-polygon-placeholder}.

\section{The point \texorpdfstring{\(q=-1\)}{q=-1}}
For integer \(n\ge2\),
\[
 [2m]_{-1}=0,
 \qquad [2m+1]_{-1}=1.
\]
Taylor expansion gives
\begin{align}
 [2m]_q&=m(q+1)+O((q+1)^2),\label{eq:qnumber-even-minusone}\\
 [2m+1]_q&=1-m(q+1)+O((q+1)^2).
 \label{eq:qnumber-odd-minusone}
\end{align}
Hence
\[
 q=-1+\frac ym+O(y^2)
\]
for even order, and
\[
 q=-1+\frac{1-y}{m}+O((1-y)^2)
\]
for odd order.  For noninteger \(x\), the value \((-1)^x\) depends on a
logarithm branch, so \(q=-1\) is itself a branch point in the base variable.

\section{The \texorpdfstring{\(q\)}{q}-factorial near \texorpdfstring{\(q=0\)}{q=0}}
Let
\[
 F_n(q)=[n]_q!=\prod_{j=1}^n[j]_q.
\]
For \(n\ge2\),
\[
 F_n(q)=1+(n-1)q+\frac{(n-2)(n+1)}2q^2+O(q^3).
\]
Thus
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qfactorial-q0-inverse}
 q=\frac{y-1}{n-1}
 -\frac{(n-2)(n+1)}{2(n-1)^3}(y-1)^2+O((y-1)^3).
\end{equation}
Since all coefficients are positive, this is the local form of the unique
positive base inverse.

\section{The \texorpdfstring{\(q\)}{q}-factorial near \texorpdfstring{\(q=1\)}{q=1}}
The degree is
\[
 N=\binom n2.
\]
Summing \eqref{eq:qnumber-q1-forward} over \(x=1,\ldots,n\) gives
\begin{align}
 \log\frac{[n]_{e^t}!}{n!}
 ={}&\frac N2t+
 \sum_{m\ge1}\frac{B_{2m}}{2m(2m)!}
 \sum_{j=1}^n(j^{2m}-1)t^{2m}.
 \label{eq:qfactorial-q1-forward}
\end{align}
Since
\[
 \sum_{j=1}^n(j^2-1)=\frac{N(2n+5)}3,
\]
with \(s=\log(y/n!)\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qfactorial-q1-inverse}
 t=\frac{2s}{N}-\frac{2n+5}{9N^2}s^2
 +\frac{(2n+5)^2}{81N^3}s^3+O(s^4).
\end{equation}

\section{Cyclotomic Puiseux inverses of the factorial}
Let \(\zeta\ne1\) be primitive of order \(m\), and put
\[
 \nu=\Floor{n/m}.
\]
Exactly the factors \([m]_q,[2m]_q,\ldots,[\nu m]_q\) vanish at \(\zeta\), each
simply.  Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qfactorial-root-unity}
 [n]_q!=C_{n,m,\zeta}(q-\zeta)^\nu(1+O(q-\zeta)),
\end{equation}
where
\[
 C_{n,m,\zeta}=
 \prod_{\substack{1\le j\le n\\m\nmid j}}[j]_\zeta
 \prod_{r=1}^{\nu}\frac{-mr\zeta^{-1}}{1-\zeta}.
\]
The inverse at target zero has \(\nu\) sheets:
\[
 q-\zeta\sim\left(\frac{y}{C_{n,m,\zeta}}\right)^{1/\nu}.
\]
At \(m=2\), \(\zeta=-1\), write \(n=2\nu\) or \(2\nu+1\).  A more precise
real expansion is
\begin{align}
 [2\nu]_q!&=\nu!(q+1)^\nu
 \left(1-\nu(\nu-1)(q+1)+O((q+1)^2)\right),\\
 [2\nu+1]_q!&=\nu!(q+1)^\nu
 \left(1-\nu^2(q+1)+O((q+1)^2)\right).
\end{align}
With \(\rho=(y/\nu!)^{1/\nu}\), the corresponding sheets satisfy
\begin{align}
 q+1&=\rho+(\nu-1)\rho^2+O(\rho^3),&&n=2\nu,\\
 q+1&=\rho+\nu\rho^2+O(\rho^3),&&n=2\nu+1.
 \label{eq:qfactorial-minusone-inverse}
\end{align}

\section{The factorial inverse at infinity}
Reciprocity gives
\[
 [n]_q!=q^N[n]_{q^{-1}}!.
\]
Since the coefficient of \(q\) at the origin is \(n-1\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qfactorial-infinity-inverse}
 q=y^{1/N}-\frac{n-1}{N}+O(y^{-1/N})
 =y^{1/N}-\frac2n+O(y^{-1/N}).
\end{equation}
The same formula, with a selected \(N\)-th root, describes all complex
asymptotic sheets.

\chapter{Inverse \texorpdfstring{\(q\)}{q}-gamma and \texorpdfstring{\(q\)}{q}-digamma functions}

\section{Forward formulas}
For \(0<q<1\),
\begin{align}
 \Gamma_q(x)&=(1-q)^{1-x}\frac{(q;q)_\infty}{(q^x;q)_\infty},
 \label{eq:qgamma-definition}\\
 \psi_q(x)&=-\log(1-q)+(\log q)
 \sum_{j\ge0}\frac{q^{x+j}}{1-q^{x+j}},
 \label{eq:qdigamma-definition}\\
 \psi_q'(x)&=(\log q)^2
 \sum_{j\ge0}\frac{q^{x+j}}{(1-q^{x+j})^2}>0.
 \label{eq:qtrigamma-positive}
\end{align}
The monotonicity consequences agree with the standard theory summarized in the
DLMF \cite{DLMF}.  Moak's \(q\)-Stirling expansion controls large \(x\)
uniformly in important regimes \cite{Moak1984}.

\section{Inverting \texorpdfstring{\(\Gamma_q\)}{Gammaq} in its argument}
Because \(\psi_q' >0\), \(\psi_q\) has at most one zero.  As
\(x\downarrow0\), \(\psi_q(x)\to-\infty\); as \(x\to\infty\),
\(\psi_q(x)\to-\log(1-q)>0\).  Hence there is a unique
\(x_q^*>0\) at which \(\Gamma_q\) attains its minimum
\[
 M_q=\Gamma_q(x_q^*).
\]

\begin{theorem}[\statusproved\ Real branch count for inverse \(q\)-gamma]
For \(y>M_q\), the equation \(\Gamma_q(x)=y\) has exactly two positive real
solutions; for \(y=M_q\), one double solution; for \(0<y<M_q\), no positive
real solution.  Near the minimum,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qgamma-minimum-inverse}
 x=x_q^*\pm
 \sqrt{\frac{2(y-M_q)}{M_q\psi_q'(x_q^*)}}+O(y-M_q).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Strict increase of \(\psi_q\) makes \(\Gamma_q\) decrease before its unique
critical point and increase afterward.  At the critical point,
\(\Gamma_q''=M_q\psi_q'(x_q^*)>0\), and fold inversion gives the expansion.
\end{proof}

\section{Left and right endpoint inverses in \texorpdfstring{\(x\)}{x}}
Using the recurrence \(\Gamma_q(x+1)=[x]_q\Gamma_q(x)\), one finds
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qgamma-left-forward}
 \log\Gamma_q(x)
 =-\log x+\log\frac{1-q}{-\log q}
 +c_qx+O(x^2),
 \qquad
 c_q=\psi_q(1)-\frac12\log q.
\end{equation}
Therefore, as \(y\to\infty\) on the left branch,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qgamma-left-inverse}
 x=\frac{A_q}{y}+\frac{A_q^2c_q}{y^2}+O(y^{-3}),
 \qquad A_q=\frac{1-q}{-\log q}.
\end{equation}

On the right branch,
\[
 \Gamma_q(x)=C_q(1-q)^{-x}
 \left(1+\frac{q^x}{1-q}+O(q^{2x})\right),
 \qquad C_q=(q;q)_\infty(1-q).
\]
Let
\[
 x_0=\frac{\log y-\log C_q}{-\log(1-q)}.
\]
Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qgamma-right-inverse}
 x=x_0-\frac{q^{x_0}}{(1-q)(-\log(1-q))}+O(q^{2x_0}).
\end{equation}

\section{Base inversion near \texorpdfstring{\(q=0\)}{q=0}}
From \eqref{eq:qgamma-definition}, one obtains the generalized expansion
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qgamma-q0-forward}
 \log\Gamma_q(x)
 =q^x+(x-2)q+\frac12q^{2x}+q^{x+1}
 +\frac{x-4}{2}q^2+\cdots,
\end{equation}
where terms must be sorted by exponent and combined when exponents collide.
This immediately gives distinct inverse regimes for \(x>0\):
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item \(0<x<1\): if \(s=\log y\downarrow0\), then
\[
 q\sim s^{1/x}.
\]
The next term comes from \(q^{2x}\) for \(x<1/2\), from \(q\) for
\(x>1/2\), and from both at \(x=1/2\).
\item \(1<x<2\): \(s\sim(x-2)q<0\), so
\[
 q\sim\frac{s}{x-2}.
\]
\item \(x>2\): the same formula holds with \(s>0\).
\item \(x=1\) and \(x=2\): \(\Gamma_q(x)=1\) identically, so base recovery
from this observable is impossible.
\end{enumerate}
This is a clean example where ordinary Taylor reversion fails solely because a
continuous parameter creates fractional exponents.

\section{Base inversion near \texorpdfstring{\(q=1\)}{q=1}}
Combining the Bernoulli expansion of \((q^x;q)_\infty\) with the eta-product
asymptotic gives, for \(q=e^{-t}\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qgamma-q1-forward}
 \log\frac{\Gamma_{e^{-t}}(x)}{\Gamma(x)}
 =-A_xt+B_xt^2+O(t^3),
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qgamma-A-B}
 A_x=\frac{(x-1)(x-2)}4,
 \qquad
 B_x=\frac{(x-1)(x-2)(2x+3)}{144}.
\end{equation}
The sign of \(A_x\) reproduces the known change of monotonicity across
\(x=1\) and \(x=2\).

For \(x\notin\{1,2\}\), set \(s=\log(y/\Gamma(x))\).  Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qgamma-q1-inverse}
 t=-\frac{s}{A_x}+\frac{B_x}{A_x^3}s^2
 -\frac{2B_x^2}{A_x^5}s^3+O(s^4),
 \qquad q=e^{-t}.
\end{equation}
At \(x=1,2\), every coefficient vanishes because the function is exactly
constant, not merely critical to finite order.

\section{The exterior base and \texorpdfstring{\(q\to\infty\)}{q to infinity}}
For \(q>1\), the standard normalization satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qgamma-reciprocity}
 \Gamma_q(x)=q^{(x-1)(x-2)/2}\Gamma_{1/q}(x).
\end{equation}
Thus, away from \(x=1,2\), the leading base inverse is the power law
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qgamma-infinity-inverse}
 q\sim y^{2/((x-1)(x-2))},
\end{equation}
with the interpretation reversed when the exponent is negative.  Corrections
are obtained by substituting the small-\(Q\) generalized expansion
\eqref{eq:qgamma-q0-forward}, \(Q=q^{-1}\), and reverting.  For complex or
negative \(q\), a branch of \(q^{(x-1)(x-2)/2}\) must be specified.

\section{Inverse \texorpdfstring{\(q\)}{q}-digamma in \texorpdfstring{\(x\)}{x}}
For fixed \(0<q<1\), \(\psi_q\) is a strictly increasing bijection
\[
 \psi_q:(0,\infty)\longrightarrow(-\infty,-\log(1-q)).
\]
Hence its real inverse exists exactly on that interval.  From
\eqref{eq:qgamma-left-forward},
\[
 \psi_q(x)=-\frac1x+c_q+O(x),
\]
so for \(y\to-\infty\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qdigamma-left-inverse}
 x=-\frac1{y-c_q}+O(y^{-3}).
\end{equation}
At the upper endpoint,
\[
 -\log(1-q)-\psi_q(x)
 \sim\frac{-\log q}{1-q}q^x,
\]
and therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qdigamma-right-inverse}
 x\sim
 \frac{\log\bigl(-\log(1-q)-y\bigr)
 +\log\bigl((1-q)/(-\log q)\bigr)}{\log q}.
\end{equation}

\section{The critical base inverse of \texorpdfstring{\(\psi_q\)}{psiq}}
Differentiating \eqref{eq:qgamma-q1-forward} in \(x\) yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qdigamma-q1-forward}
 \psi_{e^{-t}}(x)-\psi(x)
 =-\frac{2x-3}{4}t
 +\frac{6x^2-6x-5}{144}t^2+O(t^3).
\end{equation}
For \(x\ne3/2\), base inversion is regular.  At \(x=3/2\), the linear term
vanishes and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qdigamma-critical}
 \psi_{e^{-t}}(3/2)=\psi(3/2)-\frac{t^2}{288}+O(t^3).
\end{equation}
Thus the base inverse changes to a square-root branch:
\[
 t\sim\sqrt{288\bigl(\psi(3/2)-y\bigr)}.
\]
This critical value is the derivative-in-\(x\) counterpart of the fixed points
\(x=1,2\) for \(\Gamma_q\).

\chapter{Inverse \texorpdfstring{\(q\)}{q}-beta functions}

\section{Monotone parameter inverses}
Define
\[
 B_q(x,y)=\frac{\Gamma_q(x)\Gamma_q(y)}{\Gamma_q(x+y)}.
\]
For positive \(x,y\),
\begin{align*}
 \partial_x\log B_q(x,y)&=\psi_q(x)-\psi_q(x+y)<0,\\
 \partial_y\log B_q(x,y)&=\psi_q(y)-\psi_q(x+y)<0.
\end{align*}
Thus either shape parameter has a unique real inverse when the other is fixed
and the target is in range.

As \(x\downarrow0\),
\[
 B_q(x,y)\sim\frac{1-q}{(-\log q)x}.
\]
As \(x\to\infty\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qbeta-saturation}
 B_q(x,y)\longrightarrow
 S_q(y)=\Gamma_q(y)(1-q)^y
 =\frac{(q;q)_\infty(1-q)}{(q^y;q)_\infty}.
\end{equation}
Therefore the real \(x\)-inverse exists for targets above \(S_q(y)\).  Near
saturation,
\[
 B_q(x,y)=S_q(y)\left(1+[y]_qq^x+O(q^{2x})\right),
\]
so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qbeta-parameter-inverse}
 x\sim\log_q\left(\frac{B_q(x,y)/S_q(y)-1}{[y]_q}\right).
\end{equation}

\section{Base inversion near \texorpdfstring{\(q=1\)}{q=1}}
Using \eqref{eq:qgamma-q1-forward}, one finds
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qbeta-q1-forward}
 \log\frac{B_{e^{-t}}(x,y)}{B(x,y)}
 =\frac{xy-1}{2}t
 -\frac{x^2y+xy^2-xy-1}{24}t^2+O(t^3).
\end{equation}
The linear coefficient vanishes on the hyperbola \(xy=1\).

\begin{theorem}[\statusproved\ Critical curve for inverse \(q\)-beta]
If \(xy\ne1\), the base inverse near \(q=1\) is an ordinary Taylor branch.  If
\(xy=1\) but \((x,y)\ne(1,1)\), then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qbeta-critical-forward}
 \log\frac{B_{e^{-t}}(x,y)}{B(x,y)}
 =-\frac{x+y-2}{24}t^2+O(t^3),
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qbeta-critical-inverse}
 t\sim\sqrt{
 \frac{-24\log(B_{e^{-t}}(x,y)/B(x,y))}{x+y-2}}.
\end{equation}
At \((x,y)=(1,1)\), \(B_q(1,1)=1\) identically and base recovery is
impossible.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Substitute \(xy=1\) into the quadratic coefficient of
\eqref{eq:qbeta-q1-forward}; it becomes \(-(x+y-2)/24\).  Since
\(x+y\ge2\) for positive reciprocal variables, the coefficient is nonzero
except at \(x=y=1\).  Regular or fold reversion then applies.
\end{proof}

For \(xy\ne1\), if
\[
 s=\log\frac{y_0}{B(x,y)},
 \quad A=\frac{xy-1}{2},
 \quad C=-\frac{x^2y+xy^2-xy-1}{24},
\]
then
\[
 t=\frac{s}{A}-\frac{C}{A^3}s^2+O(s^3).
\]

\section{The exterior inverse}
The reciprocity of \(q\)-gamma gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qbeta-reciprocity}
 B_q(x,y)=q^{1-xy}B_{1/q}(x,y),
 \qquad q>1.
\end{equation}
Hence, for \(xy\ne1\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qbeta-infinity-inverse}
 q\sim\left(\frac{y_0}{B_0(x,y)}\right)^{1/(1-xy)},
\end{equation}
where \(B_0(x,y)=1\) in the generic positive-parameter normalization, subject
to the fractional small-\(q\) corrections inherited from
\eqref{eq:qgamma-q0-forward}.  The surface \(xy=1\) is again exceptional:
the explicit power vanishes and the first inverse information comes from the
small-\(1/q\) correction.

\chapter{Euler \texorpdfstring{\(q\)}{q}-exponentials and basic hypergeometric inverses}

\section{Exact reduction of Euler exponentials}
Use the unscaled Euler functions
\[
 e_q(z)=\frac1{(z;q)_\infty},
 \qquad
 E_q(z)=(-z;q)_\infty,
 \qquad |q|<1.
\]
Their argument inverses are exactly the argument inverse of the infinite
product:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qexp-argument-inverse}
 e_q^{-1}(y)=\Aq(1/y),
 \qquad
 E_q^{-1}(y)=-\Aq(y),
\end{equation}
with matching sheet labels.  Every endpoint expansion in the infinite-product
chapters transfers immediately.  The scaled convention
\(e_q((1-q)z)\to e^z\) merely adds a factor \((1-q)^{-1}\) after inversion.

Base inversion likewise reduces to
\[
 (z;q)_\infty=1/y
 \quad\text{or}\quad
 (-z;q)_\infty=y.
\]
Thus the radial formulas at \(q=1,-1\), and primitive roots are already a
complete base-inverse asymptotic theory for Euler \(q\)-exponentials.

\section{Local inverse of a basic hypergeometric series in its argument}
Adopt the standard convention
\begin{align}
 {}_r\phi_s\!\left(
 \begin{matrix}a_1,\ldots,a_r\\b_1,\ldots,b_s\end{matrix};q,z\right)
 =\sum_{n\ge0}c_nz^n,
 \label{eq:basic-hypergeometric-definition}\\
 c_n=
 \frac{(a_1,\ldots,a_r;q)_n}{(q,b_1,\ldots,b_s;q)_n}
 \left((-1)^nq^{\binom n2}\right)^{1+s-r}.
 \notag
\end{align}
If \(c_1\ne0\), the local inverse at target one is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:basic-hypergeometric-z-inverse}
 z=\frac{y-1}{c_1}-\frac{c_2}{c_1^3}(y-1)^2
 +\frac{2c_2^2-c_1c_3}{c_1^5}(y-1)^3+O((y-1)^4).
\end{equation}
This formula covers \(q\)-Bessel, \(q\)-Airy, and many orthogonal-polynomial
generating functions after specialization.

If \(c_1=0\), one must locate the first nonzero \(c_r\).  The inverse then has
\(r\) Puiseux sheets.  Vanishing may be caused by a numerator parameter equal
to one, by a root-of-unity base, or by cancellation between several
parameters.

\section{Inversion in a numerator parameter}
Fix all variables except \(a_1\).  Near \(a_1=1\),
\[
 (a_1;q)_n=(1-a_1)(a_1q;q)_{n-1},\qquad n\ge1.
\]
Thus the entire nonconstant part of the series turns on linearly:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hypergeometric-parameter-turnon}
 {}_r\phi_s=1+(1-a_1)\,
 \mathcal S(q,z,\bm a',\bm b)+O((1-a_1)^2),
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal S\) is an explicitly convergent basic hypergeometric sum
formed by replacing \((a_1;q)_n/(1-a_1)\) with \((q;q)_{n-1}\) at
\(a_1=1\).  If \(\mathcal S\ne0\),
\[
 a_1=1-\frac{y-1}{\mathcal S}+O((y-1)^2).
\]
At \(a_1=q^{-N}\), the series terminates.  Moving the parameter away from this
point activates the tail \(n>N\) linearly, so the inverse is regular unless a
second cancellation occurs.  Near a denominator parameter
\(b_j=q^{-N}\), poles replace zeros and reciprocal target coordinates are
more appropriate.

\section{Inversion in the base}
For fixed \(z\) inside the convergence disk, each coefficient in
\eqref{eq:basic-hypergeometric-definition} has a finite or infinite
\(q\)-Pochhammer expansion.  Near \(q=0\), a Newton polygon in the parameter
exponents gives the inverse.  Near \(q=1\), one first rescales parameters as
\(a_i=q^{\alpha_i}\), \(b_j=q^{\beta_j}\) when a nontrivial classical
hypergeometric limit is desired.  Fixed parameters and power-scaled parameters
are different asymptotic problems and should not be mixed.

At roots of unity, individual coefficients may have poles while the complete
series remains finite after cancellation.  The correct order of operations is:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
\item combine terms or use a transformation identity that makes cancellation
explicit;
\item extract the radial action and cyclic factor;
\item only then revert the target relation.
\end{enumerate}
Termwise inversion before cancellation is generally wrong.

\section{Implicit eigenvalue and zero inverses}
Many \(q\)-orthogonal polynomials satisfy a three-term recurrence
\[
 p_{n+1}(x;\lambda)=
 (x-\alpha_n(\lambda))p_n(x;\lambda)-\beta_n(\lambda)p_{n-1}(x;\lambda),
\]
where \(\lambda\) may be \(q\) or a shape parameter.  A simple zero
\(x_j(\lambda)\) obeys
\[
 \frac{\Differential x_j}{\Differential\lambda}
 =-\frac{\partial_\lambda p_n(x_j;\lambda)}
 {\partial_xp_n(x_j;\lambda)}.
\]
This is the same partial-inverse sensitivity formula as before.  At a multiple
zero, eigenvalue sheets coalesce and Puiseux expansions replace Taylor series.
The Jacobi-matrix representation supplies certified real brackets and a stable
continuation algorithm.

\chapter{Theta functions and the inverse nome}

\section{Small-nome inverse series}
For the standard theta constants,
\begin{align*}
 \vartheta_2(0,q)&=2q^{1/4}(1+q^2+q^6+\cdots),\\
 \vartheta_3(0,q)&=1+2q+2q^4+2q^9+\cdots,\\
 \vartheta_4(0,q)&=1-2q+2q^4-2q^9+\cdots.
\end{align*}
Hence, on the principal small positive branch,
\begin{align}
 q&=\left(\frac{y}{2}\right)^4
 \left(1-4\left(\frac{y}{2}\right)^8+O(y^{16})\right),
 &&y=\vartheta_2(0,q),
 \label{eq:theta2-inverse}\\
 q&=u-u^4+O(u^7),
 &&u=\frac{\vartheta_3(0,q)-1}{2},
 \label{eq:theta3-inverse}\\
 q&=v+v^4+O(v^7),
 &&v=\frac{1-\vartheta_4(0,q)}{2}.
 \label{eq:theta4-inverse}
\end{align}
The fractional fourth power in \(\vartheta_2\) is a genuine four-sheeted
complex inverse at the origin.

\section{The elliptic modulus and exact nome inverse}
Let
\[
 m=k^2=\frac{\vartheta_2(0,q)^4}{\vartheta_3(0,q)^4}.
\]
For \(0<m<1\), the nome is exactly
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nome-exact-inverse}
 q=\exp\left(-\pi\frac{K(1-m)}{K(m)}\right),
\end{equation}
where \(K\) is the complete elliptic integral.  Its small-\(m\) expansion is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nome-series}
 q=\frac{m}{16}+8\left(\frac{m}{16}\right)^2
 +84\left(\frac{m}{16}\right)^3
 +992\left(\frac{m}{16}\right)^4
 +12514\left(\frac{m}{16}\right)^5+\cdots.
\end{equation}
This is an instructive case where a difficult-looking inverse \(q\)-series has
an exact representation in a different special-function language.

\section{The modular endpoint \texorpdfstring{\(q\to1\)}{q to 1}}
Set \(q=e^{-\pi t}\).  Modular transformation gives
\[
 \vartheta_3(0,e^{-\pi t})
 =t^{-1/2}\vartheta_3(0,e^{-\pi/t})
 =t^{-1/2}\left(1+2e^{-\pi/t}+\cdots\right).
\]
Thus for a large target \(y\),
\[
 t=y^{-2}\left(1+O(e^{-\pi y^2})\right),
 \qquad
 q=\exp(-\pi y^{-2}+O(e^{-\pi y^2})).
\]
The inverse correction is exponentially small, not algebraic.  Similar
modular formulas treat \(\vartheta_2\) and \(\vartheta_4\), with branch changes
under the modular group.

\section{Root-of-unity and modular-sheet structure}
Theta functions possess both product expansions and modular transformations.
Near a rational point of the modular parameter, the most stable inverse is
usually obtained by applying a modular transformation that sends the cusp to
infinity, using a small-nome inverse there, and transforming back.  The modular
matrix is part of the inverse branch label.  This is the theta-function analog
of recording the primitive root \(\zeta\) and radial direction for a
\(q\)-Pochhammer inverse.

\part{The Fabius--Rvachev deformation and inverse parameter recovery}

\chapter{A geometrically weighted atomic-function family}

\section{Definition by a random series}
Let \(U_0,U_1,\ldots\) be independent uniform random variables on
\([-1,1]\), and let \(0<q<1\).  Define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:atomic-random-series}
 X_q=(1-q)\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}q^jU_j.
\end{equation}
The weights are positive and sum to one, so \(X_q\in[-1,1]\).  Its
characteristic function is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:atomic-characteristic}
 \widehat u_q(t)=\mathbb E e^{\ImaginaryUnit tX_q}
 =\prod_{j=0}^{\infty}\SincRad\bigl((1-q)q^jt\bigr),
 \qquad \SincRad z=\frac{\sin z}{z}.
\end{equation}
The product decays faster than every negative power on the real axis, and
therefore \(X_q\) has a compactly supported \(C^\infty\) density \(u_q\).
The distributional identity
\[
 X_q\overset d=(1-q)U_0+qX_q'
\]
with an independent copy \(X_q'\) yields the refinement equation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:atomic-refinement}
 \widehat u_q(t)=\SincRad((1-q)t)\widehat u_q(qt).
\end{equation}

At \(q=1/2\), the weights are \(2^{-j-1}\), and \(u_{1/2}\) is the standard
Rvachev up-density on \([-1,1]\).  If
\[
 F_q(x)=\Pr(X_q\le2x-1),\qquad 0\le x\le1,
\]
then \(F_{1/2}\) is the Fabius function.  On the left half of the support,
\(u_{1/2}(t)=F_{1/2}(t+1)\); symmetry supplies the right half.

\section{Exact cumulants}
Cumulants add under independent sums and scale homogeneously.  For
\(U\sim\operatorname{Unif}[-1,1]\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uniform-cumulants}
 \kappa_{2m}(U)=\frac{2^{2m}B_{2m}}{2m},
 \qquad \kappa_{2m+1}(U)=0.
\end{equation}
Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:atomic-cumulants}
 \kappa_{2m}(X_q)
 =\kappa_{2m}(U)
 \frac{(1-q)^{2m}}{1-q^{2m}},
 \qquad \kappa_{2m+1}(X_q)=0.
\end{equation}
The factors \((1-q)^{2m}/(1-q^{2m})\) are elementary \(q\)-analogs, and their
parameter inverses are part of the same theory developed earlier.

\begin{theorem}[\statusproved\ Exact variance inverse]
The variance and its inverse are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:atomic-variance-inverse}
 V(q)=\Variance X_q=\frac{1-q}{3(1+q)},
 \qquad
 q=\frac{1-3V}{1+3V}.
\end{equation}
In particular, the Rvachev value \(q=1/2\) is characterized by
\(V=1/9\).
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Use \(\Variance U=1/3\) and sum the geometric series of squared weights:
\[
 \Variance X_q=\frac13(1-q)^2\sum_{j\ge0}q^{2j}
 =\frac{1-q}{3(1+q)}.
\]
Solving the resulting linear-fractional equation gives the inverse.
\end{proof}

\caption{The exact variance observable for the geometric atomic-function
family.  It maps \(q\in(0,1)\) monotonically onto \((0,1/3)\).}

\end{figure}

\section{Every even cumulant is an inverse observable}
Normalize
\[
 R_m(q)=\frac{\kappa_{2m}(X_q)}{\kappa_{2m}(U)}
 =\frac{(1-q)^{2m}}{1-q^{2m}}.
\]

\begin{theorem}[\statusproved\ Monotone cumulant recovery]
For every \(m\ge1\), \(R_m\) is strictly decreasing from one to zero on
\((0,1)\).  Hence any nonzero even cumulant determines \(q\) uniquely through
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cumulant-polynomial-inverse}
 R_m(1-q^{2m})=(1-q)^{2m}.
\end{equation}
The endpoint inverse asymptotics are
\begin{align}
 q&=\frac{1-R_m}{2m}+O((1-R_m)^2),&&R_m\uparrow1,
 \label{eq:cumulant-q0-inverse}\\
 1-q&=(2mR_m)^{1/(2m-1)}
 \left(1+O(R_m^{1/(2m-1)})\right),&&R_m\downarrow0.
 \label{eq:cumulant-q1-inverse}
\end{align}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Logarithmic differentiation gives
\[
 \frac{R_m'}{R_m}
 =-\frac{2m}{1-q}+\frac{2mq^{2m-1}}{1-q^{2m}}<0,
\]
because
\(q^{2m-1}(1-q)<1-q^{2m}\).  The endpoint expansions follow by ordinary and
Puiseux reversion.
\end{proof}

\section{Scale-free recovery from kurtosis}
Suppose the observed variable is \(Y=cX_q\) with unknown nonzero scale \(c\).
Variance alone no longer identifies \(q\), but standardized cumulants remove
\(c\).  The excess kurtosis is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:atomic-kurtosis}
 \gamma_2(q)=\frac{\kappa_4(X_q)}{\kappa_2(X_q)^2}
 =-\frac65\frac{1-q^2}{1+q^2}.
\end{equation}
It has the exact inverse
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kurtosis-q-inverse}
 q=\sqrt{\frac{1+5\gamma_2/6}{1-5\gamma_2/6}},
 \qquad -\frac65<\gamma_2<0.
\end{equation}
The standardized sixth cumulant supplies an independent check:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:atomic-sixth-standardized}
 \frac{\kappa_6(X_q)}{\kappa_2(X_q)^3}
 =\frac{48}{7}\frac{(1-q^2)^2}{1+q^2+q^4}.
\end{equation}
Thus the deformation parameter remains exactly identifiable even when the
support scale is unknown.

\section{Conditioning}
From \eqref{eq:atomic-variance-inverse},
\[
 \frac{\Differential q}{\Differential V}=-\frac6{(1+3V)^2}.
\]
Absolute recovery remains regular throughout \(0<V<1/3\).  The relative
condition number diverges as \(q\downarrow0\), because relative error in a
parameter tending to zero is intrinsically severe; this is not an absolute
instability.

\section{Parameter derivatives of the characteristic function}
Let
\[
 w_j(q)=(1-q)q^j,
 \qquad
 w_j'(q)=q^{j-1}\bigl(j-(j+1)q\bigr).
\]
Away from zeros of the sinc factors,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:atomic-score-fourier}
 \partial_q\log\widehat u_q(t)
 =\sum_{j\ge0}w_j'(q)
 \left(t\cot(w_j(q)t)-\frac1{w_j(q)}\right).
\end{equation}
This is a rapidly convergent even-power series near \(t=0\), and its
coefficients are derivatives of \eqref{eq:atomic-cumulants}.  Fourier inversion
then gives the tangent density \(\partial_qu_q\) and tangent CDF
\(\partial_qF_q\).

If \(x_p(q)\) is a quantile defined by
\[
 \Pr(X_q\le x_p(q))=p,
\]
then, wherever \(u_q(x_p)>0\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:atomic-quantile-sensitivity}
 x_p'(q)=-\frac{\partial_q\Pr(X_q\le x)|_{x=x_p}}
 {u_q(x_p)}.
\end{equation}
This is the ordinary partial-inverse sensitivity formula in a probabilistic
disguise.

\section{Limits \texorpdfstring{\(q\to0\)}{q to 0} and
\texorpdfstring{\(q\to1\)}{q to 1}}
As \(q\downarrow0\), \(X_q\to U_0\), so the density approaches the uniform
density on \([-1,1]\).  Corrections can be developed by expanding the
refinement equation in powers of \(q\), with boundary layers at the support
endpoints.

As \(q\uparrow1\), \(X_q\to0\) in probability because its variance tends to
zero.  After standardization,
\[
 Z_q=\frac{X_q}{\sqrt{V(q)}},
\]
the largest normalized summand tends to zero, and the triangular-array central
limit theorem gives \(Z_q\Rightarrow N(0,1)\).  The standardized cumulants in
\eqref{eq:atomic-cumulants} quantify Edgeworth corrections and can themselves
be inverted to estimate \(q\) from a nearly Gaussian observed profile.

\chapter{Inverse Fabius and Rvachev observables}

\section{Recovering \texorpdfstring{\(q\)}{q} from moments}
At the exact Rvachev member, \(q=1/2\), the moment and cumulant identities give
an overdetermined set of checks:
\[
 \Variance X_{1/2}=\frac19,
 \qquad
 \gamma_2(X_{1/2})=-\frac{18}{25},
\]
with the sixth standardized cumulant obtained from
\eqref{eq:atomic-sixth-standardized}.  If numerically computed Fabius or up
values are used to estimate moments, disagreement among these recovered
values of \(q\) measures quadrature or truncation error.

\section{Recovery from a CDF value}
Fix \(x\in(0,1)\), and consider
\[
 q\longmapsto F_q(x)=\Pr(X_q\le2x-1).
\]
Unlike the variance map, this need not be globally monotone for every \(x\).
A local inverse around \(q_0\) exists when
\(\partial_qF_q(x)|_{q_0}\ne0\), and its first terms are
\begin{align}
 q-q_0={}&\frac{p-F_{q_0}(x)}{\partial_qF_{q_0}(x)}\notag\\
 &-\frac{\partial_q^2F_{q_0}(x)}
 {2(\partial_qF_{q_0}(x))^3}
 (p-F_{q_0}(x))^2+\cdots.
 \label{eq:fabius-cdf-inverse}
\end{align}
The derivatives can be obtained from
\eqref{eq:atomic-score-fourier}, from differentiated refinement equations, or
by common-random-number Monte Carlo.

CDF inversion becomes ill-conditioned near symmetry points forced to be
constant.  For example,
\[
 F_q(1/2)=\Pr(X_q\le0)=\frac12
\]
for every \(q\), so the midpoint carries no parameter information.  This is a
simple but important experimental-design constraint.

\section{Recovery from several samples}
Given values \(F_q(x_1),\ldots,F_q(x_r)\), one can solve a least-squares or
maximum-likelihood problem in \(q\).  The local Fisher-like information for
independent measurements with variances \(\sigma_i^2\) is
\[
 I(q)=\sum_{i=1}^r\frac{(\partial_qF_q(x_i))^2}{\sigma_i^2}.
\]
Sampling points should be placed where the tangent CDF is large and linearly
independent of nuisance directions such as translation and scale.  Variance
and kurtosis provide analytically orthogonalized alternatives.

\section{A connection to geometric-comb interpolation}
The weights \((1-q)q^j\) form a geometric comb.  Every moment derivative,
characteristic-function jet, and refinement iterate samples analytic functions
on this comb.  Newton interpolation on nodes \(q^j\) therefore provides a
natural discrete representation of tangent observables.  Its divided
differences are \(q\)-differences, and the products
\[
 \prod_{j=0}^{n-1}(x-q^j)
 =x^n(x^{-1};q)_n
\]
are finite \(q\)-Pochhammer factors.  Consequently, inversion of the
Fabius--Rvachev deformation and inversion of \(q\)-products are linked at the
level of both probability and interpolation: the same geometric node set
controls cumulants, Newton bases, and parameter sensitivity.

\section{Rigorous and conjectural geometry of CDF branches}
\begin{conjecture}[\statusconjectural\ Finite-fold CDF inverse]
For each fixed \(x\in(0,1)\setminus\{1/2\}\), the map
\(q\mapsto F_q(x)\) has only finitely many critical points on every compact
subinterval of \((0,1)\), all generically simple.  At a simple critical point,
the inverse has the universal square-root form.  Exceptional higher
singularities occur only at symmetry-enforced or refinement-resonant values of
\(x\).
\end{conjecture}

\begin{conjecture}[\statusconjectural\ Total cumulant identifiability]
For the larger family
\[
 X_{q,c,\mu}=\mu+c\sum_{j\ge0}q^jU_j,
\]
the first moment, variance, and kurtosis form a globally injective coordinate
system on \(\mu\in\RealNumbers\), \(c>0\), \(0<q<1\).  The explicit inverse consists of
translation from the mean, \(q\) from \eqref{eq:kurtosis-q-inverse}, and
\(c\) from the variance.
\end{conjecture}
The last statement is in fact immediate at the algebraic level; the
conjectural aspect concerns stable identifiability under natural broader
atomic-digit perturbations, where the digit law is not known exactly.

\part{Branch-aware computation, certification, and reproducibility}

\chapter{Stable numerical inversion}

\section{The forward map must be evaluated in inverse coordinates}
An inverse algorithm is only as reliable as its forward evaluator.  Directly
multiplying many nearly unit factors, subtracting two nearly equal
\(q\)-gamma logarithms, or forming a Gaussian coefficient as a quotient of
three small products can destroy the digits needed by Newton correction.  The
following representation rules are therefore part of the inverse theory, not
mere implementation details.

For a finite product away from its zeros, use
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stable-finite-log}
 \log(a;q)_n=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\log(1-aq^j),
\end{equation}
with a branch-continuous complex logarithm and a \texttt{log1p}-type
implementation.  If a factor is close to zero, remove it explicitly:
\[
 (a;q)_n=(1-aq^r)\prod_{\substack{0\le j<n\\j\ne r}}(1-aq^j).
\]
This exposes the local zero and prevents a small factor from being hidden
inside a large logarithmic sum.

For an infinite product with \(0<q<1\), truncate only after bounding the tail.
For \(0\le a<1\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qpoch-tail-bound}
 0\le-\sum_{j=N}^{\infty}\log(1-aq^j)
 \le \frac{a q^N}{(1-aq^N)(1-q)}.
\end{equation}
Indeed, \(-\log(1-x)\le x/(1-x)\), and the remaining geometric sum gives the
right-hand side.  The same argument applies to absolute values when
\(\AbsoluteValue{a q^N}<1\).  Thus a requested forward tolerance can be converted into a
certified truncation index before inversion begins.

For Gaussian coefficients, the product quotient
\[
 \GaussianBinomial{n}{k}{q}=\prod_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1-q^{n-k+j}}{1-q^j}
\]
is stable in logarithmic form away from roots of unity.  Near \(q=1\), use the
analytic function
\[
 \operatorname{exprel}(z)=\frac{e^z-1}{z}
\]
and write
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stable-gaussian-q1}
 \log\GaussianBinomial{n}{k}{q}
 =\log\binom nk+
 \sum_{j=1}^{k}
 \left[
  \log\operatorname{exprel}((n-k+j)t)
  -\log\operatorname{exprel}(jt)
 \right],
 \qquad q=e^t.
\end{equation}
At a root of unity, cancel cyclotomic factors symbolically or group exponents
by residue class before numerical evaluation.

\section{Choice of local coordinate}
The natural unknown is rarely \(q\) itself over the entire domain.  The
following coordinates regularize the main endpoints:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{0.93\textwidth}{@{}lllX@{}}
\toprule
regime & coordinate & reconstruction & reason\\
\midrule
\(q\approx0\) & \(u=q^{1/d}\) if needed & \(q=u^d\) & resolves fractional Newton polygons\\
\(q\approx1\) & \(t=\log q\) or \(-\log q\) & \(q=e^t\) & Bernoulli expansions are regular in \(t\)\\
\(q\approx-1\) & \(t=q+1\) or \(q=-e^{-t}\) & direct or radial & separates algebraic and radial limits\\
\(q\approx\zeta\) & \(t=-\log(q/\zeta)\) & \(q=\zeta e^{-t}\) & fixes a cyclotomic ray and logarithm sheet\\
\(q\to\infty\) & \(r=q^{-1}\) & \(q=r^{-1}\) & turns infinity into an ordinary origin\\
positive \(q\) globally & \(t=\log q\) & \(q=e^t\) & preserves positivity and reciprocity\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
If the first nonzero forward term is \(c u^m\), Newton should be applied in
\(v=u^m\), or the sheet should be initialized by the corresponding Puiseux
root.  Applying ordinary Newton directly in \(u\) at the critical point loses
quadratic convergence because the derivative vanishes.

\section{Logarithmic Newton and Halley corrections}
Suppose a nonzero target \(y\) is to be matched by a product-like function
\(F(p)\).  On a chosen logarithm sheet define
\[
 h(p)=\PrincipalLogarithm F(p)-\PrincipalLogarithm y.
\]
Then logarithmic Newton is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:log-newton}
 p_{r+1}=p_r-\frac{h(p_r)}{h'(p_r)}
 =p_r-\frac{\PrincipalLogarithm(F(p_r)/y)}{F'(p_r)/F(p_r)}.
\end{equation}
It avoids overflow and measures relative rather than absolute error.  For the
finite Pochhammer base inverse,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:finite-qpoch-log-derivative-q}
 \partial_q\log(a;q)_n
 =-a\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{j q^{j-1}}{1-aq^j},
\end{equation}
while for argument inversion,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:finite-qpoch-log-derivative-a}
 \partial_a\log(a;q)_n
 =-\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{q^j}{1-aq^j}.
\end{equation}
These expressions should be evaluated together with the logarithm so that the
same powers and factors are reused.

Where second derivatives are inexpensive, Halley's correction
\begin{equation}\label{eq:halley}
 p_{r+1}=p_r-
 \frac{2h h'}{2(h')^2-hh''}
\end{equation}
can be effective after a branch-safe Newton iterate has entered the local
basin.  It is not a substitute for bracketing or continuation: close to a
critical value, the denominator in \eqref{eq:halley} may itself be small and a
cubic method can jump farther than Newton.

\section{Predictor--corrector continuation of a selected sheet}
Let a branch be defined by
\[
 F(p(s),\lambda(s))=y(s).
\]
As long as \(F_p\ne0\), differentiation gives the continuation equation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:continuation-ode}
 p'(s)=\frac{y'(s)-F_\lambda(p(s),\lambda(s))\lambda'(s)}
 {F_p(p(s),\lambda(s))}.
\end{equation}
For several auxiliary parameters, replace the numerator by
\(y'-\sum_jF_{\lambda_j}\lambda_j'\).  A practical branch tracker uses one
Runge--Kutta or Taylor predictor for \eqref{eq:continuation-ode}, followed by a
Newton corrector at the new value of \(s\).  The step size is reduced when
\(\AbsoluteValue{F_p}\) decreases or the Newton correction becomes a substantial
fraction of the predicted displacement.

A logarithm or argument used in the forward function must be unwrapped
continuously along the path.  Re-evaluating every logarithm on its principal
sheet at every step changes the analytic function being followed and can
silently transport the iterate to a different inverse branch.

\section{Continuation through a fold}
At a simple fold \((p_*,y_*)\), introduce a local square coordinate
\[
 y-y_*=\sigma^2,
 \qquad
 p-p_*=c_1\sigma+c_2\sigma^2+\cdots.
\]
Continuation in \(\sigma\), rather than \(y\), makes the selected sheet
regular.  Alternatively, use pseudo-arclength continuation for the curve
\(F(p)-y=0\): append a linear arclength condition and solve for \((p,y)\)
together.  The latter passes turning points without choosing a square-root
sheet in advance.

At a cubic or higher point, the appropriate coordinate is obtained from the
Newton polygon.  The exact identity
\[
 (-1/3;q)_3=\frac{32}{27}+\frac4{27}(q+1)^3
\]
provides an ideal regression test: a correct branch tracker should recover all
three cube-root sheets and their cyclic monodromy around \(y=32/27\).

\section{Certified real inverses}
For a continuous strictly monotone real branch, interval bisection is the
fallback method.  Asymptotics supply a short bracket and Newton supplies speed,
but monotonicity supplies the proof.

\begin{theorem}[\statusproved\ Residual-to-root enclosure]\label{thm:residual-root}
Let \(f\) be differentiable on an interval \(I\), suppose \(f(p)=y\) has a
solution \(p\in I\), and assume
\[
 \inf_{x\in I}\AbsoluteValue{f'(x)}\ge m>0.
\]
Then every approximation \(p_0\in I\) satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:residual-root-bound}
 \AbsoluteValue{p-p_0}\le\frac{\AbsoluteValue{f(p_0)-y}}m.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The mean-value theorem gives
\(f(p_0)-f(p)=f'(\xi)(p_0-p)\) for some \(\xi\) between \(p\) and \(p_0\).
Taking absolute values proves the claim.
\end{proof}

For logarithmic inversion, apply the theorem to \(\log f\) and obtain a bound
from the relative residual.  Near a fold, \(m\) necessarily tends to zero; the
square coordinate restores a nonvanishing derivative and a meaningful bound.

Interval Newton sharpens bisection.  Given an interval \(X\), midpoint \(x_0\),
and an interval extension \(f'(X)\), set
\[
 N(X)=x_0-\frac{f(x_0)-y}{f'(X)}.
\]
If \(N(X)\subseteq X\), a root is enclosed; if
\(N(X)\subset\operatorname{int}X\) and zero is excluded from \(f'(X)\), the
root is unique in \(X\).  Directed rounding is essential when the result is
presented as a proof rather than as a high-precision observation.

\section{A complex disk certificate}
The following elementary Rouch\'e test certifies one local inverse sheet.

\begin{theorem}[\statusproved\ One-zero disk]\label{thm:one-zero-disk}
Let \(F\) be holomorphic on the closed disk
\(\AbsoluteValue{p-p_0}\le r\).  Suppose
\[
 \sup_{\AbsoluteValue{p-p_0}\le r}\AbsoluteValue{F''(p)}\le M
\]
and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rouche-certificate}
 \AbsoluteValue{F(p_0)-y}+\frac12Mr^2<\AbsoluteValue{F'(p_0)}r.
\end{equation}
Then \(F(p)-y\) has exactly one zero, counted with multiplicity, in the disk.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Taylor's formula with an integral remainder bounds
\[
 \AbsoluteValue{F(p)-F(p_0)-F'(p_0)(p-p_0)}\le\frac12Mr^2
\]
on the boundary.  By \eqref{eq:rouche-certificate}, the difference between
\(F(p)-y\) and \(F'(p_0)(p-p_0)\) is smaller than the latter.  Rouch\'e's
theorem gives the same number of zeros, namely one.
\end{proof}

This test is especially convenient after a truncated inverse series has
provided \(p_0\).  Bounds for \(F''\) follow directly from the product's
logarithmic derivatives or from Cauchy estimates on a slightly larger disk.

\section{Discriminants, resultants, and a zero-fiber factorization}
For finite products, algebraic singularities can be found before numerical
continuation.  The critical-value polynomial is
\[
 \Delta_n(q,y)=
 \ResidueOperator_a\bigl((a;q)_n-y,\partial_a(a;q)_n\bigr).
\]
Its zeros are precisely the finite branch values, with multiplicities.  A
subresultant sequence is usually preferable to expanding the full determinant.

At \(y=0\), the discriminant has a particularly transparent factorization.

\begin{theorem}[\statusproved\ Zero-fiber argument discriminant]
For \(n\ge2\), viewed as a polynomial in \(a\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qpoch-zero-discriminant}
 \Disc_a (a;q)_n
 =q^{n(n-1)(n-2)/3}
  \prod_{d=1}^{n-1}(1-q^d)^{2(n-d)}.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The leading coefficient is
\((-1)^nq^{n(n-1)/2}\), and the roots are \(q^{-j}\), \(0\le j<n\).  Hence
\[
 \Disc_a(a;q)_n=
 q^{n(n-1)^2}
 \prod_{0\le i<j<n}(q^{-i}-q^{-j})^2.
\]
Writing
\(q^{-i}-q^{-j}=q^{-j}(q^{j-i}-1)\), collecting the powers of \(q\), and
noting that the difference \(d=j-i\) occurs \(n-d\) times gives
\eqref{eq:qpoch-zero-discriminant}.
\end{proof}

The theorem simultaneously records every collision of argument zeros at roots
of unity.  It also gives the exact vanishing order of the discriminant, which
is useful for deciding how many precision bits are lost near a cyclotomic
collision.

\section{Certification of discrete parameter recovery}
A continuous interpolation in the order or in the Gaussian parameters is a
branch-finding device; the original problem may still require an integer.
Suppose an interpolated inverse gives \(x_*\) with an error bound \(\eta<1/2\).
There is then at most one candidate integer \(m\) with
\(\AbsoluteValue{m-x_*}\le\eta\).  Evaluate the exact recurrence at that candidate to
certify it.

For finite products,
\[
 \frac{(a;q)_{n+1}}{(a;q)_n}=1-aq^n.
\]
For Gaussian coefficients,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gaussian-ratio-discrete}
 \frac{\GaussianBinomial{n+1}{k}{q}}{\GaussianBinomial{n}{k}{q}}
 =\frac{1-q^{n+1}}{1-q^{n+1-k}},
 \qquad
 \frac{\GaussianBinomial{n}{k+1}{q}}{\GaussianBinomial{n}{k}{q}}
 =\frac{1-q^{n-k}}{1-q^{k+1}}.
\end{equation}
These ratios permit exact or interval comparisons without constructing large
coefficients.  If the target is noisy rather than exact, they also delimit the
Voronoi interval in target space assigned to each integer parameter.

\section{Regular, critical, and nonidentifiable parameters}
Every entry belongs to one of three qualitatively different classes.
\begin{description}
\item[Regular.] The partial derivative in the recovered parameter is nonzero.
The inverse is analytic and its coefficients follow the universal Bell
recurrence.
\item[Critical but identifiable.] The derivative vanishes, but a higher jet or
several observables distinguish branches.  Newton--Puiseux series and
pseudo-arclength continuation are the appropriate tools.
\item[Nonidentifiable.] The forward map is locally constant in the selected
parameter or two parameters enter only through a lower-dimensional
combination.  No numerical method can create information absent from the data;
an additional observable or normalization is required.
\end{description}
The exact identities \(\Gamma_q(1)=\Gamma_q(2)=1\),
\(F_q(1/2)=1/2\) for the symmetric atomic CDF, and
\([1]_q=1\) are canonical nonidentifiable examples.

\section{A worked multivariate recovery example}
Consider the normalized atomic family with an unknown location, scale, and
contraction ratio,
\[
 Y=\mu+cX_q,
 \qquad c>0.
\]
Let \(m\), \(v\), and \(g_2\) be its mean, variance, and excess kurtosis.
Translation and scale invariance give
\[
 \mu=m,
 \qquad
 q=\sqrt{\frac{1+5g_2/6}{1-5g_2/6}},
 \qquad
 c=\sqrt{\frac{v}{V(q)}}.
\]
Thus a seemingly nonlinear three-parameter inverse becomes triangular after
choosing invariants adapted to the symmetry group.  This is a general lesson:
before applying a multivariate Newton method, quotient out translations,
scalings, reciprocal symmetries, and parameter permutations analytically.

\section{A worked cyclotomic recovery example}
Let \(q=\zeta e^{-t}\), where \(\zeta=e^{2\pi\ImaginaryUnit/3}\), and suppose \(a\) is
known with \(\AbsoluteValue a<1\).  For a very small observed product \(y\), define
\[
 A=\frac{\Polylogarithm_2(a^3)}9,
 \qquad
 X=-\PrincipalLogarithm y+C_\zeta(a).
\]
The two-term initializer is
\[
 t_0=\frac AX+\frac{\Dcal_\zeta(a)A^2}{X^3},
 \qquad q_0=\zeta e^{-t_0}.
\]
One logarithmic Newton correction using the grouped residue-class product then
produces a high-accuracy point on the chosen radial sheet.  The same target
\(y\) may have many nonradial preimages; the path \(q/\zeta\in(0,1)\) is part
of the inverse specification.

\chapter{Algebraic and analytic frontiers}

\section{Generic monodromy of finite Pochhammer inverses}
For fixed generic \(q\), the polynomial equation
\[
 (a;q)_n=y
\]
defines an \(n\)-sheeted cover of the target sphere.  The critical points are
solutions of a rational Stieltjes-type equation, but the critical values do not
inherit the simple geometric ordering of the zeros.  The natural global
question is the monodromy group generated by loops around those values.

\begin{conjecture}[\statusconjectural\ Generic full monodromy]
For every \(n\ge3\), outside a proper algebraic exceptional set of bases \(q\),
the monodromy group of \((a;q)_n-y=0\) over the \(y\)-sphere is the full
symmetric group \(S_n\).
\end{conjecture}
A feasible proof program is to show that all generic critical points are
simple, so their local monodromies are transpositions, and then prove that the
critical graph connecting the sheets is connected.  Degeneracies at roots of
unity and secondary critical-value collisions provide the exceptional set.
Computations for moderate \(n\) should use exact specializations and certified
Galois-group algorithms rather than floating-point root permutations alone.

\section{Secondary discriminants and Maxwell loci}
The ordinary discriminant records a repeated root of the inverse equation.  A
secondary discriminant records equality of two distinct critical values.  If
\(c_i(q)\) are the critical points of \(a\mapsto(a;q)_n\), then the Maxwell
condition is
\[
 (c_i(q);q)_n=(c_j(q);q)_n,
 \qquad i\ne j.
\]
Eliminating \(c_i,c_j\) gives an algebraic locus in \(q\).  Such a collision
does not create a higher critical point of the forward map; instead, two branch
points project to the same target value and the topology of natural branch
cuts changes.

\begin{researchproblem}
Factor the secondary discriminant for \((a;q)_n\) uniformly in \(n\), identify
its cyclotomic factors, and determine the number and asymptotic distribution of
its positive roots in \((0,1)\).
\end{researchproblem}
A promising approach is to express the critical equation in the elementary
symmetric functions of the geometric roots and use subresultants twice: first
to construct the critical-value polynomial, then to take its discriminant in
\(y\).

\section{A double-scaling theory at colliding zeros}
The radial root-of-unity expansion assumed fixed \(a\) with \(a^m\ne1\).
Argument inversion at a cyclotomic collision assumes fixed \(q=\zeta\).  The
missing transition regime lets both variables approach the singular set:
\[
 q=\zeta e^{-t},
 \qquad
 a=\zeta^{-r}e^{-\alpha t}.
\]
Factors with index \(j\equiv r\pmod m\) become
\[
 1-e^{-(\alpha+j)t},
\]
and their product is governed by a scaled gamma function rather than by an
ordinary Taylor coefficient.

\begin{conjecture}[\statusconjectural\ Cyclotomic gamma transition]
After extracting an explicit power of \(t\), the double-scaled finite and
infinite Pochhammer products admit uniform expansions whose leading factors
are products of Euler gamma functions with fractional shifts determined by
residue classes modulo \(m\).  Their parameter inverses interpolate uniformly
between the fixed-argument reciprocal-log law and the fixed-base Puiseux law.
\end{conjecture}
The conjecture can be attacked first for finite products by exact residue-class
factorization, then for the infinite product by Euler--Maclaurin summation on
each arithmetic progression.

\section{Resurgence of radial inverse series}
The perturbative expansion at \(q=\zeta e^{-t}\) is accompanied by terms
exponentially small in \(1/t\), arising from singularities of the summand after
Poisson or Abel--Plana transformation.  Formal inversion transports these
terms into exponentially small corrections to \(t(y)\).  The nonlinear
composition mixes instanton sectors but preserves their exponential scales.

\begin{researchproblem}
Construct the Borel transform of the radial logarithm, locate its first
singularities for general \(a\) and \(m\), and derive the induced Stokes jumps
of the inverse base.  Compare them with the finite cyclic quantum-dilogarithm
factors appearing in root-of-unity limits \cite{IpYamazaki,GaroufalidisZagier}.
\end{researchproblem}
A numerically decisive test is hyperasymptotic: subtract the optimally
truncated algebraic series and fit the remainder to the predicted exponential
scale as \(t\downarrow0\).

\section{Global phase diagrams for \texorpdfstring{\(q\)}{q}-gamma base inversion}
The local coefficient at \(q=1\),
\[
 -\frac{(x-1)(x-2)}4,
\]
changes sign at \(x=1,2\), where the entire base dependence disappears.  Near
\(q=0\), the dominant term changes at \(x=1\) because \(q^x\) overtakes or
falls behind \(q\).  These transitions suggest a nontrivial global phase
diagram for the number of real base solutions of
\[
 \Gamma_q(x)=y,
 \qquad q\in(0,1).
\]

\begin{researchproblem}
For each fixed \(x>0\), determine the exact number of critical points of
\(q\mapsto\Gamma_q(x)\) on \((0,1)\), and classify all target values by the
number of real inverse branches.  Prove the expected monotonicity sectors or
locate counterexamples with certified interval arithmetic.
\end{researchproblem}
The logarithmic derivative is a Lambert series, so complete-monotonicity and
variation-diminishing methods are natural.  The asymptotic work of Moak and
McIntosh provides endpoint control \cite{Moak1984,McIntosh1999}; the remaining
issue is global sign variation.

\section{The critical curve of the \texorpdfstring{\(q\)}{q}-beta base inverse}
The first \(q\to1\) coefficient of
\(\log(B_q(x,z)/B(x,z))\) vanishes on \(xz=1\).  On this curve, the second
coefficient determines a square-root or higher inverse depending on whether it
also vanishes.  Solving the simultaneous equations for the first several
coefficients gives a finite algebraic stratification in the \((x,z)\)-plane.

\begin{researchproblem}
Determine the complete degeneracy hierarchy of the \(q\)-beta base inverse at
\(q=1\).  In particular, decide whether any positive \((x,z)\ne(1,1)\) makes
both the first and second nonconstant coefficients vanish, and classify the
resulting Puiseux exponents.
\end{researchproblem}
Because the coefficients are Bernoulli polynomials, this problem is accessible
to exact elimination before numerical exploration.

\chapter{Combinatorial and probabilistic frontiers}

\section{Discrete inverse Gaussian problems}
For fixed positive \(q\), the continuous interpolation
\(\Bcal_q(z,w)\) separates the analytic branch problem from the integer
constraint.  The discrete inverse asks for all integer pairs \((n,k)\) with
\[
 \GaussianBinomial{n}{k}{q}=y.
\]
Symmetry forces \((n,k)\leftrightarrow(n,n-k)\), and special bases can create
additional coincidences.

\begin{conjecture}[\statusconjectural\ Generic discrete uniqueness]
Fix a transcendental real \(q>0\), \(q\ne1\).  Equality
\[
 \GaussianBinomial{n}{k}{q}=\GaussianBinomial{n'}{k'}{q}
\]
for nontrivial indices implies
\((n',k')=(n,k)\) or \((n,n-k)\).
\end{conjecture}
For transcendental \(q\), equality is equality of Gaussian polynomials, so the
problem reduces to whether two rectangular partition-generating polynomials
can coincide.  Degree, first coefficients, cyclotomic factorization, and the
largest hook data provide successive invariants.  This appears substantially
more tractable than equality at one algebraic numerical base.

\section{Inverse universality from coefficient distributions}
A positive polynomial
\[
 P(q)=\sum_{j=0}^{D}c_jq^j
\]
can be normalized at \(q=e^t\) as a moment-generating function for the random
exponent \(J\) with probabilities proportional to \(c_j\).  Its inverse near
\(q=1\) depends on cumulants of \(J\).  If a sequence of \(q\)-analogs has
centered, scaled exponent distributions converging to a Gaussian law, then the
first inverse correction is universal and higher corrections are organized by
Edgeworth cumulants.

\begin{researchproblem}
Prove uniform inverse limit theorems for Gaussian and multinomial coefficients
when all rectangle or composition dimensions grow.  Determine the largest
target window in which a finite cumulant reversion remains uniform, and match
it to large-deviation inversion outside that window.
\end{researchproblem}
The central window uses analytic cumulant bounds; the tails should be expressed
through a Legendre transform and then inverted by saddle-point methods.

\section{Statistical recovery of the Fabius--Rvachev contraction}
Variance and kurtosis recover \(q\) exactly for the ideal geometric-uniform
model, but numerical or empirical data introduce truncation, discretization,
noise, and possible misspecification of the digit law.  A robust inverse theory
should recover \(q\) jointly with nuisance parameters and quantify model error.

\begin{researchproblem}
Derive the semiparametric information bound for estimating \(q\) when location
and scale are unknown and the symmetric digit distribution belongs to a
specified moment class.  Compare moment, characteristic-function, and CDF
estimators, especially near \(q=1\), where variance-based recovery is poorly
conditioned.
\end{researchproblem}
The characteristic function offers a natural continuum of observables.  Its
score from \eqref{eq:atomic-score-fourier} can be orthogonalized against
location and scale scores, giving an efficient weighting rule in Fourier
space.

\section{Global geometry of CDF inverse branches}
The refinement equation couples \(F_q(x)\) at geometrically rescaled arguments.
Differentiating in \(q\) produces a linear inhomogeneous refinement equation for
the tangent CDF.  Critical points of \(q\mapsto F_q(x)\) are zeros of this
tangent solution.

\begin{conjecture}[\statusconjectural\ Tame tangent zero set]
For algebraic \(x\in(0,1)\setminus\{1/2\}\), the tangent function
\(q\mapsto\partial_qF_q(x)\) has finitely many zeros on every compact
subinterval of \((0,1)\), and no interior accumulation point.
\end{conjecture}
One route is to establish real analyticity in \(q\) away from a controlled set
of refinement resonances.  Another is to approximate the refinement operator
by finite transfer matrices with certified spectral error and study zeros of
the resulting analytic determinants.

\chapter{Higher-codimension inversion and formalization}

\section{Cusp and caustic classification}
A one-parameter inverse typically meets folds.  Two or more tunable auxiliary
parameters make cusps unavoidable.  For a scalar forward function
\(F(p;\lambda_1,\lambda_2)\), a cusp occurs when
\[
 F_p=F_{pp}=0,
 \qquad F_{ppp}\ne0,
\]
with a transverse rank condition involving derivatives in the \(\lambda\)
directions.  The local normal form is
\[
 y-y_*=u^3+\alpha u+\beta.
\]
Its discriminant \(4\alpha^3+27\beta^2=0\) is the caustic separating regions
with one and three real inverse branches.

The exceptional \((a;q)_3\) slice at \((-1/3,-1)\) is not itself a cusp in the
full two-variable map; its Hessian is nondegenerate.  It illustrates a
different mechanism: a chosen parameter line can be tangent to a null cone of
a Morse quadratic form and reveal a higher-order restricted singularity.
This distinction should be enforced in any automated classifier.

\begin{researchproblem}
Compute the Thom--Boardman strata of the maps obtained by selecting two or
three observables from finite Pochhammer or Gaussian families.  Determine
which stable singularities actually occur and express their loci in
cyclotomic or partition-theoretic data.
\end{researchproblem}

\section{Multivariate Lagrange--Good coefficients as combinatorial objects}
Good's determinant formula converts simultaneous recovery into coefficient
extraction \cite{Good1960}.  For \(q\)-analogs, the Jacobian determinant itself
is assembled from Lambert sums and Gaussian polynomials.  Its coefficients
therefore have a second combinatorial interpretation superimposed on the
usual rooted-tree interpretation of multivariate reversion.

\begin{conjecture}[\statusconjectural\ Positivity after natural normalization]
For simultaneous inverses formed from nested positive \(q\)-products, there is
a canonical sign and monomial normalization after which the multivariate
inverse coefficients are nonnegative integers counting decorated forests.
\end{conjecture}
The conjecture should first be tested on the exact two-observable recovery
\(((a;q)_n,(a;q)_{n+1})\), then on adjacent Gaussian coefficients.  A
bijective proof would turn condition and identifiability determinants into
enumerative objects.

\section{A Lean formalization roadmap}
The finite theory is particularly suitable for formal proof.  A staged
formalization can separate algebra, formal power series, and analysis.

\paragraph{Stage 1: finite algebra.}
Define \((a;q)_n\) as a finite product and prove the argument-zero formula,
recurrences, reciprocal transformations, and the discriminant factorization
\eqref{eq:qpoch-zero-discriminant}.  Root-of-unity collision multiplicities
reduce to counting residues in \(\operatorname{Fin} n\).

\paragraph{Stage 2: formal reversion.}
Use the existing formal-power-series inverse theorem to prove the Bell
recurrence and instantiate it for finite products and Gaussian polynomials.
For folds, adjoin a formal square root or work in Hahn/Puiseux series.  The
exact \(n=3\) cubic can be proved by normalization and \texttt{ring}.

\paragraph{Stage 3: real branch theorems.}
Formalize coefficient positivity, strict monotonicity on \(q>0\), existence by
the intermediate-value theorem, and uniqueness.  This yields a fully certified
positive Gaussian base inverse without complex analysis.

\paragraph{Stage 4: analytic products.}
Define the infinite product through summable logarithms, prove the tail bound
\eqref{eq:qpoch-tail-bound}, and obtain local inverses from the analytic
implicit-function theorem.  Endpoint asymptotics require a reusable
Euler--Maclaurin library for functions with parameter-dependent singularities.

\paragraph{Stage 5: certified computation.}
Connect theorems to executable interval arithmetic.  A certificate should
contain a rational or dyadic box, a derivative enclosure, a residual bound,
and a branch label; a small kernel theorem then validates the claimed inverse
without trusting the search procedure.

\section{Prioritized next results}
The following sequence balances conceptual value against proof difficulty.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Prove and formalize the zero-fiber discriminant
\eqref{eq:qpoch-zero-discriminant} and all finite root-collision
multiplicities.
\item Compute secondary discriminants for \(n\le8\), factor them, and formulate
a stable pattern for cyclotomic and noncyclotomic factors.
\item Prove a uniform double-scaling expansion
\(q=\zeta e^{-t}\), \(a=\zeta^{-r}e^{-\alpha t}\) for finite products, then
pass to the infinite product.
\item Establish the complete real branch-count phase diagram for
\(q\mapsto\Gamma_q(x)\) on a substantial interval of \(x\), using interval
certification where symbolic sign arguments are unavailable.
\item Derive and solve the differentiated refinement equation for the
Fabius--Rvachev tangent CDF, and map its first critical curves in \((q,x)\).
\item Build a common branch-certificate format and use it for all numerical
inverse claims in the ProveIt \(q\)-series drafts.
\end{enumerate}
Each step produces reusable infrastructure for the next; none depends on an
unverified global conjecture.

\chapter{Coefficient machinery}

\section{Recursive construction of a scalar inverse jet}
Let
\[
 u=f(x)=\sum_{r\ge1}a_rx^r,
 \qquad a_1\ne0,
 \qquad
 x=g(u)=\sum_{r\ge1}b_ru^r.
\]
Write \([u^n]H(u)\) for coefficient extraction.  Comparing coefficients in
\(f(g(u))=u\) gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:inverse-coefficient-recursion}
 b_1=\frac1{a_1},
 \qquad
 b_n=-\frac1{a_1}
 \sum_{k=2}^{n}a_k[u^n]g(u)^k
 \quad(n\ge2),
\end{equation}
where only \(b_1,\ldots,b_{n-1}\) occur on the right.  Equivalently,
\[
 [u^n]g(u)^k=\frac{k!}{n!}
 B_{n,k}(1!b_1,2!b_2,\ldots),
\]
with \(B_{n,k}\) the partial exponential Bell polynomial.

In derivative notation \(f_r=f^{(r)}(0)\), the first five coefficients are
\begin{align*}
 b_1&=\frac1{f_1},\\
 b_2&=-\frac{f_2}{2f_1^3},\\
 b_3&=\frac{3f_2^2-f_1f_3}{6f_1^5},\\
 b_4&=\frac{-15f_2^3+10f_1f_2f_3-f_1^2f_4}{24f_1^7},\\
 b_5&=\frac{105f_2^4-105f_1f_2^2f_3+10f_1^2f_3^2
 +15f_1^2f_2f_4-f_1^3f_5}{120f_1^9}.
\end{align*}
These universal rational polynomials are used repeatedly throughout the
report.  Computing forward derivatives logarithmically and applying
\eqref{eq:inverse-coefficient-recursion} is generally simpler than expanding
the original product before every inversion.

\section{Logarithmic forward derivatives}
If \(F=e^L\), then
\[
 F^{(n)}=F\,B_n(L',L'',\ldots,L^{(n)}),
\]
where \(B_n\) is the complete Bell polynomial.  For a finite Pochhammer
product, differentiation with respect to the argument gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qpoch-log-a-derivatives}
 \partial_a^r\log(a;q)_n
 =-(r-1)!\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}
 \frac{q^{jr}}{(1-aq^j)^r},
 \qquad r\ge1.
\end{equation}
At \(a=0\), the sum is \([n]_{q^r}\), producing all argument inverse
coefficients from power sums of the geometric comb.

With \(q=e^t\),
\[
 \log(a;e^t)_n=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\log(1-ae^{jt}),
\]
and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qpoch-log-t-derivatives}
 \partial_t^r\log(a;e^t)_n\big|_{t=0}
 =-\Polylogarithm_{1-r}(a)\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}j^r,
 \qquad r\ge1.
\end{equation}
Since \(\Polylogarithm_{1-r}(a)\) is rational in \(a\) for integer \(r\ge1\), the full
\(q=1\) inverse jet is generated by Bernoulli power sums and rational
functions.

\section{Fold coefficients through the cubic correction}
Let
\[
 y-y_*=A u^2+B u^3+C u^4+O(u^5),
 \qquad A\ne0,
\]
and set \(s=(y-y_*)^{1/2}\).  The two sheets have
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fold-coefficients-appendix}
 u=\sigma\frac{s}{\sqrt A}
 -\frac{B}{2A^2}s^2
 +\sigma\frac{5B^2-4AC}{8A^{7/2}}s^3
 +O(s^4),
 \qquad \sigma\in\{+1,-1\}.
\end{equation}
In derivative notation
\(A=F''(p_*)/2\), \(B=F'''(p_*)/6\), and
\(C=F^{(4)}(p_*)/24\).  Formula
\eqref{eq:fold-coefficients-appendix} is convenient for initializing both real
branches on the side where \((y-y_*)/A>0\).

\section{Asymptotic reversion with a reciprocal logarithm}
Suppose
\[
 X=\frac At+B+Ct+Dt^2+O(t^3),
 \qquad A\ne0,
\]
and absorb \(B\) into \(\widehat X=X-B\).  Seeking
\[
 t=\frac A{\widehat X}+\frac{\alpha}{\widehat X^3}
 +\frac{\beta}{\widehat X^4}+O(\widehat X^{-5})
\]
gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reciprocal-log-reversion}
 \alpha=CA^2,
 \qquad
 \beta=DA^3.
\end{equation}
There is no \(\widehat X^{-2}\) term after the constant has been centered.  If
the forward expansion also contains \(t\log t\), the inverse acquires
\((\log X)/X^3\) terms; ordinary Laurent reversion is then insufficient.

\chapter{Derivations at special bases}

\section{The finite Pochhammer critical point at \texorpdfstring{\(q=-1\)}{q=-1}}
Put
\[
 L_n(a,q)=\log(a;q)_n.
\]
At \(q=-1\), separate even and odd indices.  The first base derivative is
\[
 \partial_qL_n(a,-1)
 =-a\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{j(-1)^{j-1}}
 {1-a(-1)^j}.
\]
For \(n=2m\), the even indices contribute
\(m(m-1)/(1-a)\), while the odd indices contribute
\(m^2/(1+a)\), with the signs inherited from \((-1)^{j-1}\).  Solving the
resulting linear numerator gives
\[
 a_*(2m)=\frac1{2m-1}.
\]
For \(n=2m+1\), the analogous sums give
\[
 a_*(2m+1)=-\frac1{2m+1}.
\]
Substitution in the second logarithmic derivative yields
\begin{align}
 \partial_q^2L_{2m}(a_*,-1)
 &=-\frac{(m+1)(2m-1)^2}{12m(m-1)},\label{eq:even-qminusone-L2}\\
 \partial_q^2L_{2m+1}(a_*,-1)
 &=-\frac{(m-1)(2m+1)^2}{12m(m+1)}.\label{eq:odd-qminusone-L2}
\end{align}
The even formula is nonzero for \(m\ge2\); the odd formula is nonzero for
\(m\ge2\).  For \(m=1\), namely \(n=3\), it vanishes and direct multiplication
gives the exact cubic identity.

\section{The Morse determinant at the exceptional cubic}
For odd \(n=2m+1\), evaluate the Hessian of
\(L_n(a,q)\) at \((a,q)=(-1/(2m+1),-1)\).  Straightforward finite-sum
simplification gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:odd-morse-determinant}
 \det\operatorname{Hess}_{a,q}L_n
 =-\frac{(2m+1)^4(m^2+m+1)}
 {48m^2(m+1)^2}<0.
\end{equation}
Because multiplication by the nonzero critical value preserves
nondegeneracy at a critical point, the Hessian of \((a;q)_n\) is also
indefinite.  In particular, at \(n=3\) the full singularity is Morse even though
the fixed-\(a\) restriction begins cubically.

\section{Radial root-of-unity decomposition}
Let \(q=\zeta e^{-t}\), with \(\zeta\) primitive of order \(m\), and
\(\AbsoluteValue a<1\).  Starting from
\[
 \log(a;q)_\infty=-\sum_{r\ge1}\frac{a^r}{r(1-q^r)},
\]
split the sum according to \(m\mid r\).  If \(r=m\ell\), then
\[
 \frac1{1-e^{-m\ell t}}
 =\frac1{m\ell t}+\frac12+\frac{m\ell t}{12}+O(t^3).
\]
The divisible terms therefore contribute
\[
 -\frac{\Polylogarithm_2(a^m)}{m^2t}
 +\frac1{2m}\log(1-a^m)
 -\frac{a^m}{12(1-a^m)}t+O(t^3).
\]
For \(m\nmid r\), Taylor expansion at \(t=0\) gives
\[
 \frac1{1-\zeta^re^{-rt}}
 =\frac1{1-\zeta^r}
 -\frac{r\zeta^r}{(1-\zeta^r)^2}t+O(t^2).
\]
Combining both classes yields
\begin{align}
 C_\zeta(a)
 &=\frac1{2m}\log(1-a^m)
 -\sum_{\substack{r\ge1\\m\nmid r}}
  \frac{a^r}{r(1-\zeta^r)},\label{eq:Czeta-appendix}\\
 \Dcal_\zeta(a)
 &=\frac{a^m}{12(1-a^m)}
 -\sum_{\substack{r\ge1\\m\nmid r}}
  \frac{a^r\zeta^r}{(1-\zeta^r)^2}.
 \label{eq:Dzeta-appendix}
\end{align}
The sums converge absolutely for \(\AbsoluteValue a<1\).  The inverse follows from
\eqref{eq:reciprocal-log-reversion}.

\section{Gaussian cumulants at \texorpdfstring{\(q=1\)}{q=1}}
For \(q=e^t\), write
\[
 \log\frac{\GaussianBinomial{n}{k}{q}}{\binom nk}
 =\sum_{j=1}^{k}
 \left[
  \log\frac{e^{(n-k+j)t}-1}{(n-k+j)t}
  -\log\frac{e^{jt}-1}{jt}
 \right].
\]
Using
\[
 \log\frac{e^x-1}{x}
 =\frac x2+
 \sum_{r\ge1}\frac{B_{2r}}{2r(2r)!}x^{2r}
\]
gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gaussian-cumulant-full}
 \log\frac{\GaussianBinomial{n}{k}{q}}{\binom nk}
 =\frac{k(n-k)}2t+
 \sum_{r\ge1}\frac{B_{2r}P_{2r}(n,k)}{2r(2r)!}t^{2r}.
\end{equation}
Thus every odd cumulant beyond the first vanishes.  This is the infinitesimal
form of palindromicity.  Applying scalar reversion to
\eqref{eq:gaussian-cumulant-full} produces the complete inverse jet without
expanding the Gaussian polynomial itself.

\section{The \texorpdfstring{\(q\)}{q}-gamma expansion near one}
With \(q=e^{-t}\), the q-gamma definition
\[
 \Gamma_q(x)=(1-q)^{1-x}
 \frac{(q;q)_\infty}{(q^x;q)_\infty}
\]
combines two nonuniform Pochhammer expansions.  Cancellation of the
\(\pi^2/(6t)\), \(\log t\), and constant terms gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qgamma-one-appendix}
 \log\frac{\Gamma_{e^{-t}}(x)}{\Gamma(x)}
 =-\frac{(x-1)(x-2)}4t
 +\frac{(x-1)(x-2)(2x+3)}{144}t^2+O(t^3).
\end{equation}
Differentiating in \(x\) gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qdigamma-one-appendix}
 \psi_{e^{-t}}(x)-\psi(x)
 =-\frac{2x-3}{4}t
 +\frac{6x^2-6x-5}{144}t^2+O(t^3).
\end{equation}
At \(x=3/2\), the linear term in the q-digamma expansion vanishes; inversion
of its base therefore begins with a square root of the target displacement.

\chapter{Implementation notes}

\section{Branch labels in data structures}
A numerical return value should carry more than a complex number.  A minimal
inverse-branch record contains
\begin{enumerate}
\item the recovered parameter and an error estimate or enclosure;
\item the parameter coordinate used, such as \(t=\log q\);
\item a base point and continuation path identifier;
\item winding numbers for every logarithm that enters the forward function;
\item the nearest critical value and an estimate of \(1/\AbsoluteValue{F_p}\);
\item a residual in both the original and logarithmic equations;
\item a status flag: asymptotic initializer, Newton-converged, interval
certified, or formally proved.
\end{enumerate}
Without these fields, two numerically equal approximations reached on different
sheets are indistinguishable to downstream code.

\section{Precision management}
Near a simple critical point, the inverse condition behaves like
\(\AbsoluteValue{y-y_*}^{-1/2}\); near an order-\(m\) point, like
\(\AbsoluteValue{y-y_*}^{-(m-1)/m}\).  The working precision should therefore be raised
before the target enters the critical neighborhood.  For a product evaluated
as a logarithmic sum, add guard digits for cancellation among summands and for
exponentiating the final logarithm.

A practical adaptive rule is
\[
 P_{\rm work}=P_{\rm goal}
 +\Ceiling{-\log_{10}\AbsoluteValue{F_p}}
 +P_{\rm cancel}+P_{\rm safety},
\]
where \(P_{\rm cancel}\) is estimated from the ratio between the sum of
absolute term magnitudes and the final logarithmic derivative.  At a known
Puiseux point, replace \(F_p\) by the derivative in the regularized root
coordinate.

\section{Regression tests}
A serious implementation should include at least the following tests.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Symbolic composition of every generated inverse jet through its claimed
order.
\item Reciprocal round trips \(q\mapsto1/q\) for palindromic polynomials.
\item Exact special values at \(q=0,1,-1\), including all parity cases.
\item Monodromy tests around one simple fold and the exact cubic.
\item Tail-bound tests for infinite products against a doubled truncation.
\item Agreement between direct, logarithmic, and recurrence evaluators in
overlapping safe regions.
\item Independent recovery of the atomic-family parameter from variance and
kurtosis.
\item Interval certificates for a representative solution on every real
branch class.
\end{enumerate}
Tests should compare residual orders, not merely decimal values at one point.

\chapter*{Conclusion}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Conclusion}
Inverse \(q\)-analysis is best understood as the geometry of partial inverse
branches rather than as a search for one universal closed form.  The same
small collection of mechanisms recurs throughout the subject: Bell-polynomial
reversion at regular points; Newton--Puiseux series at folds and collisions;
reciprocal symmetry at infinity; Bernoulli and polylogarithmic asymptotics at
\(q=1\); cyclotomic residue classes at roots of unity; and invariant
coordinates for simultaneous parameter recovery.

The finite Pochhammer family makes this structure unusually explicit.  Its
argument zeros lie on a geometric comb, its zero-fiber discriminant factors
completely, and its \(q=-1\) critical slices include both universal folds and an
exact cubic restriction of a Morse saddle.  The infinite product replaces
finite algebraic coverings by logarithmic and transseries geometry, but radial
root-of-unity inversion still has a universal reciprocal-log normal form.
Gaussian coefficients add positivity, palindromicity, partition cumulants, and
strong global real uniqueness.  The other q-special functions inherit their
inverse behavior from these products, with new degeneracy curves when leading
endpoint coefficients vanish.

The Fabius--Rvachev connection is not cosmetic.  The same geometric comb that
defines q-Pochhammer products supplies the weights of a random series whose
\(q=1/2\) member is the Rvachev distribution.  Its cumulants provide exact
inverse observables, while its CDF poses a genuinely new branch-geometry
problem governed by a differentiated refinement operator.  This creates a
concrete bridge from q-series inversion to atomic functions, parameter
identification, and geometric-comb interpolation.

The most promising next advances are uniform double scaling at roots of unity,
secondary discriminants and monodromy, global real phase diagrams for
q-gamma and q-beta base inversion, and certified tangent-CDF computation.
These problems admit a productive division of labor among symbolic algebra,
complex asymptotics, interval numerics, and formal proof.
