\section*{Asymptotic decay rate of an infinite product of sinc functions}
Asked 8 years, 4 months ago Modified 7 years, 7 months ago Viewed 1k times

\begin{itemize}
  \item[-] (related to my previous question)\\
20 Consider the function
  \item[✓] 
\begin{equation*}
f(x)=\prod_{n=0}^{\infty} \operatorname{sinc}\left(\frac{\pi x}{2^{n}}\right), \quad x \geq 0, \tag{1}
\end{equation*}

  \item[ロ] where $\operatorname{sinc}(z)$ denotes the sinc function. The function $f(x)$ has zeros at positive integers, and oscillates with a quickly decaying amplitude. Its signs on the intervals between consecutive zeros follow the same pattern as the Thue-Morse sequence.\\
\includegraphics[max width=\textwidth, alt={}, center]{c02ddc48-cf27-411a-9a03-1741e9e77c8a-1_1001_1637_1250_286}
\end{itemize}

Can we find a real-valued function $g(x)$ (elementary, if possible), such that it is analytic, positive and monotone decreasing (and having monotone derivatives of any order, if possible) and satisfies


\begin{equation*}
\lim _{t \rightarrow \infty} \frac{1}{t-t_{0}} \int_{t_{0}}^{t} \frac{|f(x)|}{g(x)} d x=1 \tag{2}
\end{equation*}


for large enough $t_{0}$ ? Or, at least,


\begin{equation*}
\exists A>0, \exists B>A, \forall t>t_{0}, A<\frac{1}{t-t_{0}} \int_{t_{0}}^{t} \frac{|f(x)|}{g(x)} d x<B \tag{3}
\end{equation*}


for large enough $t_{0}$ ?\\
The "for large enough" provision means that $g(x)$ is permitted to be not monotone, or have zeros or discontinuities, or be not defined at all for small $t$, and we only care about its "eventual" behavior.

Empirically, it looks like $g(x)$ should decay faster than any negative power of $x$, but slower than exponentially. I'm thinking something close to $\exp \left(-\log ^{2} x\right)$, but perhaps not exactly that.

Update: Maybe this expansion can be useful:


\begin{equation*}
\prod_{n=0}^{m} \operatorname{sinc}\left(\frac{\pi x}{2^{n}}\right)=\frac{2^{\binom{m}{2}}}{(\pi x)^{m+1}} \sum_{n=0}^{2^{m}-1} t_{n} \sin \left(\frac{\pi m}{2}+\frac{2 n+1}{2^{m}} \pi x\right), \tag{4}
\end{equation*}


where $t_{0}=1, t_{n}=(-1)^{n} t_{\lfloor n / 2\rfloor}$ (the signed Thue-Morse sequence).

Formulas for $\boldsymbol{\operatorname { s i n c }}(\boldsymbol{\pi} \mathbf{z})$

Using symmetry formulas for the gamma function


\begin{equation*}
\Gamma(z) \Gamma(1-z)=\frac{\pi}{\sin (\pi z)} \tag{2}
\end{equation*}


easy to obtain


\begin{gather*}
\frac{\sin (\pi z)}{\pi z}=\frac{1}{z \Gamma(z) \Gamma(1-z)}, \\
\operatorname{sinc}(\pi \mathrm{z})=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1+z) \Gamma(1-z)},  \tag{3}\\
\ln \operatorname{sinc}(\pi z)=-(\ln \Gamma(1+z)+\ln \Gamma(1-z)) . \tag{4}
\end{gather*}


Using the Maclaurin series in the form of


\begin{equation*}
\ln \Gamma(1+z)=-\ln (1+z)+(1-\gamma) z+\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}(-1)^{j} \frac{\zeta(j)-1}{j} z^{j}, \quad|z|<2 \tag{5}
\end{equation*}


where $\gamma=0.57721 \ldots$, one can get

$$
\begin{gathered}
\ln \Gamma(1-z)=-\ln (1-z)-(1-\gamma) z+\sum_{j=2}^{\infty} \frac{\zeta(j)-1}{j} z^{n} \\
\ln \operatorname{sinc}(\pi z)=\ln \left(1-z^{2}\right)-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{\zeta(2 k)-1}{k} z^{2 k}
\end{gathered}
$$

Zeta function of the even argument is


\begin{equation*}
\zeta(2 k)=\frac{(2 \pi)^{2 k}}{2(2 k)!}\left|B_{2 k}\right|, \tag{6}
\end{equation*}


where $B_{2 k}$ are the Bernoulli numbers. Then


\begin{equation*}
\ln \operatorname{sinc}(\pi z)=\ln \left(1-z^{2}\right)-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} c_{k} z^{2 k}, \tag{7}
\end{equation*}


where


\begin{equation*}
c_{k}=\frac{1}{2 k}\left(\frac{(2 \pi)^{2 k}}{(2 k)!}\left|B_{2 k}\right|-2\right) . \tag{8}
\end{equation*}


Formulas for $\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{x})$

Let us find the sum

$$
\begin{gathered}
\ln R_{m}(x)=\sum_{n=m}^{\infty} \ln \operatorname{sinc} \frac{\pi x}{2^{n}} \\
\ln R_{m}(x)=\sum_{n=m}^{\infty}\left(\ln \left(1-\left(\frac{x}{2^{n}}\right)^{2}\right)-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} c_{k}\left(\frac{x}{2^{n}}\right)^{2 k}\right)= \\
\sum_{n=m}^{\infty} \ln \left(1-\left(\frac{x}{2^{n}}\right)^{2}\right)-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(c_{k} x^{2 k} \sum_{n=m}^{\infty} 2^{-2 n k}\right)= \\
\sum_{n=m}^{\infty} \ln \left(1-\left(\frac{x}{2^{n}}\right)^{2}\right)-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{c_{k}}{1-2^{-2 k}}\left(\frac{x}{2^{m}}\right)^{2 k} .
\end{gathered}
$$

The first term can be presented over the q-Pochhammer Symbol in the form of


\begin{equation*}
\sum_{n=0}^{m} \ln \left(1-\left(\frac{x}{2^{n}}\right)^{2}\right)=\ln \left(x^{2}, \frac{1}{4}\right)_{m} . \tag{9}
\end{equation*}


This allows to obtain the formulas


\begin{equation*}
R(x)=\prod_{n=0}^{m-1} \operatorname{sinc} \frac{\pi x}{2^{n}} \cdot \frac{\left(x^{2}, \frac{1}{4}\right)_{\infty}}{\left(x^{2}, \frac{1}{4}\right)_{m}} \cdot \exp \left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{c_{k}}{1-2^{-m k}}\left(\frac{x}{2^{m}}\right)^{2 k}\right), \quad|x|<2^{m+1} . \tag{(10}
\end{equation*}


✓ C

Radius of convergence in the formula (10) shows that it gives $R(x)=0$ outside this radius, such as the Stirling asymptotics. Easy to see that parameter $m$ allows to manage this radius. At the same time, it allows to provide the reqired length of sum.

If the bounds of the required interval are predefined, then paraneter $m$ can be considered as constant. This allows to use the common function for this interval.

For example, for the predefined interval $x=(10,100)$ the value $m=6$ can be chosen. Then formula (10) presents the function $|R(x)|$ as the production of the three factors:\\
