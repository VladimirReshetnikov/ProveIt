\begin{abstract}
The Lambert $W$ function is the inverse, in a multivalued sense, of the map
$w\mapsto w\e^w$.  It is the natural function for equations in which an
unknown occurs both outside and inside an exponential.  This article develops
the subject from first principles.  Particular attention is paid to the two
real branches: the principal branch $W_0$ and the lower branch $W_{-1}$.  We
prove their existence and geometry; derive differential, integral, and
algebraic identities; obtain Taylor and power series by Lagrange inversion;
develop the common square-root expansion at $-1/\e$; derive a unified
logarithmic asymptotic expansion for $W_0(x)$ as $x\to+\infty$ and for
$W_{-1}(x)$ as $x\to0^-$; prove elementary and sharp branch-specific bounds;
and explain integral, continued-fraction, and Schröder-type representations.

The numerical part derives Newton, Halley, logarithmic Newton, and safeguarded
iterations, including their error equations and conditioning.  Reproducible
high-precision experiments compare local series, asymptotic truncations, and
global approximations.  Applications include exponential-linear equations,
$x^x=a$, infinite power towers, delay differential equations, diode circuits,
Michaelis--Menten kinetics, Wien-type equations, rooted trees, and asymptotic
inversion in prime-number estimates.  A final section introduces the complex
branches $W_k$.  The exposition assumes only single-variable calculus, basic
power series, and elementary complex numbers.  The branch-specific assertions and
formulas developed here are proved in the text.
\end{abstract}

\section{The inverse problem behind Lambert \texorpdfstring{$W$}{W}}
\label{sec:real-branches}

\subsection{Why a new function is needed}

The equations
\[
  3x+7=19,\qquad x^2=5,\qquad \e^x=11
\]
are solved by subtraction and division, a square root, and a logarithm,
respectively.  But consider
\begin{equation}
  x\e^x=11.                                           \label{eq:basic-inverse}
\end{equation}
Taking a logarithm gives $\log x+x=\log 11$, which still contains $x$ in two
different ways.  No finite sequence of the usual elementary inverse operations
isolates it.  The appropriate inverse function is called Lambert $W$.

\begin{definition}[Lambert $W$]
A value $w$ of the Lambert $W$ function at $z$ is any solution of
\begin{equation}
  w\e^w=z.                                             \label{eq:defining}
\end{equation}
The complex solutions are organized into branches $W_k(z)$, indexed by
integers $k$.  On the real axis, the two branches that can be real are denoted
$W_0$ and $W_{-1}$.
\end{definition}

Thus \eqref{eq:basic-inverse} has the solution $x=W_0(11)$.  The subscript is
not decorative: on part of the negative real axis there are two answers, and
the branch must be named.

\subsection{The complete real geometry}

Set
\[
  f(w)=w\e^w,\qquad w\in\mathbb R.
\]
The next theorem contains the entire reason for the two real branches.

\begin{theorem}[Real branches]
\label{thm:real-branches}
The map $f(w)=w\e^w$ has the following properties.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
\item It is strictly decreasing on $(-\infty,-1]$ and maps that interval onto
      $[-1/\e,0)$.
\item It is strictly increasing on $[-1,\infty)$ and maps that interval onto
      $[-1/\e,\infty)$.
\end{enumerate}
Consequently:
\begin{itemize}
\item $W_0:[-1/\e,\infty)\to[-1,\infty)$ is the inverse of $f$ restricted to
      $[-1,\infty)$ and is strictly increasing;
\item $W_{-1}:[-1/\e,0)\to(-\infty,-1]$ is the inverse of $f$ restricted to
      $(-\infty,-1]$ and is strictly decreasing;
\item for $-1/\e<x<0$, the equation $w\e^w=x$ has exactly the two real
      solutions $W_0(x)$ and $W_{-1}(x)$;
\item at $x=-1/\e$ the two branches meet at the value $-1$.
\end{itemize}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Differentiate:
\[
  f'(w)=\e^w(1+w).
\]
Because $\e^w>0$, the derivative is negative for $w<-1$, zero at $w=-1$,
and positive for $w>-1$.  Hence $f$ decreases up to $-1$ and increases after
$-1$.  Its value at the turning point is
\[
  f(-1)=-\frac1\e.
\]
Moreover,
\[
  \lim_{w\to-\infty}w\e^w=0^-,\qquad
  \lim_{w\to+\infty}w\e^w=+\infty.
\]
(The first limit follows, for example, by writing $w=-t$ and using
$t/\e^t\to0$.)  The asserted ranges now follow from continuity and strict
monotonicity.  A continuous strictly monotone function has a continuous
strictly monotone inverse on its range, which gives both inverse branches and
the solution count.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Special values and inverse identities}

The definition immediately gives useful exact values:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  W_0(0)&=0, & W_0(\e)&=1, & W_0(2\e^2)&=2,\\
  W_0(-1/\e)&=-1, & W_{-1}(-1/\e)&=-1,
  &W_{-1}(-2/\e^2)&=-2.
\end{aligned}                                           \label{eq:special-values}
\end{equation}
More generally, whenever $a$ lies in the range of a specified real branch,
\begin{equation}
  W_j(a\e^a)=a.                                         \label{eq:inverse-on-range}
\end{equation}
The phrase ``lies in the range'' is essential.  If $a=-2$, then
$a\e^a=-2/\e^2$ belongs to both real domains, but
\[
  W_{-1}(-2/\e^2)=-2,
  \qquad
  W_0(-2/\e^2)\in(-1,0).
\]
The equation determines a value; the branch determines which value.

A second identity is obtained by dividing \eqref{eq:defining} by $W$:
\begin{equation}
  \e^{W_j(z)}=\frac{z}{W_j(z)},                         \label{eq:exp-W}
\end{equation}
provided $z\ne0$.  At $z=0$ on the principal branch, both sides have the
continuous limiting value $1$ if the quotient is interpreted by a limit.
Taking a real logarithm on either real branch gives
\begin{equation}
  W_j(x)+\log\abs{W_j(x)}=\log\abs{x},                 \label{eq:log-equation}
\end{equation}
for $x\ne0$.  This logarithmic form is central to asymptotics and numerical
evaluation.

\begin{proposition}[Limits at the endpoints]
\label{prop:endpoint-limits}
One has
\[
\lim_{x\to\infty}W_0(x)=\infty,
\qquad
\lim_{x\to0^-}W_{-1}(x)=-\infty,
\qquad
\lim_{x\to0}W_0(x)=0.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
These are immediate from the ranges and monotonicity in
\cref{thm:real-branches}.  For example, if $W_{-1}(x)$ remained bounded below
as $x\to0^-$, a convergent subsequence would tend to a finite $w\le-1$ with
$w\e^w=0$, which is impossible.
\end{proof}

\section{Algebraic transformations and branch selection}
\label{sec:algebra}

Lambert $W$ is useful not because every transcendental equation can be forced
into it, but because a large and recognizable family can.  The target pattern
is
\[
  U\e^U=\hbox{known quantity}.
\]
The safest method is to introduce a new variable $U$, transform exactly, and
only then apply a branch.

\subsection{A master linear--exponential equation}

\begin{theorem}[Linear factor times exponential]
\label{thm:linear-exponential}
Let $a,c\ne0$.  Every solution of
\begin{equation}
  (ax+b)\e^{cx+d}=r                                  \label{eq:linear-exp}
\end{equation}
that belongs to a branch $W_k$ is
\begin{equation}
  x=\frac1c W_k\!\left(\frac{cr}{a}
       \e^{-d+cb/a}\right)-\frac ba.                  \label{eq:linear-exp-solution}
\end{equation}
Conversely, every value in \eqref{eq:linear-exp-solution} for which the branch
is defined solves \eqref{eq:linear-exp}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Put $y=x+b/a$, so that $ax+b=ay$ and
$cx+d=cy-cb/a+d$.  Then \eqref{eq:linear-exp} is equivalent to
\[
  ay\e^{cy-cb/a+d}=r,
\]
or
\[
  (cy)\e^{cy}=\frac{cr}{a}\e^{-d+cb/a}.
\]
By the definition of $W_k$,
\[
  cy=W_k\!\left(\frac{cr}{a}\e^{-d+cb/a}\right).
\]
Solving for $x=y-b/a$ gives \eqref{eq:linear-exp-solution}.  Every algebraic
step was reversible, proving the converse.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}
Solve $(2x-3)\e^{5x}=7$.  Here $a=2$, $b=-3$, $c=5$, $d=0$, $r=7$, so
\[
  x=\frac15W_k\!\left(\frac{35}{2}\e^{-15/2}\right)+\frac32.
\]
The argument is positive, hence only $W_0$ is real and there is exactly one
real solution.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Two real answers]
Consider $(x+2)\e^x=-1/10$.  Formula
\eqref{eq:linear-exp-solution} gives
\[
  x=W_k(-\e^2/10)-2.
\]
Since $-\e^2/10<-1/\e$, there is no real solution.  If the right-hand side is
changed to $-1/100$, then the argument $-\e^2/100$ lies in $(-1/\e,0)$, and
both $k=0$ and $k=-1$ give real solutions.  The branch count is therefore a
geometric fact, not a formal choice made after the calculation.
\end{example}

\subsection{Useful equivalent forms}

The equation $y\log y=A$ is converted by putting $u=\log y$:
\[
  u\e^u=A,
  \qquad
  y=\e^{W_k(A)}=\frac{A}{W_k(A)}.
\]
Similarly, if
\[
  \log y+\alpha y=\beta,
\]
then exponentiation yields $y\e^{\alpha y}=\e^\beta$, and therefore
\begin{equation}
  y=\frac1\alpha W_k(\alpha\e^\beta)                 \label{eq:log-linear}
\end{equation}
when $\alpha\ne0$.

\begin{proposition}[A verification principle]
\label{prop:verification-principle}
Let $R$ be a proposed real formula for a branch value at $x$.
Then $R=W_0(x)$ if and only if
\[
  R\e^R=x\quad\text{and}\quad R\ge-1,
\]
and $R=W_{-1}(x)$ if and only if
\[
  R\e^R=x\quad\text{and}\quad R\le-1.
\]
At $x=-1/\e$ the two tests agree.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Necessity is the defining equation together with the branch ranges in
\cref{thm:real-branches}.  Conversely, on each stated range the map
$R\mapsto R\e^R$ is one-to-one.  Hence a value satisfying the equation in the
correct range must equal the corresponding inverse branch.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Lambert $W$ does not obey the naive scaling rules of a logarithm.  The safest
way to test any proposed identity is exactly the preceding proposition: call
the proposed right-hand side $R$, compute $R\e^R$, and check the branch
range.
\end{remark}

\section{Differential and integral calculus}
\label{sec:calculus}

Throughout this section, $W$ means any differentiable branch at the point in
question.  The same calculations therefore apply simultaneously to $W_0$ and
$W_{-1}$ on the interiors of their real domains.

\subsection{The first derivative and its differential equation}

\begin{theorem}[Derivative]
\label{thm:derivative}
If $W(x)\ne-1$ and $W(x)\e^{W(x)}=x$, then
\begin{equation}
  W'(x)=\frac{\e^{-W(x)}}{1+W(x)}
       =\frac{W(x)}{x(1+W(x))}.                       \label{eq:first-derivative}
\end{equation}
The second form is used only when $x\ne0$.  On the principal branch,
$W_0'(0)=1$ by continuity.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Differentiate $W\e^W=x$ implicitly:
\[
  W'\e^W+W\e^W W'=1.
\]
Factoring gives $W'\e^W(1+W)=1$, which proves the first expression.  Since
$x=W\e^W$, replacing $\e^{-W}$ by $W/x$ proves the second.  Finally,
$W_0(x)/x=\e^{-W_0(x)}\to1$ as $x\to0$, and
$1+W_0(x)\to1$, so \eqref{eq:first-derivative} tends to $1$.
\end{proof}

Equivalently, every differentiable branch satisfies the nonlinear differential
equation
\begin{equation}
  x(1+W)W'=W.                                          \label{eq:W-ode}
\end{equation}
It is singular at the real branch point because $1+W=0$ there.

\subsection{Higher derivatives}

Repeated differentiation has a clean polynomial structure.

\begin{theorem}[All higher derivatives]
\label{thm:higher-derivatives}
Define polynomials $P_n$ by
\begin{equation}
  P_1(w)=1,
  \qquad
  P_{n+1}(w)=(1+w)P_n'(w)-(nw+3n-1)P_n(w).             \label{eq:P-recurrence}
\end{equation}
Then, for $n\ge1$,
\begin{equation}
  W^{(n)}(x)=\e^{-nW(x)}
      \frac{P_n(W(x))}{(1+W(x))^{2n-1}}.              \label{eq:nth-derivative}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The formula for $n=1$ is \eqref{eq:first-derivative}.  Suppose it holds for
$n$.  Since
\[
  \frac{\dd}{\dd x}=W'(x)\frac{\dd}{\dd w}
  =\frac{\e^{-w}}{1+w}\frac{\dd}{\dd w}
  \quad\text{when }w=W(x),
\]
we obtain
\begin{align*}
W^{(n+1)}(x)
 &=\frac{\e^{-w}}{1+w}\frac{\dd}{\dd w}
   \left(\e^{-nw}\frac{P_n(w)}{(1+w)^{2n-1}}\right)\\
 &=\e^{-(n+1)w}\frac{(1+w)P_n'(w)-(nw+3n-1)P_n(w)}
                    {(1+w)^{2n+1}}.
\end{align*}
This is exactly \eqref{eq:nth-derivative} with $n$ replaced by $n+1$ and
$P_{n+1}$ defined by \eqref{eq:P-recurrence}.  Induction completes the proof.
\end{proof}

The first few polynomials are
\begin{align*}
 P_1(w)&=1,\\
 P_2(w)&=-(w+2),\\
 P_3(w)&=2w^2+8w+9,\\
 P_4(w)&=-(6w^3+36w^2+79w+64).
\end{align*}
The recurrence is often more useful than an expanded closed form, because it
allows exact derivatives of arbitrary order to be generated symbolically.

\subsection{Monotonicity, concavity, and the inflection of \texorpdfstring{$W_{-1}$}{W-1}}

Putting $P_2(w)=-(w+2)$ into \eqref{eq:nth-derivative} gives
\begin{equation}
  W''(x)=-\e^{-2W(x)}\frac{W(x)+2}{(W(x)+1)^3}.        \label{eq:second-derivative}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Shape of the real branches]
\label{thm:shape}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
\item $W_0$ is strictly increasing and strictly concave on
      $(-1/\e,\infty)$.
\item $W_{-1}$ is strictly decreasing on $(-1/\e,0)$.
      It is strictly convex on $(-1/\e,-2/\e^2)$, has an inflection point at
      $x=-2/\e^2$, and is strictly concave on $(-2/\e^2,0)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For $W_0$, one has $W_0>-1$, so \eqref{eq:first-derivative} is positive.  Also
$W_0+2>0$ and $(W_0+1)^3>0$, so
\eqref{eq:second-derivative} is negative.

For $W_{-1}$, one has $W_{-1}<-1$, so its first derivative is negative.  In
\eqref{eq:second-derivative}, the denominator $(W_{-1}+1)^3$ is negative.
Therefore the sign of $W_{-1}''$ is the sign of $W_{-1}+2$.  It is positive
when $-2<W_{-1}<-1$, zero at $W_{-1}=-2$, and negative when
$W_{-1}<-2$.  Because $W_{-1}$ is decreasing and
$(-2)\e^{-2}=-2/\e^2$, these value intervals correspond to the stated
$x$-intervals.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Conditioning}

Suppose $x$ is perturbed by a small relative amount.  The relative condition
number of a nonzero value $W(x)$ is
\begin{equation}
 \kappa_{\mathrm{rel}}(x)
   =\abs{\frac{xW'(x)}{W(x)}}
   =\frac1{\abs{1+W(x)}}.                             \label{eq:condition}
\end{equation}
This formula follows directly from \eqref{eq:first-derivative}.
It explains three numerical facts at once:
\begin{itemize}
\item both real branches are ill-conditioned at $x=-1/\e$, where $W=-1$;
\item $W_0$ becomes relatively better conditioned as $x\to\infty$;
\item $W_{-1}$ becomes relatively better conditioned as $x\to0^-$, even
      though its absolute magnitude diverges.
\end{itemize}
The blow-up at $-1/\e$ is not a defect of an algorithm.  It belongs to the
inverse problem itself.

\subsection{Antiderivatives}

The substitution $x=w\e^w$ is remarkably effective because
\begin{equation}
  \dd x=\e^w(1+w)\dd w.                               \label{eq:dx-substitution}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Basic antiderivatives]
\label{thm:antiderivatives}
On any interval belonging to one differentiable branch,
\begin{align}
 \int W(x)\dd x
   &=\e^{W(x)}\bigl(W(x)^2-W(x)+1\bigr)+C              \label{eq:int-W-a}\\
   &=xW(x)-x+\e^{W(x)}+C,                              \label{eq:int-W-b}\\
 \int \frac{W(x)}x\dd x
   &=W(x)+\frac12W(x)^2+C,                             \label{eq:int-W-over-x}\\
 \int \frac{\dd x}{1+W(x)}
   &=\e^{W(x)}+C.                                      \label{eq:int-one-over}
\end{align}
More generally, if $r\ne0,-1$ and a consistent real or complex power is used,
\begin{equation}
 \int \frac{W(x)^r}{x}\dd x
   =\frac{W(x)^r}{r}+\frac{W(x)^{r+1}}{r+1}+C.         \label{eq:int-power-over-x}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Set $w=W(x)$ and use \eqref{eq:dx-substitution}.  Then
\[
 \int W(x)\dd x=\int w(1+w)\e^w\dd w
   =\e^w(w^2-w+1)+C,
\]
because differentiating the last expression gives
$\e^w(w^2+w)$.  Since $x=w\e^w$, this is also
$xw-x+\e^w+C$.

Next,
\[
  \frac{\dd x}{x}=\frac{1+w}{w}\dd w.
\]
Multiplication by $w$ gives \eqref{eq:int-W-over-x}; multiplication by $w^r$
gives
\[
 \int(w^{r-1}+w^r)\dd w,
\]
which proves \eqref{eq:int-power-over-x}.  Finally,
$\dd x/(1+w)=\e^w\dd w$, proving \eqref{eq:int-one-over}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Some exact definite integrals]
\label{cor:definite-integrals}
\begin{align}
 \int_{-1/\e}^{0}W_0(x)\dd x&=1-\frac3\e,             \label{eq:def-int-w0neg}\\
 \int_{-1/\e}^{0}W_{-1}(x)\dd x&=-\frac3\e,           \label{eq:def-int-wm1}\\
 \int_0^{\e}W_0(x)\dd x&=\e-1.                        \label{eq:def-int-w0pos}
\end{align}
The second integral is improper at $0$ but converges.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Use the antiderivative $A(w)=\e^w(w^2-w+1)$.  On $W_0$, the endpoint values
are $w=-1,0,1$ for $x=-1/\e,0,\e$.  Thus
$A(-1)=3/\e$, $A(0)=1$, and $A(1)=\e$, proving the first and third formulas.
On $W_{-1}$, $w\to-\infty$ as $x\to0^-$, and
$\e^w(w^2-w+1)\to0$.  Subtracting $A(-1)=3/\e$ proves the second formula.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The convergence of \eqref{eq:def-int-wm1} may look surprising because
$W_{-1}(x)\to-\infty$.  Its divergence is only logarithmic; the interval
shrinks fast enough for the area to remain finite.  The substitution
$x=w\e^w$ makes this transparent.
\end{remark}

\section{Power series at the origin}
\label{sec:taylor}

Only the principal branch passes through the origin, so an ordinary Taylor
series at $x=0$ belongs to $W_0$.  The lower branch has no finite value there;
its local description near $0^-$ is logarithmic and will be developed in
\cref{sec:asymptotics}.

\subsection{Lagrange inversion}

The coefficient engine is the following form of the Lagrange inversion
theorem.  We state it for analytic functions, although the proof can also be
read purely as an identity of formal power series.

\begin{theorem}[Lagrange inversion]
\label{thm:lagrange}
Let $\phi$ be analytic near $0$ with $\phi(0)\ne0$, and let $w=w(x)$ be the
unique analytic solution near $0$ of
\begin{equation}
  w=x\phi(w),\qquad w(0)=0.                            \label{eq:lagrange-implicit}
\end{equation}
If $F$ is analytic near $0$, then for every $n\ge1$,
\begin{equation}
  [x^n]F(w(x))=\frac1n[t^{n-1}]F'(t)\phi(t)^n.         \label{eq:lagrange-coeff}
\end{equation}
Here $[x^n]$ means ``the coefficient of $x^n$.''
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We use the residue notation
\[
  [x^n]G(x)=\Res_{x=0}\frac{G(x)}{x^{n+1}}\dd x.
\]
This is simply Cauchy's coefficient formula.  In
\eqref{eq:lagrange-implicit}, write $x=t/\phi(t)$.  Then
\[
  \dd x=\frac{\phi(t)-t\phi'(t)}{\phi(t)^2}\dd t.
\]
Changing variables in the residue gives
\begin{align}
[x^n]F(w(x))
 &=\Res_{t=0}F(t)
   \frac{\phi(t)^{n+1}}{t^{n+1}}
   \frac{\phi(t)-t\phi'(t)}{\phi(t)^2}\dd t \notag\\
 &=\Res_{t=0}\left(
   \frac{F(t)\phi(t)^n}{t^{n+1}}
  -\frac{F(t)\phi(t)^{n-1}\phi'(t)}{t^n}\right)\dd t.
                                                               \label{eq:lagrange-residue-middle}
\end{align}
The residue of a derivative is zero.  Apply this to
$F(t)\phi(t)^n/t^n$:
\[
0=\Res_{t=0}\left(
 \frac{F'(t)\phi(t)^n}{t^n}
 +n\frac{F(t)\phi(t)^{n-1}\phi'(t)}{t^n}
 -n\frac{F(t)\phi(t)^n}{t^{n+1}}
 \right)\dd t.
\]
Rearranging shows that the residue in
\eqref{eq:lagrange-residue-middle} equals
\[
  \frac1n\Res_{t=0}\frac{F'(t)\phi(t)^n}{t^n}\dd t
  =\frac1n[t^{n-1}]F'(t)\phi(t)^n.
\]
This proves \eqref{eq:lagrange-coeff}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The Taylor series of \texorpdfstring{$W_0$}{W0}}

The defining equation can be written
\begin{equation}
  W_0(x)=x\e^{-W_0(x)},                                \label{eq:W-lagrange-form}
\end{equation}
so $\phi(t)=\e^{-t}$.  Taking $F(t)=t$ in
\cref{thm:lagrange} gives the classical series.

\begin{theorem}[Taylor series]
\label{thm:W-taylor}
For $|x|<1/\e$,
\begin{equation}
  W_0(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}
     \frac{(-n)^{n-1}}{n!}x^n.                        \label{eq:W-taylor}
\end{equation}
In particular,
\begin{equation}
 W_0(x)=x-x^2+\frac32x^3-\frac83x^4
       +\frac{125}{24}x^5-\frac{54}{5}x^6
       +\frac{16807}{720}x^7-\cdots.                  \label{eq:W-taylor-first}
\end{equation}
The radius of convergence is exactly $1/\e$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By \cref{thm:lagrange} and \eqref{eq:W-lagrange-form},
\[
[x^n]W_0(x)
 =\frac1n[t^{n-1}]\e^{-nt}
 =\frac1n\frac{(-n)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}
 =\frac{(-n)^{n-1}}{n!}.
\]
This proves the formal series and hence the analytic expansion in some
neighborhood of $0$.

To find its radius, let
$a_n=(-n)^{n-1}/n!$.  Stirling's formula gives
\[
 |a_n|=\frac{n^{n-1}}{n!}
 \sim \frac{\e^n}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,n^{3/2}}.
\]
Therefore $\lim |a_n|^{1/n}=\e$, and the root test gives radius $1/\e$.
This is also geometrically natural: the nearest singularity of the inverse
branch is the critical value $-1/\e$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[What happens on the boundary]
At both $x=1/\e$ and $x=-1/\e$, the absolute value of the $n$th term is
asymptotic to a constant times $n^{-3/2}$.  Hence the series converges
absolutely at both endpoints.  At $-1/\e$ it sums to $-1$ but cannot be
continued as an ordinary power series through that point, because the local
behavior is a square root rather than an integer power series.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Powers and a generalized binomial identity}

Lagrange inversion gives much more than $W_0$ itself.

\begin{theorem}[Integer powers]
\label{thm:W-powers}
For a positive integer $r$ and $|x|<1/\e$,
\begin{equation}
 W_0(x)^r
 =\sum_{n=r}^{\infty}
   \frac{r}{n}\frac{(-n)^{n-r}}{(n-r)!}x^n.           \label{eq:W-power-series}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Apply \cref{thm:lagrange} with $F(t)=t^r$.  For $n\ge r$,
\[
[x^n]W_0(x)^r
 =\frac1n[t^{n-1}]r t^{r-1}\e^{-nt}
 =\frac rn[t^{n-r}]\e^{-nt}
 =\frac rn\frac{(-n)^{n-r}}{(n-r)!}.
\]
For $n<r$ the coefficient is zero, since $W_0(x)^r$ begins with $x^r$.
\end{proof}

A useful complex-parameter version is especially compact.

\begin{theorem}[Powers of $W_0(x)/x$]
\label{thm:W-over-x-alpha}
For any complex constant $\alpha$ and $|x|<1/\e$,
\begin{equation}
 \left(\frac{W_0(x)}x\right)^\alpha
 =\e^{-\alpha W_0(x)}
 =\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}
   \frac{\alpha(n+\alpha)^{n-1}}{n!}(-x)^n,           \label{eq:generalized-tree-series}
\end{equation}
where the $n=0$ term is interpreted as $1$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The first equality follows from $W_0(x)/x=\e^{-W_0(x)}$.  For $n\ge1$, use
\cref{thm:lagrange} with $F(t)=\e^{-\alpha t}$:
\begin{align*}
[x^n]\e^{-\alpha W_0(x)}
 &=\frac1n[t^{n-1}](-\alpha)
       \e^{-(n+\alpha)t}\\
 &=-\frac\alpha n\frac{(-(n+\alpha))^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}
 =\frac{\alpha(n+\alpha)^{n-1}}{n!}(-1)^n.
\end{align*}
The constant term is $F(0)=1$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The tree function and rooted labeled trees}

Define
\begin{equation}
  T(x)=-W_0(-x).                                       \label{eq:tree-function}
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
  T(x)=x\e^{T(x)},                                     \label{eq:tree-functional}
\end{equation}
and \eqref{eq:W-taylor} becomes
\begin{equation}
  T(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{n^{n-1}}{n!}x^n.      \label{eq:tree-series}
\end{equation}
\begin{theorem}[Cayley, rooted form]
\label{thm:cayley}
The number of rooted trees whose vertices carry the distinct labels
$1,\ldots,n$ is $n^{n-1}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For $n\ge2$, the Pr\"ufer code gives a bijection between unrooted labeled trees
and sequences of length $n-2$ with entries in $\{1,\ldots,n\}$.  To encode a
tree, repeatedly remove its smallest-labeled leaf and record the label of that
leaf's unique neighbor.  After $n-2$ removals, two vertices remain, and the
recorded sequence has length $n-2$.

To decode a sequence, give each label initial degree one plus the number of
times it occurs in the sequence.  Read the sequence from left to right.  At
each step, join the current sequence entry to the smallest label whose current
degree is one, then reduce both relevant degrees by one and delete the current
entry.  At the end, join the two labels of degree one.  This produces a tree,
and the degree bookkeeping shows that encoding and decoding undo one another.
Thus there are $n^{n-2}$ unrooted labeled trees.  Choosing one of the $n$
vertices as root gives $n\cdot n^{n-2}=n^{n-1}$ rooted labeled trees.  For
$n=1$ there is one tree, agreeing with $1^{0}=1$.
\end{proof}

Thus Lambert $W$ is not merely a device for transcendental equations; its
Taylor coefficients encode one of the basic families of combinatorial
objects.  The functional equation also has a direct combinatorial
interpretation.  A rooted labeled tree consists of one distinguished root
attached to an unordered set of rooted labeled trees.  In exponential
generating functions, a labeled atom contributes $x$ and an unordered labeled
set contributes an exponential, giving $T=x\e^T$.  Lagrange inversion then
recovers the coefficient $n^{n-1}/n!$.

\subsection{A local rational approximation}

Power series are accurate near $0$, but a rational function often reaches
farther with the same number of coefficients.  Match
\[
  R(x)=\frac{x(1+ax)}{1+bx+cx^2}
\]
to \eqref{eq:W-taylor-first} through $x^4$.

\begin{proposition}[A Pad\'e approximant of type $(2,2)$]
\label{prop:pade}
The unique rational function of the displayed form whose Taylor expansion
agrees with $W_0(x)$ through degree four is
\begin{equation}
  R_{2,2}(x)=
  \frac{x(1+\frac43x)}{1+\frac73x+\frac56x^2}.        \label{eq:pade22}
\end{equation}
Consequently $W_0(x)-R_{2,2}(x)=\mathcal O(x^5)$ as $x\to0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The condition
\[
 (1+bx+cx^2)W_0(x)-x(1+ax)=\mathcal O(x^5)
\]
and \eqref{eq:W-taylor-first} give, from the coefficients of
$x^2,x^3,x^4$,
\[
 -1+b-a=0,
 \qquad
 \frac32-b+c=0,
 \qquad
 -\frac83+\frac32b-c=0.
\]
Solving yields $b=7/3$, $a=4/3$, and $c=5/6$.
\end{proof}

The approximant is not a substitute for branch-aware algorithms, especially
near $-1/\e$, but it is a useful hand calculation on a moderate interval
around zero.  Its sampled error is reported in
\cref{tab:numerical-experiments}.

\section{The square-root branch point at \texorpdfstring{$-1/\e$}{-1/e}}
\label{sec:branch-point}

The two real branches meet where the derivative of $w\e^w$ vanishes.  An
ordinary inverse-function Taylor series cannot exist there.  The correct local
coordinate is a square root, and---importantly---one signed formula describes
both $W_0$ and $W_{-1}$.

\subsection{Finding the natural local variable}

Write
\begin{equation}
  W(x)=-1+u.                                           \label{eq:w-minus-one-u}
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
  \e x=(u-1)\e^u,
  \qquad
  1+\e x=1+(u-1)\e^u.                                 \label{eq:eta-u}
\end{equation}
Expanding at $u=0$ gives
\begin{equation}
  1+(u-1)\e^u
   =\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{n-1}{n!}u^n
   =\frac{u^2}{2}+\frac{u^3}{3}+\frac{u^4}{8}
      +\frac{u^5}{30}+\cdots.                         \label{eq:eta-u-series}
\end{equation}
The missing linear term is the analytic signature of the critical point.
Define the signed parameter
\begin{equation}
 p=\begin{cases}
  +\sqrt{2(1+\e x)},& W=W_0,\\[1ex]
  -\sqrt{2(1+\e x)},& W=W_{-1}.
 \end{cases}                                           \label{eq:signed-p}
\end{equation}
Near the meeting point, $p$ and $u$ have the same sign and $p=u+\mathcal
O(u^2)$.

\subsection{The complete local expansion}

\begin{theorem}[Common Puiseux expansion]
\label{thm:puiseux}
For $x$ sufficiently close to $-1/\e$ on either real branch, define $p$ by
\eqref{eq:signed-p}.  Then
\begin{equation}
 W(x)=-1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_n p^n,                  \label{eq:puiseux-general}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
 c_n=\frac1n[t^{n-1}]
 \left(\frac{t^2}{2\{1+(t-1)\e^t\}}\right)^{n/2}.    \label{eq:puiseux-coeff}
\end{equation}
The first terms are
\begin{align}
 W(x)={}&-1+p-\frac13p^2+\frac{11}{72}p^3
 -\frac{43}{540}p^4+\frac{769}{17280}p^5
 -\frac{221}{8505}p^6 \notag\\
 &+\frac{680863}{43545600}p^7
 -\frac{1963}{204120}p^8+\mathcal O(p^9).             \label{eq:puiseux-first}
\end{align}
The same coefficients are used on both branches; only the sign of $p$ changes.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Define
\[
 \psi(u)=\frac{\sqrt{2\{1+(u-1)\e^u\}}}{u},
\]
where the square root is chosen to have value $1$ at $u=0$.  This apparent
quotient singularity is removable: by \eqref{eq:eta-u-series}, the expression
inside the square root equals $u^2(1+\mathcal O(u))/u^2$, so $\psi$ is analytic
near $0$ and $\psi(0)=1$.  Equations \eqref{eq:eta-u} and
\eqref{eq:signed-p} become
\begin{equation}
  p=u\psi(u).                                          \label{eq:p-u-psi}
\end{equation}
Thus $u=p\phi(u)$ with $\phi(u)=1/\psi(u)$.  Lagrange inversion
\cref{thm:lagrange}, applied with $F(u)=u$, gives
\[
 [p^n]u(p)=\frac1n[t^{n-1}]\phi(t)^n
 =\frac1n[t^{n-1}]
   \left(\frac{t^2}{2\{1+(t-1)\e^t\}}\right)^{n/2}.
\]
Since $W=-1+u$, this proves \eqref{eq:puiseux-general} and
\eqref{eq:puiseux-coeff}.  Expanding the coefficient formula gives the listed
rational numbers.  The analytic inverse-function theorem applies to
\eqref{eq:p-u-psi}, because its derivative at $u=0$ is $1$, so the series
converges in some neighborhood of $p=0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Why this is called a Puiseux series]
A Puiseux series permits fractional powers of the original variable.  Since
$p$ is proportional to $(x+1/\e)^{1/2}$, odd powers of $p$ are half-integer
powers of $x+1/\e$.  The series is an ordinary Taylor series in $p$, but a
half-power series in $x+1/\e$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Leading behavior and derivative blow-up}

Taking only the first term of \eqref{eq:puiseux-first} yields the most important
local approximation.

\begin{corollary}[Leading branch-point behavior]
\label{cor:branch-leading}
As $x\downarrow-1/\e$,
\begin{align}
 W_0(x)&=-1+\sqrt{2\e\left(x+\frac1\e\right)}
            +\mathcal O\!\left(x+\frac1\e\right),     \label{eq:w0-branch-leading}\\
 W_{-1}(x)&=-1-\sqrt{2\e\left(x+\frac1\e\right)}
            +\mathcal O\!\left(x+\frac1\e\right).   \label{eq:wm1-branch-leading}
\end{align}
Moreover,
\begin{align}
 W_0'(x)&\sim +\sqrt{\frac{\e}{2(x+1/\e)}},\\
 W_{-1}'(x)&\sim -\sqrt{\frac{\e}{2(x+1/\e)}}.
                                                               \label{eq:branch-derivative-leading}
\end{align}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Since $p^2=2(1+\e x)=2\e(x+1/\e)$,
\eqref{eq:puiseux-first} gives $W=-1+p+\mathcal O(p^2)$, proving the first two
formulas.  Differentiating $p^2=2(1+\e x)$ gives
$p'=\e/p$.  Also $\dd W/\dd p=1+\mathcal O(p)$, so
$W'(x)=\e/p+\mathcal O(1)$.  Substituting the signed value of $p$ proves
\eqref{eq:branch-derivative-leading}.
\end{proof}

The derivative singularity agrees with the condition number in
\eqref{eq:condition}.  It also explains why a numerical routine should switch
to the $p$-series near $-1/\e$: direct inversion asks floating-point arithmetic
to resolve a nearly horizontal graph.

\caption{Sampled scaled errors of truncations of the signed-$p$ expansion on

\subsection{A coefficient recurrence for computation}

Formula \eqref{eq:puiseux-coeff} is explicit, but a recurrence is convenient
when many coefficients are desired.  Let
\[
 u(p)=\sum_{n\ge1}c_np^n.
\]
Substituting into
\begin{equation}
  \frac{p^2}{2}=1+(u-1)\e^u                         \label{eq:puiseux-implicit}
\end{equation}
and comparing coefficients determines $c_n$ successively.  Indeed, the
coefficient of $p^{N+1}$ is linear in $c_N$: the quadratic leading term
$u^2/2$ contributes $c_N p^{N+1}$ through
$c_1c_Np^{N+1}$, and $c_1=1$; all other contributions involve only
$c_1,\ldots,c_{N-1}$.  Therefore the recurrence is triangular and exact.
This is the method used in the accompanying code to check printed
coefficients.

\begin{proposition}[Branch separation]
\label{prop:branch-separation}
As $x\downarrow-1/\e$,
\begin{equation}
 W_0(x)-W_{-1}(x)
 =2\sqrt{2(1+\e x)}
  +\frac{11}{36}\{2(1+\e x)\}^{3/2}
  +\mathcal O((1+\e x)^{5/2}).                        \label{eq:branch-separation}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Evaluate \eqref{eq:puiseux-first} at $p=q$ and $p=-q$, where
$q=\sqrt{2(1+\e x)}$.  The even powers cancel in the difference and the odd
powers double.  Keeping $p$ and $p^3$ gives the stated formula.
\end{proof}

\section{Logarithmic asymptotic expansions}
\label{sec:asymptotics}

There are two real limits in which $|W|$ becomes large:
\[
  W_0(x)\to+\infty\quad(x\to+\infty),
  \qquad
  W_{-1}(x)\to-\infty\quad(x\to0^-).
\]
The same expansion governs both.  This is one of the most useful
principal-branch results that extends almost unchanged to $W_{-1}$.

\subsection{The first two terms}

For $x>0$, \eqref{eq:log-equation} reads
\[
 W_0(x)+\log W_0(x)=\log x.
\]
The logarithm is smaller than its argument, so a first guess is
$W_0(x)\approx\log x$.  Feeding this back into the logarithm gives
\begin{equation}
  W_0(x)\approx\log x-\log\log x.                     \label{eq:first-asymptotic-w0}
\end{equation}
For $W_{-1}(x)$, $x<0$ and $W_{-1}(x)<0$, so
\[
 W_{-1}(x)+\log(-W_{-1}(x))=\log(-x).
\]
As $x\to0^-$, the right side tends to $-\infty$, and the analogous first
approximation is
\begin{equation}
 W_{-1}(x)\approx
 \log(-x)-\log\bigl(-\log(-x)\bigr).                  \label{eq:first-asymptotic-wm1}
\end{equation}
The second logarithm has the same form in both cases once absolute values are
inserted.

\subsection{A unified coefficient formula}

For either of the two limits, define
\begin{equation}
 L_1=\begin{cases}
       \log x,&W_0(x),\ x\to+\infty,\\
       \log(-x),&W_{-1}(x),\ x\to0^- ,
     \end{cases}
 \qquad
 L_2=\log|L_1|.                                        \label{eq:L1-L2-real}
\end{equation}
Thus $L_1\to+\infty$ in the first case and $L_1\to-\infty$ in the second.
In both cases $L_2>0$ eventually.

\begin{theorem}[Unified real asymptotic expansion]
\label{thm:unified-asymptotic}
In either real limit described in \eqref{eq:L1-L2-real}, and for every fixed
integer $N\ge0$,
\begin{equation}
 W(x)=L_1-L_2+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{P_j(L_2)}{L_1^j}
 +\mathcal O\!\left(\frac{L_2^{N+1}}{|L_1|^{N+1}}\right),               \label{eq:asymptotic-P}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
 P_j(L)=\frac1j[z^{j-1}]
       \frac{(L+\log(1-z))^j}{1-z}.                   \label{eq:Pj-coeff}
\end{equation}
The first four correction polynomials are
\begin{align}
 P_1(L)&=L,\\
 P_2(L)&=\frac{L(-2+L)}2,\\
 P_3(L)&=\frac{L(6-9L+2L^2)}6,\\
 P_4(L)&=\frac{L(-12+36L-22L^2+3L^3)}{12}.            \label{eq:first-P-polynomials}
\end{align}
Consequently,
\begin{align}
W(x)={}&L_1-L_2+\frac{L_2}{L_1}
 +\frac{L_2(-2+L_2)}{2L_1^2}
 +\frac{L_2(6-9L_2+2L_2^2)}{6L_1^3}\notag\\
&+\frac{L_2(-12+36L_2-22L_2^2+3L_2^3)}{12L_1^4}
 +\mathcal O\!\left(\frac{L_2^5}{|L_1|^5}\right).    \label{eq:asymptotic-first-four}
\end{align}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
In either real regime, $W$ and $L_1$ have the same sign.  Write
\begin{equation}
  W=L_1-L+v,
  \qquad L=L_2,
  \qquad t=\frac1{L_1}.                               \label{eq:asymptotic-substitution}
\end{equation}
Then
\[
 |W|=|L_1|\bigl(1-tL+tv\bigr)
\]
for sufficiently large $|L_1|$.  Substitution into
$W+\log|W|=L_1$ yields
\begin{equation}
  v=-\log(1-tL+tv).                                    \label{eq:v-implicit}
\end{equation}
Set
\begin{equation}
  u=t(L-v).                                            \label{eq:u-asymptotic}
\end{equation}
Then $1-u=1-tL+tv$, so
\begin{equation}
  v=-\log(1-u),
  \qquad
  u=t\bigl(L+\log(1-u)\bigr).                         \label{eq:u-lagrange-asymptotic}
\end{equation}
For the moment regard $L$ as a fixed parameter.  Apply Lagrange inversion to
$u=t\phi(u)$ with
$\phi(u)=L+\log(1-u)$ and $F(u)=-\log(1-u)$.  Since
$F'(u)=1/(1-u)$,
\[
 [t^j]v(t)=\frac1j[z^{j-1}]
 \frac{(L+\log(1-z))^j}{1-z}=P_j(L).
\]
This proves the formal expansion
$v=\sum_{j\ge1}P_j(L)t^j$ and formula \eqref{eq:Pj-coeff}.  Direct expansion
gives \eqref{eq:first-P-polynomials}.

It remains to justify asymptotic use when $L=\log|L_1|$ is not fixed.  Let
$v_N=\sum_{j=1}^N P_j(L)t^j$ and
$w_N=L_1-L+v_N$.  Since $|t|L\to0$, the Taylor expansion of the logarithm in
\eqref{eq:v-implicit} is valid for all sufficiently large $|L_1|$.
The coefficient construction cancels every power through $t^N$, so
\[
 w_N+\log|w_N|-L_1
 =\mathcal O(|t|^{N+1}L^{N+1}).                       \label{eq:asymptotic-residual}
\]
Let $F(w)=w+\log|w|-L_1$.  On the relevant large-magnitude interval,
$F'(w)=1+1/w$ is bounded away from zero.  By the mean value theorem, for some
$\xi$ between the exact root $W$ and $w_N$,
\[
 W-w_N=-\frac{F(w_N)}{F'(\xi)}.
\]
Combining this with \eqref{eq:asymptotic-residual} proves the remainder in
\eqref{eq:asymptotic-P}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Stirling numbers and the double expansion}

The unsigned Stirling number of the first kind
$\stirlingone{n}{r}$ counts permutations of $n$ objects having exactly $r$
cycles.  Algebraically it is defined by
\begin{equation}
  y(y+1)\cdots(y+n-1)
    =\sum_{r=0}^{n}\stirlingone{n}{r}y^r.             \label{eq:stirling-definition}
\end{equation}
These numbers give a closed form for every polynomial $P_j$.

\begin{lemma}[Stirling form of the coefficient polynomials]
\label{lem:P-stirling}
For $j\ge1$,
\begin{equation}
 P_j(L)=\sum_{m=1}^{j}
   \frac{(-1)^{j-m}}{m!}
   \stirlingone{j}{j-m+1}L^m.                         \label{eq:P-stirling}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The standard exponential generating function for unsigned Stirling numbers is
\begin{equation}
 \frac{(-\log(1-z))^r}{r!}
   =\sum_{n=r}^{\infty}\stirlingone{n}{r}\frac{z^n}{n!}.
                                                               \label{eq:stirling-egf}
\end{equation}
Differentiate \eqref{eq:stirling-egf} with $r=q+1$ to obtain
\begin{equation}
 [z^{j-1}]\frac{(\log(1-z))^q}{1-z}
  =(-1)^q\frac{q!}{(j-1)!}\stirlingone{j}{q+1}.       \label{eq:log-power-coeff}
\end{equation}
Expand $(L+\log(1-z))^j$ in \eqref{eq:Pj-coeff}.  The coefficient of $L^m$
uses $q=j-m$ powers of the logarithm and equals
\begin{align*}
 \frac1j\binom jm
 [z^{j-1}]\frac{(\log(1-z))^{j-m}}{1-z}
 &=\frac1j\binom jm(-1)^{j-m}
   \frac{(j-m)!}{(j-1)!}\stirlingone{j}{j-m+1}\\
 &=\frac{(-1)^{j-m}}{m!}\stirlingone{j}{j-m+1}.
\end{align*}
This proves \eqref{eq:P-stirling}.
\end{proof}

Putting $j=n+m$ in \eqref{eq:P-stirling} gives the traditional bivariate form.

\begin{corollary}[Comtet--de Bruijn double series]
\label{cor:double-asymptotic}
Formally, and asymptotically when truncated by total degree,
\begin{equation}
 W=L_1-L_2+
 \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}
 \frac{(-1)^n}{m!}\stirlingone{n+m}{n+1}
 \frac{L_2^m}{L_1^{n+m}}.                             \label{eq:double-asymptotic}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
In \eqref{eq:P-stirling}, set $j=n+m$.  Then
$j-m+1=n+1$, and the term $P_j(L_2)/L_1^j$ becomes exactly the summand in
\eqref{eq:double-asymptotic}.  Summing over all $j\ge1$ is equivalent to
summing over $n\ge0$ and $m\ge1$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{How to read the expansion on each branch}

For $W_0(x)$ at large positive $x$,
\[
  L_1=\log x,
  \qquad
  L_2=\log\log x.
\]
All denominators in \eqref{eq:asymptotic-first-four} are positive.  For
$W_{-1}(x)$ near $0^-$,
\[
  L_1=\log(-x)<0,
  \qquad
  L_2=\log(-\log(-x)).
\]
Odd powers of $L_1$ are negative.  No separate coefficient table is needed:
the signs appropriate to the lower branch are already carried by $L_1$.

\begin{example}
Take $x=-10^{-20}$.  Then
$L_1=-20\log 10\approx-46.0517$ and
$L_2\approx3.8298$.  The first two terms give
$L_1-L_2\approx-49.8815$; adding $L_2/L_1$ moves the approximation to about
$-49.9647$.  The exact value is approximately $-49.9630$.  The lower branch is
therefore not approximated by $\log(-x)$ alone: the second logarithm is a
visible correction even at very small $|x|$.
\end{example}

\subsection{A simple rigorous two-term enclosure for \texorpdfstring{$W_0$}{W0}}

The first two asymptotic terms also form elementary bounds.

\begin{proposition}[Logarithmic enclosure]
\label{prop:w0-log-bounds}
For $x>\e$, put $L_1=\log x$ and $L_2=\log L_1$.  Then
\begin{equation}
  L_1-L_2<W_0(x)<L_1.                                 \label{eq:w0-log-bounds}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Here $W_0(x)>1$.  From
$W_0=L_1-\log W_0$ and $\log W_0>0$, one gets $W_0<L_1$.  Since the logarithm
is increasing, this implies $\log W_0<\log L_1=L_2$.  Substitution back into
$W_0=L_1-\log W_0$ gives $W_0>L_1-L_2$.
\end{proof}

The lower endpoint in \eqref{eq:w0-log-bounds} is not merely a bound; it is an
excellent starting value for a monotone quadratic iteration developed in
\cref{sec:numerics}.

\section{Inequalities on the real branches}
\label{sec:bounds}

Approximations tell us what is likely; inequalities tell us what is guaranteed.
This section develops bounds that require only elementary calculus.  The
principal branch and the lower branch need different natural variables.

\subsection{Global elementary bounds for \texorpdfstring{$W_0$}{W0}}

\begin{theorem}[Three bounds for nonnegative arguments]
\label{thm:w0-elementary-bounds}
For every $x\ge0$,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{x}{1+x}\le W_0(x)\le\log(1+x)\le x.           \label{eq:w0-elementary-bounds}
\end{equation}
The first two inequalities are strict when $x>0$ except for limiting equality
at $0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Put $w=W_0(x)\ge0$, so $x=w\e^w$.

First, $W_0(x)\le x$ follows immediately from $\e^w\ge1$.  The familiar
inequality $\log(1+x)\le x$ follows because
$x-\log(1+x)$ has derivative $x/(1+x)\ge0$ and vanishes at $0$.

To prove $w\le\log(1+x)$, it is enough to prove $\e^w\le1+x$.  But
\[
  1+x-\e^w=1+(w-1)\e^w.
\]
The right side is zero at $w=0$ and has derivative $w\e^w\ge0$, so it is
nonnegative.

Finally, $x/(1+x)\le w$ is equivalent, for $w>0$, to
\[
  \e^w\le1+w\e^w,
\]
or $1\le w+\e^{-w}$.  The function
$w+\e^{-w}$ equals $1$ at $0$ and has derivative $1-\e^{-w}\ge0$.  Thus the
inequality holds.  The case $x=0$ follows directly.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[A bracket for computation]
\label{cor:w0-positive-bracket}
For $x>0$,
\begin{equation}
 \frac{x}{1+x}<W_0(x)<\log(1+x).                       \label{eq:w0-positive-bracket}
\end{equation}
Thus these two elementary expressions provide a finite bracket for bisection
or safeguarded Newton iteration on the entire positive half-line.
\end{corollary}

For $-1/\e<x<0$, the simplest exact bracket is already supplied by the branch
range:
\begin{equation}
  -1<W_0(x)<0.                                         \label{eq:w0-negative-bracket}
\end{equation}
Near $-1/\e$, the Puiseux expansion gives much narrower local brackets if its
remainder is controlled.

\subsection{A natural coordinate for \texorpdfstring{$W_{-1}$}{W-1}}

Every $x\in(-1/\e,0)$ can be written uniquely as
\begin{equation}
  x=-\e^{-u-1},\qquad u=-1-\log(-x)>0.                 \label{eq:u-for-wm1}
\end{equation}
Write
\begin{equation}
  W_{-1}(x)=-1-y,
  \qquad y>0.                                          \label{eq:y-for-wm1}
\end{equation}
Substituting into $W\e^W=x$ gives
\[
 -(1+y)\e^{-1-y}=-\e^{-1-u},
\]
so
\begin{equation}
  u=y-\log(1+y).                                       \label{eq:u-g-y}
\end{equation}
The function $g(y)=y-\log(1+y)$ is positive and strictly increasing for
$y>0$, because $g'(y)=y/(1+y)>0$.  Bounding its inverse produces bounds for
$W_{-1}$.

\subsection{Sharp elementary bounds for \texorpdfstring{$W_{-1}$}{W-1}}

The following inequalities, adapted from Chatzigeorgiou's branch-specific
bounds \cite{Chatzigeorgiou2013}, work on the entire domain and have the correct
square-root scale at the branch point.

\begin{theorem}[Global $W_{-1}$ enclosure]
\label{thm:wm1-bounds}
Let $-1/\e<x<0$ and define $u=-1-\log(-x)>0$.  Then
\begin{equation}
 -1-\sqrt{2u}-u
 < W_{-1}(x)
 < -1-\sqrt{2u}-\frac23u.                              \label{eq:wm1-bounds}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $y=-1-W_{-1}(x)>0$.  By \eqref{eq:u-g-y},
$u=g(y)=y-\log(1+y)$.  We will prove
\begin{equation}
  \frac23g(y)<y-\sqrt{2g(y)}<g(y).                     \label{eq:g-middle-bounds}
\end{equation}
Replacing $g(y)$ by $u$ and rearranging then gives
\[
  \sqrt{2u}+\frac23u<y<\sqrt{2u}+u.
\]
Since $W_{-1}=-1-y$, reversing signs produces
\eqref{eq:wm1-bounds}.

For the right inequality in \eqref{eq:g-middle-bounds}, observe that
$y-g(y)=\log(1+y)$.  Put $t=\log(1+y)>0$, so
$y=\e^t-1$ and $g(y)=\e^t-1-t$.  The desired inequality is equivalent to
\[
  t<\sqrt{2(\e^t-1-t)}.
\]
After squaring, this follows from
\[
  \e^t>1+t+\frac{t^2}{2}\qquad(t>0),
\]
which is the exponential series with positive omitted terms.

For the left inequality in \eqref{eq:g-middle-bounds}, we need
\[
  y-\frac23g(y)>\sqrt{2g(y)}.
\]
The left side is positive and equals
$(\e^t-1+2t)/3$.  Squaring is therefore legitimate.  The squared inequality
is
\begin{equation}
  (\e^t-1+2t)^2>18(\e^t-1-t).                          \label{eq:wm1-square-ineq}
\end{equation}
Expanding both exponentials gives the exact series
\begin{equation}
 (\e^t-1+2t)^2-18(\e^t-1-t)
 =\sum_{n=4}^{\infty}\frac{2^n+4n-20}{n!}t^n.         \label{eq:wm1-positive-series}
\end{equation}
For $n\ge4$, $2^n+4n-20>0$, so every term is positive.  This proves
\eqref{eq:wm1-square-ineq}, hence the left inequality in
\eqref{eq:g-middle-bounds}, and therefore the theorem.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Behavior of the enclosure]
As $u\to0$, both bounds begin with $-1-\sqrt{2u}$, exactly matching the
square-root branch behavior.  As $u\to\infty$, their gap is $u/3$.  The gap is
not asymptotically small compared with $|W_{-1}|\sim u$, but the bounds remain
finite, explicit, and branch-safe for every $x\in(-1/\e,0)$, making them
valuable as a guaranteed numerical bracket.
\end{remark}

\begin{corollary}[A monotone lower starter]
\label{cor:wm1-lower-starter}
With $u$ as above,
\begin{equation}
  \beta_0=-1-\sqrt{2u}-u                              \label{eq:wm1-starter}
\end{equation}
obeys $\beta_0<W_{-1}(x)<-1$.  It can therefore initialize the monotone
logarithmic Newton iteration proved in \cref{thm:wm1-monotone-newton}.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The first inequality is the left half of \eqref{eq:wm1-bounds}; the second is
the range of $W_{-1}$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{A comparison principle}

Many further bounds can be proved without manipulating $W$ directly.

\begin{proposition}[Comparison by the defining map]
\label{prop:comparison}
Let $x$ lie in the domain of a real branch.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
\item On the principal range $[-1,\infty)$, if $a,b\ge-1$ and
      $a\e^a\le x\le b\e^b$, then
      $a\le W_0(x)\le b$.
\item On the lower range $(-\infty,-1]$, if $a\le b\le-1$ and
      $a\e^a\ge x\ge b\e^b$, then
      $a\le W_{-1}(x)\le b$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
On $[-1,\infty)$ the defining map is increasing; on
$(-\infty,-1]$ it is decreasing, by \cref{thm:real-branches}.  Applying the
appropriate inverse preserves or reverses inequalities exactly as stated.
\end{proof}

This comparison principle is a practical recipe.  To prove a proposed upper
bound $B(x)$ for $W_0$, it is enough to prove $B(x)\ge-1$ and
$B(x)\e^{B(x)}\ge x$.  For $W_{-1}$, the monotonicity direction is reversed.

\section{Integral, continued-fraction, and inverse-series representations}
\label{sec:representations}

A special function becomes more useful when it can be represented in several
ways.  Series are good near a center, continued fractions can reorganize
series, integrals connect the function to harmonic analysis, and inverse
series generate high-order root corrections.

\subsection{A logarithmic integral representation}

The following representation is a convenient consequence of the Taylor
series.  Corcino, Corcino, and Mez\H{o} extend a closely related formula to a
larger real interval; the restricted form below is enough for an elementary,
fully justified proof.

\begin{theorem}[Logarithmic integral]
\label{thm:integral-representation}
For real $x$ with $|x|<1/\e$,
\begin{equation}
 W_0(x)=\frac1\pi\operatorname{Re}\int_0^\pi
 \log\!\left(
   \frac{\exp(\e^{\ii t})-x\e^{-\ii t}}
        {\exp(\e^{\ii t})-x\e^{\ii t}}
 \right)\dd t.                                         \label{eq:integral-representation}
\end{equation}
The logarithm is the analytic determination obtained from its convergent
power series in the proof.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We first prove the Fourier identity
\begin{equation}
 \frac{a^n}{n!}=\frac2\pi\operatorname{Im}
   \int_0^\pi \exp(a\e^{\ii t})\sin(nt)\dd t          \label{eq:fourier-identity}
\end{equation}
for real $a$ and positive integer $n$.  Expand the exponential uniformly on
$[0,\pi]$:
\[
 \exp(a\e^{\ii t})=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{a^m}{m!}\e^{\ii mt}.
\]
The imaginary part is
$\sum_{m\ge1}a^m\sin(mt)/m!$.  Orthogonality gives
\[
 \int_0^\pi\sin(mt)\sin(nt)\dd t=
 \begin{cases}\pi/2,&m=n,\\0,&m\ne n,\end{cases}
\]
which proves \eqref{eq:fourier-identity}.

Apply \eqref{eq:fourier-identity} with $a=-n$.  Since
$(-n)^{n-1}=-(-n)^n/n$, the Taylor series
\eqref{eq:W-taylor} becomes
\begin{equation}
 W_0(x)=-\frac2\pi\operatorname{Im}\int_0^\pi
  \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}
    \frac{\{x\exp(-\e^{\ii t})\}^n}{n}\sin(nt)\dd t.  \label{eq:integral-before-sum}
\end{equation}
For $|x|<1/\e$,
\[
 \left|x\exp(-\e^{\ii t})\right|
 =|x|\exp(-\cos t)\le |x|\e<1,
\]
so the series converges uniformly and may be integrated term by term.  Put
$r=x\exp(-\e^{\ii t})$.  The logarithm series gives
\begin{align*}
 \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{r^n}{n}\sin(nt)
 &=\frac1{2\ii}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}
     \frac{(r\e^{\ii t})^n-(r\e^{-\ii t})^n}{n}\\
 &=\frac1{2\ii}
   \log\!\left(\frac{1-r\e^{-\ii t}}{1-r\e^{\ii t}}\right).
\end{align*}
For any complex number $L$, $-2\operatorname{Im}(L/(2\ii))=
\operatorname{Re}L$.  Therefore \eqref{eq:integral-before-sum} is
\[
 \frac1\pi\operatorname{Re}\int_0^\pi
 \log\!\left(\frac{1-r\e^{-\ii t}}{1-r\e^{\ii t}}\right)\dd t.
\]
Multiplying numerator and denominator inside the logarithm by
$\exp(\e^{\ii t})$ gives \eqref{eq:integral-representation}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The representation is not intended as the fastest numerical algorithm.  Its
value is structural: the rooted-tree coefficients of $W_0$ emerge from
Fourier orthogonality, and an inverse function is expressed as an average of a
logarithm.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Euler's continued fraction for a series}

We use the generalized continued-fraction notation
\[
 b_0+\underset{m=1}{\mathbf K^N}\frac{a_m}{b_m}
 =b_0+\cfrac{a_1}{b_1+\cfrac{a_2}{b_2+\cdots+
                 \cfrac{a_N}{b_N}}}.
\]

\begin{lemma}[Euler transformation]
\label{lem:euler-fraction}
Let $c_1,\ldots,c_N$ be nonzero and put $r_m=c_{m+1}/c_m$.  Then
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{j=0}^{N}c_j
 =c_0+\cfrac{c_1}{1+
   \cfrac{-r_1}{1+r_1+
   \cfrac{-r_2}{1+r_2+\ddots+
   \cfrac{-r_{N-1}}{1+r_{N-1}}}}}.                   \label{eq:euler-finite}
\end{equation}
If $\sum c_j$ converges and the finite continued fractions have a limit, the
corresponding infinite fraction equals the series sum.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For $1\le m\le N-1$, define
\[
 Q_m=1+r_m+r_mr_{m+1}+\cdots+r_mr_{m+1}\cdots r_{N-1}.
\]
Then
\begin{equation}
 Q_m=1+r_m Q_{m+1},\qquad Q_{N-1}=1+r_{N-1}.           \label{eq:Q-rec}
\end{equation}
A backward induction using \eqref{eq:Q-rec} shows that the denominator
beginning at level $m$ in \eqref{eq:euler-finite} equals $Q_m/Q_{m+1}$, with
the natural convention $Q_N=1$.  At the top level,
\[
 1-\frac{r_1}{Q_1/Q_2}
 =1-\frac{r_1Q_2}{Q_1}
 =\frac1{Q_1},
\]
by $Q_1=1+r_1Q_2$.  Hence the fraction after $c_0$ equals
$c_1Q_1$.  But
\[
 c_1Q_1=c_1+c_1r_1+c_1r_1r_2+\cdots
       =c_1+c_2+\cdots+c_N.
\]
Adding $c_0$ proves the finite identity.  Passing to a limit proves the last
statement whenever that limit exists.
\end{proof}

\subsection{A continued fraction for \texorpdfstring{$W_0$}{W0}}

Apply \cref{lem:euler-fraction} to the Taylor terms
\[
 c_m=\frac{(-m)^{m-1}}{m!}x^m,
 \qquad m\ge1.
\]
Their ratios are
\begin{equation}
 \frac{c_m}{c_{m-1}}
 =-\left(\frac{m}{m-1}\right)^{m-2}x,
 \qquad m\ge2.                                        \label{eq:W-term-ratio}
\end{equation}
Define
\begin{equation}
 b_m=\left(1-\frac1m\right)^{2-m}
     =\left(\frac{m}{m-1}\right)^{m-2}>0.             \label{eq:b-m-cf}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Euler continued fraction for $W_0$]
\label{thm:W-continued-fraction}
For $|x|<1/\e$,
\begin{equation}
 W_0(x)=
 \cfrac{x}{1+
  \cfrac{b_2x}{1-b_2x+
  \cfrac{b_3x}{1-b_3x+
  \cfrac{b_4x}{1-b_4x+\ddots}}}},                    \label{eq:W-continued-fraction}
\end{equation}
where $b_m$ is given by \eqref{eq:b-m-cf}.  Equivalently, with
$a_m=-b_m$,
\begin{equation}
 W_0(x)=\frac{x}{1+
   \underset{m=2}{\mathbf K^\infty}
     \dfrac{-a_mx}{1+a_mx}}.                          \label{eq:W-associated-fraction}
\end{equation}
Every finite convergent in the Euler construction equals the corresponding
partial sum of the Taylor series.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Equation \eqref{eq:W-term-ratio} says that the ratio entering Euler's
fraction at level $m-1$ is $-b_mx$.  Substitution into
\eqref{eq:euler-finite} gives the finite version of
\eqref{eq:W-continued-fraction}.  By
\cref{lem:euler-fraction}, its value is the Taylor partial sum.  Those partial
sums converge to $W_0(x)$ for $|x|<1/\e$ by
\cref{thm:W-taylor}; therefore the continued-fraction convergents have the
same limit.
\end{proof}

The first coefficients are $b_2=1$, $b_3=3/2$, $b_4=16/9$, and
$b_5=125/64$.  This particular fraction is an exact reorganization of the
Taylor series, not a claim that every continued fraction converges beyond the
series disk.  More sophisticated $C$- and $J$-fractions can have different
convergence behavior; see \cite{Corcino2018}.

\subsection{Schröder-type inverse series}

Newton's method uses only the first term of a local series for the correction
to an approximate root.  The whole series can be generated by Lagrange
inversion.

\begin{theorem}[General inverse correction series]
\label{thm:schroeder-general}
Let $F$ be analytic near $a$, with $F'(a)\ne0$, and suppose a simple root
$r=a+h$ lies sufficiently close to $a$.  Set
\begin{equation}
 q=\frac{F(a)}{F'(a)},
 \qquad
 A(t)=\frac{F(a+t)-F(a)}{F'(a)t},                      \label{eq:q-A-schroeder}
\end{equation}
where $A(0)=1$.  Then
\begin{equation}
 h=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-q)^n}{n}
    [t^{n-1}]A(t)^{-n}.                               \label{eq:schroeder-series}
\end{equation}
In particular, if
\[
 a_2=\frac{F''(a)}{2F'(a)},
 \qquad
 a_3=\frac{F'''(a)}{6F'(a)},
\]
then
\begin{equation}
 h=-q-a_2q^2+(a_3-2a_2^2)q^3+\mathcal O(q^4).         \label{eq:schroeder-first}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The root equation $F(a+h)=0$ and \eqref{eq:q-A-schroeder} give
\[
 0=F(a)+F'(a)hA(h),
 \qquad
 h=\frac{-q}{A(h)}.
\]
This has Lagrange form $h=z\phi(h)$ with $z=-q$ and
$\phi(t)=A(t)^{-1}$.  Formula \eqref{eq:schroeder-series} follows from
\cref{thm:lagrange} with $F(t)=t$.

Now $A(t)=1+a_2t+a_3t^2+\mathcal O(t^3)$.  Expanding
$A(t)^{-n}$ in \eqref{eq:schroeder-series} gives, successively,
$-q$, $-a_2q^2$, and $(a_3-2a_2^2)q^3$, proving
\eqref{eq:schroeder-first}.
\end{proof}

For the Lambert equation $F(w)=w\e^w-x$, the normalized function is explicit:
\begin{equation}
 A_a(t)=\frac{(a+t)\e^t-a}{(1+a)t}.                   \label{eq:A-lambert}
\end{equation}
Also
\begin{equation}
 a_2=\frac{a+2}{2(a+1)},
 \qquad
 a_3=\frac{a+3}{6(a+1)}.                              \label{eq:lambert-a2-a3}
\end{equation}
The first term $h=-q$ is Newton's correction.  Keeping more terms produces
Schröder-type high-order approximations.  Howard's work in the supplied
archive develops such series to high order for both $W_0$ and $W_{-1}$ and
studies branch-compatible starting functions \cite{Howard2025}.

\subsection{The Wright omega function}

Exponentiating a huge number merely to call $W$ can be undesirable.  Define
the Wright omega function by
\begin{equation}
  \omega(z)=W(\e^z).                                  \label{eq:omega-definition}
\end{equation}
On the real axis the unambiguous choice is
$\omega(y)=W_0(\e^y)>0$.  It satisfies
\begin{equation}
  \omega(y)+\log\omega(y)=y.                          \label{eq:omega-equation}
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}
For real $y$, the equation \eqref{eq:omega-equation} has exactly one positive
solution.  It is strictly increasing and
\begin{equation}
  \omega'(y)=\frac{\omega(y)}{1+\omega(y)}.            \label{eq:omega-derivative}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The function $g(w)=w+\log w$ is strictly increasing from $-\infty$ to
$+\infty$ on $(0,\infty)$ because $g'(w)=1+1/w>0$.  It therefore has a unique
positive inverse.  Differentiating \eqref{eq:omega-equation} gives
$\omega'(1+1/\omega)=1$, which is \eqref{eq:omega-derivative}; it is positive.
\end{proof}

The omega formulation is the logarithmic equation used later for stable
numerical evaluation.  It also removes one layer of exponential growth from
asymptotic analysis.

\section{Numerical evaluation}
\label{sec:numerics}

A reliable evaluator must solve three different problems:
\begin{enumerate}
\item choose the correct branch;
\item obtain a starting value in a safe region;
\item refine without overflow, loss of branch, or amplification of the
      intrinsic branch-point conditioning.
\end{enumerate}
No single formula is best everywhere.  Near $-1/\e$ the signed-$p$ series is
natural; near $0^-$ on $W_{-1}$ the logarithmic asymptotic series is natural;
away from singular points, high-order iteration is efficient.

\subsection{The simple logarithmic fixed-point iteration}

From \eqref{eq:exp-W}, any nonzero real branch value satisfies
\begin{equation}
  W=\log\left(\frac{x}{W}\right),                     \label{eq:fixed-log}
\end{equation}
where $x/W>0$.  This suggests
\begin{equation}
  w_{n+1}=G_x(w_n),
  \qquad
  G_x(w)=\log\left(\frac{x}{w}\right).                \label{eq:fixed-log-iteration}
\end{equation}
It is attractive because it is simple, but its convergence is branch- and
range-dependent.

\begin{proposition}[Local behavior of logarithmic fixed-point iteration]
\label{prop:fixed-point-convergence}
At a real fixed point $W\ne0$,
\begin{equation}
  G_x'(W)=-\frac1W.                                    \label{eq:fixed-point-derivative}
\end{equation}
Thus the iteration is locally attracting when $|W|>1$ and locally repelling
when $|W|<1$.  In particular, it is locally attracting on $W_0$ only for
$x>\e$, and on $W_{-1}$ for every $-1/\e<x<0$; it loses contraction at the
branch point and at $W_0(\e)=1$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Differentiate $G_x(w)=\log|x|-\log|w|$ to get $G_x'(w)=-1/w$.  The elementary
fixed-point theorem says that a differentiable map is locally contractive
when the derivative magnitude at the fixed point is less than $1$, and
repelling when it is greater than $1$.  Translate $|W_0(x)|>1$ and
$|W_{-1}(x)|>1$ using the branch ranges and special values.
\end{proof}

Even where it converges, \eqref{eq:fixed-log-iteration} is only linear.  The
next methods are quadratic or cubic.

\subsection{Newton, Halley, and logarithmic Newton}

For
\[
  f_x(w)=w\e^w-x,
\]
Newton's method is
\begin{equation}
 N_x(w)=w-\frac{w\e^w-x}{\e^w(1+w)}
       =w-\frac{w-x\e^{-w}}{1+w}.                     \label{eq:newton-original}
\end{equation}
Halley's method uses the second derivative:
\begin{equation}
 H_x(w)=w-
 \frac{f_x(w)}{f_x'(w)-\dfrac{f_x(w)f_x''(w)}{2f_x'(w)}},
 \quad
 f_x'(w)=\e^w(1+w),\quad f_x''(w)=\e^w(2+w).           \label{eq:halley}
\end{equation}

A particularly useful alternative applies Newton's method to the logarithmic
equation
\begin{equation}
 F_x(w)=w+\log|w|-\log|x|=0.                           \label{eq:log-F}
\end{equation}
Its update is
\begin{align}
 S_x(w)
 &=w-\frac{w+\log|w|-\log|x|}{1+1/w}                 \notag\\
 &=\frac{w}{1+w}\left(1+\log\frac{x}{w}\right).      \label{eq:log-newton}
\end{align}
The quotient $x/w$ is positive on either real branch.  Formula
\eqref{eq:log-newton} avoids constructing $\e^w$, an important advantage when
$x$ and $W_0(x)$ are enormous.

\begin{theorem}[Local error equations]
\label{thm:local-errors}
Let $W\ne-1,0$ be a simple real branch value and let $e=w-W$.  One step of the
three methods has the following local errors:
\begin{align}
 N_x(w)-W
   &=\frac{W+2}{2(W+1)}e^2+\mathcal O(e^3),            \label{eq:newton-error}\\
 S_x(w)-W
   &=-\frac{1}{2W(W+1)}e^2+\mathcal O(e^3),            \label{eq:log-newton-error}\\
 H_x(w)-W
   &=\frac{W^2+4W+6}{12(W+1)^2}e^3+\mathcal O(e^4).   \label{eq:halley-error}
\end{align}
Thus Newton and logarithmic Newton are quadratic and Halley is cubic, except
where the displayed denominators reveal the branch-point singularity.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For a smooth function $q$ with simple root $W$, Taylor expansion of Newton's
map gives the standard formula
\begin{equation}
 e_{\mathrm{new}}=\frac{q''(W)}{2q'(W)}e^2
                   +\mathcal O(e^3).                  \label{eq:generic-newton-error}
\end{equation}
To verify it directly, write
$q(W+e)=q'(W)e+q''(W)e^2/2+\mathcal O(e^3)$ and
$q'(W+e)=q'(W)+q''(W)e+\mathcal O(e^2)$, divide the two series, and subtract
from $e$.

For $q=f_x$, the ratio is
$f_x''(W)/f_x'(W)=(W+2)/(W+1)$, proving
\eqref{eq:newton-error}.  For $q=F_x$,
\[
 F_x'(W)=\frac{W+1}{W},
 \qquad
 F_x''(W)=-\frac1{W^2},
\]
which proves \eqref{eq:log-newton-error}.

For Halley's method, a direct division of Taylor series gives
\begin{equation}
 e_{\mathrm{new}}=
 \left(\frac{q''(W)^2}{4q'(W)^2}
       -\frac{q'''(W)}{6q'(W)}\right)e^3
 +\mathcal O(e^4).                                    \label{eq:generic-halley-error}
\end{equation}
For $f_x$, one has
$f_x'''(W)=\e^W(W+3)$.  Substitution into
\eqref{eq:generic-halley-error} gives
\[
 \frac{(W+2)^2}{4(W+1)^2}-\frac{W+3}{6(W+1)}
 =\frac{W^2+4W+6}{12(W+1)^2},
\]
proving \eqref{eq:halley-error}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Which Newton form is faster?]
For large positive $W$, the coefficient in \eqref{eq:newton-error} tends to
$1/2$, while the coefficient in \eqref{eq:log-newton-error} is of order
$1/W^2$.  Logarithmic Newton therefore becomes dramatically more accurate per
step at large $x$.  Near $W=0$, however, its coefficient is large and ordinary
Newton is preferable.  This quantitative comparison was emphasized by Iacono
and Boyd \cite{IaconoBoyd2017}.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Monotone quadratic evaluation of \texorpdfstring{$W_0$ for $x>\e$}{W0 for x > e}}

The two-term lower bound in \cref{prop:w0-log-bounds} gives a starting value
that never crosses the root.

\begin{theorem}[Monotone logarithmic Newton on $W_0$]
\label{thm:w0-monotone-newton}
Let $x>\e$, set
\begin{equation}
 \beta_0=\log x-\log\log x,                            \label{eq:w0-beta0}
\end{equation}
and define $\beta_{n+1}=S_x(\beta_n)$ by
\eqref{eq:log-newton}.  Then
\begin{equation}
 1<\beta_0<\beta_1<\cdots<W_0(x),                     \label{eq:w0-monotone-chain}
\end{equation}
and $\beta_n\to W_0(x)$.  If $w\in(1,W_0(x))$, then
\begin{equation}
 0<W_0(x)-S_x(w)
 <\frac{(W_0(x)-w)^2}{(1+w)W_0(x)}.                   \label{eq:w0-log-newton-bound}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $W=W_0(x)>1$.  By \cref{prop:w0-log-bounds}, $\beta_0<W$.  Also, for
$L=\log x>1$, the function $L-\log L$ has minimum $1$ at $L=1$, so
$\beta_0>1$.

Fix $1<w<W$ and put
\[
 r=\frac{W-w}{w}>0.
\]
Using $\log x=W+\log W$ in \eqref{eq:log-newton}, a direct simplification
gives
\begin{equation}
 W-S_x(w)=\frac{w}{1+w}\bigl(r-\log(1+r)\bigr).       \label{eq:exact-log-newton-error}
\end{equation}
For $r>0$,
\[
 0<r-\log(1+r)<\frac{r^2}{1+r}.                       \label{eq:r-log-ineq}
\]
The first inequality follows from $\log(1+r)<r$.  For the second, subtract
the left side from the proposed upper bound:
\[
 \frac{r^2}{1+r}-r+\log(1+r)
 =\log(1+r)-\frac{r}{1+r}.
\]
This expression is zero at $0$ and has derivative
$r/(1+r)^2>0$.  Substitution into
\eqref{eq:exact-log-newton-error} proves
\eqref{eq:w0-log-newton-bound}; in particular $S_x(w)<W$.

Moreover,
\[
 S_x(w)-w
 =\frac{w}{1+w}\bigl(W-w+\log(W/w)\bigr)>0,
\]
so every iterate increases and remains below $W$.  It therefore converges to
some limit $\ell\le W$.  Continuity gives $S_x(\ell)=\ell$, which is
$\ell+\log\ell=\log x$.  This equation has a unique positive solution, so
$\ell=W$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Monotone quadratic evaluation of \texorpdfstring{$W_{-1}$}{W-1}}

The lower branch also admits a global monotone scheme, but the direction is
easiest to see through concavity of the logarithmic equation.

\begin{theorem}[Monotone logarithmic Newton on $W_{-1}$]
\label{thm:wm1-monotone-newton}
Let $-1/\e<x<0$, put $u=-1-\log(-x)$, and start with
\[
 \beta_0=-1-\sqrt{2u}-u.
\]
Define $\beta_{n+1}=S_x(\beta_n)$.  Then
\begin{equation}
 \beta_0<\beta_1<\cdots<W_{-1}(x)<-1,                 \label{eq:wm1-monotone-chain}
\end{equation}
and $\beta_n\to W_{-1}(x)$ quadratically.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let
\[
 F(w)=w+\log(-w)-\log(-x),\qquad w<-1.
\]
Then
\[
 F'(w)=1+\frac1w=\frac{w+1}{w}>0,
 \qquad
 F''(w)=-\frac1{w^2}<0.
\]
Thus $F$ is strictly increasing and strictly concave, with root
$W=W_{-1}(x)$.  By \cref{thm:wm1-bounds}, $\beta_0<W$.

Suppose $w<W$.  Since $F$ is increasing, $F(w)<0$, so the Newton update
$S_x(w)=w-F(w)/F'(w)$ is greater than $w$.  Concavity says that the graph lies
below its tangent:
\[
 F(W)\le F(w)+F'(w)(W-w).
\]
Since $F(W)=0$, this implies
$-F(w)/F'(w)\le W-w$, hence $S_x(w)\le W$.  Strict concavity gives strict
inequality.  Induction proves \eqref{eq:wm1-monotone-chain}.  The increasing
bounded sequence has a limit; continuity and uniqueness of the root force the
limit to be $W$.  Quadratic convergence follows from
\eqref{eq:log-newton-error}, because $W\ne-1,0$ in the open domain.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Guaranteed branch-safe refinement}

Monotone iterations are attractive, but a general library routine benefits
from a bracket and a safeguarded high-order step.

\begin{algorithm}[Safeguarded Halley--bisection]
\label{alg:safeguarded}
For a requested real branch and argument:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Handle $x=-1/\e$ and $x=0$ exactly.
\item Construct a bracket $[a,b]$:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item for $W_0$ with $-1/\e<x<0$, use $[-1,0]$;
  \item for $W_0$ with $x>0$, use
        $[x/(1+x),\log(1+x)]$;
  \item for $W_{-1}$, use the two bounds in
        \eqref{eq:wm1-bounds}.
  \end{itemize}
\item From a current point $w$, compute a Halley candidate $h=H_x(w)$.
Accept it only if it is finite and lies in the middle half
$[a+(b-a)/4,b-(b-a)/4]$; otherwise use the midpoint $h=(a+b)/2$.
\item Update the bracket using monotonicity of $f(w)=w\e^w$:
  on $W_0$, replace $a$ by $h$ if $f(h)<x$ and replace $b$ otherwise;
  on $W_{-1}$, reverse this test because $f$ is decreasing.
\item Stop when the bracket width or the residual is below the requested
      tolerance.
\end{enumerate}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{theorem}[Convergence of the safeguarded algorithm]
\label{thm:safeguarded-convergence}
Algorithm \ref{alg:safeguarded} preserves a bracket containing the requested
branch value and converges to it.  Every accepted Halley step taken
sufficiently close to a non-branch-point root obeys the cubic local error law
in \eqref{eq:halley-error}; the unconditional global guarantee is geometric
contraction of the bracket.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The initial brackets are valid by \eqref{eq:w0-negative-bracket},
\cref{cor:w0-positive-bracket}, and \cref{thm:wm1-bounds}.  The update rule is
exactly the comparison principle \cref{prop:comparison}, so the root remains
inside every new bracket.

An accepted candidate lies in the middle half, so whichever endpoint is
replaced, the new width is at most $3/4$ of the old width.  A rejected
candidate is replaced by the midpoint, which halves the width.  Therefore the
width tends to zero.  The nested closed brackets have one common point, and
continuity plus the residual sign change identifies it as the branch root.

At a simple root, \cref{thm:local-errors} proves that any Halley step accepted
in the local regime has cubic error.  The middle-half safeguard may still
insert occasional bisections when the root lies very near one endpoint of the
current bracket, so cubic convergence is asserted for accepted local Halley
steps rather than as an unconditional rate for the hybrid sequence.  The
bracket contraction already proves global convergence.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Analytic starting approximations}

For $x>0$, Iacono and Boyd propose
\begin{equation}
 \alpha(x)=\frac1{1+\frac12\log(1+x)},
 \qquad
 G_+(x)=\log(1+\alpha(x)x).                            \label{eq:iacono-positive}
\end{equation}
It reproduces the first two Taylor terms at $0$ and the first two logarithmic
terms at infinity.  It is an approximation, not a rigorous enclosure.

A single expression covering the entire principal branch is built from
$y=\sqrt{1+\e x}$:
\begin{equation}
 G_0(x)=-1+a\log\left(
     \frac{1+by}{1+c\log(1+y)}\right),                 \label{eq:iacono-global}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
 a=2.036,
 \qquad
 c=\frac{\e^{1/a}-1-\sqrt2/a}{1-\e^{1/a}\log2},
 \qquad
 b=\frac{\sqrt2}{a}+c.                                \label{eq:iacono-constants}
\end{equation}
The printed decimal values are approximately
$b=1.14956131$ and $c=0.45495740$.  Writing them through
\eqref{eq:iacono-constants} makes $G_0(0)=0$ exact instead of introducing a
tiny rounding offset.  The square-root variable gives the correct singular
shape at $-1/\e$, while the logarithm gives the correct growth at infinity.

For $W_{-1}$, three branch-aware choices cover the domain:
\begin{itemize}
\item use the negative signed-$p$ series near $-1/\e$;
\item use the lower rigorous bound \eqref{eq:wm1-starter} when a guaranteed
      monotone iteration is desired;
\item use the logarithmic asymptotic expansion
      \eqref{eq:asymptotic-first-four} near $0^-$.
\end{itemize}

\section{Complex branches}
\label{sec:complex}

The real picture is the visible edge of a multivalued complex inverse.  This
section gives a working introduction without requiring a full course in
Riemann surfaces.

\subsection{Local inverses and the exceptional value}

Let
\[
  f(w)=w\e^w,
  \qquad w\in\mathbb C.
\]
Its complex derivative is still
\[
  f'(w)=\e^w(1+w).
\]
Because the exponential never vanishes, the only critical point is $w=-1$,
and its critical value is $-1/\e$.

\begin{theorem}[Local complex inverse]
\label{thm:local-complex-inverse}
If $w_*$ satisfies $w_*\e^{w_*}=z_*$ and $w_*\ne-1$, then there are
neighborhoods $U$ of $w_*$ and $V$ of $z_*$ such that $f:U\to V$ is one-to-one
and has an analytic inverse.  The derivative of that inverse is
\[
  \frac{\e^{-w}}{1+w}.
\]
At $w_*=-1$ no analytic inverse in the variable $z+1/\e$ exists; the local
inverse instead has the square-root form of \cref{thm:puiseux}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The complex inverse-function theorem says that an analytic function with
nonzero derivative at a point is one-to-one in a sufficiently small
neighborhood and has an analytic local inverse.  Here
$f'(w_*)=\e^{w_*}(1+w_*)\ne0$ exactly when $w_*\ne-1$.
Differentiating the identity $f(W(z))=z$ gives
$W'(z)=1/f'(W(z))=\e^{-W(z)}/(1+W(z))$.

At $w_*=-1$, put $u=w+1$ and $\zeta=z+1/\e$.  Equation
\eqref{eq:eta-u-series} can be written
\[
  \e\zeta=\frac{u^2}{2}h(u),
\]
where $h$ is analytic and $h(0)=1$.  Suppose an ordinary analytic inverse
$u=u(\zeta)$ existed with $u(0)=0$.  If its zero at $0$ had order $m\ge1$,
then $u(\zeta)^2h(u(\zeta))$ would have a zero of even order $2m$, whereas
$\e\zeta$ has a simple zero.  This contradiction rules out an ordinary
analytic inverse.  Taking a square root instead produces the two local
inverses constructed explicitly in \cref{thm:puiseux}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Branch labels and cuts}

The standard branches are denoted $W_k$, $k\in\mathbb Z$, but branch cuts
are conventions rather than intrinsic objects.  We follow the convention of
Corless et al.\ \cite{Corless1996}.  The principal branch $W_0$ is normalized
by $W_0(0)=0$ and is analytic on the plane cut along
$(-\infty,-1/\e]$.  The branches $W_{-1}$ and $W_1$ are exceptional: each has
a logarithmic branch point at $0$ and also shares the square-root branch point
at $-1/\e$.  They are represented with two leftward cuts, one issuing from
each branch point; the cuts overlap on $(-\infty,-1/\e)$.  The remaining
nonprincipal branches have the logarithmic cut $(-\infty,0]$.  Boundary values
on any cut must be specified by approaching from above or below.  With the
standard closure convention, $W_{-1}$ takes the real values described earlier
on $[-1/\e,0)$.

Why are integers natural labels?  Taking a logarithm of
$w\e^w=z$ gives, with branch choices,
\begin{equation}
  w+\log w=\Log z+2\pi\ii k.                           \label{eq:complex-log-equation}
\end{equation}
For large $|k|$ or large $|z|$, the dominant first approximation is the
right-hand side, so one solution lies near each vertical translate by
$2\pi\ii k$.

\subsection{Asymptotics on every complex branch}

Let $\Log$ denote the principal complex logarithm.  In a standard cut-free
sector, the logarithmic equation for branch $k$ is organized by
\begin{equation}
  L_1=\Log z+2\pi\ii k,
  \qquad
  L_2=\Log L_1.                                        \label{eq:complex-L1-L2}
\end{equation}
Along a more general continuation path, the logarithms are chosen
continuously and the integer on the right of
\eqref{eq:complex-log-equation} may require a constant relabeling.  What
matters below is that $L_1$ is the continuous right-hand side of that equation.
The same coefficient polynomials from the real theory reappear.

\begin{theorem}[Complex branch asymptotics]
\label{thm:complex-asymptotic}
Follow a branch $W_k(z)$ along a cut-free path on which
$|W_k(z)|\to\infty$.  Choose continuous logarithms so that
\begin{equation}
  W_k(z)+\log W_k(z)=L_1,
  \qquad L_2=\log L_1.                                 \label{eq:complex-continuous-L}
\end{equation}
Then $|L_1|\to\infty$, $L_2/L_1\to0$, and, for every fixed $N$,
\begin{equation}
 W_k(z)=L_1-L_2+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{P_j(L_2)}{L_1^j}
 +\mathcal O\!\left(\frac{|L_2|^{N+1}}{|L_1|^{N+1}}\right),             \label{eq:complex-asymptotic}
\end{equation}
with $P_j$ given by \eqref{eq:Pj-coeff}.  In particular,
\[
  W_k(z)=L_1-L_2+\frac{L_2}{L_1}+\cdots.
\]
In the usual large-$z$ sectors, $L_1$ and $L_2$ may be taken as in
\eqref{eq:complex-L1-L2}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Write $W=W_k(z)$.  From \eqref{eq:complex-continuous-L},
$L_1=W+\log W$.  Since $|W|\to\infty$,
$\log W/W\to0$ along the chosen determination, so
$L_1/W\to1$.  Hence $|L_1|\to\infty$ and
$L_2/L_1\to0$.

Substitute $W=L_1-L_2+v$, $t=1/L_1$, and $L=L_2$.  Since
\[
 W=L_1(1-tL+tv),
\]
the selected logarithm satisfies, for all sufficiently advanced points on the
path,
\[
 \log W=L_2+\log(1-tL+tv).
\]
Equation \eqref{eq:complex-continuous-L} therefore becomes exactly
\eqref{eq:v-implicit}.  The Lagrange-inversion calculation in the proof of
\cref{thm:unified-asymptotic} is algebraic over the complex numbers and gives
the same polynomials $P_j$.

It remains to justify the remainder without using a nonexistent complex mean
value theorem.  Let
\[
 w_N=L_1-L_2+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{P_j(L_2)}{L_1^j}
\]
and let
\[
 R_N=w_N+\log w_N-L_1.
\]
The same coefficient cancellation as in the real proof gives
\[
 R_N=\mathcal O\!\left(\frac{|L_2|^{N+1}}{|L_1|^{N+1}}\right).
\]
Seek the exact root as $W=w_N+\delta$.  On the same local logarithm sheet, its
equation is
\begin{equation}
 \delta=-R_N-\log\left(1+\frac{\delta}{w_N}\right).   \label{eq:complex-delta-fixed}
\end{equation}
Because $w_N\sim L_1$ and $R_N\to0$, for sufficiently large $|L_1|$ the
right-hand side of \eqref{eq:complex-delta-fixed} maps the closed disk
$|\delta|\le2|R_N|$ into itself.  Indeed, when $|\zeta|\le1/2$, the logarithm
series gives $|\log(1+\zeta)|\le2|\zeta|$, and therefore
\[
 \left|-R_N-\log\left(1+\frac{\delta}{w_N}\right)\right|
 \le |R_N|+\frac{2|\delta|}{|w_N|}
 \le 2|R_N|
\]
for all sufficiently large $|w_N|$.  Its derivative with respect to $\delta$
has magnitude $1/|w_N+\delta|<1/2$ there.  The contraction principle supplies
a unique fixed point with $|\delta|\le2|R_N|$.  It is the continuation of the
chosen branch, and the required error estimate follows.
\end{proof}

For fixed nonzero $z$, letting $|k|\to\infty$ describes the remote branches.
For fixed $k$, the expansion applies as $|z|\to\infty$ in sectors avoiding the
cuts.  As $z\to0$, it applies to the nonprincipal branches whose magnitudes
diverge; it does \emph{not} apply to $W_0(z)$, which instead satisfies
$W_0(z)=z+\mathcal O(z^2)$.

\subsection{Conjugation symmetry}

\begin{proposition}[Conjugate branches]
\label{prop:conjugation}
Away from the conventional branch cuts, standard branch labels satisfy
\begin{equation}
  \overline{W_k(z)}=W_{-k}(\overline z).               \label{eq:conjugation}
\end{equation}
In particular, $W_0(\overline z)=\overline{W_0(z)}$ wherever both sides are
continued without crossing the principal cut.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
If $w\e^w=z$, then complex conjugation gives
$\overline w\e^{\overline w}=\overline z$.  Hence conjugation maps inverse
values at $z$ to inverse values at $\overline z$.  To identify the label, use
\eqref{eq:complex-asymptotic}: conjugation changes
$\Log z+2\pi\ii k$ into
$\Log\overline z-2\pi\ii k$ along conjugate paths, which is the leading
quantity for branch $-k$.  Analytic continuation preserves this identification
through any cut-free connected region.
\end{proof}

\begin{warning}
On a branch cut, an expression such as $W_k(x)$ may mean an upper or lower
boundary value.  Formula \eqref{eq:conjugation} then relates opposite boundary
values, not necessarily two symbols interpreted with the same one-sided
convention.  The real $W_{-1}$ on $(-1/\e,0)$ is best treated directly by the
real inverse construction of \cref{thm:real-branches}.
\end{warning}

\subsection{The complex square-root interchange}

Near $z=-1/\e$, let
\[
 p=\sqrt{2(1+\e z)}
\]
with either local square-root choice.  Formula \eqref{eq:puiseux-first} gives
two analytic functions of $p$.  Circling once around $-1/\e$ changes $p$ to
$-p$ and interchanges the two local inverse sheets.  On the real axis to the
right of the branch point, those sheets are precisely $W_0$ and $W_{-1}$.
This is the geometric meaning of a square-root branch point.

\section{Applications}
\label{sec:applications}

The point of a special function is not to rename an equation; it is to expose
structure.  A Lambert $W$ solution makes branch counts, derivatives,
asymptotics, and parameter dependence available without resolving the
transcendental equation from scratch.

\subsection{The equation \texorpdfstring{$x^x=a$}{x to the x = a}}

\begin{theorem}[Complete positive-real classification]
\label{thm:x-to-x}
Let $a>0$ and seek solutions $x>0$ of
\begin{equation}
  x^x=a.                                               \label{eq:x-to-x}
\end{equation}
They are
\begin{equation}
  x=\exp\bigl(W_k(\log a)\bigr),                      \label{eq:x-to-x-solution}
\end{equation}
with the following real count:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
Range of $a$ & Positive solutions \\
\midrule
$0<a<\e^{-1/\e}$ & none \\
$a=\e^{-1/\e}$ & one: $x=1/\e$ \\
$\e^{-1/\e}<a<1$ & two: branches $k=0,-1$ \\
$a=1$ & one: $x=1$ \\
$a>1$ & one: branch $k=0$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Put $y=\log x$, so $x=\e^y$.  Taking a logarithm of
\eqref{eq:x-to-x} gives
\[
  x\log x=\log a,
  \qquad
  y\e^y=\log a.
\]
Thus $y=W_k(\log a)$ and \eqref{eq:x-to-x-solution} follows.  The real count is
exactly the branch count for the argument $\log a$:
there are no real values below $-1/\e$, one at $-1/\e$, two between
$-1/\e$ and $0$, and one above $0$.  At $a=1$, the finite value is
$W_0(0)=0$, giving $x=1$; $W_{-1}(0)$ is not finite and does not supply an
additional positive solution.
\end{proof}

This theorem recovers the familiar minimum of $x^x$ on $(0,\infty)$ without a
separate optimization: the two real branches merge exactly when
$\log a=-1/\e$.

\subsection{Infinite power towers}

For $a>0$, define the finite towers by
\begin{equation}
 t_0=1,
 \qquad
 t_{n+1}=a^{t_n}.                                      \label{eq:tower-iteration}
\end{equation}
If $t_n$ converges to $T$, then $T=a^T$.

\begin{theorem}[Convergence interval for real power towers]
\label{thm:power-tower}
The sequence \eqref{eq:tower-iteration} converges to a finite positive limit
if and only if
\begin{equation}
  \e^{-\e}\le a\le\e^{1/\e}.                          \label{eq:tower-interval}
\end{equation}
On this interval its limit is
\begin{equation}
 T=\begin{cases}
 \dfrac{W_0(-\log a)}{-\log a},&a\ne1,\\[2ex]
 1,&a=1.
 \end{cases}                                           \label{eq:tower-limit}
\end{equation}
For $1<a<\e^{1/\e}$ there is also a larger, unstable fixed point obtained from
$W_{-1}(-\log a)$, but the tower beginning at $1$ converges to the $W_0$
fixed point.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Write $b=\log a$ and $g(x)=\e^{bx}$.  A positive fixed point $T$ satisfies
$\log T=bT$, or
\begin{equation}
  (-bT)\e^{-bT}=-b.                                   \label{eq:tower-W-transform}
\end{equation}
This gives \eqref{eq:tower-limit}.  We now prove the exact convergence
interval.

\emph{Case 1: $b\ge0$.}
A fixed point has $T\ge1$ and satisfies
\[
  b=\frac{\log T}{T}.
\]
The function $\phi(T)=\log T/T$ increases from $0$ to $1/\e$ on $[1,\e]$ and
then decreases to $0$, because
$\phi'(T)=(1-\log T)/T^2$.  Hence a fixed point exists only if
$b\le1/\e$.  When $0\le b\le1/\e$, let $T\in[1,\e]$ be the smaller fixed
point.  Since $g$ is increasing, $t_0=1\le T$ implies $t_n\le T$ for all $n$.
Also $t_1\ge t_0$, and monotonicity of $g$ then gives
$t_{n+1}\ge t_n$.  Thus $t_n$ increases and is bounded, so it converges; its
limit must be the fixed point $T$.  If $b>1/\e$, no fixed point exists, so a
finite limit is impossible.

\emph{Case 2: $b<0$.}
Put $c=-b>0$, so $g(x)=\e^{-cx}$ is decreasing.  There is a unique fixed point
$T\in(0,1)$ because
\[
 c=\frac{-\log T}{T}
\]
and the right side decreases continuously from $+\infty$ to $0$ on $(0,1)$.
The condition $c\le\e$ is equivalent to $T\ge1/\e$.

Assume first $c\le\e$.  Since $t_0=1>T$, decreasingness gives
$t_1=g(t_0)<g(T)=T$.  It follows inductively that the even subsequence lies
above $T$ and the odd subsequence below $T$.  Moreover, $t_2<t_0$ and
$t_3>t_1$; because $g$ reverses inequalities, the even subsequence decreases
and the odd subsequence increases.  They therefore have limits $q\ge T$ and
$p\le T$, satisfying
\begin{equation}
 p=\e^{-cq},\qquad q=\e^{-cp}.                         \label{eq:two-cycle-equations}
\end{equation}
We show that $p=q$ whenever $c\le\e$.  Suppose instead $0<p<q$ and write
$r=q/p>1$, $s=\log r>0$.  Taking logarithms in
\eqref{eq:two-cycle-equations} gives
\[
 s=c(q-p)=cp(r-1),
 \qquad
 \log p=-cpr=-\frac{rs}{r-1}.
\]
Thus
\begin{equation}
 c=\frac{s}{\e^s-1}
    \exp\!\left(\frac{s\e^s}{\e^s-1}\right).         \label{eq:c-two-cycle}
\end{equation}
Let $H(s)$ be the logarithm of the right side divided by $\e$:
\[
 H(s)=\log\frac{s}{\e^s-1}
      +\frac{s\e^s}{\e^s-1}-1.
\]
A calculation gives
\[
 H'(s)=\frac{(\e^s-1)^2-s^2\e^s}
              {s(\e^s-1)^2}>0,
\]
because
$\e^s-1=2\e^{s/2}\sinh(s/2)>s\e^{s/2}$ for $s>0$.
Also $H(s)\to0$ as $s\to0^+$.  Hence $H(s)>0$, so
\eqref{eq:c-two-cycle} implies $c>\e$, a contradiction.  Therefore $p=q=T$
and the entire sequence converges.

Finally suppose $c>\e$.  Then the fixed point has $T<1/\e$, so
\[
 |g'(T)|=cT=-\log T>1.
\]
If $t_n$ converged to $T$, the mean value theorem would give
\[
 \frac{|t_{n+1}-T|}{|t_n-T|}=|g'(\xi_n)|\longrightarrow|g'(T)|>1
\]
for points $\xi_n$ between $t_n$ and $T$.  Since $t_0\ne T$, this eventually
forces the errors to grow rather than tend to zero, a contradiction.
Thus convergence occurs exactly for $c\le\e$, that is,
$a=\e^{-c}\ge\e^{-\e}$.

Combining the two cases gives \eqref{eq:tower-interval}.  The convergent fixed
point has $-bT\ge-1$, so it is represented by $W_0$ in
\eqref{eq:tower-limit}; for $b>0$ the second value from $W_{-1}$ corresponds
to the larger unstable fixed point.
\end{proof}

\subsection{A Wien-type transcendental equation}

Many spectral maxima reduce to an equation of the form
\begin{equation}
  m(1-\e^{-x})=x.                                      \label{eq:wien-equation}
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Branch roles in the Wien equation]
\label{prop:wien}
For $m>0$, every solution of \eqref{eq:wien-equation} is
\begin{equation}
  x=m+W_k(-m\e^{-m}).                                  \label{eq:wien-solution}
\end{equation}
If $m>1$, $W_{-1}$ gives the trivial root $x=0$, while $W_0$ gives the unique
positive root.  If $0<m<1$, $W_0$ gives $x=0$ and the other real branch gives
a negative root.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Equation \eqref{eq:wien-equation} is equivalent to
$m-x=m\e^{-x}$.  Put $y=x-m$.  Then
\[
 -y=m\e^{-m-y},
 \qquad
 y\e^y=-m\e^{-m},
\]
which proves \eqref{eq:wien-solution}.  Since
$(-m)\e^{-m}=-m\e^{-m}$, the branch whose range contains $-m$ gives
$W_k(-m\e^{-m})=-m$ and hence $x=0$.  For $m>1$, $-m<-1$, so this is
$W_{-1}$; the principal value lies in $(-1,0)$ and yields
$x>m-1>0$.  For $0<m<1$, $-m\in(-1,0)$ belongs to the principal range, so
$W_0$ gives the zero root; the lower value is less than $-1$ and yields a
negative root.  Uniqueness in each range follows from the two-branch theorem.
\end{proof}

This example is a model branch-selection lesson: blindly using $W_0$ can
return either the physically interesting root or a trivial root, depending on
the parameter.

\subsection{Delay differential equations}

Consider the linear delay equation
\begin{equation}
  y'(t)=a\,y(t-\tau),\qquad \tau>0.                    \label{eq:delay}
\end{equation}
Seek exponential modes $y(t)=\e^{\lambda t}$.  Substitution gives
\[
  \lambda\e^{\lambda t}=a\e^{\lambda(t-\tau)},
\]
so
\begin{equation}
  \lambda\e^{\lambda\tau}=a,
  \qquad
  \lambda_k=\frac1\tau W_k(a\tau).                   \label{eq:delay-modes}
\end{equation}
Every complex branch contributes a characteristic exponent.  Stability is
controlled by the real parts of this infinite family, illustrating why the
complex branches are not optional in delay problems.

\begin{proposition}
Every exponential solution of \eqref{eq:delay} is of the form
$C\exp(\lambda_k t)$ with \eqref{eq:delay-modes}; conversely every such mode
solves the equation.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The preceding substitution proves necessity.  Conversely, if
$\lambda_k\tau\exp(\lambda_k\tau)=a\tau$, then
$\lambda_k=a\exp(-\lambda_k\tau)$.  Therefore
\[
 \frac{\dd}{\dd t}\e^{\lambda_k t}
 =\lambda_k\e^{\lambda_k t}
 =a\e^{\lambda_k(t-\tau)},
\]
which is \eqref{eq:delay}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Michaelis--Menten depletion kinetics}

Let a substrate concentration $S(t)>0$ satisfy
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd S}{\dd t}=-\frac{V S}{K+S},
  \qquad S(0)=S_0>0,                                  \label{eq:mm-ode}
\end{equation}
where $V,K>0$.

\begin{theorem}[Closed time course]
\label{thm:mm}
The solution of \eqref{eq:mm-ode} is
\begin{equation}
 S(t)=K W_0\!\left(
   \frac{S_0}{K}\exp\!\left(\frac{S_0}{K}-\frac{Vt}{K}\right)
 \right).                                              \label{eq:mm-solution}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Separate variables:
\[
 \left(1+\frac KS\right)\dd S=-V\dd t.
\]
Integration and the initial condition give
\[
 S+K\log S=S_0+K\log S_0-Vt.
\]
Put $y=S/K$ and rearrange:
\[
 y+\log y=\frac{S_0}{K}+\log\frac{S_0}{K}-\frac{Vt}{K}.
\]
Exponentiating gives
\[
 y\e^y=\frac{S_0}{K}
       \exp\!\left(\frac{S_0}{K}-\frac{Vt}{K}\right)>0.
\]
The argument is positive, so the only real branch is $W_0$.  Multiplication by
$K$ proves \eqref{eq:mm-solution}.  Reversing the calculation verifies that it
satisfies the differential equation and initial value.
\end{proof}

This explicit solution is one reason Lambert $W$ is useful in biochemical
kinetics \cite{Golicnik2012}: it can be differentiated with respect to
parameters, integrated, and fitted without an implicit root solve at every
time point.

\subsection{A diode with series resistance}

A standard generalized diode law is
\begin{equation}
 I=I_s\left[
  \exp\!\left(\frac{V-RI}{nV_T}\right)-1
 \right],                                              \label{eq:diode}
\end{equation}
where $I_s,R,n,V_T>0$.

\begin{theorem}[Explicit diode current]
\label{thm:diode}
Equation \eqref{eq:diode} has the physical real solution
\begin{equation}
 I=\frac{nV_T}{R}W_0\!\left(
   \frac{RI_s}{nV_T}
   \exp\!\left(\frac{V+RI_s}{nV_T}\right)
 \right)-I_s.                                          \label{eq:diode-solution}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Set $J=I+I_s$.  Then \eqref{eq:diode} becomes
\[
 J=I_s\exp\!\left(\frac{V-R(J-I_s)}{nV_T}\right)
  =I_s\exp\!\left(\frac{V+RI_s}{nV_T}\right)
       \exp\!\left(-\frac{RJ}{nV_T}\right).
\]
Therefore
\[
 \left(\frac{RJ}{nV_T}\right)
 \exp\!\left(\frac{RJ}{nV_T}\right)
 =\frac{RI_s}{nV_T}
  \exp\!\left(\frac{V+RI_s}{nV_T}\right).
\]
The right side is positive, so only $W_0$ is real.  Solve for $J$ and subtract
$I_s$ to obtain \eqref{eq:diode-solution}.
\end{proof}

This closed form, used in transistor and diode circuit analysis
\cite{Banwell2000}, smoothly connects the exponential low-current region to
the resistance-dominated high-current region.

\subsection{Asymptotic inversion in prime-number estimates}

The prime number theorem suggests the rough relation
\begin{equation}
  n\approx\frac{x}{\log x}                             \label{eq:pnt-inversion-model}
\end{equation}
when $x$ is near the $n$th prime.  Solving this model exactly requires the
lower real branch.

\begin{proposition}[Lambert proxy for inverse logarithmic growth]
\label{prop:prime-proxy}
For $n>\e$, the solution $x>\e$ of
$n=x/\log x$ is
\begin{equation}
  x=-nW_{-1}(-1/n).                                    \label{eq:prime-proxy}
\end{equation}
As $n\to\infty$,
\begin{equation}
 -nW_{-1}(-1/n)
 =n\left(\log n+\log\log n
   +\frac{\log\log n}{\log n}+\cdots\right).          \label{eq:prime-proxy-expansion}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Put $y=\log x>1$, so $x=\e^y$.  Equation
$n=\e^y/y$ becomes
\[
 (-y)\e^{-y}=-\frac1n.
\]
Because $-y<-1$, the correct branch is $W_{-1}$:
$-y=W_{-1}(-1/n)$.  Hence
\[
 x=\e^y=\e^{-W_{-1}(-1/n)}.
\]
Using $W\e^W=-1/n$ gives $\e^{-W}=-nW$, proving
\eqref{eq:prime-proxy}.  Formula \eqref{eq:prime-proxy-expansion} follows by
substituting $L_1=-\log n$ and $L_2=\log\log n$ into
\cref{thm:unified-asymptotic} and multiplying by $-n$.
\end{proof}

This is a proxy, not an exact formula for primes.  It correctly captures the
first two growing terms in the scale of the $n$th prime; refined number-theory
approximations supply additional constants and corrections.  The branch
choice is nevertheless exact and informative: $W_0(-1/n)$ would correspond
to a small solution with $\log x<1$, not the large inverse needed in prime
asymptotics \cite{Visser2018}.

\subsection{A general modeling lesson}

In each example the decisive step was not ``apply $W$'' but rather:
\begin{enumerate}
\item identify a transformed variable $u$;
\item reach $u\e^u=z$ by reversible algebra;
\item determine the admissible range of $u$;
\item select $W_0$, $W_{-1}$, or a complex $W_k$ from that range.
\end{enumerate}
This branch-first viewpoint is especially important in physical and biological
models, where one branch can represent a trivial, unstable, negative, or
otherwise inadmissible solution.

\section{Further analytic consequences}
\label{sec:further}

This section collects results that follow systematically from the preceding
machinery and are useful when Lambert $W$ appears inside a larger calculation.

\subsection{Taylor expansion about an arbitrary regular point}

Let $x_0=w_0\e^{w_0}$ with $w_0\ne-1$.  The branch through $(x_0,w_0)$ is
analytic, so \cref{thm:higher-derivatives} immediately gives all local
coefficients.

\begin{proposition}[Regular-point Taylor series]
\label{prop:regular-taylor}
For $h$ sufficiently small,
\begin{align}
 W(x_0+h)={}&w_0
 +\frac{\e^{-w_0}}{1+w_0}h
 -\frac{\e^{-2w_0}(w_0+2)}{2(1+w_0)^3}h^2\notag\\
 &+\frac{\e^{-3w_0}(2w_0^2+8w_0+9)}{6(1+w_0)^5}h^3
 +\mathcal O(h^4),                                    \label{eq:regular-taylor}
\end{align}
and in general
\begin{equation}
 [h^n]W(x_0+h)
 =\frac{\e^{-nw_0}P_n(w_0)}{n!(1+w_0)^{2n-1}}.        \label{eq:regular-taylor-general}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Taylor's theorem gives
$W(x_0+h)=\sum_{n\ge0}W^{(n)}(x_0)h^n/n!$.  Substitute
\eqref{eq:nth-derivative}.  The first three displayed terms use
$P_1=1$, $P_2=-(w+2)$, and $P_3=2w^2+8w+9$.
\end{proof}

This form is often preferable to expanding at $0$.  If many values are needed
near a known $x_0$, one high-accuracy evaluation of $w_0$ supplies a local
polynomial whose coefficients require only arithmetic.

\subsection{Sensitivity to parameters}

Suppose a model produces
\begin{equation}
  w(\theta)\e^{w(\theta)}=q(\theta).                  \label{eq:parametric-W}
\end{equation}
Then $w(\theta)=W_k(q(\theta))$ on a fixed branch.

\begin{proposition}[First parameter derivative]
\label{prop:parameter-derivative}
If $q(\theta)\ne0$ and $w(\theta)\ne-1$, then
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\dd w}{\dd\theta}
 =\frac{w}{q(1+w)}\frac{\dd q}{\dd\theta}.            \label{eq:parameter-derivative}
\end{equation}
If $q(\theta_0)=0$ on $W_0$, the continuous limiting formula is
$w'(\theta_0)=q'(\theta_0)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Apply the chain rule to $w=W_k(q)$ and use
\eqref{eq:first-derivative}.  At a principal-branch zero,
$W_0'(0)=1$ by \cref{thm:derivative}.
\end{proof}

For a vector of parameters, the same formula gives the gradient
\[
  \nabla_{\theta}w
  =\frac{w}{q(1+w)}\nabla_{\theta}q.
\]
The common factor exhibits the branch-point sensitivity independently of the
application: all parameter derivatives inherit the factor $(1+w)^{-1}$.

\subsection{A family of elementary antiderivatives}

The substitution $x=w\e^w$ makes many integrals elementary, not only the ones
in \cref{thm:antiderivatives}.  For $a\ne0$ and an integer $n\ge0$, define
\begin{equation}
 E_n(a,w)=\e^{aw}\sum_{j=0}^{n}
   \frac{(-1)^j n!}{(n-j)!a^{j+1}}w^{n-j}.            \label{eq:E-n}
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}
\label{lem:E-n}
One has
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial}{\partial w}E_n(a,w)=w^n\e^{aw}.     \label{eq:E-n-derivative}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Differentiate \eqref{eq:E-n}.  Multiplication of the sum by $a$ produces
$w^n$ plus terms that cancel, term by term, against the derivative of the
powers $w^{n-j}$.  Explicitly, the derivative of the $j$th power term for
$j<n$ is
\[
 \e^{aw}\frac{(-1)^j n!}{(n-j-1)!a^{j+1}}w^{n-j-1},
\]
which cancels the exponential derivative of the $(j+1)$st term.  Only the
$j=0$ exponential derivative leaves $w^n\e^{aw}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Polynomial moments of a branch]
\label{thm:W-polynomial-moments}
Let $m,r$ be nonnegative integers.  On any differentiable branch interval,
\begin{equation}
 \int x^m W(x)^r\dd x
 =E_{m+r}(m+1,W(x))+E_{m+r+1}(m+1,W(x))+C.             \label{eq:W-moment-integral}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Set $w=W(x)$.  Then
$x^m=w^m\e^{mw}$ and
$\dd x=\e^w(1+w)\dd w$, so
\begin{align*}
 \int x^mW(x)^r\dd x
 &=\int w^{m+r}(1+w)\e^{(m+1)w}\dd w\\
 &=\int\left(w^{m+r}+w^{m+r+1}\right)\e^{(m+1)w}\dd w.
\end{align*}
Apply \cref{lem:E-n} to the two terms.
\end{proof}

Thus every integral of a nonnegative integer power of $x$ times a
nonnegative integer power of $W$ has an elementary expression in
$W(x)$ and $\e^{W(x)}$.  For noninteger powers the same substitution leads to
incomplete gamma functions rather than finite polynomials.

\subsection{Implicit differentiation without explicitly evaluating \texorpdfstring{$W$}{W}}

Sometimes one wants a derivative of the solution but not the solution's
closed form.  If $w$ is defined by $w\e^w=x$, then
\[
 \frac{\dd x}{\dd w}=\e^w(1+w).
\]
This gives, for any differentiable function $H$,
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\dd}{\dd x}H(W(x))
 =\frac{H'(W(x))\e^{-W(x)}}{1+W(x)}.                  \label{eq:composition-derivative}
\end{equation}
The formula is often simpler than first differentiating a complicated
expression in which $W$ appears repeatedly.

\begin{example}
Let
\[
  Q(x)=\frac{W(x)^2}{1+W(x)}.
\]
Differentiating with respect to $w$ first gives
\[
  \frac{\dd}{\dd w}\frac{w^2}{1+w}
  =\frac{w(w+2)}{(1+w)^2}.
\]
Therefore
\[
 Q'(x)=\e^{-W(x)}\frac{W(x)(W(x)+2)}{(1+W(x))^3}.
\]
This route avoids a longer quotient-rule calculation involving $W'(x)$.
\end{example}

\subsection{Branch values as coordinates}

The substitution
\begin{equation}
  x=w\e^w                                             \label{eq:w-coordinate}
\end{equation}
parameterizes each real branch without numerical inversion:
\begin{itemize}
\item $w\in[-1,\infty)$ traces the graph of $W_0$;
\item $w\in(-\infty,-1]$ traces the graph of $W_{-1}$.
\end{itemize}
This is useful for plotting, quadrature, and testing inequalities.  For
example, a proposed principal-branch inequality
$A(x)\le W_0(x)\le B(x)$ can be transformed into
\[
 A(w\e^w)\le w\le B(w\e^w),\qquad w\ge-1,
\]
which may be an ordinary one-variable inequality with no inverse function
left in it.

\section{Perspective and further directions}
\label{sec:perspective}

The Lambert $W$ function sits at a productive boundary between elementary and
special functions.  Its defining map is elementary, yet its inverse has
branches, a square-root critical point, logarithmic singularities, nontrivial
asymptotics, and rich combinatorial coefficients.

Several themes recur throughout the article.

\paragraph{One geometry, two real branches.}
The principal branch and the lower branch are not unrelated formulas.  They
are the two inverse pieces of the same real curve.  At $-1/\e$ they share one
signed Puiseux series; at their large-magnitude ends they share one logarithmic
asymptotic expansion.  Their differences---monotonicity, concavity, endpoint
behavior, and branch selection---follow from which side of $w=-1$ is used.

\paragraph{Representations should be matched to regimes.}
The Taylor series is natural near $0$ on $W_0$, the signed-$p$ series near the
branch point, the logarithmic series for large $|W|$, and safeguarded
iteration in the broad middle.  No honest numerical implementation should
expect one truncated formula to be uniformly excellent on both branches and
all endpoints.

\paragraph{Bounds are algorithmic objects.}
The inequalities in \cref{sec:bounds} are not only estimates.  They create
branch-safe starting intervals, prove monotone convergence, and separate
intrinsic conditioning from algorithmic failure.  Modern guaranteed
evaluation methods refine this idea with interval arithmetic and carefully
chosen recursions \cite{Loczi2022}.

\paragraph{High-order inverse series remain an active tool.}
The general correction formula \eqref{eq:schroeder-series} turns any suitable
approximation into a hierarchy of higher-order corrections.  Recent work
studies how to choose initial functions and organize these coefficients for
both real branches \cite{Howard2025}.  Continued fractions, rational
approximants, Chebyshev expansions, and verified complex arithmetic provide
complementary approaches \cite{Barry2000,IaconoBoyd2017,Corcino2018}.

\paragraph{Generalized Lambert functions.}
Equations with several linear factors, such as
\[
  \e^x\frac{(x-t_1)\cdots(x-t_p)}{(x-s_1)\cdots(x-s_q)}=a,
\]
lead to generalized Lambert functions.  Their branch geometry is richer, but
the same strategy persists: analyze the forward map, locate critical values,
construct local inverse series, and use logarithmic asymptotics at large
solutions.  The ordinary $W$ function is the basic model on which that wider
theory is built.

\paragraph{A practical final rule.}
When Lambert $W$ appears in a calculation, always record the transformed
variable's admissible range before simplifying.  The line
\[
  u=W_k(z)
\]
is incomplete until the branch $k$ is justified.  On the real axis, most
serious mistakes involving $W$ are branch mistakes rather than algebra
mistakes.

\section{A compact decision map for numerical evaluation}
\label{app:numerical-map}

For a target precision of ordinary floating-point size, the following regime
map is effective.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Exact endpoints.}
Return $W_0(0)=0$ and $W_0(-1/\e)=W_{-1}(-1/\e)=-1$ exactly when the input
representation permits.

\item \textbf{Near $-1/\e$.}
Compute $\eta=1+\e x$ carefully and use the signed-$p$ series.  This avoids
dividing by the small derivative of the forward map.

\item \textbf{Near $0$ on $W_0$.}
Use the Taylor series or the $[2/2]$ Pad\'e starter, followed by ordinary
Newton or Halley.

\item \textbf{Large positive $x$.}
Use $\log x-\log\log x$ or \eqref{eq:iacono-positive}, followed by logarithmic
Newton.  Work with $\log x$ directly when $x$ itself would overflow; this is
the Wright omega formulation.

\item \textbf{Near $0^-$ on $W_{-1}$.}
Use the logarithmic asymptotic expansion with
$L_1=\log(-x)$ and $L_2=\log(-L_1)$, followed by logarithmic Newton.

\item \textbf{Guaranteed fallback.}
Use the brackets in \cref{cor:w0-positive-bracket,thm:wm1-bounds} and the
safeguarded algorithm \ref{alg:safeguarded}.
\end{enumerate}

A production implementation should also distinguish input error from
iteration error.  Near $-1/\e$, the condition number in
\eqref{eq:condition} can make a perfectly converged result contain few correct
digits if the input itself was rounded too coarsely.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=bash]
python lambert_w_experiments.py --output-dir .

latexmk -pdf -interaction=nonstopmode -halt-on-error lambert_w_article.tex

separate Python function, making it straightforward to change grids or add a
new candidate formula.

\section{Bibliographic notes on the supplied source archive}
\label{app:source-notes}

The foundational modern reference is Corless et al.\ \cite{Corless1996}, which
systematized the branch notation, complex asymptotics, symbolic integration,
and numerical evaluation.  The DLMF entry \cite{DLMF} gives a concise
reference for standard branch conventions and expansions.

The numerical and approximation literature represented in the supplied
archive includes the piecewise real approximations of Barry et al.
\cite{Barry2000}, the global principal-branch approximations and logarithmic
Newton iteration of Iacono and Boyd \cite{IaconoBoyd2017}, the guaranteed
high-precision analysis of L\'oczi \cite{Loczi2022}, and Howard's
Schröder-type expansions \cite{Howard2025}.  The lower-branch enclosure proved
in \cref{thm:wm1-bounds} is based on the inequalities of Chatzigeorgiou
\cite{Chatzigeorgiou2013}.  Corcino, Corcino, and Mez\H{o}
\cite{Corcino2018} study integral and continued-fraction representations.

The application papers in the archive illustrate why explicit branch-aware
forms matter: Banwell treats transistor and diode circuits
\cite{Banwell2000}; Goli\v{c}nik treats biochemical kinetics
\cite{Golicnik2012}; Lehtonen surveys ecological and evolutionary models
\cite{Lehtonen2016}; and Visser discusses prime-number approximations
\cite{Visser2018}.  These applications motivated several examples in
\cref{sec:applications}, but all derivations in this article have been carried
out independently.
