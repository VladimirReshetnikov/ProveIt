\begin{abstract}
This report develops an integral calculus for the Fabius function \(\F\), its inverse
\(\Q=\F^{-1}\), and Rvachev's compactly supported function \(\upf\).  The starting
point is the probability model
\[
  Y=\sum_{k\ge1}2^{-k}U_k,\qquad Z=2Y-1,
\]
where the \(U_k\) are independent uniform random variables on \([0,1]\), \(\F\) is
the distribution function of \(Y\), and \(\upf\) is the density of \(Z\).  This model
turns integration into a combination of quantile transport, moment calculus, and
infinite-product transforms.

The principal results are: a two-function inverse-pair integration identity containing
all weighted area formulas for \(\F\) and \(\Q\); a bivariate Laplace--Fourier duality;
an entire complex moment function \(\M(s)=\E Y^s=\int_0^1\Q(v)^s\dd v\) with two
rapidly convergent Newton-type series; exact series for negative and fractional powers;
a stable recurrence for negative moments and a positive normalized limit
\(C_*\), giving
\[
  \M(-r)\sim C_*\frac{2^{r(r+1)/2}}{\Gamma(r)};
\]
Riemann--Liouville primitives of arbitrary order for powers of \(\F\) and \(\Q\);
beta-weighted formulas, Bernoulli-polynomial integrals, order-statistic representations,
Cauchy transforms, and Gini/autocorrelation integrals expressed through the infinite
sinc product.  Numerical experiments are supplied in a reproducible Python script.

A post-snapshot Lean audit now provides the generic continuous-linear naturality and
conditional operator/scalar affine-recursion substrate for weighted uniform laws, together
with the named geometric weights \((1-q)q^n\), their probability law, self-similarity,
reflection, carried-unit-interval results, and the half-base bridges.  This does not
formalize the report's inverse-pair, fractional-power, complex-Mellin, density, transform,
moment, or fixed-point uniqueness consequences.

Here ``new'' means absent from the recursively audited LaTeX corpus of the referenced
ProveIt directory at the time of writing.  It is not a claim of worldwide priority.
Each statement is labelled in context as proved, computational, or conjectural.
\end{abstract}

\subsection{Conventions}

All powers of positive real variables use the real logarithm:
\(x^s=\exp(s\log x)\).  The totalized Fabius distribution function is
\[
 \F(x)=0\quad(x\le0),\qquad \F(x)=1\quad(x\ge1).
\]
Its inverse \(\Q:(0,1)\to(0,1)\) is the ordinary increasing inverse; endpoint values
are understood by continuity.  Angular frequency is used for Fourier transforms:
\(\widehat f(t)=\int_\R e^{itx}f(x)\dd x\).

\section{Probability model and transform baseline}

\subsection{The dyadic uniform sum}

Let \(U_1,U_2,\ldots\) be independent random variables uniformly distributed on
\([0,1]\), and put
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Ydef}
 Y=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{U_k}{2^k},
 \qquad
 Z=2Y-1=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{2U_k-1}{2^k}.
\end{equation}
Then \(Y\in[0,1]\), \(Z\in[-1,1]\), and the laws are symmetric:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:symmetry-laws}
 Y\overset d=1-Y,
 \qquad Z\overset d=-Z.
\end{equation}
The Fabius function is the distribution function of \(Y\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Fdist}
 \F(x)=\Prob(Y\le x),
\end{equation}
and \(\upf\) is the density of \(Z\).  Thus
\begin{equation}\label{eq:F-up-relations}
 \F'(x)=2\upf(2x-1),\qquad
 \upf(x)=
 \begin{cases}
   \F(x+1),&-1\le x\le0,\\
   \F(1-x)=1-\F(x),&0\le x\le1,\\
   0,&|x|>1.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
In particular \(\F(x)+\F(1-x)=1\) on \([0,1]\), and the quantile obeys
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Qsymmetry}
 \Q(1-v)=1-\Q(v).
\end{equation}

The self-decomposition
\begin{equation}\label{eq:smoothing}
 Y\overset d=\frac{U+Y'}2
\end{equation}
holds with \(U\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]\) independent of a copy \(Y'\) of \(Y\).
It will generate both the positive- and negative-moment recurrences.

\subsection{Moment-generating and Fourier products}

Define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:P-U-products}
 P(t)=\E e^{tY},\qquad \mathcal U(t)=\E e^{tZ}.
\end{equation}
Independence in \cref{eq:Ydef} gives the locally uniformly convergent products
\begin{align}
 P(t)&=\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}
 \frac{e^{t/2^k}-1}{t/2^k},\label{eq:P-product}\\
 \mathcal U(t)&=\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}
 \frac{\sinh(t/2^k)}{t/2^k},\label{eq:U-product}\\
 P(t)&=e^{t/2}\mathcal U(t/2).\label{eq:P-U-relation}
\end{align}
The characteristic function of the up law is therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:phi-product}
 \phi(t)=\widehat{\upf}(t)
 =\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sin(t/2^k)}{t/2^k}.
\end{equation}
The scaling equation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:P-pantograph}
 P(2t)=\frac{e^t-1}{t}P(t)
\end{equation}
is the exponential generating-function form of the moment recurrence below.

\subsection{Rational moments, cumulants, and Bell polynomials}

Write
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mn-mun}
 m_n=\E Y^n=\int_0^1\Q(v)^n\dd v,
 \qquad
 \mu_n=\E Z^n=\int_{-1}^1x^n\upf(x)\dd x.
\end{equation}
Taking the \(n\)-th moment in \cref{eq:smoothing} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:moment-recurrence}
 (2^n-1)m_n
 =\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\binom nk\frac{m_k}{n-k+1},
 \qquad m_0=1.
\end{equation}
Thus every \(m_n\) is rational.  The first values are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:first-moments}
 1,\ \frac12,\ \frac5{18},\ \frac16,\ \frac{143}{1350},\
 \frac{19}{270},\ \frac{1153}{23814},\ \frac{583}{17010},\ldots.
\end{equation}

The logarithm of \cref{eq:U-product} and
\(
 \log(\sinh z/z)
 =\sum_{r\ge1}2^{2r-1}B_{2r}z^{2r}/(r(2r)!)
\)
give the cumulants
\begin{align}
 \kappa_1(Y)&=\frac12,\nonumber\\
 \kappa_{2r}(Y)&=\frac{B_{2r}}{2r(2^{2r}-1)},
 &\kappa_{2r+1}(Y)&=0\quad(r\ge1),\label{eq:Y-cumulants}\\
 \kappa_{2r}(Z)&=\frac{2^{2r}B_{2r}}{2r(2^{2r}-1)},
 &\kappa_{2r+1}(Z)&=0.\label{eq:Z-cumulants}
\end{align}
Consequently \(m_n\) and \(\mu_n\) are complete exponential Bell polynomials in
these cumulants.  A useful bridge between the one-sided and centered moments is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mu-m-relation}
 \mu_{2j}
 =2\int_0^1x^{2j}\upf(x)\dd x
 =\frac{2m_{2j+1}}{2j+1},
 \qquad \mu_{2j+1}=0.
\end{equation}
For example
\begin{equation}\label{eq:first-central-moments}
 \mu_2=\frac19,\qquad
 \mu_4=\frac{19}{675},\qquad
 \mu_6=\frac{583}{59535}.
\end{equation}

\section{A universal integration calculus for \texorpdfstring{\(\F\)}{F} and
\texorpdfstring{\(\Q\)}{Q}}

The classical area identity for a function and its inverse is only the first member of
a much larger family.  The following theorem is the main algebraic engine of the report.

\begin{statusbox}[Exact boundary of the formal quantile theorems]
The foundational measure-transport theorem is support-free.  Let \(\mu\) be any
probability measure on \(\R\), and let \(Q\) be almost-everywhere measurable for uniform
measure on \([0,1]\).  If, for every \(x\in\R\),
\[
 Q(y)\le x\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad y\le\operatorname{cdf}_{\mu}(x)
 \quad\text{for almost every \(y\in[0,1]\)},
\]
then \(Q_*\lambda_{[0,1]}=\mu\).  There is no support assumption on \(\mu\) and no
requirement that \(Q\) take its source into \([0,1]\).  A compact specialization replaces
the almost-everywhere sublevel hypothesis by a pointwise order characterization on the
unit square, together with a.e. support and interval preservation.  Both theorems permit
atoms.

The Fabius specialization uses the globally clamped inverse but restricts the uniform
source to \([0,1]\); every strongly measurable Banach-valued statistic has the
corresponding integral identity,
with no integrability assumption because both Bochner integrals are totalized.

The exact declarations are
\nolinkurl{Fabius.map_quantile_eq},
\nolinkurl{Fabius.map_inverseCDF_volume_restrict_Icc},
\nolinkurl{Fabius.map_fabiusInv_restrict_Icc_eq_weightedSumDistribution}, and
\nolinkurl{Fabius.integral_comp_fabiusInv_restrict_Icc_eq_weightedSumDistribution}

\begin{theorem}[two-function inverse-pair identity]\label{thm:AB}
Let \(A,B\) be absolutely continuous on \([0,1]\).  For every \(z\in[0,1]\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:AB-incomplete}
 A(z)B(\F(z))-A(0)B(0)
 =\int_0^z A'(x)B(\F(x))\dd x
 +\int_0^{\F(z)}A(\Q(v))B'(v)\dd v.
\end{equation}
At \(z=1\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:AB-complete}
 A(1)B(1)-A(0)B(0)
 =\int_0^1A'(x)B(\F(x))\dd x
 +\int_0^1A(\Q(v))B'(v)\dd v.
\end{equation}
The formulas remain valid under the usual improper-integral hypotheses whenever an
endpoint is singular.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Differentiate the product \(A(x)B(\F(x))\), integrate from \(0\) to \(z\), and in
the term containing \(\F'(x)\) use the increasing substitution \(v=\F(x)\),
so that \(x=\Q(v)\).
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
No special functional equation of the Fabius function is needed in
\cref{thm:AB}; it is a quantile identity.  What is special here is that the moments and
transforms of this particular quantile can be calculated by dyadic products and rational
recurrences.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Power duality and logarithmic derivatives}

Taking \(A(x)=x^p\), \(B(v)=v^q\) gives the following Mellin-type duality.

\begin{corollary}[power duality]\label{cor:power-duality}
Initially for \(\Re p,\Re q>0\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:power-incomplete}
 p\int_0^z x^{p-1}\F(x)^q\dd x
 +q\int_0^{\F(z)}\Q(v)^p v^{q-1}\dd v
 =z^p\F(z)^q.
\end{equation}
In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:power-complete}
 p\int_0^1x^{p-1}\F(x)^q\dd x
 +q\int_0^1v^{q-1}\Q(v)^p\dd v=1.
\end{equation}
Both sides admit meromorphic or entire continuation in parameters whenever the endpoint
flatness cancels the apparent singularities.
\end{corollary}

For all logarithmic derivatives at once, define
\begin{align}
 J_{r,s}(p,q)&=\int_0^1x^{p-1}(\log x)^r\F(x)^q(\log\F(x))^s\dd x,\label{eq:Jrs}\\
 K_{r,s}(p,q)&=\int_0^1v^{q-1}(\log v)^s\Q(v)^p(\log\Q(v))^r\dd v.\label{eq:Krs}
\end{align}
Differentiating \cref{eq:power-complete} gives, for \(r+s>0\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:log-hierarchy}
 pJ_{r,s}+rJ_{r-1,s}+qK_{r,s}+sK_{r,s-1}=0,
\end{equation}
with \(pJ_{0,0}+qK_{0,0}=1\).  Equation \cref{eq:log-hierarchy} is a compact
general formula for every mixed derivative of the two Mellin kernels.

\subsection{A bivariate Laplace--Fourier identity}

The exponential choice \(A(x)=e^{sx}\), \(B(v)=e^{tv}\) produces an especially
simple transform relation.

\begin{theorem}[bivariate inverse-pair transform]\label{thm:bivariate-exp}
For all \(s,t\in\C\) and \(z\in[0,1]\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bivariate-incomplete}
 s\int_0^z e^{sx+t\F(x)}\dd x
 +t\int_0^{\F(z)}e^{tv+s\Q(v)}\dd v
 =e^{sz+t\F(z)}-1.
\end{equation}
Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bivariate-complete}
 s\int_0^1e^{sx+t\F(x)}\dd x
 +t\int_0^1e^{tv+s\Q(v)}\dd v
 =e^{s+t}-1.
\end{equation}
The identities are entire in \((s,t)\); the cases \(s=0\) or \(t=0\) are understood
by continuity.
\end{theorem}

Every mixed power integral involving \(x\) and \(\F(x)\), together with its quantile
dual, is obtained by coefficient extraction from \cref{eq:bivariate-complete}.  Purely
imaginary \(s,t\) give a bivariate Fourier identity.

\subsection{Weighted antiderivatives}

Taking \(B(v)=v\) and a primitive \(A\) of a prescribed weight yields the useful
master formula
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weighted-antiderivative-master}
 \int_0^x A'(t)\F(t)\dd t
 =A(x)\F(x)-A(0)\F(0)-\int_0^{\F(x)}A(\Q(v))\dd v.
\end{equation}
For monomial weights this becomes the promised indefinite integral.

\begin{corollary}[monomial--Fabius antiderivative]\label{cor:monomial-F}
For \(p\ne-1\), initially where the integrals converge and then by continuation,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:monomial-F-antiderivative}
 \int_0^x t^p\F(t)\dd t
 =\frac{x^{p+1}\F(x)-\displaystyle\int_0^{\F(x)}\Q(v)^{p+1}\dd v}{p+1}.
\end{equation}
The value at \(p=-1\) is removable:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:F-over-x-incomplete}
 \int_0^x\frac{\F(t)}t\dd t
 =\F(x)\log x-\int_0^{\F(x)}\log\Q(v)\dd v.
\end{equation}
On \([0,1]\), where \(\upf=1-\F\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:monomial-up-half-antiderivative}
 \int_0^x t^p\upf(t)\dd t
 =\frac{x^{p+1}(1-\F(x))+\displaystyle\int_0^{\F(x)}\Q(v)^{p+1}\dd v}{p+1}.
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

More generally, from \cref{eq:power-incomplete},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Fpower-antiderivative}
 \int_0^x t^{p-1}\F(t)^q\dd t
 =\frac{x^p\F(x)^q}{p}
 -\frac{q}{p}\int_0^{\F(x)}\Q(v)^p v^{q-1}\dd v.
\end{equation}
Thus products of \(\F\) and the positive half of \(\upf\) reduce to beta-weighted
quantile integrals after expanding \((1-\F)^n\).

\section{The entire complex moment function}

\begin{definition}\label{def:M}
For \(s\in\C\), set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Mdef}
 \M(s)=\E Y^s=\int_0^1\Q(v)^s\dd v.
\end{equation}
For nonnegative integers, \(\M(n)=m_n\).
\end{definition}

\subsection{Entire continuation}

The endpoint flatness of \(\F\) is much stronger than what is needed for ordinary
positive moments: for every \(N>0\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:F-flat-bound}
 \F(x)=O_N(x^N)\qquad(x\downarrow0).
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[entire quantile Mellin function]\label{thm:entireM}
The function \(\M\) in \cref{eq:Mdef} is entire.  For every \(r\ge0\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:M-derivatives}
 \M^{(r)}(s)=\int_0^1\Q(v)^s(\log\Q(v))^r\dd v
 =\E\bigl[Y^s(\log Y)^r\bigr].
\end{equation}
In particular all negative moments and all logarithmic moments exist.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let \(K\subset\C\) be compact and choose \(A>0\) with \(\Re s\ge-A\) on \(K\).
For every fixed \(r\), the integrand is bounded in modulus by
\(Y^{-A}|\log Y|^r\).  Integration by parts gives
\[
 \E Y^{-A}=1+A\int_0^1x^{-A-1}\F(x)\dd x<\infty,
\]
using \cref{eq:F-flat-bound} with an exponent larger than \(A\); inserting powers of
\(|\log x|\) does not alter convergence.  Dominated differentiation on compact sets
proves holomorphy and \cref{eq:M-derivatives}.
\end{proof}

A first transform triangle follows immediately:
\begin{align}
 \int_0^1x^{s-1}\F(x)\dd x&=\frac{1-\M(s)}s,
 \label{eq:Mellin-F}\\
 \int_0^1x^{s-1}\upf(x)\dd x&=\frac{\M(s)}s,
 \label{eq:Mellin-up-half}\\
 \int_0^1\Q(v)^{s-1}\dd v&=\M(s-1).
 \label{eq:Mellin-Q}
\end{align}
The first quotient has a removable singularity at \(s=0\) and is entire; the second
is the meromorphic continuation of the classical Mellin transform of \(\upf|_{[0,1]}\)
and has exactly one pole, at \(s=0\), with residue \(1\).  Evenness gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:absolute-Mellin-up}
 \int_{-1}^1|x|^{s-1}\upf(x)\dd x=\frac{2\M(s)}s.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Superpolynomial moment decay}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:moment-decay}
For every \(N>0\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:moment-decay}
 m_k=O_N(k^{-N})\qquad(k\to\infty).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For \(k>0\), the tail formula and symmetry give
\[
 m_k=k\int_0^1x^{k-1}(1-\F(x))\dd x
 =k\int_0^1x^{k-1}\F(1-x)\dd x.
\]
By \cref{eq:F-flat-bound}, \(\F(1-x)\le C_N(1-x)^N\), hence
\[
 m_k\le C_N kB(k,N+1)=O(k^{-N}).
\]
\end{proof}

\subsection{Two globally convergent Newton series}

\begin{theorem}[Newton and central-even series]\label{thm:newton}
Both of the following series converge locally uniformly for all \(s\in\C\):
\begin{align}
 \M(s)&=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^k\binom{s}{k}m_k,
 \label{eq:M-Newton}\\
 \M(s)&=2^{-s}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\binom{s}{2j}\mu_{2j}
 =2^{-s}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\binom{s}{2j}\frac{2m_{2j+1}}{2j+1}.
 \label{eq:M-central}
\end{align}
They therefore represent the entire function \(\M\) on the whole plane.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For \(0<Y<1\), expand
\(Y^s=(1-(1-Y))^s\).  Taking expectations and using
\(1-Y\overset d=Y\) gives \cref{eq:M-Newton}.  On every compact set in \(s\), the
binomial coefficients have at most polynomial growth in \(k\); \cref{lem:moment-decay}
therefore justifies absolute, locally uniform convergence and interchange of sum and
expectation.

For \cref{eq:M-central}, write \(Y=(1+Z)/2\), expand \((1+Z)^s\), and use the
evenness of the law of \(Z\).  The relation \cref{eq:mu-m-relation} and
\cref{lem:moment-decay} again give locally uniform convergence.
\end{proof}

Several useful specializations are immediate.  For an integer \(r\ge1\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:negative-moment-rational-series}
 \M(-r)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\binom{r+k-1}{k}m_k.
\end{equation}
Every term is rational and positive.  In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Mminus1-series}
 \M(-1)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}m_k.
\end{equation}
Differentiation of \cref{eq:M-Newton} at the origin yields logarithmic moments in
terms of signed Stirling numbers of the first kind \(s(k,r)\):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:log-moment-Stirling}
 \M^{(r)}(0)
 =r!\sum_{k=r}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^ks(k,r)}{k!}m_k.
\end{equation}
For \(r=1\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:log-moment-constant}
 -\E\log Y
 =\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{m_k}{k}
 =\int_0^1\frac{\F(x)}x\dd x.
\end{equation}
Numerically,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:log-moment-numeric}
 -\E\log Y=0.758240000404408656\ldots.
\end{equation}

\subsection{A mixed reflected Mellin function}

The inverse symmetry \cref{eq:Qsymmetry} suggests the two-variable transform
\begin{equation}\label{eq:M2def}
 \M_2(a,b)=\int_0^1\Q(v)^a\Q(1-v)^b\dd v
 =\E\bigl[Y^a(1-Y)^b\bigr].
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:M2}
The function \(\M_2\) is entire on \(\C^2\), symmetric in \(a,b\), and has the
locally uniformly convergent expansion
\begin{equation}\label{eq:M2-series}
 \M_2(a,b)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^k\binom bk\M(a+k).
\end{equation}
It also has the central expansion
\begin{equation}\label{eq:M2-central}
 \M_2(a,b)=2^{-a-b}
 \sum_{j,k\ge0}(-1)^k\binom aj\binom bk\mu_{j+k}.
\end{equation}
If either parameter is a nonnegative integer, the corresponding binomial expansion
terminates.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Endpoint flatness at both \(0\) and \(1\) gives local domination for arbitrary negative
real parts, proving entire dependence on two parameters.  Expand \((1-Y)^b\) to obtain
\cref{eq:M2-series}; superpolynomial decay of shifted moments gives local uniformity.
The central formula follows from \(Y=(1+Z)/2\), \(1-Y=(1-Z)/2\).
\end{proof}

For example,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:M2-examples}
 \int_0^1\Q(v)\Q(1-v)\dd v=\frac29,
 \qquad
 \int_0^1\Q(v)^2\Q(1-v)^2\dd v=\frac{34}{675},
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:M2-negative-example}
 \int_0^1\frac{\dd v}{\Q(v)(1-\Q(v))}=2\M(-1).
\end{equation}

\section{Negative moments: an exact recurrence and a new asymptotic constant}

The Newton series \cref{eq:negative-moment-rational-series} is effective for moderate
negative orders.  A second construction is much more stable for large orders and reveals
a new normalization law.

\subsection{The recurrence}

For \(s>0\), define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:A-D-def}
 A(s)=\M(-s)=\E Y^{-s},
 \qquad
 D(s)=\E(1+Y)^{-s}.
\end{equation}
Conditioning in \cref{eq:smoothing} and integrating the uniform variable gives the
following exact relation.

\begin{theorem}[negative-moment recurrence]\label{thm:negative-recurrence}
For every real \(s>0\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:negative-recurrence}
 A(s+1)=\frac{2^{s+1}}s\bigl(A(s)-D(s)\bigr).
\end{equation}
Equivalently, for \(s>1\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:negative-recurrence-shifted}
 A(s)=\frac{2^s}{s-1}\bigl(A(s-1)-D(s-1)\bigr).
\end{equation}
Moreover \(D(s)\) has the rapidly convergent centered series
\begin{equation}\label{eq:D-centered-series}
 D(s)=\left(\frac23\right)^s
 \sum_{j=0}^{\infty}
 \frac{(s)_{2j}}{(2j)!\,3^{2j}}\mu_{2j},
\end{equation}
where \((s)_n=s(s+1)\cdots(s+n-1)\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By \cref{eq:smoothing}, for \(s>0\),
\[
 A(s+1)=2^{s+1}\E\int_0^1(U+Y')^{-s-1}\dd U.
\]
For \(y>0\),
\[
 \int_0^1(u+y)^{-s-1}\dd u
 =\frac{y^{-s}-(1+y)^{-s}}s,
\]
which gives \cref{eq:negative-recurrence}.  For \cref{eq:D-centered-series}, write
\(1+Y=(3+Z)/2\), expand \((1+Z/3)^{-s}\), and use the vanishing of odd moments.
The factor \(3^{-2j}\) makes the series geometrically stable in addition to the
superpolynomial decay of \(\mu_{2j}\).
\end{proof}

The first values generated by this recurrence are
\begin{align}
 A(1)&=2.31488614400581905\ldots,\nonumber\\
 A(2)&=6.55899183845822858\ldots,\nonumber\\
 A(3)&=24.3890791927221220\ldots,\nonumber\\
 A(4)&=128.368907270620532\ldots,\nonumber\\
 A(5)&=1025.15473735960333\ldots.\label{eq:negative-values}
\end{align}

\subsection{A normalized limit and a periodic phase profile}

Define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:H-def}
 H(s)=\frac{\Gamma(s)A(s)}{2^{s(s+1)/2}},
 \qquad
 \rho(s)=\frac{D(s)}{A(s)}.
\end{equation}
Then \cref{eq:negative-recurrence} becomes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:H-recurrence}
 H(s+1)=H(s)(1-\rho(s)).
\end{equation}
Both factors are positive.  Since \(\Prob(Y\le1/2)=1/2\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rho-bound}
 A(s)\ge2^{s-1},\qquad D(s)\le1,
 \qquad 0<\rho(s)\le2^{1-s}.
\end{equation}
This elementary estimate is enough to prove convergence.

\begin{theorem}[periodic limiting profile]\label{thm:phase-limit}
For \(0<\theta\le1\), the product
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Ctheta-product}
 C(\theta)=H(\theta)
 \prod_{j=0}^{\infty}\bigl(1-\rho(\theta+j)\bigr)
\end{equation}
converges to a positive number.  It extends by \(C(\theta+1)=C(\theta)\) to a
positive, continuous, one-periodic function.  As \(n\to\infty\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:A-phase-asymptotic}
 A(n+\theta)=C(\theta)
 \frac{2^{(n+\theta)(n+\theta+1)/2}}{\Gamma(n+\theta)}
 \bigl(1+O_\theta(2^{-n})\bigr).
\end{equation}
For integer \(r\to\infty\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:integer-negative-asymptotic}
 \boxed{\displaystyle
 \M(-r)=C_*\frac{2^{r(r+1)/2}}{\Gamma(r)}
 \bigl(1+O(2^{-r})\bigr),}
 \qquad C_*=C(1)>0.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Iteration of \cref{eq:H-recurrence} gives
\[
 H(n+\theta)=H(\theta)
 \prod_{j=0}^{n-1}(1-\rho(\theta+j)).
\]
The sum of the tail bounds in \cref{eq:rho-bound} is geometric.  Hence the infinite
product converges to a positive limit, and its omitted tail differs from \(1\) by
\(O_\theta(2^{-n})\).  This proves \cref{eq:A-phase-asymptotic}.  Shifting the
initial point by one and using \cref{eq:H-recurrence} proves exact periodicity.
Uniform convergence on compact subintervals gives continuity; exact periodicity
then removes the apparent endpoint at \(\theta=0\).
The integer formula is the case \(\theta=1\).
\end{proof}

For integers the normalized sequence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Cr-def}
 C_r=\frac{(r-1)!\,\M(-r)}{2^{r(r+1)/2}}
\end{equation}
is strictly decreasing and satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Cr-rec}
 C_{r+1}=C_r\left(1-\frac{D(r)}{A(r)}\right),
 \qquad C_r\downarrow C_*.
\end{equation}
The product representation is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Cstar-product}
 C_*=\frac{\M(-1)}2
 \prod_{j=1}^{\infty}
 \left(1-\frac{D(j)}{A(j)}\right).
\end{equation}
The computation described in \cref{sec:numerics} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Cstar-numeric}
 C_*=0.750721980407870959\ldots.
\end{equation}
The rapid stabilization is visible in
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}rcccccc@{}}
\toprule
\(r\)&1&2&4&6&8&12\\
\midrule
\(C_r\)&1.15744307&0.81987398&0.75216157&0.75072879&
0.75072199&0.7507219804\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsection{Logarithmic asymptotics and entire-function growth}

Stirling's expansion in \cref{eq:integer-negative-asymptotic} gives a complete
algebraic asymptotic series:
\begin{align}
 \log\M(-r)
 ={}&\frac{\log2}{2}r^2-r\log r
 +\left(1+\frac{\log2}{2}\right)r
 +\frac12\log r
 +\log\frac{C_*}{\sqrt{2\pi}}
 \nonumber\\
 &-\frac1{12r}+\frac1{360r^3}-\frac1{1260r^5}+\cdots,
 \label{eq:log-negative-asymptotic}
\end{align}
where
\begin{equation}\label{eq:log-Cstar-numeric}
 \log\frac{C_*}{\sqrt{2\pi}}
 =-1.20565842815389633\ldots.
\end{equation}
This refines the growth one would obtain by a direct saddle-point transfer from the
Lambert--\(W\) endpoint expansion.

There is also a clean entire-function consequence.  If
\(M_{\M}(R)=\max_{|s|=R}|\M(s)|\), then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:maxmod-equality}
 |\M(s)|\le\E Y^{\Re s}\le\E Y^{-R}=\M(-R),
 \qquad |s|=R,
\end{equation}
while equality occurs at \(s=-R\).  Thus
\begin{equation}\label{eq:maxmod-exact}
 M_{\M}(R)=\M(-R).
\end{equation}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:order-type}
The entire function \(\M\) has order \(2\) and type \((\log2)/2\).
\end{corollary}

\subsection{Singular Fabius integrals}

Putting \(s=p+1\) in \cref{eq:Mellin-F} shows that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weighted-F-complete}
 \int_0^1x^p\F(x)\dd x
 =\frac{1-\M(p+1)}{p+1}
\end{equation}
is an entire function of \(p\), despite the apparent pole at \(p=-1\).  Therefore
all negative powers are integrable against \(\F\).  For integers \(k\ge2\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:singular-F-integers}
 \int_0^1\frac{\F(x)}{x^k}\dd x
 =\frac{\M(1-k)-1}{k-1}.
\end{equation}
The first examples are
\begin{align}
 \int_0^1\frac{\F(x)}{x}\dd x
 &=0.758240000404408656\ldots,\nonumber\\
 \int_0^1\frac{\F(x)}{x^2}\dd x
 &=1.31488614400581905\ldots,\nonumber\\
 \int_0^1\frac{\F(x)}{x^3}\dd x
 &=2.77949591922911429\ldots,\nonumber\\
 \int_0^1\frac{\F(x)}{x^4}\dd x
 &=7.79635973090737400\ldots.\label{eq:singular-F-values}
\end{align}
By \cref{eq:integer-negative-asymptotic},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:singular-F-asymptotic}
 \int_0^1\frac{\F(x)}{x^k}\dd x
 \sim C_*\frac{2^{k(k-1)/2}}{\Gamma(k)}.
\end{equation}
This is an asymptotic law for a natural family of increasingly singular antiderivatives.

\section{Definite weighted integrals}

\subsection{Polynomial and analytic weights}

Let \(g\) be integrable and let \(G(x)=\int_0^xg(t)\dd t\).  Taking \(A=G\),
\(B(v)=v\) in \cref{thm:AB} gives
\begin{align}
 \int_0^1g(x)\F(x)\dd x&=G(1)-\E G(Y),
 \label{eq:analytic-kernel-F}\\
 \int_0^1g(x)(1-\F(x))\dd x&=\E G(Y).
 \label{eq:analytic-kernel-up}
\end{align}
For a polynomial \(g\), every quantity on the right is a rational combination of the
moments \(m_n\).  For an entire \(g\), the same formulas give convergent series whenever
the Taylor expansion can be integrated termwise.

The monomial cases are
\begin{align}
 \int_0^1x^p\F(x)\dd x&=\frac{1-\M(p+1)}{p+1},
 \label{eq:monomial-F-definite}\\
 \int_0^1x^p\upf(x)\dd x&=\frac{\M(p+1)}{p+1}
 \qquad(\upf=1-\F\text{ on }[0,1]).
 \label{eq:monomial-up-definite}
\end{align}
For \(p\in\N\) these are rational.  For example,
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}c|cccc@{}}
\toprule
\(p\)&0&1&2&3\\
\midrule
\(\int_0^1x^p\F(x)\dd x\)&\(1/2\)&\(13/36\)&\(5/18\)&\(1207/5400\)\\
\(\int_0^1x^p\upf(x)\dd x\)&\(1/2\)&\(5/36\)&\(1/18\)&\(143/5400\)\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

For symmetric weights \(g(x)=g(1-x)\), symmetry alone gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:symmetric-weight}
 \int_0^1g(x)\F(x)\dd x=\frac12\int_0^1g(x)\dd x.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Bernoulli-polynomial weights}

Let
\begin{equation}\label{eq:beta-n-def}
 \beta_n=\E B_n(Y).
\end{equation}
Their exponential generating function follows from \cref{eq:P-product}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:beta-egf}
 \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\beta_n\frac{t^n}{n!}
 =\frac{tP(t)}{e^t-1}.
\end{equation}
Symmetry implies \(\beta_{2j+1}=0\).  Since
\(B'_{n+1}=(n+1)B_n\), \cref{eq:analytic-kernel-F} gives, for \(n\ge1\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Bernoulli-F-integral}
 \int_0^1B_n(x)\F(x)\dd x
 =-\frac{\beta_{n+1}-B_{n+1}}{n+1}.
\end{equation}
The first nontrivial expected Bernoulli values are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:beta-values}
 \beta_2=-\frac1{18},\quad
 \beta_4=\frac{23}{1350},\quad
 \beta_6=-\frac{1543}{119070},\quad
 \beta_8=\frac{1196113}{65063250}.
\end{equation}
This supplies a direct integral bridge between the Bernoulli-polynomial structure and
the Fabius distribution.

\subsection{Beta weights}

For \(\Re a,\Re b>0\), define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:IFab}
 I_F(a,b)=\int_0^1x^{a-1}(1-x)^{b-1}\F(x)\dd x.
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[beta-weighted Fabius integrals]\label{thm:beta-F}
For \(\Re a,\Re b>0\),
\begin{align}
 I_F(a,b)
 &=\E B_Y(b,a)
 =\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}
 \frac{(1-a)_n}{n!(b+n)}\M(b+n),
 \label{eq:beta-F-series}\\
 \int_0^1x^{a-1}(1-x)^{b-1}(1-\F(x))\dd x
 &=\E B_Y(a,b)
 =\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}
 \frac{(1-b)_n}{n!(a+n)}\M(a+n).
 \label{eq:beta-up-series}
\end{align}
Here \(B_y\) is the incomplete beta function.  If \(a\) in
\cref{eq:beta-F-series}, or \(b\) in \cref{eq:beta-up-series}, is a positive integer,
the corresponding series terminates.  If both parameters are positive integers, the
integrals are rational.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Use \(\F(x)=\E\ind_{\{Y\le x\}}\) and Fubini:
\[
 I_F(a,b)=\E\int_Y^1x^{a-1}(1-x)^{b-1}\dd x
 =\E B_{1-Y}(b,a)=\E B_Y(b,a),
\]
where the last equality uses \(1-Y\overset d=Y\).  The hypergeometric expansion
\[
 B_y(b,a)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}
 \frac{(1-a)_n}{n!(b+n)}y^{b+n}
\]
gives \cref{eq:beta-F-series}.  The endpoint flatness and
\cref{lem:moment-decay} justify convergence beyond the ordinary boundary restrictions
of the pointwise hypergeometric series.  The second identity is analogous.
\end{proof}

For example, symmetry or direct termination gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:beta-example}
 \int_0^1x(1-x)\F(x)\dd x=\frac1{12}.
\end{equation}
Differentiating \cref{eq:beta-F-series} in \(a\) and \(b\) produces all beta-logarithmic
integrals with powers of \(\log x\) and \(\log(1-x)\).

\subsection{A dyadic half-interval identity}

The lower-half differential equation \(\F'(x)=2\F(2x)\) for \(0\le x\le1/2\)
leads to exact quantile truncations.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:half-identities}
For every complex \(p\), with removable values interpreted by continuation,
\begin{align}
 \int_0^{1/2}\Q(v)^p\dd v
 &=2^{-p}\frac{1-\M(p+1)}{p+1},
 \label{eq:half-Q}\\
 \int_0^{1/2}x^p\F(x)\dd x
 &=\frac{2^{-p-1}}{p+1}
 \left[\frac12-\frac{1-\M(p+2)}{p+2}\right].
 \label{eq:half-F}
\end{align}
In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:half-F-zero}
 \int_0^{1/2}\F(x)\dd x=\frac5{72}.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Change variables \(v=\F(x)\) in the first integral and use the lower-half equation:
\[
 \int_0^{1/2}\Q(v)^p\dd v
 =\int_0^{1/2}x^p\F'(x)\dd x
 =2^{-p}\int_0^1y^p\F(y)\dd y.
\]
This is \cref{eq:half-Q}.  Substitute it into the incomplete formula
\cref{eq:monomial-F-antiderivative} at \(x=1/2\) to obtain \cref{eq:half-F}.
\end{proof}

\section{Fractional and integer antiderivatives}

Repeated integration has a particularly transparent probabilistic meaning.  The same
calculus extends without change from integer order to the Riemann--Liouville operator.
For \(\Re\alpha>0\), write
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RLdef}
 (I_0^\alpha f)(x)
 =\frac1{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_0^x(x-t)^{\alpha-1}f(t)\dd t.
\end{equation}

\begin{statusbox}[Integer versus fractional proof status]
For \(n\in\N\), the operator \(I_0^n\) here is the formal normalized Volterra operator
\(J_0^n\).  In particular, Lean proves
\[
 I_0^n\F(x)=2^{\binom n2}\F\!\left(\frac{x}{2^n}\right)
 \qquad(x\le1),
\]
including \(n=0\), and proves the analogous global identity for the signed extension.
It also proves every natural-monomial weight as a finite dyadic sum.  The probabilistic
truncated-moment representation below, its inverse companion, and every noninteger
\(\alpha\) remain outside that formal result.
\end{statusbox}

\subsection{Fractional inverse-pair duality}

\begin{theorem}[fractional power duality]\label{thm:fractional-duality}
Let \(\Re\alpha>0\).  Initially for \(\Re q>0\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL-Fq}
 (I_0^\alpha\F^q)(x)
 =\frac{q}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}
 \int_0^{\F(x)}(x-\Q(v))^\alpha v^{q-1}\dd v.
\end{equation}
Initially for \(\Re p>0\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL-Qp}
 (I_0^\alpha\Q^p)(v)
 =\frac{p}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}
 \int_0^{\Q(v)}(v-\F(x))^\alpha x^{p-1}\dd x.
\end{equation}
Both formulas continue in \(p\) or \(q\) wherever the endpoint terms admit continuation.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Since \(\dd(x-t)^\alpha=-\alpha(x-t)^{\alpha-1}\dd t\), integration by parts gives
\[
 I_0^\alpha\F^q(x)
 =\frac1{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}
 \int_0^x(x-t)^\alpha\dd(\F(t)^q).
\]
The boundary terms vanish when \(\Re q>0\).  Using
\(\dd(\F^q)=q\F^{q-1}\dd\F\) and substituting \(v=\F(t)\) proves
\cref{eq:RL-Fq}.  The proof of \cref{eq:RL-Qp} is identical after interchanging the
inverse pair.
\end{proof}

The case \(q=1\) has a direct truncated-moment interpretation.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:RL-F-prob}
For \(\Re\alpha>0\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL-F-prob}
 (I_0^\alpha\F)(x)
 =\frac{\E[(x-Y)_+^\alpha]}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}.
\end{equation}
In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL-F-at-one}
 (I_0^\alpha\F)(1)
 =\frac{\M(\alpha)}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}.
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
In \cref{eq:RL-Fq}, the integral over \(v\) is the expectation of
\((x-\Q(V))_+^\alpha\) for a uniform \(V\).  Since \(\Q(V)\overset d=Y\), this is
\cref{eq:RL-F-prob}.  At \(x=1\), use \(1-Y\overset d=Y\).
\end{proof}

Formula \cref{eq:RL-F-at-one} says that the entire complex moment function is exactly
the endpoint value of the fractional antiderivative, multiplied by
\(\Gamma(\alpha+1)\).  Thus the Newton series in \cref{eq:M-Newton} is at the same
time a global series for fractional repeated integration.

\subsection{Repeated primitives of Rvachev's up function}

For \(\Re\alpha>0\), define the left-anchored fractional integral
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL-up-def}
 (I_{-1}^\alpha\upf)(x)
 =\frac1{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{-1}^x(x-t)^{\alpha-1}\upf(t)\dd t.
\end{equation}
Because \(\upf\) is the density of \(Z\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL-up-prob}
 (I_{-1}^\alpha\upf)(x)
 =\frac{\E[(x-Z)_+^{\alpha-1}]}{\Gamma(\alpha)}.
\end{equation}
At the right endpoint,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL-up-at-one}
 (I_{-1}^\alpha\upf)(1)
 =\frac{2^{\alpha-1}\M(\alpha-1)}{\Gamma(\alpha)}.
\end{equation}
For an integer \(n\ge1\), \cref{eq:RL-up-prob} becomes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nth-up-primitive}
 (I_{-1}^n\upf)(x)
 =\frac1{(n-1)!}\E[(x-Z)_+^{n-1}].
\end{equation}

\subsection{Appell polynomial tails}

For \(x\ge1\), the positive-part signs in \cref{eq:RL-F-prob,eq:nth-up-primitive}
disappear.  Hence
\begin{align}
 (I_0^n\F)(x)&=\frac1{n!}\E(x-Y)^n,\qquad x\ge1,
 \label{eq:F-Appell-tail}\\
 (I_{-1}^n\upf)(x)&=\frac1{(n-1)!}\E(x-Z)^{n-1},\qquad x\ge1.
 \label{eq:up-Appell-tail}
\end{align}
The polynomial sequences
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Appell-polynomials}
 P_n(x)=\E(x-Y)^n,
 \qquad R_n(x)=\E(x-Z)^n
\end{equation}
are Appell sequences:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Appell-derivative}
 P_n'(x)=nP_{n-1}(x),\qquad R_n'(x)=nR_{n-1}(x).
\end{equation}
Their exponential generating functions are
\begin{align}
 \sum_{n\ge0}P_n(x)\frac{t^n}{n!}
 &=e^{tx}P(-t),\label{eq:Pn-egf}\\
 \sum_{n\ge0}R_n(x)\frac{t^n}{n!}
 &=e^{tx}\mathcal U(-t).\label{eq:Rn-egf}
\end{align}
These polynomial tails and their inverse-moment projection role are part of the existing
Rvachev--Appell research in the repository; here they arise as the exterior part of the
fractional-truncated-moment formula.

\subsection{Ordinary \(n\)-fold antiderivatives as incomplete moments}

For an integer \(n\ge1\), the anchored \(n\)-fold antiderivative of \(\F\) is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nth-F-primitive}
 I_0^n\F(x)=\frac1{n!}
 \int_0^{\F(x)}(x-\Q(v))^n\dd v.
\end{equation}
The same expression can be expanded finitely:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nth-F-incomplete-moments}
 I_0^n\F(x)
 =\frac1{n!}\sum_{j=0}^n(-1)^j\binom njx^{n-j}
 \int_0^{\F(x)}\Q(v)^j\dd v.
\end{equation}
Thus every repeated primitive is determined by incomplete quantile moments.  At a dyadic
\(x\), these can in turn be reduced by the exact dyadic machinery already developed in
the repository.

For inverse powers, \cref{eq:RL-Qp} gives the complementary expression
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nth-Q-primitive}
 I_0^n(\Q^p)(v)
 =\frac{p}{n!}\int_0^{\Q(v)}x^{p-1}(v-\F(x))^n\dd x.
\end{equation}
The special case \(p=1\) is a fractional extension of the area identity:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nth-Q-area}
 I_0^n\Q(v)=\frac1{n!}\int_0^{\Q(v)}(v-\F(x))^n\dd x.
\end{equation}
For \(n=1\), this is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:classic-inverse-area}
 \int_0^v\Q(w)\dd w=\int_0^{\Q(v)}(v-\F(x))\dd x,
\end{equation}
the usual area formula in a form adapted to the Fabius quantile.

\section{Derivatives of arbitrary order}

\subsection{Derivatives of the Fabius function}

On the lower dyadic branch, repeated differentiation of
\(\F'(x)=2\F(2x)\) gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:F-nth-lower}
 \F^{(n)}(x)=2^{n(n+1)/2}\F(2^nx),
 \qquad 0<x<2^{-n}.
\end{equation}
The full interval is obtained by the known dyadic/Thue--Morse reflection formulas.
We do not duplicate those formulas here; the role of \cref{eq:F-nth-lower} is to show
why every inverse derivative becomes extremely singular near the endpoints.

\subsection{Inverse derivatives and Bell polynomials}

Let \(f_j=\F^{(j)}(x)\).  Define rational differential expressions recursively by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Rinverse-rec}
 \mathcal R_1(x)=\frac1{\F'(x)},
 \qquad
 \mathcal R_{n+1}(x)=\frac1{\F'(x)}\frac{\dd}{\dd x}\mathcal R_n(x).
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[all inverse derivatives]\label{prop:inverse-derivatives}
For \(0<v<1\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Q-nth-operator}
 \Q^{(n)}(v)=\mathcal R_n(\Q(v)).
\end{equation}
Equivalently, for \(n\ge2\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Q-Bell-rec}
 \Q^{(n)}(v)
 =-\frac1{\F'(\Q(v))}
 \sum_{k=2}^n\F^{(k)}(\Q(v))
 B_{n,k}\bigl(\Q'(v),\ldots,\Q^{(n-k+1)}(v)\bigr),
\end{equation}
where \(B_{n,k}\) is the partial exponential Bell polynomial.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The operator relation follows by applying \(\dd/\dd v=(\F'(x))^{-1}\dd/\dd x\) at
\(x=\Q(v)\).  The Bell recurrence follows by differentiating
\(\F(\Q(v))=v\) \(n\) times and isolating the term containing \(\Q^{(n)}\).
\end{proof}

The first expressions are
\begin{align}
 \Q'&=\frac1{f_1},\nonumber\\
 \Q''&=-\frac{f_2}{f_1^3},\nonumber\\
 \Q'''&=\frac{3f_2^2-f_1f_3}{f_1^5},\nonumber\\
 \Q''''&=\frac{-15f_2^3+10f_1f_2f_3-f_1^2f_4}{f_1^7},
 \label{eq:Q-first-derivatives}
\end{align}
with every \(f_j\) evaluated at \(x=\Q(v)\).  Combining these formulas with the
Lambert--\(W\) inverse endpoint expansion from the repository gives asymptotics of any
fixed derivative order.

\subsection{Derivatives of integral transforms}

Since \(\M\) is entire,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:M-nth-derivative}
 \M^{(n)}(s)=\int_0^1\Q(v)^s(\log\Q(v))^n\dd v.
\end{equation}
The bilateral up transform satisfies, away from its complex zeros,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:U-Bell-derivative}
 \mathcal U^{(n)}(t)
 =\mathcal U(t)\,
 B_n\bigl(L_1(t),\ldots,L_n(t)\bigr),
\end{equation}
where \(B_n\) is the complete exponential Bell polynomial and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Lj-def}
 L_j(t)=\frac{\dd^j}{\dd t^j}\log\mathcal U(t)
 =\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}2^{-kj}
 \ell^{(j)}(t/2^k),
 \qquad \ell(z)=\log\frac{\sinh z}{z}.
\end{equation}
At \(t=0\), these are precisely the cumulants in \cref{eq:Z-cumulants}; the
continuation of \cref{eq:U-Bell-derivative} through zeros is obtained by cancelling the
apparent logarithmic singularities.

\section{Laplace, Fourier, Mellin, and resolvent transforms}

\subsection{One-sided Laplace transforms}

Integration by parts against the distribution measure \(\dd\F\) gives, for all
\(t\in\C\) with removable values at \(t=0\),
\begin{align}
 \int_0^1e^{tx}\F(x)\dd x
 &=\frac{e^t-P(t)}t,
 \label{eq:Laplace-F}\\
 \int_0^1e^{tx}(1-\F(x))\dd x
 &=\frac{P(t)-1}t.
 \label{eq:Laplace-up-half}
\end{align}
Consequently
\begin{align}
 \int_0^1x^ne^{tx}\F(x)\dd x
 &=\frac{\dd^n}{\dd t^n}\left(\frac{e^t-P(t)}t\right),
 \label{eq:exp-poly-F}\\
 \int_0^1x^ne^{tx}\upf(x)\dd x
 &=\frac{\dd^n}{\dd t^n}\left(\frac{P(t)-1}t\right).
 \label{eq:exp-poly-up}
\end{align}
Purely imaginary \(t\) yields the corresponding sine and cosine transforms.  On the
full line,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bilateral-up-transform}
 \int_{-1}^1e^{tx}\upf(x)\dd x=\mathcal U(t),
 \qquad
 \int_{-1}^1e^{i\omega x}\upf(x)\dd x=\phi(\omega).
\end{equation}

\subsection{Stieltjes transforms and all resolvent powers}

For \(z\in\C\setminus[0,1]\), define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:GY-def}
 G_Y(z)=\E\frac1{z-Y}
 =\int_0^1\frac{\dd v}{z-\Q(v)}.
\end{equation}
For \(|z|>1\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:GY-moment-series}
 G_Y(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{m_n}{z^{n+1}}.
\end{equation}
Similarly, for \(z\notin[-1,1]\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:GZ-def}
 G_Z(z)=\int_{-1}^1\frac{\upf(x)}{z-x}\dd x
 =\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\mu_n}{z^{n+1}},
 \qquad |z|>1.
\end{equation}

Applying \cref{eq:analytic-kernel-F} to a resolvent primitive gives all higher powers.
For every integer \(n\ge1\),
\begin{align}
 \int_0^1\frac{\F(x)}{(z-x)^{n+1}}\dd x
 &=\frac1n\left[\frac1{(z-1)^n}-\E\frac1{(z-Y)^n}\right],
 \label{eq:resolvent-F-n}\\
 \int_0^1\frac{1-\F(x)}{(z-x)^{n+1}}\dd x
 &=\frac1n\left[\E\frac1{(z-Y)^n}-\frac1{z^n}\right].
 \label{eq:resolvent-up-n}
\end{align}
For \(n=1\),
\begin{align}
 \int_0^1\frac{\F(x)}{(z-x)^2}\dd x
 &=\frac1{z-1}-G_Y(z),\label{eq:resolvent-F-2}\\
 \int_0^1\frac{1-\F(x)}{(z-x)^2}\dd x
 &=G_Y(z)-\frac1z.\label{eq:resolvent-up-2}
\end{align}
Using the logarithmic primitive, with a branch analytic off \([0,1]\),
\begin{align}
 \int_0^1\frac{\F(x)}{z-x}\dd x
 &=\E\log(z-Y)-\log(z-1),
 \label{eq:log-resolvent-F}\\
 \int_0^1\frac{1-\F(x)}{z-x}\dd x
 &=\log z-\E\log(z-Y).
 \label{eq:log-resolvent-up}
\end{align}
These formulas furnish analytic continuations and boundary-value formulas for Hilbert
transforms by approaching the cut from above and below.

\subsection{Entire-kernel calculus}

If \(h(x)=\sum_{n\ge0}a_nx^n\) is entire and the series is dominated on the compact
support, then
\begin{align}
 \int_{-1}^1h(x)\upf(x)\dd x
 &=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_n\mu_n,
 \label{eq:entire-kernel-up}\\
 \int_0^1h(x)\dd\F(x)
 &=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_nm_n.
 \label{eq:entire-kernel-dF}
\end{align}
For instance,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Bessel-up}
 \int_{-1}^1J_0(tx)\upf(x)\dd x
 =\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}
 \frac{(-1)^n(t^2/4)^n}{(n!)^2}\mu_{2n}.
\end{equation}
Together with \cref{eq:analytic-kernel-F}, this gives convergent moment series for
Bessel, Airy, hypergeometric, and polylogarithmic kernels without requiring a separate
quadrature of the flat endpoint function.

\section{Products, order statistics, and correlation integrals}

Integrals containing products of \(\F\) and \(\upf\) are generally not rational, but
they have exact probabilistic meanings that lead to useful transform formulas and
asymptotics.

\subsection{Products of distribution and survival powers}

Let \(Y_1,\ldots,Y_{m+n}\) be independent copies of \(Y\), and define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Amax-Bmin}
 A_m=\max(Y_1,\ldots,Y_m),
 \qquad
 B_n=\min(Y_{m+1},\ldots,Y_{m+n}).
\end{equation}
The two blocks are independent and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gap-event}
 \Prob(A_m\le x<B_n)=\F(x)^m(1-\F(x))^n.
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[weighted order-statistic gap]\label{thm:order-gap}
For integers \(m,n\ge1\) and \(\Re p>-1\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weighted-gap}
 \int_0^1x^p\F(x)^m(1-\F(x))^n\dd x
 =\frac1{p+1}\E\left[
 \bigl(B_n^{p+1}-A_m^{p+1}\bigr)\ind_{\{A_m<B_n\}}
 \right].
\end{equation}
In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gap-unweighted}
 \int_0^1\F(x)^m(1-\F(x))^n\dd x
 =\E(B_n-A_m)_+.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Insert the indicator of the event in \cref{eq:gap-event}, integrate, and apply Fubini.
For each realization, the allowed interval is \([A_m,B_n)\) if \(A_m<B_n\), and empty
otherwise.  Its \(x^p\)-integral is the right side of \cref{eq:weighted-gap}.
\end{proof}

Since \(\upf(x)=1-\F(x)\) on \([0,1]\), this theorem covers all integer products
\(\F^m\upf^n\) on the positive half of the support.  Two useful special cases are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Fm-order}
 \int_0^1\F(x)^m\dd x=\E(1-A_m)=\E\min(Y_1,\ldots,Y_m),
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:F2-gini-relation}
 \int_0^1\F(x)^2\dd x
 =\frac12-\int_0^1\F(x)(1-\F(x))\dd x.
\end{equation}

When the density factor is present, substitution gives a closed beta value for arbitrary
complex exponents in their convergence domain:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:density-beta}
 \int_0^1\F(x)^a(1-\F(x))^b\F'(x)\dd x
 =B(a+1,b+1).
\end{equation}
Using \(\F'(x)=2\upf(2x-1)\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:up-density-beta}
 2\int_0^1\F(x)^a(1-\F(x))^b\upf(2x-1)\dd x
 =B(a+1,b+1).
\end{equation}

\subsection{Quantiles and ordinary order statistics}

If \(Y_{r:n}\) is the \(r\)-th order statistic of \(n\) independent Fabius variables,
then \(Y_{r:n}=\Q(V_{r:n})\) in distribution, where \(V_{r:n}\) has beta density.
Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{eq:quantile-order-stat}
 \E Y_{r:n}^p
 =\frac1{B(r,n-r+1)}
 \int_0^1v^{r-1}(1-v)^{n-r}\Q(v)^p\dd v.
\end{equation}
Symmetry gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:order-stat-symmetry}
 \E Y_{r:n}^p
 =\E(1-Y_{n+1-r:n})^p,
 \qquad
 \E Y_{r:n}+\E Y_{n+1-r:n}=1.
\end{equation}
Expanding the beta polynomial in \cref{eq:quantile-order-stat} reduces every integer
\(n,r\) to a finite combination of the mixed quantile integrals appearing in
\cref{eq:power-complete}.

\subsection{The Gini integral and the sinc product}

For two independent copies \(Y,Y'\), the standard distribution identity is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Gini-prob}
 \int_0^1\F(x)(1-\F(x))\dd x
 =\frac12\E|Y-Y'|.
\end{equation}
The difference is \((Z-Z')/2\), and its characteristic function is
\(\phi(t/2)^2\).  The absolute-moment formula
\begin{equation}\label{eq:absolute-char}
 \E|X|=\frac2\pi\int_0^\infty
 \frac{1-\Re\varphi_X(t)}{t^2}\dd t
\end{equation}
therefore yields an integral containing only the infinite sinc product.

\begin{theorem}[Gini--sinc identity]\label{thm:gini-sinc}
With \(\phi\) as in \cref{eq:phi-product},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Gini-sinc}
 \boxed{\displaystyle
 \int_0^1\F(x)(1-\F(x))\dd x
 =\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^\infty
 \frac{1-\phi(t)^2}{t^2}\dd t.}
\end{equation}
Numerically,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Gini-numeric}
 \int_0^1\F(x)(1-\F(x))\dd x
 =0.0955632320160066\ldots,
\end{equation}
so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:F2-numeric}
 \int_0^1\F(x)^2\dd x
 =0.4044367679839934\ldots.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

This is a direct link between a nonlinear real-space integral of \(\F\) and the square
of the Rvachev infinite product.  It does not appear to simplify to the rational moment
constants.

\subsection{Autocorrelation and \(L^2\) energy}

Define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Cautocorr}
 \mathcal C(a)=\int_\R\upf(x)\upf(x+a)\dd x.
\end{equation}
Since \(\upf\) is even, the Wiener--Khinchin formula gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:autocorr-Fourier}
 \mathcal C(a)=\frac1{2\pi}
 \int_\R e^{-ita}\phi(t)^2\dd t.
\end{equation}
At \(a=0\), Parseval's identity gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:up-L2}
 \int_\R\upf(x)^2\dd x
 =\frac1\pi\int_0^\infty\phi(t)^2\dd t
 =0.808873535967971\ldots.
\end{equation}
More generally, derivatives of \(\mathcal C\) at the origin are convergent spectral
moments whenever the corresponding weighted sinc-product integral converges; the fast
decay results developed elsewhere in the repository provide quantitative truncation
bounds.

\section{Additional integral-transform identities}

\subsection{A transform relation for inverse resolvents}

The general identity \cref{thm:AB} can be applied in the \(v\)-variable as well.  Taking
\(A(x)=x\) and
\(B(v)=\log(z/(z-v))\) gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:inverse-log-resolvent}
 \int_0^1\frac{\Q(v)}{z-v}\dd v
 +\int_0^1\log\frac{z}{z-\F(x)}\dd x
 =\log\frac{z}{z-1}.
\end{equation}
With \(B(v)=(z-v)^{-1}\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:inverse-square-resolvent}
 \int_0^1\frac{\Q(v)}{(z-v)^2}\dd v
 +\int_0^1\frac{\dd x}{z-\F(x)}
 =\frac1{z-1}.
\end{equation}
These are nonlinear resolvent dualities: a transform of the quantile is paired with a
composition transform of the distribution function.

\subsection{Frullani representations for logarithmic constants}

For \(0<Y\le1\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Frullani-log}
 -\log Y=\int_0^\infty\frac{e^{-tY}-e^{-t}}t\dd t.
\end{equation}
Averaging and using \(P(-t)\) gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:log-Frullani-product}
 \int_0^1\frac{\F(x)}x\dd x
 =\int_0^\infty\frac{P(-t)-e^{-t}}t\dd t,
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Pminus-product}
 P(-t)=\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}
 \frac{1-e^{-t/2^k}}{t/2^k}.
\end{equation}
For \(a>0\), the negative moment itself has the Mellin--Laplace representation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:negative-Mellin-Laplace}
 \M(-a)=\frac1{\Gamma(a)}\int_0^\infty t^{a-1}P(-t)\dd t.
\end{equation}
This is an independent numerical route to \cref{eq:negative-moment-rational-series} and
connects the negative-moment constant \(C_*\) to the dyadic product on the positive
Laplace axis.

\subsection{A smoothing difference equation for \texorpdfstring{\(\M\)}{M}}

Let
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Ndef}
 N(s)=\E(1+Y)^s
 =\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\binom skm_k.
\end{equation}
The series is entire because of \cref{lem:moment-decay}.  Integrating the uniform
variable in \cref{eq:smoothing} with an arbitrary complex exponent gives the entire
difference relation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:M-difference}
 (s+1)2^s\M(s)=N(s+1)-\M(s+1),
\end{equation}
with the value at \(s=-1\) interpreted by continuity.  At nonnegative integer \(s\),
coefficient comparison recovers \cref{eq:moment-recurrence}; at negative \(s\), it is
equivalent to \cref{eq:negative-recurrence}.  Thus the positive rational moments and
the superexponentially growing negative moments are two sides of one entire
difference equation.

\subsection{Numerical error notes}

The moment recurrence is cancellation-free.  Truncating the Newton series after \(K\)
terms is controlled by the superpolynomial tail \(m_k=O_N(k^{-N})\); comparing
\(K=6000,8000,10000,12000\) stabilizes the displayed fractional values.
For \(C_*\), the recurrence itself supplies an a posteriori bound: after index \(r\),
the remaining relative product error is at most a constant multiple of
\(\sum_{j\ge r}2^{1-j}=O(2^{-r})\).

For the Gini integral with cutoff \(T\),
\[
 \int_T^\infty\frac{1-\phi(t)^2}{t^2}\dd t
 =\frac1T-\int_T^\infty\frac{\phi(t)^2}{t^2}\dd t.
\]
Bounding the first three squared sinc factors gives an omitted correction
\(O(T^{-7})\).  For the \(L^2\) integral, five factors give an \(O(T^{-9})\) tail.
The script uses \(T=500\), making these errors smaller than the digits printed.

\section{Conjectures and frontier directions}

\subsection{Near-rigidity of the negative-moment phase}

\begin{conjecture}[phase rigidity]\label{conj:phase-constant}
The continuous one-periodic limiting profile \(C(\theta)\) in
\cref{eq:Ctheta-product} is constant:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Ctheta-conjecture}
 C(\theta)\equiv C_*.
\end{equation}
\end{conjecture}

A weaker and perhaps more realistic alternative is that \(C\) is nonconstant but its
first Fourier harmonics are suppressed by the same spectral mechanism responsible for
the extremely small periodic oscillations in the endpoint asymptotics studied elsewhere
in the repository.  The exact recurrence reduces this question to the Fourier analysis
of the periodic product \cref{eq:Ctheta-product}.  A rigorous high-precision
test should compute \(A(\theta)\) independently by \cref{eq:negative-Mellin-Laplace},
rather than by the same moment data used in the recurrence.

\subsection{Completely regular growth of the entire Mellin function}

The maximum modulus is known exactly through \cref{eq:maxmod-exact}, but directional
cancellation remains open.

\begin{conjecture}[indicator function]\label{conj:indicator}
The entire function \(\M\) has completely regular growth of order \(2\) with indicator
\begin{equation}\label{eq:indicator-conjecture}
 h(\vartheta)=\frac{\log2}{2}
 \max\{0,-\cos\vartheta\}^2.
\end{equation}
\end{conjecture}

The upper bound follows immediately from
\(|\M(re^{i\vartheta})|\le\M(r\max(0,-\cos\vartheta))\).  The difficult part is a
matching lower bound away from the negative real axis, where oscillation in
\(e^{i\Im(s)\log Y}\) may create zeros and exceptional directions.

\subsection{Arithmetic nature of the new constants}

The quantities
\begin{equation}\label{eq:new-constants}
 C_*,\qquad \M(-r),\qquad -\E\log Y,
 \qquad \int_0^1\F(1-\F),\qquad \int_\R\upf^2
\end{equation}
are defined by positive rational series or infinite sinc products, but no reduction to
standard constants is visible.

\begin{conjecture}\label{conj:irrationality}
The constants \(C_*\), \(\M(-r)\) for every positive integer \(r\), and
\(-\E\log Y\) are irrational; at least one of the stronger properties of
transcendence or algebraic independence should hold among them.
\end{conjecture}

This is deliberately stated as a research target, not as numerical evidence for a
specific transcendence theorem.  Possible tools include Mahler-type functional
equations for \(P(t)\), arithmetic estimates for the rational Newton partial sums, and
Pad\'e approximants controlled by the moment recurrence.

\subsection{Complete monotonicity of normalized errors}

The sequence \(C_r\) is decreasing by \cref{eq:Cr-rec}.  Numerics suggest much more.

\begin{conjecture}\label{conj:complete-monotone}
The error sequence \(C_r-C_*\) is completely monotone:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:complete-monotone-conj}
 (-1)^k\Delta^k(C_r-C_*)\ge0
 \qquad(r\ge1,\ k\ge0).
\end{equation}
\end{conjecture}

A proof might represent \(C_r-C_*\) as a Hausdorff moment sequence derived from the
positive tail product \(\prod_{j\ge r}(1-\rho(j))\).

\subsection{Order-statistic endpoint regimes}

When the sample size \(n\) grows, the minimum \(Y_{1:n}\) probes precisely the
Lambert--\(W\) endpoint regime.  Since
\[
 \Prob(Y_{1:n}>x)=(1-\F(x))^n,
\]
its moments are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:min-order-moment}
 \E Y_{1:n}^p=p\int_0^1x^{p-1}(1-\F(x))^n\dd x.
\end{equation}
A promising direction is a uniform saddle-point expansion in \(p\) and \(n\), with the
periodic endpoint phase transported into order-statistic oscillations.  The corresponding
maximum follows by symmetry.  This would connect the present integral calculus directly
to the repository's inverse-Lambert and half-integer spectral programs.

\subsection{General base and \texorpdfstring{\(q\)}{q}-deformations}

Replace the dyadic weights by \(b^{-k}\), normalized so the support remains \([0,1]\).
The distributional smoothing, inverse-pair calculus, and fractional identities survive
unchanged, while the product \(P(t)\), Bernoulli cumulants, and negative-moment
normalization acquire base \(b\).  The expected leading law is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:baseb-heuristic}
 \E Y_b^{-r}
 \asymp C_b\frac{b^{r(r+1)/2}}{\Gamma(r)}.
\end{equation}
Determining \(C_b\), its phase profile, and its relationship with \(q=1/b\) binomial
coefficients is a natural extension.

\subsection{Formalization roadmap}

The most accessible Lean targets are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item extend the formal support-free quantile pushforward to the two-function identity
      \cref{thm:AB};
\item the power identity \cref{eq:power-complete} and its polynomial corollaries;
\item the positive and negative moment recurrences;
\item positivity, monotonicity, and convergence of \(C_r\);
\item the order-statistic gap identity;
\item finite truncations and certified tail bounds for the Newton and sinc-product
series.
\end{enumerate}
The entire-function and fractional-integral portions require more analytic infrastructure,
but can be decomposed into dominated-convergence, holomorphic-parameter integration,
and beta-integral lemmas.

\section{Summary of principal formulas}

For convenient reference, the central formulas are collected here.

\begin{resultbox}[Distribution and transforms]
\begin{align*}
 Y&=\sum_{k\ge1}2^{-k}U_k,\qquad Z=2Y-1,\\
 P(t)&=\prod_{k\ge1}\frac{e^{t/2^k}-1}{t/2^k},\qquad
 \phi(t)=\prod_{k\ge1}\frac{\sin(t/2^k)}{t/2^k},\\
 \int_0^1e^{tx}\F(x)\dd x&=\frac{e^t-P(t)}t,\qquad
 \int_0^1e^{tx}(1-\F(x))\dd x=\frac{P(t)-1}t.
\end{align*}
\end{resultbox}

\begin{resultbox}[Inverse-pair integration]
\begin{align*}
 A(z)B(\F(z))-A(0)B(0)
 &=\int_0^zA'B\circ\F
 +\int_0^{\F(z)}A\circ\Q\,B',\\
 p\int_0^1x^{p-1}\F(x)^q\dd x
 +q\int_0^1v^{q-1}\Q(v)^p\dd v&=1,\\
 s\int_0^1e^{sx+t\F(x)}\dd x
 +t\int_0^1e^{tv+s\Q(v)}\dd v&=e^{s+t}-1.
\end{align*}
\end{resultbox}

\begin{resultbox}[Entire moment calculus]
\begin{align*}
 \M(s)&=\int_0^1\Q(v)^s\dd v,\\
 \M(s)&=\sum_{k\ge0}(-1)^k\binom skm_k
 =2^{-s}\sum_{j\ge0}\binom{s}{2j}\mu_{2j},\\
 \int_0^1x^{s-1}\F(x)\dd x&=\frac{1-\M(s)}s,\qquad
 \int_0^1x^{s-1}\upf(x)\dd x=\frac{\M(s)}s.
\end{align*}
\end{resultbox}

\begin{resultbox}[Negative moments and fractional primitives]
\begin{align*}
 A(s+1)&=\frac{2^{s+1}}s(A(s)-D(s)),\\
 \M(-r)&\sim C_*\frac{2^{r(r+1)/2}}{\Gamma(r)},\\
 I_0^\alpha\F^q(x)
 &=\frac{q}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}
 \int_0^{\F(x)}(x-\Q(v))^\alpha v^{q-1}\dd v,\\
 I_0^\alpha\Q^p(v)
 &=\frac{p}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}
 \int_0^{\Q(v)}(v-\F(x))^\alpha x^{p-1}\dd x.
\end{align*}
\end{resultbox}

\section{Conclusion}

The probability/quantile viewpoint converts the integral theory of the Fabius--Rvachev
system into a coherent calculus rather than a list of isolated evaluations.  The
inverse-pair identity organizes indefinite weighted integrals; the entire moment function
organizes fractional and negative powers; Riemann--Liouville operators organize repeated
antiderivatives; and the infinite sinc product organizes correlation integrals.  The
negative-moment recurrence is especially useful: it extracts a new constant and an
analytic periodic phase directly from the dyadic smoothing law, with exponentially fast
normalization.

The next decisive steps are to determine whether the phase profile is exactly constant,
to connect its Fourier modes with the spectral corrections already present in endpoint
asymptotics, and to formalize the inverse-pair, fractional-order, and moment recurrences.
The reusable integer-order Volterra layer and its exact Fabius specialization are already
formal; these remaining directions are sufficiently concrete to support both
high-precision experiments and Lean proofs.
