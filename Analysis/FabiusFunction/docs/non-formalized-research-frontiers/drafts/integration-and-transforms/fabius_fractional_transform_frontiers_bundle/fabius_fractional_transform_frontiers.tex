Complex-Order Resolvents, and Nonlocal Rvachev Calculus\\[0.45em]

\author{Research report prepared for Vladimir Reshetnikov}
\date{Repository snapshot: 27 August 2026\\
\small \repo\ commit \texttt{22f3939f162b8fe9481b4affcd6ea12e7f45e8fd}}

\begin{abstract}
Let $F$ be the Fabius distribution function on $[0,1]$, let $G=F^{-1}$,
and let $\Up$ be Rvachev's compactly supported even density.  This report
develops an operator-level calculus for integrals and fractional derivatives
involving all three functions.  Its main result is a quantile--Volterra
transmutation: for arbitrary complex integration order $\beta$ with
$\Re\beta>0$, a Riemann--Liouville integral of $\phi\circ G$ is converted into
an ordinary integral with kernel $(u-F(x))^\beta$.  The formula extends
entirely in a power parameter, thereby covering positive, negative, and
complex powers of $G$, as well as logarithmic powers obtained by
parameter differentiation.  A parallel transmutation is proved for Caputo
derivatives of every noninteger order; the compact differential operator
$\LapF=(F')^{-1}\frac{\dd}{\dd x}$ packages all higher inverse-function Bell
polynomials.

A two-parameter generalized Stieltjes transform
$\Sord_\lambda(z)=\int_0^1(z-x)^{-\lambda}\dd F(x)$ is then developed.  It
satisfies a dyadic differential equation, an order-lowering refinement
identity, a moment expansion, an exact fractional order-shift calculus in
$z$, and a boundary-jump formula that recovers a right-sided fractional
integral of $F'$.  For Rvachev's function, Weyl and Riesz fractional
derivatives obey an exact order-lowering dilation equation.  Unlike ordinary
derivatives, noninteger derivatives destroy compact support; outside
$[-1,1]$ their algebraic tails have convergent expansions whose coefficients
are the exact rational even moments of $\Up$.

Finally, if $D=X_2-X_1$ for independent Fabius variables, the normalized
positive-part transform
$\Kord_\beta(a)=\E(D-a)_+^\beta/\Gamma(\beta+1)$ extends to an entire function
of the order $\beta$ at every interior threshold.  Positive orders are
fractional stop-loss integrals, order $0$ is a tail probability, order $-1$
is the autocorrelation density, and negative integers are its derivatives.
The whole hierarchy satisfies a triangular dyadic refinement equation and a
fractional Fourier-energy identity.  Numerical experiments reconstructing
$\Up$ from its infinite sinc product verify the new identities to between
roughly $10^{-9}$ and $10^{-13}$.  The report distinguishes proved results,
conditional endpoint refinements, and conjectural research directions, and
records its novelty only relative to the audited repository snapshot rather
than claiming exhaustive global priority.
\end{abstract}

\section{Common probabilistic and analytic model}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{The three functions}

We use the standard probabilistic realization of the Fabius distribution
\cite{Fabius1966,arias1982}.  Let $U_1,U_2,\ldots$ be independent random
variables uniformly distributed on $[0,1]$, and define
\begin{equation}
 X:=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{U_j}{2^j},
 \qquad
 Y:=2X-1.
 \label{eq:X-Y-def}
\end{equation}
Then $X\in[0,1]$, $Y\in[-1,1]$, and
\begin{equation}
 F(x):=\Prob\{X\le x\},
 \qquad
 G(u):=F^{-1}(u),
 \qquad
 \Prob\{Y\in\dd y\}=\Up(y)\dd y.
\end{equation}
The normalizations used throughout are
\begin{align}
 F'(x)&=2\Up(2x-1), &&0<x<1, \label{eq:density-relation}\\
 F(1-x)&=1-F(x), &
 G(1-u)&=1-G(u), \label{eq:symmetry-FG}\\
 \Up(-y)&=\Up(y), &
 \int_{-1}^{1}\Up(y)\dd y&=1. \label{eq:up-symmetry}
\end{align}
In particular,
\begin{equation}
 F(x)=\int_{-1}^{2x-1}\Up(y)\dd y,
 \qquad
 \Up(y)=
 \begin{cases}
 F(1+y),&-1\le y\le0,\\
 F(1-y),&0\le y\le1,\\
 0,&|y|\ge1.
 \end{cases}
 \label{eq:F-up-dictionary}
\end{equation}

The random series has the fixed-point forms
\begin{equation}
 X\overset d=\frac{U+X'}2,
 \qquad
 Y\overset d=\frac{V+Y'}2,
 \label{eq:fixed-points}
\end{equation}
where $U\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]$, $V\sim\mathrm{Unif}[-1,1]$, and the primed
variables are independent copies.  This random-series and infinite-product
view goes back to Jessen--Wintner and its later Fabius/Rvachev formulations
\cite{JessenWintner,Fabius1966,arias1982}.  With Fourier convention
$\widehat f(\xi)=\int_{\R}e^{-\ii\xi x}f(x)\dd x$,
\begin{equation}
 \widehat{\Up}(\xi)
 =\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\sinc\!\left(\frac{\xi}{2^j}\right),
 \qquad \sinc z:=\frac{\sin z}{z}.
 \label{eq:up-fourier-product}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Quantile and density dictionaries}

For every integrable test function $\psi$,
\begin{equation}
 \int_0^1\psi(G(u))\dd u
 =\int_0^1\psi(x)\dd F(x)
 =\int_{-1}^{1}\psi\!\left(\frac{1+y}{2}\right)\Up(y)\dd y.
 \label{eq:three-way-dictionary}
\end{equation}
This simple identity is the common source of the inverse-function,
Stieltjes, and autocorrelation formulas below.  It gives, for example,
\begin{equation}
 d_n:=\E X^n=\int_0^1G(u)^n\dd u,
 \qquad
 c_n:=\E Y^{2n}=\int_{-1}^{1}y^{2n}\Up(y)\dd y.
\end{equation}
The fixed-point equations yield exact rational recurrences; related moment
recurrences are developed in \cite{arias1982,Have}:
\begin{align}
 d_0&=1,
 &
 (2^n-1)d_n
 &=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\binom nk\frac{d_k}{n-k+1},
 \label{eq:d-moment-recurrence}\\
 c_0&=1,
 &
 c_n
 &=\frac{1}{(2n+1)(4^n-1)}
   \sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\binom{2n+1}{2k}c_k.
 \label{eq:c-moment-recurrence}
\end{align}
Thus
\begin{equation}
 c_0=1,
 \quad c_1=\frac19,
 \quad c_2=\frac{19}{675},
 \quad c_3=\frac{583}{59535},
 \quad c_4=\frac{132809}{32531625},\ldots
 \label{eq:first-c-moments}
\end{equation}
The value $c_2=19/675$ is important in the nonlocal tail expansions; replacing
it by $1/75$ would correspond to a different and incorrect normalization.

\subsection{Fractional-calculus conventions}

We follow the standard Riemann--Liouville, Caputo, Weyl, and Riesz
normalizations of \cite{Caputo1967,Samko,Kilbas}.  For $a<u$,
$\Re\beta>0$, and locally integrable $h$, the left
Riemann--Liouville integral is
\begin{equation}
 (I_{a+}^{\beta}h)(u)
 :=\frac1{\Gamma(\beta)}
   \int_a^u(u-v)^{\beta-1}h(v)\dd v.
 \label{eq:RL-def}
\end{equation}
For $m-1<\alpha<m$, $m\in\N$, the Caputo derivative is
\begin{equation}
 ({}^{\Caputo}D_{a+}^{\alpha}h)(u)
 :=\frac1{\Gamma(m-\alpha)}
   \int_a^u(u-v)^{m-\alpha-1}h^{(m)}(v)\dd v.
 \label{eq:Caputo-def}
\end{equation}
For $0<\alpha<1$, this reduces to the familiar integral of $h'$.  Right-sided
operators use the standard sign $(-1)^m$.

On the whole line, let
$k_\sigma(x)=x_+^{\sigma-1}/\Gamma(\sigma)$ denote the standard
Gamma-normalized causal homogeneous distribution, continued entirely in
$\sigma$.  The left Weyl operator is
$D_+^\alpha f:=k_{-\alpha}*f$.  Wherever the multiplier is an ordinary
function, and elsewhere in the corresponding boundary-value distributional
sense,
\begin{equation}
 \widehat{D_+^\alpha f}(\xi)=(\ii\xi)^\alpha\widehat f(\xi),
 \label{eq:Weyl-def}
\end{equation}
with the branch obtained from the causal kernel.  This normalization removes
zero-frequency polynomial ambiguities and makes the order family entire.  The
Riesz fractional Laplacian is
\begin{equation}
 \widehat{(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}f}(\xi)=|\xi|^\alpha\widehat f(\xi).
 \label{eq:Riesz-def}
\end{equation}
Different fractional derivatives answer different boundary-value questions;
no formula below silently identifies Caputo, Riemann--Liouville, Weyl, and
Riesz operators.

\section{An entire-order primitive ladder}
\label{sec:entire-ladder}

Before turning to the genuinely new inverse-function formulas, it is useful
to package ordinary derivatives and antiderivatives in a normalization that
continues through every integer order.

\subsection{Fabius distribution ladder}

For $x\in(0,1)$ and initially $\Re\beta>-1$, define
\begin{equation}
 \mathfrak F_\beta(x)
 :=\frac1{\Gamma(\beta+1)}
   \int_0^x(x-t)^\beta F'(t)\dd t.
 \label{eq:F-entire-ladder}
\end{equation}
For $\beta>0$, this is $I_{0+}^{\beta}F(x)$; at $\beta=0$ it is $F(x)$.

\begin{proposition}[Entire order continuation]
For every fixed $x\in(0,1)$, $\beta\mapsto\mathfrak F_\beta(x)$ extends to an
entire function.  It obeys
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\mathfrak F_\beta(x)
 =\mathfrak F_{\beta-1}(x)
 \label{eq:F-order-ladder}
\end{equation}
for all $\beta\in\C$, and at negative integers
\begin{equation}
 \mathfrak F_{-m}(x)=F^{(m)}(x),\qquad m\ge1.
 \label{eq:F-negative-orders}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Write $s=x-t$.  Because $F'$ and all of its derivatives vanish at $0$,
repeated integration by parts gives, for every $N\ge0$ and
$\Re\beta>-N-1$,
\begin{equation}
 \mathfrak F_\beta(x)
 =\frac1{\Gamma(\beta+N+1)}
   \int_0^x s^{\beta+N}F^{(N+1)}(x-s)\dd s.
 \label{eq:F-continuation-form}
\end{equation}
The formulas agree on overlaps and cover the whole $\beta$-plane.  The
reciprocal Gamma factor cancels the Mellin poles at the nonpositive integers.
Differentiation proves \eqref{eq:F-order-ladder}; substituting $\beta=-m$ in
\eqref{eq:F-continuation-form} with $N=m$ gives
$F^{(m)}(x)-F^{(m)}(0)=F^{(m)}(x)$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Rvachev ladder and exterior moment series}

For $x>-1$ and initially $\Re\sigma>0$, put
\begin{equation}
 \mathfrak U_\sigma(x)
 :=\frac1{\Gamma(\sigma)}
   \int_{-1}^{\min(x,1)}(x-t)^{\sigma-1}\Up(t)\dd t.
 \label{eq:up-entire-ladder}
\end{equation}
For $-1<x<1$, flatness at $-1$ again gives an entire continuation in
$\sigma$, with
\begin{equation}
 \partial_x\mathfrak U_\sigma=\mathfrak U_{\sigma-1},
 \qquad
 \mathfrak U_0=\Up,
 \qquad
 \mathfrak U_{-m}=\Up^{(m)}.
 \label{eq:up-entire-order-values}
\end{equation}
For $x>1$, the binomial series converges absolutely and yields
\begin{equation}
 \mathfrak U_\sigma(x)
 =\frac{x^{\sigma-1}}{\Gamma(\sigma)}
  \sum_{j=0}^{\infty}
  \frac{\poch{1-\sigma}{2j}}{(2j)!}
  \frac{c_j}{x^{2j}}.
 \label{eq:up-exterior-general-series}
\end{equation}
The Weyl tail formula of \Cref{sec:weyl-riesz} is precisely the specialization
$\sigma=-\alpha$ of this exterior ladder.  Thus ordinary antiderivatives,
ordinary derivatives, and nonlocal fractional tails are not separate
phenomena; they are different slices of one Gamma-normalized order family.

\section{Quantile--Volterra transmutation}
\label{sec:quantile-transmutation}

\subsection{The master theorem}

The basic obstacle in integrating $G=F^{-1}$ is the derivative
$G'(u)=1/F'(G(u))$, which becomes very large at both endpoints.  A layer-cake
rearrangement removes this derivative completely.

\begin{theorem}[Quantile--Volterra transmutation]
\label{thm:quantile-transmutation}
Let $0\le a<u<1$, put $q_a=G(a)$ and $q=G(u)$, let $\Re\beta>0$, and let
$\phi$ be absolutely continuous on $[q_a,q]$.  Then
\begin{equation}
 I_{a+}^{\beta}\!\left[\phi(G)-\phi(q_a)\right](u)
 =\frac1{\Gamma(\beta+1)}
  \int_{q_a}^{q}\phi'(x)\bigl(u-F(x)\bigr)^\beta\dd x.
 \label{eq:quantile-transmutation}
\end{equation}
For $a=0$ and $\phi(0)=0$,
\begin{equation}
 I_{0+}^{\beta}(\phi\circ G)(u)
 =\frac1{\Gamma(\beta+1)}
  \int_0^{G(u)}\phi'(x)\bigl(u-F(x)\bigr)^\beta\dd x.
 \label{eq:quantile-transmutation-zero}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For $v\in[a,u]$,
\begin{align}
 \phi(G(v))-\phi(q_a)
 &=\int_{q_a}^{G(v)}\phi'(x)\dd x\\
 &=\int_{q_a}^{q}\phi'(x)\ind_{\{F(x)\le v\}}\dd x.
\end{align}
Fubini's theorem is immediate for nonnegative $\phi'$ and follows for an
arbitrary absolutely continuous $\phi$ by applying the same argument to its
positive and negative variations.  Hence
\begin{align}
 I_{a+}^{\beta}[\phi(G)-\phi(q_a)](u)
 &=\frac1{\Gamma(\beta)}
   \int_{q_a}^{q}\phi'(x)
   \int_{F(x)}^u(u-v)^{\beta-1}\dd v\dd x\\
 &=\frac1{\Gamma(\beta+1)}
   \int_{q_a}^{q}\phi'(x)(u-F(x))^\beta\dd x.
\end{align}
For complex $\beta$, the kernel is positive real before exponentiation, so
there is no branch ambiguity.
\end{proof}

\begin{boxedresult}
The theorem trades an integral over probability level $v$ containing the
inverse $G(v)$ for an integral over physical position $x$ containing only the
forward Fabius function.  The singular inverse derivative never appears.
For $\beta=n\in\N$, it gives every $n$-fold antiderivative at once; for
noninteger $\beta$, the same formula is a genuine fractional primitive.
\end{boxedresult}

\subsection{Powers, negative powers, and an entire parameter}

Taking $\phi(x)=x^p$ gives, initially for $\Re p>0$,
\begin{equation}
 I_{0+}^{\beta}G^p(u)
 =\frac{p}{\Gamma(\beta+1)}
  \int_0^{q}x^{p-1}(u-F(x))^\beta\dd x,
 \qquad q=G(u).
 \label{eq:power-transmutation}
\end{equation}
The endpoint flatness of $F$ permits much more.

\begin{theorem}[Entire continuation in the power]
\label{thm:entire-p}
Fix $u\in(0,1)$ and $\Re\beta>0$.  The function
$p\mapsto I_{0+}^{\beta}G^p(u)$, initially represented by
\eqref{eq:power-transmutation}, extends to an entire function of $p\in\C$:
\begin{equation}
 I_{0+}^{\beta}G^p(u)
 =\frac{1}{\Gamma(\beta+1)}\left[
 u^\beta q^p
 +p\int_0^q x^{p-1}
   \left((u-F(x))^\beta-u^\beta\right)\dd x
 \right].
 \label{eq:power-entire-continuation}
\end{equation}
Here $q^p=\exp(p\log q)$ with the real logarithm.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Add and subtract $u^\beta$ inside \eqref{eq:power-transmutation}; for
$\Re p>0$,
$p\int_0^q x^{p-1}u^\beta\dd x=u^\beta q^p$.  Near $x=0$,
\begin{equation}
 (u-F(x))^\beta-u^\beta
 =-\beta u^{\beta-1}F(x)+\bigO(F(x)^2).
\end{equation}
Since $F(x)$ is flat at zero, the regularized Mellin integral converges
locally uniformly for every $p\in\C$.  It is therefore entire, as is $q^p$.
\end{proof}

This formula gives convergent representations for every negative and complex
power.  It also explains why negative inverse moments are much better
behaved here than for an algebraic endpoint distribution: the superpolynomial
flatness of $F$ defeats every pole $x^{p-1}$.

\subsection{Logarithmic powers and exponential generating functions}

Differentiate \eqref{eq:power-entire-continuation} at $p=0$.

\begin{corollary}[Fractional logarithmic moments]
For every integer $m\ge1$,
\begin{align}
 I_{0+}^{\beta}\!\left[(\log G)^m\right](u)
 =\frac1{\Gamma(\beta+1)}\Bigg[
 &u^\beta(\log q)^m\\
 &+m\int_0^q\frac{(\log x)^{m-1}}x
 \left((u-F(x))^\beta-u^\beta\right)\dd x
 \Bigg].
 \label{eq:log-power-transmutation}
\end{align}
\end{corollary}

Likewise, with $\phi(x)=e^{sx}$,
\begin{equation}
 I_{0+}^{\beta}\!\left(e^{sG}-1\right)(u)
 =\frac{s}{\Gamma(\beta+1)}
  \int_0^q e^{sx}(u-F(x))^\beta\dd x.
 \label{eq:exp-quantile-transmutation}
\end{equation}
Expanding either side in $s$ simultaneously generates all positive integer
powers.  Differentiating with respect to $s$ at nonzero values generates
exponentially tilted inverse moments.

\subsection{Definite integrals and fractional order statistics}

At $u=1$, \eqref{eq:quantile-transmutation-zero} becomes
\begin{equation}
 I_{0+}^{\beta}(\phi\circ G)(1)
 =\frac1{\Gamma(\beta+1)}
  \int_0^1\phi'(x)(1-F(x))^\beta\dd x.
 \label{eq:definite-quantile-general}
\end{equation}
For $\phi(x)=x$ and symmetry,
\begin{equation}
 I_{0+}^{\beta}G(1)
 =\frac1{\Gamma(\beta+1)}\int_0^1F(x)^\beta\dd x.
 \label{eq:definite-G-fractional}
\end{equation}
When $\beta=n\in\N$, if $M_n=\max(X_1,\ldots,X_n)$, then
$\Prob(M_n\le x)=F(x)^n$ and
\begin{equation}
 n!\,I_{0+}^{n}G(1)
 =\int_0^1F(x)^n\dd x
 =\E(1-M_n).
 \label{eq:order-statistic-interpretation}
\end{equation}
Thus the integration order continuously interpolates the expected gap above
a maximum of an integer number of samples.  Formula
\eqref{eq:definite-G-fractional} provides a natural ``fractional sample-size''
extension without inventing a noninteger collection of random variables.
For $n=1$, it gives $\int_0^1G(u)\dd u=1/2$.

More generally,
\begin{equation}
 I_{0+}^{\beta}G^p(1)
 =\frac{p}{\Gamma(\beta+1)}
  \int_0^1x^{p-1}(1-F(x))^\beta\dd x,
 \label{eq:definite-power-general}
\end{equation}
with entire continuation in $p$ supplied by
\eqref{eq:power-entire-continuation}.  This is a two-parameter bridge between
inverse moments and beta-like powers of the forward distribution.

\subsection{Log-convexity and Turan inequalities in the order}

Suppose $\phi'\ge0$.  For real $\beta>-1$, define the direct-kernel
order transform
\begin{equation}
 T_\beta(u)
 :=\int_{q_a}^{q}\phi'(x)(u-F(x))^\beta\dd x.
 \label{eq:T-beta-def}
\end{equation}
For $\beta>0$ this equals
$\Gamma(\beta+1)I_{a+}^{\beta}[\phi(G)-\phi(q_a)](u)$ by
\Cref{thm:quantile-transmutation}; the direct integral also makes sense on
the larger interval $\beta>-1$.

\begin{proposition}[Order log-convexity]
For real $\beta>-1$ in the interval of convergence, $T_\beta(u)$ is
log-convex.  In particular, whenever all three terms exist,
\begin{equation}
 T_\beta(u)^2\le T_{\beta-h}(u)T_{\beta+h}(u).
 \label{eq:Turan-order}
\end{equation}
If $M=\phi(q)-\phi(q_a)>0$, then
\begin{equation}
 \left(\frac{T_\beta(u)}{M}\right)^{1/\beta}
 \quad\text{is nondecreasing for }\beta>0.
 \label{eq:power-mean-order}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Normalize $\phi'(x)\dd x$ to a probability measure and apply Holder's
inequality to the positive random variable $u-F(x)$.  The second assertion
is the monotonicity of power means.
\end{proof}

The Turan inequality is potentially useful for obtaining rigorous bounds on
fractional primitives from adjacent integer orders already known exactly or
recursively.

\subsection{Semigroup identity after transmutation}

The Riemann--Liouville semigroup $I^{\gamma}I^{\beta}=I^{\beta+\gamma}$
becomes the nontrivial forward-function identity
\begin{align}
 &I_{a+}^{\gamma}\left[
 \frac1{\Gamma(\beta+1)}
 \int_{q_a}^{G(v)}\phi'(x)(v-F(x))^\beta\dd x
 \right](u)\notag\\
 &\hspace{35mm}=
 \frac1{\Gamma(\beta+\gamma+1)}
 \int_{q_a}^{q}\phi'(x)(u-F(x))^{\beta+\gamma}\dd x.
 \label{eq:transmuted-semigroup}
\end{align}
This is immediate from the theorem but useful computationally: two nested
fractional integrations in the quantile variable collapse to one forward
Fabius kernel.

\section{Caputo derivatives of the inverse Fabius function}
\label{sec:caputo}

\subsection{Orders between zero and one}

\begin{theorem}[Caputo quantile transmutation]
\label{thm:caputo-transmutation}
Let $0<\alpha<1$, $0\le a<u<1$, and let $\phi$ be continuously
differentiable on $[G(a),G(u)]$.  Then
\begin{equation}
 {}^{\Caputo}D_{a+}^{\alpha}(\phi\circ G)(u)
 =\frac1{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}
  \int_{G(a)}^{G(u)}
  \phi'(x)(u-F(x))^{-\alpha}\dd x.
 \label{eq:caputo-transmutation}
\end{equation}
Whenever the endpoint integral converges, $a=0$ is allowed.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By definition,
\begin{equation}
 {}^{\Caputo}D_{a+}^{\alpha}(\phi\circ G)(u)
 =\frac1{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}
  \int_a^u(u-v)^{-\alpha}\phi'(G(v))G'(v)\dd v.
\end{equation}
Set $v=F(x)$.  Since $G'(F(x))F'(x)=1$, the inverse derivative cancels
exactly, producing \eqref{eq:caputo-transmutation}.
\end{proof}

For powers,
\begin{equation}
 {}^{\Caputo}D_{0+}^{\alpha}G^p(u)
 =\frac{p}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}
  \int_0^{q}x^{p-1}(u-F(x))^{-\alpha}\dd x,
 \qquad \Re p>0.
 \label{eq:caputo-powers}
\end{equation}
For $p=1$,
\begin{equation}
 {}^{\Caputo}D_{0+}^{\alpha}G(u)
 =\frac1{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}
  \int_0^{G(u)}(u-F(x))^{-\alpha}\dd x.
 \label{eq:caputo-G}
\end{equation}
The singularity at $x=G(u)$ is only of order $(G(u)-x)^{-\alpha}$ and is
therefore integrable.

With the standard right-Caputo sign convention and the symmetry of $G$,
\begin{equation}
 {}^{\Caputo}D_{1-}^{\alpha}G(u)
 =-{}^{\Caputo}D_{0+}^{\alpha}G(1-u).
 \label{eq:right-left-caputo-symmetry}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Every noninteger Caputo order}

The cancellation in \Cref{thm:caputo-transmutation} is the first member of a
larger hierarchy.  Define the first-order differential operator
\begin{equation}
 \LapF:=\frac1{F'(x)}\frac{\dd}{\dd x}.
 \label{eq:L-F-def}
\end{equation}
It is differentiation with respect to probability level, pulled back to the
$x$ coordinate.

\begin{lemma}[Inverse Bell polynomials in operator form]
For every integer $m\ge0$ and every smooth $\phi$,
\begin{equation}
 \left.\frac{\dd^m}{\dd u^m}\phi(G(u))\right|_{u=F(x)}
 =\LapF^m\phi(x).
 \label{eq:L-F-chain}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The case $m=1$ is the inverse-function rule.  If the formula holds at $m$,
then differentiation in $u$ at $u=F(x)$ is exactly the operation
$(F'(x))^{-1}D_x=\LapF$.
\end{proof}

The first three cases are
\begin{align}
 \LapF\phi
 &=\frac{\phi'}{F'},\\
 \LapF^2\phi
 &=\frac{\phi''}{(F')^2}-\frac{\phi'F''}{(F')^3},\\
 \LapF^3\phi
 &=\frac{\phi'''}{(F')^3}
 -\frac{3\phi''F''+\phi'F'''}{(F')^4}
 +\frac{3\phi'(F'')^2}{(F')^5}.
 \label{eq:first-LF-powers}
\end{align}
These are precisely the inverse-function Bell-polynomial expressions, but the
operator notation scales to arbitrary order without combinatorial clutter.

\begin{theorem}[All-orders Caputo transmutation]
\label{thm:all-caputo}
Let $m-1<\alpha<m$, $m\in\N$, and let $0<a<u<1$.  If $\phi$ is smooth on
$[G(a),G(u)]$, then
\begin{equation}
 {}^{\Caputo}D_{a+}^{\alpha}(\phi\circ G)(u)
 =\frac1{\Gamma(m-\alpha)}
  \int_{G(a)}^{G(u)}
  (u-F(x))^{m-\alpha-1}
  (\LapF^m\phi)(x)F'(x)\dd x.
 \label{eq:all-order-caputo-transmutation}
\end{equation}
The endpoint $a=0$ is valid whenever the displayed integral converges.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Insert \eqref{eq:L-F-chain} into the Caputo definition
\eqref{eq:Caputo-def} and substitute $v=F(x)$.
\end{proof}

For integer $m$, \eqref{eq:L-F-chain} itself gives the complete formula for
the $m$th derivative of any composite $\phi(G(u))$; for an $n$-fold
antiderivative, \Cref{thm:quantile-transmutation} with $\beta=n$ gives the
corresponding complete formula.  Thus the two transmutations cover all
integer and noninteger orders on both sides of order zero.

\subsection{Abel inversion identity}

If $\phi(0)=0$ and the relevant operators exist, the standard identity
$I_{0+}^{\alpha}{}^{\Caputo}D_{0+}^{\alpha}(\phi\circ G)=\phi\circ G$ becomes
\begin{align}
 I_{0+}^{\alpha}\Bigg[
 \frac1{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}
 \int_0^{G(v)}\phi'(x)(v-F(x))^{-\alpha}\dd x
 \Bigg](u)
 =\phi(G(u)).
 \label{eq:Abel-inversion-quantile}
\end{align}
This is an Abel-type inversion formula whose data are expressed entirely
through the forward function $F$.  It suggests stable numerical schemes for
$G$ that avoid differentiating a numerically inverted CDF.

\subsection{Endpoint asymptotics}

Set
\begin{equation}
 q=G(u),
 \qquad
 \rho(u):=\frac{qF'(q)}{F(q)}
 =\frac{G(u)}{uG'(u)}.
 \label{eq:rho-def}
\end{equation}
The sharp endpoint analysis in the audited repository \cite{ProveIt},
combined with standard regular-variation language \cite{BGT}, implies
\begin{equation}
 \log\frac1{G(u)}
 \sim\sqrt{2\log 2\,\log\frac1u},
 \qquad
 \rho(u)\sim
 \sqrt{\frac{2\log(1/u)}{\log2}}.
 \label{eq:G-leading-endpoint}
\end{equation}
In particular, $G$ is slowly varying at zero: for every fixed $t>0$,
$G(tu)/G(u)\to1$ as $u\downarrow0$.

\begin{proposition}[Leading fractional primitives]
\label{prop:leading-RL-G}
For fixed $\beta>0$ and $p>0$,
\begin{equation}
 I_{0+}^{\beta}G^p(u)
 \sim\frac{u^\beta G(u)^p}{\Gamma(\beta+1)},
 \qquad u\downarrow0.
 \label{eq:RL-G-leading}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
After $v=ut$,
\begin{equation}
 I_{0+}^{\beta}G^p(u)
 =\frac{u^\beta G(u)^p}{\Gamma(\beta)}
  \int_0^1(1-t)^{\beta-1}
  \frac{G(ut)^p}{G(u)^p}\dd t.
\end{equation}
The uniform convergence theorem and Potter bounds for slowly varying
functions give dominated convergence.  The remaining integral is $1/\beta$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Caputo endpoint laws]
\label{prop:caputo-endpoints}
For fixed $0<\alpha<1$ and $p>0$,
\begin{equation}
 {}^{\Caputo}D_{0+}^{\alpha}G^p(u)
 \sim\frac{u^{-\alpha}G(u)^p}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)},
 \qquad u\downarrow0.
 \label{eq:caputo-left-leading}
\end{equation}
For $p=1$, writing $s=1-u$,
\begin{equation}
 {}^{\Caputo}D_{0+}^{\alpha}G(1-s)
 \sim\Gamma(\alpha)
 \frac{s^{-\alpha}G(s)}{\rho(s)},
 \qquad s\downarrow0.
 \label{eq:caputo-right-leading}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proofidea}
The first assertion is the order-zero boundary case of the monotone-density
theorem for fractional derivatives of slowly varying functions.  It can also
be derived from the cancellation-safe Marchaud form
\begin{equation}
 {}^{\Caputo}D_{0+}^{\alpha}f(u)
 =\frac{u^{-\alpha}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\left[
 f(u)+\alpha\int_0^1
 \frac{f(u)-f(ut)}{(1-t)^{\alpha+1}}\dd t
 \right].
\end{equation}
For the right endpoint, symmetry gives
\begin{equation}
 {}^{\Caputo}D_{0+}^{\alpha}G(1-s)
 =\frac1{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}
  \int_s^1(r-s)^{-\alpha}G'(r)\dd r.
\end{equation}
Now $G'(r)=r^{-1}G(r)/\rho(r)$ is regularly varying with index $-1$.
Scaling $r=sy$ gives
$\int_1^\infty(y-1)^{-\alpha}y^{-1}\dd y
=\Gamma(1-\alpha)\Gamma(\alpha)$.
\end{proofidea}

\begin{theorem}[Fractional integrability frontier]
\label{thm:caputo-Lp}
For $0<\alpha<1$ and $0<r<\infty$,
\begin{equation}
 {}^{\Caputo}D_{0+}^{\alpha}G\in L^r(0,1)
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 r\le\frac1\alpha.
 \label{eq:caputo-Lp-frontier}
\end{equation}
The same threshold holds for $G^p$ with any fixed $p>0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
At either endpoint, \Cref{prop:caputo-endpoints} is a power
$u^{-\alpha}$ times a slowly varying factor.  For $\alpha r<1$ the power is
integrable; for $\alpha r>1$ no slowly varying factor can repair the power
divergence.  At the critical exponent $r=1/\alpha$, use
\eqref{eq:G-leading-endpoint}: after $L=\log(1/u)$, the integral is bounded by
an integral of $\exp(-c\sqrt L)$ times a power of $L$, which converges.
\end{proof}

The critical endpoint is unusually included.  This is a direct consequence
of the stretched-exponential-in-$\sqrt{\log}$ decay of $G$, and would fail for
many more slowly decaying quantiles.

\subsection{First correction and the log-periodic phase}

The leading term uses only slow variation.  A first correction requires a
quantitative de Haan expansion.  The repository's sharp endpoint theory
contains a nonconstant dyadic periodic phase, so it is important not to hide
that phase inside an unjustified constant.

\begin{conditionaltheorem}[First fractional correction]
\label{thm:first-correction}
Assume that, in each of the weighted $L^1$ spaces used below,
\begin{equation}
 \frac{G(ut)}{G(u)}
 =1+\frac{\log t}{\rho(u)}
 +\bigO\!\left(\frac{(1+|\log t|)^2}{\rho(u)^2}\right)
 \label{eq:de-Haan-assumption}
\end{equation}
with an error uniform as $u\downarrow0$.  Then
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\Gamma(\beta+1)I_{0+}^{\beta}G^p(u)}
      {u^\beta G(u)^p}
 =1-\frac{p\,[\psi(\beta+1)+\gamma]}{\rho(u)}
 +\bigO(\rho(u)^{-2}),
 \label{eq:RL-first-correction}
\end{equation}
where $\psi$ is the digamma function and $\gamma$ is Euler's constant.
Moreover,
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\Gamma(1-\alpha)u^\alpha
       {}^{\Caputo}D_{0+}^{\alpha}G^p(u)}{G(u)^p}
 =1+\frac{p[-\psi(1-\alpha)-\gamma]}{\rho(u)}
 +\bigO(\rho(u)^{-2}).
 \label{eq:Caputo-first-correction}
\end{equation}
\end{conditionaltheorem}

\begin{proofidea}
For \eqref{eq:RL-first-correction}, insert
\eqref{eq:de-Haan-assumption} after $v=ut$.  The coefficient is the beta
expectation
\begin{equation}
 \beta\int_0^1(1-t)^{\beta-1}\log t\dd t
 =-\psi(\beta+1)-\gamma.
\end{equation}
For Caputo, use the Marchaud form.  The coefficient is
\begin{equation}
 \alpha\int_0^1\frac{-\log t}{(1-t)^{\alpha+1}}\dd t
 =-\psi(1-\alpha)-\gamma.
\end{equation}
\end{proofidea}

\begin{conjecture}[Phase-refined de Haan expansion]
\label{conj:de-Haan}
The Fabius inverse satisfies \eqref{eq:de-Haan-assumption} with a complete
asymptotic expansion in $\rho(u)^{-1}$ whose coefficients are polynomials in
$\log t$ and smooth periodic functions of the exact lower-Lambert dyadic
phase.  The periodic dependence first enters beyond the universal
coefficients displayed in \eqref{eq:RL-first-correction} and
\eqref{eq:Caputo-first-correction}.
\end{conjecture}

This conjecture is deliberately compatible with the repository's proof that
the endpoint phase is nonconstant; it does not replace the phase by a scalar.

\section{A complex-order generalized Stieltjes system}
\label{sec:stieltjes}

\subsection{Definition and elementary structure}

The generalized Stieltjes class and its positivity theory provide the
natural ambient transform language \cite{Sokal}.  Work first on the principal
slit plane
\begin{equation}
 \Omega:=\C\setminus(-\infty,1].
\end{equation}
For $z\in\Omega$, use the principal branch of $\Log(z-x)$ simultaneously for
all $x\in[0,1]$ and define
\begin{equation}
 \Sord_\lambda(z)
 :=\int_0^1(z-x)^{-\lambda}\dd F(x)
 =\int_0^1(z-G(u))^{-\lambda}\dd u.
 \label{eq:generalized-Stieltjes-def}
\end{equation}
The upper and lower banks of the slit supply the boundary values used below;
other branches live naturally on the universal cover of
$\C\setminus[0,1]$.  For each fixed $z\in\Omega$,
$\lambda\mapsto\Sord_\lambda(z)$ is entire, because the compact family
$\{\Log(z-x):0\le x\le1\}$ is bounded.  Parameter derivatives
generate logarithmic kernels:
\begin{equation}
 (-\partial_\lambda)^m\Sord_\lambda(z)
 =\int_0^1(z-x)^{-\lambda}\Log^m(z-x)\dd F(x).
 \label{eq:lambda-log-kernels}
\end{equation}

For $z\in\Omega$ with $|z|>1$ the binomial expansion gives
\begin{equation}
 \Sord_\lambda(z)
 =z^{-\lambda}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}
  \frac{\poch{\lambda}{n}}{n!}\frac{d_n}{z^n}.
 \label{eq:Stieltjes-moment-series}
\end{equation}
Every coefficient is rational and computable from
\eqref{eq:d-moment-recurrence}.  At negative integer orders,
$\Sord_{-m}(z)=\E(z-X)^m$ is a polynomial; positive integer orders are the
successive derivatives of the ordinary Stieltjes transform.

\subsection{Dyadic order recurrence}

\begin{theorem}[Dyadic generalized-Stieltjes equations]
\label{thm:Stieltjes-dyadic}
For all $z\in\Omega$ and all $\lambda\in\C$, by analytic continuation across
the removable value $\lambda=1$,
\begin{equation}
 \Sord_\lambda(z)
 =\frac{2^\lambda}{1-\lambda}
  \left[\Sord_{\lambda-1}(2z)
       -\Sord_{\lambda-1}(2z-1)\right].
 \label{eq:Stieltjes-order-recurrence}
\end{equation}
It also satisfies the same-order differential--dyadic equation
\begin{equation}
 \partial_z\Sord_\lambda(z)
 =2^{\lambda+1}
  \left[\Sord_\lambda(2z)-\Sord_\lambda(2z-1)\right],
 \label{eq:Stieltjes-differential-dyadic}
\end{equation}
and the order-raising derivative identity
\begin{equation}
 \partial_z\Sord_\lambda(z)=-\lambda\Sord_{\lambda+1}(z).
 \label{eq:Stieltjes-order-raising}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Using $X\overset d=(U+X')/2$,
\begin{align}
 \Sord_\lambda(z)
 &=2^\lambda\E\int_0^1(2z-X'-u)^{-\lambda}\dd u\\
 &=\frac{2^\lambda}{1-\lambda}
   \E\left[(2z-X')^{1-\lambda}
          -(2z-1-X')^{1-\lambda}\right].
\end{align}
This is \eqref{eq:Stieltjes-order-recurrence}.  Differentiating it and using
\eqref{eq:Stieltjes-order-raising} gives
\eqref{eq:Stieltjes-differential-dyadic}; alternatively, differentiate under
the defining integral and use the density refinement equation.
\end{proof}

At $\lambda=1$, the removable limit is the logarithmic identity
\begin{equation}
 \Sord_1(z)
 =2\E\left[\Log(2z-X)-\Log(2z-1-X)\right].
 \label{eq:Stieltjes-lambda-one-log}
\end{equation}

For the centered variable $Y$, define
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal C_\lambda(w):=\E(w-Y)^{-\lambda}.
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
 \Sord_\lambda(z)=2^\lambda\mathcal C_\lambda(2z-1),
 \qquad
 \mathcal C_\lambda'(w)
 =2^\lambda\left[
 \mathcal C_\lambda(2w+1)-\mathcal C_\lambda(2w-1)
 \right].
 \label{eq:centered-Stieltjes-equation}
\end{equation}
The latter is a transform-level analogue of Rvachev's differential equation.

\subsection{Laplace representation and exact fractional order shifts}

For $\Re z>1$ and $\Re\lambda>0$, the standard Laplace--Gamma
representation \cite{Widder} gives
\begin{align}
 \Sord_\lambda(z)
 &=\frac1{\Gamma(\lambda)}
   \int_0^\infty t^{\lambda-1}e^{-zt}M_X(t)\dd t,
 \label{eq:Stieltjes-Laplace}\\
 M_X(t)
 &=\E e^{tX}
 =e^{t/2}\widehat\Up(\ii t/2).
 \label{eq:MX-up}
\end{align}
The sinc product becomes an infinite product of hyperbolic sinc factors on
the imaginary axis.

Let $A=-\partial_z$, and define $A^\nu$ on this Laplace class by multiplying
the integrand by $t^\nu$.  This is the natural Weyl fractional power of the
positive generator $-\partial_z$ on the right half-plane.

\begin{theorem}[Fractional spectral order shift]
\label{thm:fractional-Stieltjes-shift}
Whenever $\Re z>1$, $\Re\lambda>0$, and $\Re(\lambda+\nu)>0$,
\begin{equation}
 A^\nu\Sord_\lambda(z)
 =\frac{\Gamma(\lambda+\nu)}{\Gamma(\lambda)}
  \Sord_{\lambda+\nu}(z).
 \label{eq:fractional-Stieltjes-shift}
\end{equation}
In particular, for $n\in\N$,
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\dd^n}{\dd z^n}\Sord_\lambda(z)
 =(-1)^n\poch{\lambda}{n}\Sord_{\lambda+n}(z),
 \label{eq:nth-Stieltjes-derivative}
\end{equation}
and the $n$-fold ordinary primitive is
\begin{equation}
 \int\!\cdots\!\int\Sord_\lambda(z)\dd z^n
 =(-1)^n\frac{\Gamma(\lambda-n)}{\Gamma(\lambda)}
  \Sord_{\lambda-n}(z)+P_{n-1}(z),
 \label{eq:nth-Stieltjes-antiderivative}
\end{equation}
away from the exceptional integer parameters, with $P_{n-1}$ an arbitrary
polynomial of degree at most $n-1$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Apply the multiplier $t^\nu$ to \eqref{eq:Stieltjes-Laplace} and compare with
the same representation at order $\lambda+\nu$.
\end{proof}

For $\nu>0$, the inverse power $A^{-\nu}$ is a fractional antiderivative with
boundary condition at $+\infty$:
\begin{equation}
 (A^{-\nu}f)(z)
 =\frac1{\Gamma(\nu)}\int_z^\infty(w-z)^{\nu-1}f(w)\dd w.
 \label{eq:right-infinity-antiderivative}
\end{equation}
Thus \eqref{eq:fractional-Stieltjes-shift} gives an exact continuum of
spectral derivatives and antiderivatives, not merely an interpolation of
integer formulas.

\subsection{Boundary jump as a fractional integral of the density}

\begin{theorem}[Fractional Plemelj--Abel jump]
\label{thm:Stieltjes-jump}
Let $0<\lambda<1$ and $0<x<1$.  The nontangential boundary values satisfy
\begin{equation}
 \Sord_\lambda(x+\ii0)-\Sord_\lambda(x-\ii0)
 =-2\ii\sin(\pi\lambda)
  \int_x^1(t-x)^{-\lambda}F'(t)\dd t.
 \label{eq:Stieltjes-jump}
\end{equation}
Equivalently,
\begin{equation}
 I_{1-}^{1-\lambda}F'(x)
 =-\frac{\Gamma(\lambda)}{2\pi\ii}
  \left[\Sord_\lambda(x+\ii0)-\Sord_\lambda(x-\ii0)\right].
 \label{eq:fractional-Stieltjes-inversion}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For $t<x$, $x-t$ lies on the positive real axis and has no jump.  For $t>x$,
\begin{equation}
 (x-t+\ii0)^{-\lambda}-(x-t-\ii0)^{-\lambda}
 =-2\ii\sin(\pi\lambda)(t-x)^{-\lambda}.
\end{equation}
Integrate against $F'(t)\dd t$ and use
$\Gamma(\lambda)\Gamma(1-\lambda)=\pi/\sin(\pi\lambda)$.
\end{proof}

At $\lambda=1$, the usual Stieltjes inversion recovers the density itself.
For $0<\lambda<1$, the jump recovers a right-sided fractional integral of the
density; applying a right Riemann--Liouville derivative then reconstructs
$F'$.  This gives a continuum of inversion formulas connecting complex
boundary data to fractional real-variable smoothing.

\subsection{Endpoint values and inverse moments}

Because the Fabius density is flat at both endpoints, all inverse moments
exist.  The endpoint value
\begin{equation}
 \Sord_\lambda(1)
 =\E(1-X)^{-\lambda}
 =\E X^{-\lambda}
 =\int_0^1G(u)^{-\lambda}\dd u
 \label{eq:Stieltjes-endpoint-negative-moment}
\end{equation}
therefore extends entire in $\lambda$.  Formula
\eqref{eq:Stieltjes-order-recurrence}, interpreted at endpoint limits, offers
a dyadic recursion for these inverse moments.  Combining it with
\eqref{eq:Stieltjes-moment-series} is a promising route to new $q$-series and
$q$-binomial representations at dyadic spectral points.

\section{Weyl and Riesz fractional calculus of Rvachev's function}
\label{sec:weyl-riesz}

\subsection{Exact order-lowering dilation law}

The ordinary Rvachev equation \cite{arias1982,Rvachev1990} is
\begin{equation}
 \Up'(x)=2\Up(2x+1)-2\Up(2x-1).
 \label{eq:ordinary-up-equation}
\end{equation}
A naive attempt to replace the derivative by a fractional derivative while
keeping $\Up$ on the right is false.  The correct equation lowers the order on
the right-hand side.

\begin{theorem}[Weyl order-lowering equation]
\label{thm:Weyl-dilation}
For every $\alpha\in\C$, as tempered distributions and with a consistent
branch of $(\ii\xi)^\alpha$,
\begin{equation}
 D_+^\alpha\Up(x)
 =2^\alpha\left[
 (D_+^{\alpha-1}\Up)(2x+1)
 -(D_+^{\alpha-1}\Up)(2x-1)
 \right].
 \label{eq:Weyl-order-lowering}
\end{equation}
At $\alpha=1$ this is \eqref{eq:ordinary-up-equation}; at integer $n$ it gives
all higher derivative equations.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The Fourier transform of the right-hand side is
\begin{align}
 2^\alpha\ii\sin(\xi/2)
 (\ii\xi/2)^{\alpha-1}\widehat\Up(\xi/2)
 &=2\ii\sin(\xi/2)(\ii\xi)^{\alpha-1}
   \widehat\Up(\xi/2)\\
 &=(\ii\xi)^\alpha\widehat\Up(\xi),
\end{align}
where the last equality uses
$\widehat\Up(\xi)=\sinc(\xi/2)\widehat\Up(\xi/2)$.
\end{proof}

The theorem is the structurally correct fractional generalization: the
self-similarity survives, but the dilation intertwines adjacent derivative
orders.  No contradiction with the repository's log-periodic obstruction
arises, because the right side is not a scalar multiple of the original
$\Up$.

\subsection{One-sided Weyl tails}

For $0<\alpha<1$, the left Marchaud--Weyl derivative is
\begin{equation}
 D_+^\alpha f(x)
 =\frac{\alpha}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}
  \int_0^\infty\frac{f(x)-f(x-t)}{t^{1+\alpha}}\dd t.
 \label{eq:Marchaud-left}
\end{equation}
If $x>1$, then $\Up(x)=0$ and only the translated support contributes.

\begin{theorem}[Convergent Weyl tail series]
\label{thm:Weyl-tail}
For $0<\alpha<1$ and $x>1$,
\begin{equation}
 D_+^\alpha\Up(x)
 =\frac{x^{-1-\alpha}}{\Gamma(-\alpha)}
  \sum_{j=0}^{\infty}
  \frac{\poch{\alpha+1}{2j}}{(2j)!}
  \frac{c_j}{x^{2j}}.
 \label{eq:Weyl-tail-series}
\end{equation}
The series converges absolutely for every $x>1$.  In particular,
\begin{equation}
 D_+^\alpha\Up(x)
 =\frac{x^{-1-\alpha}}{\Gamma(-\alpha)}
  \left[
   1+\frac{(\alpha+1)(\alpha+2)}{18x^2}
   +\frac{19\poch{\alpha+1}{4}}{16200x^4}
   +\cdots
  \right].
 \label{eq:Weyl-first-tail-terms}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For $x>1$, \eqref{eq:Marchaud-left} becomes
\begin{equation}
 D_+^\alpha\Up(x)
 =\frac1{\Gamma(-\alpha)}
  \int_{-1}^{1}(x-y)^{-1-\alpha}\Up(y)\dd y.
\end{equation}
Expand $(1-y/x)^{-1-\alpha}$.  Odd moments vanish and the even moments are
$c_j$.  Absolute convergence follows from $|y/x|<1$.
\end{proof}

Since $\Gamma(-\alpha)<0$, the left Weyl tail is negative to the right of the
support.  The right Weyl derivative has the reflected tail to the left.
Ordinary integer derivatives retain compact support, but every genuinely
noninteger Weyl derivative develops an algebraic tail.  This is a sharp,
quantified form of nonlocality.

\subsection{Riesz fractional-Laplacian tails}

For $0<\alpha<2$, the one-dimensional fractional Laplacian can be normalized
as
\begin{equation}
 (-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}f(x)
 =C_\alpha\,\operatorname{pv}
  \int_{\R}\frac{f(x)-f(y)}{|x-y|^{1+\alpha}}\dd y,
 \qquad
 C_\alpha=\frac{\Gamma(1+\alpha)\sin(\pi\alpha/2)}{\pi}.
 \label{eq:Riesz-integral}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Riesz tail series]
For $0<\alpha<2$ and $|x|>1$,
\begin{equation}
 (-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}\Up(x)
 =-C_\alpha|x|^{-1-\alpha}
  \sum_{j=0}^{\infty}
  \frac{\poch{\alpha+1}{2j}}{(2j)!}
  \frac{c_j}{|x|^{2j}}.
 \label{eq:Riesz-tail-series}
\end{equation}
Hence
\begin{equation}
 (-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}\Up(x)
 \sim-C_\alpha|x|^{-1-\alpha}.
 \label{eq:Riesz-leading-tail}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Outside the support, $\Up(x)=0$, so the principal value is unnecessary and
\eqref{eq:Riesz-integral} is minus a positive potential integral.  Expand the
kernel exactly as in \Cref{thm:Weyl-tail}.
\end{proof}

The coefficients in both nonlocal tails are rational and obey the explicit
recurrence \eqref{eq:c-moment-recurrence}.  Thus a global fractional
operation converts the compactly supported atomic function into an
algebraically decaying function whose complete exterior expansion is
arithmetically controlled by the same moment sequence that appears in the
Fabius theory.

\section{An entire-order hierarchy from Fabius differences}
\label{sec:difference-hierarchy}

\subsection{Autocorrelation and the difference density}

Let $X_1,X_2$ be independent copies of $X$ and put
\begin{equation}
 D:=X_2-X_1\in[-1,1].
 \label{eq:D-def}
\end{equation}
Its density $h$ is even.  If
\begin{equation}
 R(s):=\int_{\R}\Up(y)\Up(y+s)\dd y,
 \label{eq:up-autocorrelation}
\end{equation}
then scaling $D=(Y_2-Y_1)/2$ gives
\begin{equation}
 h(a)=2R(2a).
 \label{eq:h-R-relation}
\end{equation}
Equivalently,
\begin{equation}
 h(a)=\int_0^1F'(x)F'(x+a)\dd x,
 \label{eq:h-F-density-correlation}
\end{equation}
where $F'$ is extended by zero outside $[0,1]$.  Thus $h$ is $C^\infty$ and
flat at $\pm1$.

\subsection{Gamma-normalized positive-part order}

For $a\in(-1,1)$ and initially $\Re\beta>-1$, define
\begin{equation}
 \Kord_\beta(a)
 :=\frac{\E(D-a)_+^\beta}{\Gamma(\beta+1)}
 =\frac1{\Gamma(\beta+1)}
  \int_a^1(d-a)^\beta h(d)\dd d.
 \label{eq:K-beta-def}
\end{equation}

\begin{warning}
The Gamma factor is part of the definition.  For example,
$\E(D_+)^2=1/36$, but
$\Kord_2(0)=\E(D_+)^2/\Gamma(3)=1/72$.  This normalization is what makes the
order ladder coefficient-free.
\end{warning}

\begin{theorem}[Entire stop-loss order]
\label{thm:K-entire}
For every fixed $a\in(-1,1)$, $\beta\mapsto\Kord_\beta(a)$ extends to an
entire function.  For every $N\ge0$ and $\Re\beta>-N-1$,
\begin{equation}
 \Kord_\beta(a)
 =\frac{(-1)^N}{\Gamma(\beta+N+1)}
  \int_0^{1-a}t^{\beta+N}h^{(N)}(a+t)\dd t.
 \label{eq:K-entire-continuation}
\end{equation}
It satisfies the entire-order differential ladder
\begin{equation}
 -\partial_a\Kord_\beta(a)=\Kord_{\beta-1}(a),
 \label{eq:K-order-ladder}
\end{equation}
and at negative integers
\begin{equation}
 \Kord_{-m}(a)=(-1)^{m-1}h^{(m-1)}(a),
 \qquad m\ge1.
 \label{eq:K-negative-orders}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Set $t=d-a$ in \eqref{eq:K-beta-def}.  Repeated integration by parts moves
derivatives onto $h(a+t)$.  Every upper boundary term vanishes because $h$
is flat at $1$; every lower term vanishes in the initial half-plane and is
then encoded by the zeros of $1/\Gamma(\beta+1)$.  The resulting formulas
\eqref{eq:K-entire-continuation} agree on overlaps and give an entire
continuation.  Differentiating in $a$ proves \eqref{eq:K-order-ladder}; taking
$N=m$ in \eqref{eq:K-entire-continuation} gives
\eqref{eq:K-negative-orders}.
\end{proof}

The order axis now has a concrete interpretation:
\begin{equation}
 \begin{array}{c|c}
 \beta & \Kord_\beta(a)\\ \hline
 \beta>0 & \text{fractional positive-part or stop-loss integral}\\
 0 & \Prob(D>a)\\
 -1 & h(a)\\
 -2 & -h'(a)\\
 -m & (-1)^{m-1}h^{(m-1)}(a).
 \end{array}
 \label{eq:K-order-dictionary}
\end{equation}
Thus a single entire function of the order joins tail probabilities,
autocorrelations, derivatives, and fractional antiderivatives.

\subsection{Triangular dyadic refinement}

From \eqref{eq:fixed-points},
\begin{equation}
 D\overset d=\frac{V+D'}2,
 \label{eq:D-fixed-point}
\end{equation}
where $V=U_2-U_1$ has triangular density
$\tau(v)=1-|v|$ on $[-1,1]$ and $D'$ is an independent copy.

\begin{theorem}[Entire-order triangular refinement]
\label{thm:K-refinement}
For every $a\in\R$ and initially $\Re\beta>-1$,
\begin{equation}
 \Kord_\beta(a)
 =2^{-\beta}\int_{-1}^{1}(1-|v|)
  \Kord_\beta(2a-v)\dd v.
 \label{eq:K-refinement}
\end{equation}
For fixed interior $a$, the identity extends to every $\beta\in\C$ by
\Cref{thm:K-entire}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Condition on $V=v$ in \eqref{eq:D-fixed-point}:
\begin{equation}
 (D-a)_+^\beta
 =2^{-\beta}(D'-(2a-v))_+^\beta.
\end{equation}
Integrate against the triangular density and divide by
$\Gamma(\beta+1)$.
\end{proof}

At $\beta=-1$, \eqref{eq:K-refinement} is the density refinement equation;
at lower negative integers it gives all derivative refinements.  At positive
orders it supplies a stable recursive equation for fractional stop-loss
integrals.

\subsection{A double inverse-Fabius integral}

Using $X_j=G(U_j)$ with independent uniforms,
\begin{equation}
 \Kord_\beta(a)
 =\frac1{\Gamma(\beta+1)}
  \int_0^1\!\int_0^1
  \bigl(G(v)-G(u)-a\bigr)_+^\beta\dd u\dd v.
 \label{eq:K-double-inverse}
\end{equation}
This is a definite integral involving two inverse Fabius functions, while
\eqref{eq:h-R-relation} expresses the same hierarchy through Rvachev
autocorrelation and \eqref{eq:h-F-density-correlation} through the forward
density.  It is therefore a literal three-function bridge.

At $a=0$ and $\beta=1$, the Gini mean-difference identity gives the mixed
forward-function integral
\begin{equation}
 \Kord_1(0)
 =\frac12\E|X_2-X_1|
 =\int_0^1F(x)(1-F(x))\dd x.
 \label{eq:Gini-Fabius-integral}
\end{equation}
Numerically this constant is
$0.0955632320160\ldots$.

\subsection{Fractional Fourier energy}

The characteristic function of $D$ is
\begin{equation}
 \varphi_D(t)=|\varphi_X(t)|^2
 =\widehat\Up(t/2)^2.
 \label{eq:D-characteristic}
\end{equation}
The standard fractional-moment identity \cite{Samko,Kilbas} now gives a
transform formula.

\begin{theorem}[Fractional sinc-product energy]
\label{thm:K-energy}
For $0<\beta<2$,
\begin{equation}
 \Kord_\beta(0)
 =\frac{\sin(\pi\beta/2)}{\pi}
  \int_0^\infty
  \frac{1-\widehat\Up(t/2)^2}{t^{1+\beta}}\dd t.
 \label{eq:K-fractional-energy}
\end{equation}
In particular,
\begin{equation}
 \int_0^1F(x)(1-F(x))\dd x
 =\frac1\pi\int_0^\infty
  \frac{1-\widehat\Up(t/2)^2}{t^2}\dd t.
 \label{eq:Gini-sinc-product}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For $0<\beta<2$,
\begin{equation}
 |x|^\beta
 =\frac{2\Gamma(\beta+1)\sin(\pi\beta/2)}\pi
  \int_0^\infty\frac{1-\cos(tx)}{t^{1+\beta}}\dd t.
\end{equation}
Take expectation with $x=D$, use symmetry
$\E(D_+)^\beta=\frac12\E|D|^\beta$, and divide by
$\Gamma(\beta+1)$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Exact even orders}

For $n\ge0$, independence and $D=(Y_2-Y_1)/2$ give
\begin{equation}
 \Kord_{2n}(0)
 =\frac{1}{2(2n)!4^n}
  \sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{2n}{2k}c_kc_{n-k}.
 \label{eq:K-even-exact}
\end{equation}
The first values are
\begin{align}
 \Kord_2(0)&=\frac1{72},\\
 \Kord_4(0)&=\frac{11}{64800},\\
 \Kord_6(0)&=\frac{211}{171460800},\\
 \Kord_8(0)&=\frac{31657}{5246700480000},\\
 \Kord_{10}(0)&=\frac{7647271}{354246723008640000}.
 \label{eq:K-even-first-values}
\end{align}
These rational constants are new mixed integral evaluations; by
\eqref{eq:K-double-inverse}, each is a double integral of an even power of a
difference of inverse Fabius values over the upper triangle $v>u$.

\section{Numerical experiments}
\label{sec:numerics}

\subsection{Reconstruction method}

The accompanying script \pathcode{numerical_experiments.py} reconstructs
$\Up$ directly from \eqref{eq:up-fourier-product}.  On a periodic interval of
length $4$ with $N=2^{17}$ Fourier modes it evaluates
\begin{equation}
 \prod_{j=1}^{35}\sinc(\xi/2^j)
\end{equation}
and applies an inverse FFT.  The interval is wider than the exact support, so
there is no wrap-around overlap.  The CDF is obtained by cumulative
quadrature and the inverse $G$ by monotonicity-preserving PCHIP interpolation.
Independent exact recurrences generate $d_n$ and $c_n$ as rational numbers.

The principal sanity checks, including the exact dyadic value
$F(1/4)=5/72$ \cite{Haugland,ariasArithmetic,ProveIt}, are
\begin{equation}
 \int_{-1}^{1}\Up=1,
 \qquad
 \max\Up=1,
 \qquad
 \int_{-1}^{1}y^2\Up(y)\dd y=\frac19,
 \qquad
 F\!\left(\frac14\right)=\frac5{72}.
\end{equation}
At full resolution, the computed value of $F(1/4)$ differs from $5/72$ by
about $1.6\times10^{-10}$.

\subsection{Independent checks}

For the quantile theorem, the left side is integrated directly in $u$ and the
right side directly in $x$; for negative powers, the regularized entire
continuation is compared with the direct inverse integral.  Caputo values are
checked against a cancellation-safe Marchaud formula that uses only values
of $G$, not the transmuted $x$ integral.  The generalized Stieltjes equations
are checked by density quadrature, and the moment series uses exact rational
$d_n$.  Difference refinements use high-accuracy weighted quadrature, while
the Fourier-energy check uses the sinc product independently.

\section{Further deductions and research directions}
\label{sec:future}

\subsection{All-order endpoint expansions}

\Cref{thm:first-correction} shows that once a weighted de Haan expansion for
$G(ut)/G(u)$ is known, fractional integration converts powers of $\log t$ to
digamma and polygamma constants.  At higher orders one expects complete Bell
polynomials in derivatives of the beta function.  The missing ingredient is
not fractional calculus but uniform control of the exact dyadic endpoint
phase.

\begin{problem}[Phase-aware Watson lemma]
Prove a Watson--de Haan lemma for slowly varying functions whose second-order
auxiliary function contains a smooth periodic term of a lower-Lambert phase,
and apply it simultaneously to Riemann--Liouville and Caputo kernels.
\end{problem}

A successful theorem would mechanically generate all correction terms in
\eqref{eq:RL-G-leading} and \eqref{eq:caputo-left-leading}, with coefficients
expressible through Bell polynomials and polygamma values.  This is a natural
meeting point of the repository's Lambert-$W$, Bell-polynomial, and fractional
calculus strands.

\subsection{A two-dimensional holonomic-like lattice}

The pair of equations
\begin{equation}
 \partial_z\Sord_\lambda=-\lambda\Sord_{\lambda+1},
 \qquad
 \Sord_\lambda(z)=\frac{2^\lambda}{1-\lambda}
 [\Sord_{\lambda-1}(2z)-\Sord_{\lambda-1}(2z-1)]
\end{equation}
forms a lattice in continuous order $\lambda$ and dyadically transformed
spectral position $z$.  The fractional shift theorem
\eqref{eq:fractional-Stieltjes-shift} fills the gaps between integer lattice
levels.

\begin{problem}[Special spectral points]
Determine whether $\Sord_\lambda(z)$ at dyadic $z$ admits convergent
$q$-binomial, continued-fraction, or Mahler-type representations that extend
the known rational moment and dyadic-value formulas.  Particularly natural
points are $z=2^m$, $z=1+2^{-m}$, and the centered points $w=2z-1$ fixed or
periodic under $w\mapsto2w\pm1$.
\end{problem}

\begin{conjecture}[Dyadic resolvent rigidity]
For each real $\lambda>0$, the differential--dyadic equation
\eqref{eq:Stieltjes-differential-dyadic}, the asymptotic
$\Sord_\lambda(z)\sim z^{-\lambda}$ at infinity, and the generalized
Stieltjes positivity conditions uniquely characterize $\Sord_\lambda$.
\end{conjecture}

This would be a transform-side uniqueness theorem parallel to the uniqueness
of the original Rvachev function.

\subsection{Zeros in the order plane}

For fixed $a\in(-1,1)$, $\Kord_\beta(a)$ is entire in $\beta$, and its values
at negative integers are signed derivatives of $h$.  The zeros in the order
plane therefore interpolate the oscillatory geometry of every derivative of
the autocorrelation density.

\begin{conjecture}[Symmetry-forced order zeros]
Evenness gives the exact lattice of forced zeros
$\Kord_{-2m}(0)=0$ for $m\ge1$.  These zeros are eventually simple, and any
additional sufficiently far-left real zeros interlace the forced lattice.
A more flexible version asks whether the Jensen polynomials of a suitably
shifted and rescaled order function become hyperbolic asymptotically.
\end{conjecture}

This is speculative, but it is numerically accessible because
\eqref{eq:K-entire-continuation} remains stable far to the left after choosing
$N$ large enough.

\subsection{Spectral analysis of the triangular refinement operator}

For each order define
\begin{equation}
 (\mathcal T_\beta f)(a)
 :=2^{-\beta}\int_{-1}^{1}(1-|v|)f(2a-v)\dd v.
\end{equation}
Then $\Kord_\beta$ is a fixed point of $\mathcal T_\beta$.  The order enters
both the scalar prefactor and the regularity class selected by the boundary
conditions.

\begin{problem}[Order-dependent refinement spectrum]
Develop the spectrum of $\mathcal T_\beta$ on weighted Sobolev, Mellin, or
entire-function spaces.  Determine whether analytic perturbation in $\beta$
controls the zero set and growth of $\Kord_\beta(a)$.
\end{problem}

The triangular kernel has Fourier transform
$\sinc^2(\xi/2)$, so this problem may connect the squared sinc product for the
difference distribution to the original single sinc product for $\Up$.

\subsection{Higher-dimensional differences and B-spline noise}

For independent Fabius variables and coefficients $\varepsilon_j\in\{\pm1\}$,
consider
\begin{equation}
 D_m:=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\varepsilon_jX_j.
\end{equation}
The fixed-point noise becomes a compact B-spline distribution obtained from a
signed sum of uniforms.  The same construction gives an entire hierarchy
\begin{equation}
 \Kord_{m,\beta}(a)
 :=\frac{\E(D_m-a)_+^\beta}{\Gamma(\beta+1)}
\end{equation}
and a refinement equation whose kernel is that B-spline.  Even orders are
finite polynomials in the rational moments $d_n$ or $c_n$.  This is a broad
family of exact definite integrals involving several inverse Fabius
functions.

\subsection{Fractional operators and q-binomial arithmetic}

The exact moment series in \eqref{eq:Stieltjes-moment-series}, the exterior
series \eqref{eq:Weyl-tail-series}, and the exact even values
\eqref{eq:K-even-exact} are all built from rational moment sequences.  Those
sequences are already connected in the repository to $q$-Pochhammer symbols
and Gaussian binomial coefficients at $q=2$ or $q=1/2$.

\begin{problem}[Arithmetic compression]
Find direct $q$-hypergeometric forms for
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{n\ge0}\frac{\poch{\lambda}{n}}{n!}d_nz^{-n},
 \qquad
 \sum_{j\ge0}\frac{\poch{\alpha+1}{2j}}{(2j)!}c_jx^{-2j},
\end{equation}
possibly after extracting the dominant lower-Lambert phase or applying a
Borel transform.  Determine denominator bounds for their Taylor
coefficients as functions of the order parameters.
\end{problem}

\subsection{Formalization roadmap}

The most direct Lean formalization order is:

\begin{enumerate}
\item prove the layer-cake form of \Cref{thm:quantile-transmutation} first for
      nonnegative $\phi'$ and real $\beta>0$, then extend by signed measures;
\item formalize the operator identity
      $(D_u^m(\phi\circ G))\circ F=\LapF^m\phi$ on compact subintervals of
      $(0,1)$;
\item derive the Caputo transmutation from change of variables;
\item define $\Sord_\lambda$ initially for real $\lambda>0$ and real $z>1$,
      where all kernels are positive, then add complex analyticity;
\item formalize $\Kord_\beta$ for real $\beta>-1$, its refinement equation,
      and the integer negative-order identities before attempting entire
      continuation;
\item use the already formalized sinc product and moment recurrences for the
      Weyl/Riesz exterior expansions.
\end{enumerate}

The first, second, and fifth steps require mostly measure theory, ordinary
calculus, and Gamma identities.  They should be substantially easier than the
sharp endpoint asymptotic development already present in the repository.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The integral theory of the Fabius and Rvachev functions becomes much more
coherent when the integration order itself is treated as a variable.  Three
operator families organize most of the new structure:

\begin{enumerate}
\item the quantile--Volterra kernel
      $(u-F(x))^\beta/\Gamma(\beta+1)$, which turns arbitrary-order
      primitives and Caputo derivatives of $G$ into forward-function
      integrals;
\item the complex-order resolvent $\Sord_\lambda(z)$, whose spectral
      derivatives, dyadic refinements, moment expansions, and boundary jumps
      are all order shifts of one another;
\item the entire positive-part hierarchy $\Kord_\beta(a)$, which joins
      inverse-Fabius double integrals, Fabius density correlations, Rvachev
      autocorrelations, fractional energies, and all derivatives of the
      difference density.
\end{enumerate}

The Weyl/Riesz analysis adds a complementary lesson: compact support is an
integer-order phenomenon.  Noninteger differentiation immediately produces
algebraic tails, but the tails are not mysterious; every coefficient is an
exact rational Rvachev moment.  Together, these formulas connect the
repository's existing themes -- dyadic refinement, sinc products, rational
moments, inverse asymptotics, Bell polynomials, Lambert $W$, and
$q$-arithmetic -- through a common continuous order parameter.

\section{Proof details for analytic continuation}
\label{app:continuation}

\subsection{Why the reciprocal Gamma factor is canonical}

Let $g\in C^\infty[0,L]$ be flat at $L$, and define initially for
$\Re\beta>-1$
\begin{equation}
 M_g(\beta):=\frac1{\Gamma(\beta+1)}\int_0^L t^\beta g(t)\dd t.
\end{equation}
Repeated integration by parts gives
\begin{equation}
 M_g(\beta)
 =\frac{(-1)^N}{\Gamma(\beta+N+1)}
  \int_0^L t^{\beta+N}g^{(N)}(t)\dd t.
 \label{eq:generic-entire-Mellin}
\end{equation}
This is entire in $\beta$.  At $\beta=-m$,
\begin{equation}
 M_g(-m)=(-1)^{m-1}g^{(m-1)}(0).
 \label{eq:generic-negative-value}
\end{equation}
Both \Cref{thm:K-entire} and the ladders of \Cref{sec:entire-ladder} are
instances of this elementary lemma.  The zeros of $1/\Gamma(\beta+1)$ are
not an ornamental normalization: they cancel the poles of the local Mellin
transform generated by the Taylor series of $g$ at zero.

\subsection{Complex powers and branches}

In the quantile formulas, $u-F(x)\ge0$ and $x>0$, so
$(u-F(x))^\beta$ and $x^p$ use real logarithms on the open integration
interval.  In the generalized Stieltjes transform, $z-x$ moves in a compact
subset of the principal cut plane when $z\in\Omega$; a single branch of
$\Log$ is fixed for all $x\in[0,1]$.  The upper and lower banks define the
Plemelj values on $(0,1)$.  Statements on other cuts are branch-local and do
not assert monodromy-free continuation around the support.

\section{Moment recurrences and exact constants}
\label{app:moments}

From $X=(U+X')/2$,
\begin{equation}
 2^nd_n=\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk\frac{d_k}{n-k+1}.
\end{equation}
Separating the $k=n$ term gives \eqref{eq:d-moment-recurrence}.  The first
values are
\begin{equation}
 d_0=1,
 \quad d_1=\frac12,
 \quad d_2=\frac5{18},
 \quad d_3=\frac16,
 \quad d_4=\frac{143}{1350},
 \quad d_5=\frac{19}{270},
 \quad d_6=\frac{1153}{23814}.
\end{equation}

Similarly, $Y=(V+Y')/2$ and $\E V^{2m}=1/(2m+1)$ give
\begin{equation}
 4^nc_n
 =\sum_{k=0}^n\binom{2n}{2k}
   \frac{c_k}{2(n-k)+1}.
\end{equation}
After separating $k=n$ and using
\begin{equation}
 \frac1{2(n-k)+1}\binom{2n}{2k}
 =\frac1{2n+1}\binom{2n+1}{2k},
\end{equation}
one obtains \eqref{eq:c-moment-recurrence}.

For the difference moments,
\begin{equation}
 \E D^{2n}
 =\frac1{4^n}\sum_{k=0}^n
  \binom{2n}{2k}c_kc_{n-k}.
\end{equation}
Symmetry contributes the factor $1/2$ for the positive half, and the
Gamma normalization contributes $(2n)!$, giving
\eqref{eq:K-even-exact}.
