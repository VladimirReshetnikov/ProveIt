\begin{abstract}
Let $F$ be the bounded Fabius distribution function, let $Q=F^{-1}$ on
$(0,1)$, and let $\Up$ denote Rvachev's even compactly supported density.
The current \texttt{ProveIt} corpus already contains a substantial ordinary
and Riemann--Liouville integration theory: exact integer primitive ladders,
finite formulas for monomial weights, entire Newton--Mellin continuations,
inverse-area identities, moment recurrences, sinc-product transforms, and a
Lean formalization of the normalized integer Volterra ladder.  This report
uses those results as a baseline and develops a genuinely fractional and
complex-order layer that was not found as a systematic subject in the
recursively audited corpus.

The first new construction is an exact fractional calculus in the
\emph{Fabius clock}.  Conjugating ordinary Riemann--Liouville and Caputo
operators by the change of variable $y=F(x)$ gives
\[
 (I_F^\alpha h)(x)=\frac1{\Gamma(\alpha)}
 \int_0^x(F(x)-F(t))^{\alpha-1}h(t)F'(t)\dd t,
 \qquad D_F=\frac1{F'}\frac{\dd}{\dd x}.
\]
It has an exact semigroup, diagonalizes powers of $F$, and admits
Mittag--Leffler eigenfunctions.  The dual $Q$-clock calculus has the same
properties.  In ordinary time, a transmutation theorem gives, for
$0<\beta<1$,
\[
 {}^{\Ccap}D_{0+}^{\beta}[H(Q(y))]
 =\frac1{\Gamma(1-\beta)}
 \int_0^{Q(y)}(y-F(x))^{-\beta}H'(x)\dd x.
\]
For $H(x)=x^r$ this yields a closed integral formula for the Caputo derivative
of every positive power of the inverse Fabius function and the endpoint law
\[
 {}^{\Ccap}D_{0+}^{\beta}Q(y)^r
 \sim \frac{Q(y)^r y^{-\beta}}{\Gamma(1-\beta)}
 \qquad(y\downarrow0).
\]

A second exact bridge connects standard fractional derivatives of $F$ to
fractional primitives of $\Up$:
\[
 D_{0+}^{\beta}F(x)
 =2^\beta\bigl(I_{-1+}^{1-\beta}\Up\bigr)(2x-1),
 \qquad 0<\beta<1.
\]
On the whole line, the Weyl family
$W_\sigma=\Four^{-1}[(\ii\xi)^\sigma\widehat{\Up}(\xi)]$
satisfies the complex-order refinement hierarchy
\[
 W_\sigma(x)=2^\sigma
 \bigl(W_{\sigma-1}(2x+1)-W_{\sigma-1}(2x-1)\bigr).
\]
This continuously interpolates Rvachev's differential--difference equation.

The transform side is organized by the entire complex-order generalized
Stieltjes function
\[
 S_\lambda(z)=\int_{-1}^1\frac{\Up(t)}{(z-t)^\lambda}\dd t.
\]
It obeys a dyadic order-lowering equation, an exact derivative hierarchy, and
an even-moment series.  It also controls nonlocal operators.  For
$0<\beta<2$ and $|x|>1$,
\[
 (-\Delta)^{\beta/2}\Up(x)
 =-C_{1,\beta}S_{1+\beta}(|x|),
\]
so the exterior fractional Laplacian has a full rational-moment expansion.
A parallel formula is proved for Riesz potentials.  Numerical Fourier
experiments suggest that $(-\Delta)^{\beta/2}\Up$ has exactly one positive
zero for every $0<\beta<2$; this is stated explicitly as a conjecture.

The report also develops complex-order resolvents containing $F$ and $Q$,
fractional autocorrelation energies of the infinite sinc product, a catalog
of definite and indefinite integral identities, reproducible numerical
checks, and a Lean formalization roadmap.  ``New'' throughout means new
relative to the pinned repository corpus after a targeted collision audit;
it is not a claim of universal publication priority.
\end{abstract}

\section{Normalization and structural input}
\label{sec:normalization}

\subsection{The bounded function, inverse, and compact density}

Following the probabilistic construction of Fabius and the compact-support
formulation of Rvachev/Arias de Reyna \cite{Fabius1966,Arias1982,Arias2017},
let $U_1,U_2,\ldots$ be independent uniform random variables on $[0,1]$ and
set
\begin{equation}
  Y=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}2^{-j}U_j,
  \qquad
  Z=2Y-1=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}2^{-j}V_j,
  \label{eq:random-series}
\end{equation}
where $V_j=2U_j-1$ is uniform on $[-1,1]$.  The bounded Fabius function is the
CDF of $Y$:
\begin{equation}
 F(x)=\Prob(Y\le x),
 \qquad
 F(x)=0\ (x\le0),
 \qquad
 F(x)=1\ (x\ge1).
 \label{eq:F-cdf}
\end{equation}
The variable $Z$ has the even density $\Up$ supported on $[-1,1]$.  Thus
\begin{align}
 F'(x)&=2\Up(2x-1), &&0<x<1,\label{eq:Fprime-up}\\
 \Up(t)&=F(1-|t|), &&|t|\le1,\label{eq:up-F}\\
 F(1-x)&=1-F(x),& Q(1-y)&=1-Q(y).\label{eq:symmetry}
\end{align}
On $[0,1]$, $\Up(x)=1-F(x)=F(1-x)$.  The inverse
$Q=F^{-1}$ is continuous on $[0,1]$, smooth on $(0,1)$, and satisfies
\begin{equation}
 Q'(y)=\frac1{F'(Q(y))}
      =\frac1{2\Up(2Q(y)-1)}.
 \label{eq:Qprime}
\end{equation}

The left-half self-differential equation is
\begin{equation}
 F'(x)=2F(2x),\qquad 0\le x\le\frac12,
 \label{eq:F-selfdiff}
\end{equation}
and Rvachev's global differential--difference equation is
\begin{equation}
 \Up'(x)=2\bigl(\Up(2x+1)-\Up(2x-1)\bigr).
 \label{eq:up-dde}
\end{equation}
Every derivative of $F$ vanishes at $0$ and $1$, and every derivative of
$\Up$ vanishes at $\pm1$.

\subsection{Fourier products and moments}

We use the angular-frequency convention
\begin{equation}
 \widehat f(\xi)=\int_{\R}f(x)\e^{-\ii\xi x}\dd x,
 \qquad
 f(x)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\R}\widehat f(\xi)\e^{\ii\xi x}\dd\xi.
 \label{eq:Fourier-convention}
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
 \phi(\xi):=\widehat\Up(\xi)
 =\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\sinc\!\left(\frac{\xi}{2^j}\right),
 \qquad \sinc z=\frac{\sin z}{z},
 \label{eq:sinc-product}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
 \phi(\xi)=\sinc(\xi/2)\phi(\xi/2).
 \label{eq:phi-refine}
\end{equation}
The one-sided moment generating function is
\begin{equation}
 P(s)=\E\e^{sY}
 =\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{\e^{s/2^j}-1}{s/2^j},
 \qquad
 P(2s)=\frac{\e^s-1}{s}P(s).
 \label{eq:mgf-product}
\end{equation}
Write
\begin{equation}
 m_n=\E Y^n,
 \qquad
 c_k=\E Z^{2k}=\int_{-1}^1t^{2k}\Up(t)\dd t.
 \label{eq:moments}
\end{equation}
All these moments are rational.  The first centered values are
\begin{equation}
 c_0=1,
 \qquad c_1=\frac19,
 \qquad c_2=\frac{19}{675},
 \qquad c_3=\frac{583}{59535}.
 \label{eq:first-c}
\end{equation}
The recurrence
\begin{equation}
 (2^n-1)m_n
 =\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\binom nj\frac{m_j}{n-j+1}
 \label{eq:moment-rec}
\end{equation}
will be used for numerical coefficients.

\subsection{Endpoint rapid variation}

The repository's Lambert--$W$ analysis implies, in particular, that $F$ is
rapidly varying at $0$:
\begin{equation}
 \frac{F(tx)}{F(x)}\longrightarrow0
 \qquad(x\downarrow0)
 \quad\text{for every fixed }0\le t<1.
 \label{eq:rapid-variation}
\end{equation}
For $x\le1/2$, $F$ is convex because differentiating
\cref{eq:F-selfdiff} gives $F''(x)=4F'(2x)\ge0$.  Consequently
\begin{equation}
 F(tx)\le tF(x),
 \qquad 0\le t\le1,
 \quad 0\le x\le\frac12.
 \label{eq:convex-bound}
\end{equation}
The combination of \cref{eq:rapid-variation,eq:convex-bound} is the key to the
fractional inverse endpoint limits in \cref{sec:endpoint-fractional}.

\section{Existing ordinary and Volterra calculus: a compact atlas}
\label{sec:baseline-atlas}

This section fixes notation and records the strongest formulas already present
in the current corpus.  They are indispensable inputs, but they are not
novelty claims of this report.

\subsection{Integer derivative and primitive orbits}

Let $\Fext$ be the signed global extension satisfying
$\Fext'(x)=2\Fext(2x)$ and agreeing with $F$ on $(-\infty,1]$.  For
$n\in\N_0$,
\begin{equation}
 D^n\Fext(x)=2^{\binom{n+1}{2}}\Fext(2^nx).
 \label{eq:integer-derivative-orbit}
\end{equation}
For the normalized zero-based Volterra operator
\begin{equation}
 (J^nf)(x)=
 \begin{cases}
 f(x),&n=0,\\[1mm]
 \displaystyle\frac1{(n-1)!}\int_0^x(x-t)^{n-1}f(t)\dd t,&n\ge1,
 \end{cases}
 \label{eq:J-def}
\end{equation}
its exact inverse orbit is
\begin{equation}
 J^n\Fext(x)=2^{\binom n2}\Fext\!\left(\frac{x}{2^n}\right).
 \label{eq:integer-primitive-orbit}
\end{equation}
The same formula holds for bounded $F$ whenever the entire integration segment
lies in the agreement range; in particular, for $0\le x\le1$.
Every other $n$-fold antiderivative differs from \cref{eq:integer-primitive-orbit}
by a polynomial of degree at most $n-1$.

\subsection{Monomially weighted primitives}

For $x>0$, $n\ge1$, and complex $a$, endpoint flatness makes the following
Newton--Volterra series locally normally convergent in $(a,x)$:
\begin{equation}
 \frac1{(n-1)!}\int_0^x(x-t)^{n-1}t^aF(t)\dd t
 =\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}(-1)^r
 \binom{n+r-1}{r}\fall{a}{r}
 x^{a-r}2^{\binom{n+r}{2}}
 F\!\left(\frac{x}{2^{n+r}}\right).
 \label{eq:weighted-newton-volterra}
\end{equation}
For $a\in\N_0$ it terminates and is a finite native closed form.  Negative and
fractional powers are covered by the same convergent series.  On $[0,1]$,
weights multiplying $1-F=\Up$ follow by subtracting
$J^n(t^aF(t))$ from the elementary Volterra integral of $t^a$.

\subsection{Inverse primitives and ordinary derivatives}

For $q=Q(y)$ and $\Re p>0$,
\begin{align}
 \int_0^yQ(v)^p\dd v
 &=yq^p-p\int_0^qx^{p-1}F(x)\dd x,
 \label{eq:Q-power-primitive}\\
 \int_0^yQ(v)\dd v
 &=yQ(y)-F\!\left(\frac{Q(y)}2\right).
 \label{eq:Q-first-primitive}
\end{align}
The $n$-fold inverse primitive has the incomplete-moment form
\begin{equation}
 I_{0+}^nQ^p(y)
 =\frac{p}{n!}\int_0^{Q(y)}x^{p-1}(y-F(x))^n\dd x.
 \label{eq:Q-n-primitive}
\end{equation}
For ordinary derivatives, introduce
\begin{equation}
 L_F=\frac1{F'(x)}\frac{\dd}{\dd x}.
 \label{eq:LF}
\end{equation}
Then the exact inverse-chain identity is
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\dd^n}{\dd y^n}H(Q(y))
 =\bigl(L_F^nH\bigr)(Q(y)).
 \label{eq:inverse-chain}
\end{equation}
In particular, $Q^{(n)}=(L_F^n\operatorname{id})\circ Q$.  Equivalently,
differentiating $F(Q(y))=y$ gives the Bell-polynomial recurrence
\begin{equation}
 Q^{(n)}(y)
 =-\frac1{F'(Q(y))}
 \sum_{k=2}^nF^{(k)}(Q(y))
 B_{n,k}\bigl(Q'(y),\ldots,Q^{(n-k+1)}(y)\bigr).
 \label{eq:Q-Bell}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Standard Riemann--Liouville primitives}

For $\Re\alpha>0$, using the standard Riemann--Liouville convention
\cite{Samko,Podlubny},
\begin{equation}
 (I_{0+}^\alpha f)(x)
 =\frac1{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_0^x(x-t)^{\alpha-1}f(t)\dd t.
 \label{eq:RL-def}
\end{equation}
The corpus already proves the probabilistic formulas
\begin{align}
 I_{0+}^\alpha F(x)
 &=\frac{\E[(x-Y)_+^\alpha]}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)},
 \label{eq:RL-F-prob}\\
 I_{-1+}^\alpha\Up(x)
 &=\frac{\E[(x-Z)_+^{\alpha-1}]}{\Gamma(\alpha)},
 \label{eq:RL-up-prob}\\
 I_{0+}^\alpha Q^p(y)
 &=\frac{p}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}
   \int_0^{Q(y)}x^{p-1}(y-F(x))^\alpha\dd x.
 \label{eq:RL-Qp-baseline}
\end{align}
For $\Re\alpha>1$,
\begin{equation}
 I_{0+}^\alpha F(x)
 =2^{\alpha-1}I_{0+}^{\alpha-1}F(x/2),
 \label{eq:RL-dyadic-recurrence}
\end{equation}
which interpolates the integer primitive ladder but does not collapse a
standard noninteger derivative to a single dilated Fabius value.

\section{Exact fractional calculus in the Fabius and inverse clocks}
\label{sec:F-clock}

Standard fractional calculus measures memory in the Euclidean difference
$x-t$.  The Fabius function supplies a second natural clock: probability mass
$F(x)-F(t)$.  Fractional calculus with respect to a function is classical in
general form \cite{Oumarou2021}; the exact Fabius specialization below appears
to be absent from the audited corpus.

\subsection{Conjugacy definition}

Define the composition operator
\begin{equation}
 (\mathcal C_Fh)(y)=h(Q(y)),
 \qquad
 (\mathcal C_F^{-1}g)(x)=g(F(x)).
 \label{eq:CF}
\end{equation}

\begin{definition}[Fabius-clock integral and derivative]
For $\Re\alpha>0$,
\begin{equation}
 I_F^\alpha=\mathcal C_F^{-1}I_{0+}^\alpha\mathcal C_F.
 \label{eq:IF-conjugacy}
\end{equation}
The first-order Fabius-clock derivative is
\begin{equation}
 D_F=\mathcal C_F^{-1}D\mathcal C_F
 =\frac1{F'}\frac{\dd}{\dd x}.
 \label{eq:DF-conjugacy}
\end{equation}
For $n-1<\Re\beta<n$, transport the usual Riemann--Liouville and Caputo
operators \cite{Caputo1967,Podlubny} by defining
\begin{align}
 {}^{\mathrm{RL}}D_F^\beta
 &=D_F^nI_F^{n-\beta},
 \label{eq:RL-DF}\\
 {}^{\Ccap}D_F^\beta
 &=I_F^{n-\beta}D_F^n.
 \label{eq:Caputo-DF}
\end{align}
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[kernel, semigroup, and exact conjugacy]
For suitable $h$,
\begin{equation}
 (I_F^\alpha h)(x)
 =\frac1{\Gamma(\alpha)}
 \int_0^x(F(x)-F(t))^{\alpha-1}h(t)F'(t)\dd t.
 \label{eq:IF-kernel}
\end{equation}
Moreover,
\begin{equation}
 I_F^\alpha I_F^\gamma=I_F^{\alpha+\gamma},
 \qquad
 {}^{\Ccap}D_F^\beta
 =\mathcal C_F^{-1}{}^{\Ccap}D_{0+}^\beta\mathcal C_F,
 \qquad
 {}^{\mathrm{RL}}D_F^\beta
 =\mathcal C_F^{-1}D_{0+}^\beta\mathcal C_F.
 \label{eq:F-clock-conjugacies}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Evaluate \cref{eq:IF-conjugacy} at $y=F(x)$ and substitute $v=F(t)$:
\[
 \frac1{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_0^{F(x)}
 (F(x)-v)^{\alpha-1}h(Q(v))\dd v
 =\frac1{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_0^x
 (F(x)-F(t))^{\alpha-1}h(t)F'(t)\dd t.
\]
Every operator identity is then transported from ordinary fractional calculus
by conjugacy.  In particular, the semigroup is literal rather than formal.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Power laws and Mittag--Leffler eigenfunctions}

\begin{theorem}[diagonal action on powers]
For $\Re\lambda>-1$ and $\Re\alpha>0$,
\begin{equation}
 I_F^\alpha F(x)^\lambda
 =\frac{\Gamma(\lambda+1)}{\Gamma(\lambda+\alpha+1)}
  F(x)^{\lambda+\alpha}.
 \label{eq:F-clock-power-integral}
\end{equation}
For the Riemann--Liouville derivative, wherever both sides are defined,
\begin{equation}
 {}^{\mathrm{RL}}D_F^\beta F(x)^\lambda
 =\frac{\Gamma(\lambda+1)}{\Gamma(\lambda-\beta+1)}
  F(x)^{\lambda-\beta}.
 \label{eq:F-clock-power-RL}
\end{equation}
For $n-1<\beta<n$, the same formula holds for the Caputo derivative when
$\Re\lambda>n-1$; the usual low-degree polynomial exceptions apply.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Under $\mathcal C_F$, the function $F(x)^\lambda$ becomes $y^\lambda$.
The assertions are exactly the beta-integral power laws for ordinary
Riemann--Liouville and Caputo operators.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[fractional eigenproblem]
For $0<\beta\le1$ and $a\in\C$,
\begin{equation}
 {}^{\Ccap}D_F^\beta
 E_\beta\bigl(aF(x)^\beta\bigr)
 =aE_\beta\bigl(aF(x)^\beta\bigr),
 \qquad
 E_\beta(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^k}{\Gamma(\beta k+1)}.
 \label{eq:F-clock-Mittag}
\end{equation}
Thus the ordinary exponential $\e^{aF(x)}$ is recovered at $\beta=1$.
\end{corollary}

\subsection{The dual inverse clock}

The inverse function supplies the dual clock on the probability interval.
Define
\begin{equation}
 (I_Q^\alpha k)(y)
 =\frac1{\Gamma(\alpha)}
  \int_0^y(Q(y)-Q(v))^{\alpha-1}k(v)Q'(v)\dd v,
 \qquad
 D_Q=\frac1{Q'}\frac{\dd}{\dd y}.
 \label{eq:IQ-DQ}
\end{equation}
This is conjugate to ordinary fractional calculus by $x=Q(y)$.  Hence
\begin{align}
 I_Q^\alpha Q(y)^\lambda
 &=\frac{\Gamma(\lambda+1)}{\Gamma(\lambda+\alpha+1)}
 Q(y)^{\lambda+\alpha},
 \label{eq:Q-clock-power-I}\\
 {}^{\mathrm{RL}}D_Q^\beta Q(y)^\lambda
 &=\frac{\Gamma(\lambda+1)}{\Gamma(\lambda-\beta+1)}
 Q(y)^{\lambda-\beta}.
 \label{eq:Q-clock-power-D}
\end{align}
The pair $(I_F,D_F)$ and $(I_Q,D_Q)$ is therefore exactly dual under the
mutually inverse coordinate maps $F$ and $Q$.

\begin{remark}[What this does and does not say]
The Fabius-clock derivative is not the standard fractional derivative of
$F$ as a function of Euclidean $x$.  It is a nonlinear-coordinate operator in
which the intricate Fabius geometry is moved into the kernel.  Its advantage
is exact algebra; the standard Caputo and Weyl derivatives studied below
retain the original Euclidean clock and are analytically more delicate.
\end{remark}

\section{Caputo and Riemann--Liouville transmutation through the inverse}
\label{sec:inverse-transmutation}

The ordinary inverse-chain rule \cref{eq:inverse-chain} has a nonlocal
fractional counterpart.  It converts a singular derivative of $H\circ Q$ in
the $y$ variable into an integral over the much better behaved $x$ variable.

\subsection{Left fractional integrals and derivatives}

\begin{theorem}[general inverse-composition transmutation]
\label{thm:left-transmutation}
Let $q=Q(y)$ and $\Re\alpha>0$.  If $H$ is integrable against the displayed
kernels, then
\begin{align}
 I_{0+}^\alpha[H\circ Q](y)
 &=\frac1{\Gamma(\alpha)}
   \int_0^q(y-F(x))^{\alpha-1}H(x)F'(x)\dd x,
 \label{eq:inverse-RL-substitution}\\
 &=\frac{H(0)y^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}
   +\frac1{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}
    \int_0^q(y-F(x))^\alpha H'(x)\dd x,
 \label{eq:inverse-RL-parts}
\end{align}
where the second identity assumes absolute continuity of $H$.
For $0<\beta<1$,
\begin{equation}
 {}^{\Ccap}D_{0+}^\beta[H\circ Q](y)
 =\frac1{\Gamma(1-\beta)}
  \int_0^q(y-F(x))^{-\beta}H'(x)\dd x,
 \label{eq:inverse-Caputo-transmutation}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
 D_{0+}^\beta[H\circ Q](y)
 =\frac{H(0)y^{-\beta}}{\Gamma(1-\beta)}
  +{}^{\Ccap}D_{0+}^\beta[H\circ Q](y).
 \label{eq:inverse-RL-vs-Caputo}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Substitute $v=F(x)$ in the definition of $I_{0+}^\alpha[H(Q(v))]$ to obtain
\cref{eq:inverse-RL-substitution}.  Since
\[
 \dd (y-F(x))^\alpha
 =-\alpha(y-F(x))^{\alpha-1}F'(x)\dd x,
\]
integration by parts gives \cref{eq:inverse-RL-parts}.
For the Caputo derivative,
\[
 \frac{\dd}{\dd v}H(Q(v))=H'(Q(v))Q'(v).
\]
After $v=F(x)$, the factors $Q'(F(x))F'(x)$ cancel exactly, leaving
\cref{eq:inverse-Caputo-transmutation}.  The final identity is the standard
Riemann--Liouville/Caputo endpoint correction.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[powers of the inverse Fabius function]
For $r>0$ and $0<\beta<1$,
\begin{equation}
 {}^{\Ccap}D_{0+}^\beta Q(y)^r
 =\frac{r}{\Gamma(1-\beta)}
   \int_0^{Q(y)}x^{r-1}(y-F(x))^{-\beta}\dd x.
 \label{eq:Caputo-Q-power}
\end{equation}
Because $Q(0)=0$, the Riemann--Liouville and Caputo derivatives coincide for
$Q^r$ in this range whenever the integral is finite.
\end{corollary}

\subsection{All positive noninteger orders}

Let $n-1<\beta<n$.  From \cref{eq:inverse-chain},
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\dd^n}{\dd y^n}[H(Q(y))]
 =\bigl(L_F^nH\bigr)(Q(y)).
\end{equation}
Therefore:

\begin{proposition}[higher-order Caputo transmutation]
Suppose $H\circ Q$ belongs to the classical Caputo domain on $[0,y]$ and
$(L_F^nH)(x)F'(x)$ is integrable against the displayed kernel.  Then
\begin{equation}
 {}^{\Ccap}D_{0+}^\beta[H\circ Q](y)
 =\frac1{\Gamma(n-\beta)}
  \int_0^{Q(y)}(y-F(x))^{n-\beta-1}
  (L_F^nH)(x)F'(x)\dd x.
 \label{eq:higher-Caputo-transmutation}
\end{equation}
The Riemann--Liouville derivative is obtained by adding the usual finite
endpoint jet
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{k=0}^{n-1}
 \frac{(H\circ Q)^{(k)}(0+)}{\Gamma(k-\beta+1)}y^{k-\beta},
 \label{eq:higher-RL-jet}
\end{equation}
when these endpoint values exist.
\end{proposition}

\begin{warningbox}[Higher-order inverse derivatives]
The inverse Fabius function has singular derivatives at $0$ and $1$.
Consequently, a formula obtained by formally inserting $H(x)=x$ into
\cref{eq:higher-Caputo-transmutation} need not define a classical Caputo
derivative for every $\beta>1$.  The theorem is deliberately stated in terms
of the actual Caputo domain.  Distributional or weighted-space extensions are
promising, but require a separate endpoint-domain analysis.
\end{warningbox}

\subsection{Right-sided transmutation}

For $0<\beta<1$ and $q=Q(y)$, the right-sided counterparts are
\begin{align}
 I_{1-}^\alpha[H\circ Q](y)
 &=\frac{H(1)(1-y)^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}
   -\frac1{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}
    \int_q^1(F(x)-y)^\alpha H'(x)\dd x,
 \label{eq:right-RL-transmutation}\\
 {}^{\Ccap}D_{1-}^\beta[H\circ Q](y)
 &=-\frac1{\Gamma(1-\beta)}
   \int_q^1(F(x)-y)^{-\beta}H'(x)\dd x,
 \label{eq:right-Caputo-transmutation}\\
 D_{1-}^\beta[H\circ Q](y)
 &=\frac{H(1)(1-y)^{-\beta}}{\Gamma(1-\beta)}
   +{}^{\Ccap}D_{1-}^\beta[H\circ Q](y).
 \label{eq:right-RL-Caputo}
\end{align}
They follow by the same substitution and integration by parts.  Symmetry
$Q(1-y)=1-Q(y)$ converts left endpoint statements into right endpoint
statements without recomputing the Fabius asymptotics.

\section{Fractional endpoint laws for inverse powers}
\label{sec:endpoint-fractional}

The integral formulas above permit endpoint asymptotics without differentiating
the Lambert--$W$ inverse expansion term by term.

\begin{theorem}[fractional primitive and Caputo endpoint laws]
\label{thm:Q-fractional-endpoint}
Let $r>0$ and $\alpha>0$.  Then
\begin{equation}
 I_{0+}^\alpha Q(y)^r
 \sim\frac{Q(y)^r y^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}
 \qquad(y\downarrow0).
 \label{eq:Q-RL-endpoint}
\end{equation}
For $0<\beta<1$,
\begin{equation}
 {}^{\Ccap}D_{0+}^\beta Q(y)^r
 \sim\frac{Q(y)^r y^{-\beta}}{\Gamma(1-\beta)}
 \qquad(y\downarrow0).
 \label{eq:Q-Caputo-endpoint}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Put $q=Q(y)$ and scale $x=qt$ in
\cref{eq:RL-Qp-baseline}.  The ratio of the two sides of
\cref{eq:Q-RL-endpoint} is
\begin{equation}
 r\int_0^1t^{r-1}
 \left(1-\frac{F(qt)}{F(q)}\right)^\alpha\dd t.
 \label{eq:RL-endpoint-ratio}
\end{equation}
For each fixed $t<1$, rapid variation \cref{eq:rapid-variation} makes the
parenthesis tend to $1$.  The integrand is bounded by $rt^{r-1}$, so dominated
convergence gives $1$.

Likewise, \cref{eq:Caputo-Q-power} yields the ratio
\begin{equation}
 r\int_0^1t^{r-1}
 \left(1-\frac{F(qt)}{F(q)}\right)^{-\beta}\dd t.
 \label{eq:Caputo-endpoint-ratio}
\end{equation}
For $q\le1/2$, convexity gives $F(qt)/F(q)\le t$; hence the integrand is bounded
by $rt^{r-1}(1-t)^{-\beta}$, which is integrable because $r>0$ and
$\beta<1$.  Dominated convergence again gives $1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[right endpoint]
As $y\uparrow1$, the corresponding right-sided laws hold for
$(1-Q(y))^r$ with $1-y$ in place of $y$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}[Slow convergence]
The limit in \cref{eq:Q-Caputo-endpoint} is rigorous but numerically slow.
For $r=1$, $\beta=0.4$, the ratio in
\cref{eq:Caputo-endpoint-ratio} decreases only from approximately $1.1835$ at
$y=10^{-2}$ to $1.1127$ at $y=10^{-7}$.  This is consistent with a first
correction on the Lambert scale $\rho(y)^{-1}$, where
\begin{equation}
 \rho(y)=\sqrt{\frac{2\log(1/y)}{\log2}}.
 \label{eq:rho-y}
\end{equation}
A phase-resolved conjecture is stated in \cref{conj:Caputo-phase}.
\end{remark}

\section{A standard fractional bridge from Fabius to Rvachev}
\label{sec:F-up-bridge}

Because $F$ is flat at $0$, its left Riemann--Liouville and Caputo derivatives
coincide for every positive noninteger order.  Unlike the Fabius-clock
operator, the following theorem concerns the ordinary Euclidean clock.

\begin{theorem}[all-order fractional $F$--$\Up$ bridge]
\label{thm:F-up-bridge}
Let $n-1<\beta<n$ and $0<x<1$.  Then
\begin{equation}
 D_{0+}^\beta F(x)
 ={}^{\Ccap}D_{0+}^\beta F(x)
 =2^\beta
   \left(I_{-1+}^{n-\beta}\Up^{(n-1)}\right)(2x-1).
 \label{eq:F-up-bridge-general}
\end{equation}
In particular, for $0<\beta<1$,
\begin{equation}
 D_{0+}^\beta F(x)
 =2^\beta\left(I_{-1+}^{1-\beta}\Up\right)(2x-1).
 \label{eq:F-up-bridge-simple}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Flatness removes every endpoint correction between the two fractional
derivatives.  Since $F'(t)=2\Up(2t-1)$,
\begin{equation}
 F^{(n)}(t)=2^n\Up^{(n-1)}(2t-1).
\end{equation}
Insert this into
$I_{0+}^{n-\beta}F^{(n)}(x)$ and substitute $s=2t-1$.  The factors are
$2^n$ from differentiation, $2^{-1}$ from $\dd t$, and
$2^{-(n-\beta-1)}$ from the kernel, leaving exactly $2^\beta$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Interpolation endpoints]
For $0<\beta<1$, letting $\beta\downarrow0$ in
\cref{eq:F-up-bridge-simple} gives
\[
 F(x)=\int_{-1}^{2x-1}\Up(t)\dd t,
\]
whereas $\beta\uparrow1$ gives $F'(x)=2\Up(2x-1)$.  Thus the identity is a
literal continuum between the CDF and its density.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Fractional derivatives of \texorpdfstring{$\Up$}{up} from the support boundary}

On the left half of its support, $\Up(x)=F(x+1)$.  Therefore, for
$-1<x<0$,
\begin{equation}
 D_{-1+}^\beta\Up(x)
 =2^\beta
  \left(I_{-1+}^{n-\beta}\Up^{(n-1)}\right)(2x+1),
 \qquad n-1<\beta<n.
 \label{eq:up-left-fractional}
\end{equation}
On the right half, reflection gives
\begin{equation}
 D_{1-}^\beta\Up(x)
 =2^\beta
  \left(I_{-1+}^{n-\beta}\Up^{(n-1)}\right)(1-2x),
 \qquad 0<x<1,
 \label{eq:up-right-fractional}
\end{equation}
with the standard right-Caputo sign convention.  These are boundary-anchored
identities; they should not be confused with the translation-invariant Weyl
operators in the next section.

\subsection{Fractional primitives of \texorpdfstring{$1-F$}{1-F}}

On $[0,1]$, $\Up(x)=1-F(x)$.  Hence
\begin{equation}
 I_{0+}^\alpha\Up(x)
 =\frac{x^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}-I_{0+}^\alpha F(x),
 \label{eq:half-up-RL}
\end{equation}
and the probabilistic formula \cref{eq:RL-F-prob} gives
\begin{equation}
 I_{0+}^\alpha\Up(x)
 =\frac{x^\alpha-\E[(x-Y)_+^\alpha]}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}.
 \label{eq:half-up-truncated}
\end{equation}
At $x=1$, symmetry yields
\begin{equation}
 I_{0+}^\alpha F(1)
 =\frac{\Mell(\alpha)}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)},
 \qquad
 \Mell(s)=\E Y^s=\int_0^1Q(y)^s\dd y,
 \label{eq:RL-endpoint-Mellin}
\end{equation}
so endpoint fractional integration is exactly the entire inverse-moment
function already developed in the corpus.

\section{Whole-line Weyl derivatives and complex-order refinement}
\label{sec:Weyl}

Boundary-anchored fractional derivatives break translation invariance.  The
compact support and entire Fourier product of $\Up$ make Weyl calculus the
natural whole-line alternative.

\subsection{Fourier-multiplier definition}

Following the standard Weyl multiplier conventions \cite{Samko}, choose
principal branches and define, initially on Schwartz functions and then by
tempered-distribution continuation,
\begin{align}
 \mathfrak D_+^\sigma f
 &=\Four^{-1}\bigl[(\ii\xi)^\sigma\widehat f(\xi)\bigr],
 \label{eq:Weyl-left}\\
 \mathfrak D_-^\sigma f
 &=\Four^{-1}\bigl[(-\ii\xi)^\sigma\widehat f(\xi)\bigr].
 \label{eq:Weyl-right}
\end{align}
For $\Re\alpha>0$, the left Weyl integral is
\begin{equation}
 (\mathfrak I_+^\alpha f)(x)
 =\frac1{\Gamma(\alpha)}
  \int_{-\infty}^x(x-t)^{\alpha-1}f(t)\dd t,
 \label{eq:Weyl-integral}
\end{equation}
whose multiplier is $(\ii\xi)^{-\alpha}$ in the boundary-value sense.

\subsection{The complex-order hierarchy}

\begin{theorem}[Weyl--Rvachev refinement hierarchy]
\label{thm:Weyl-hierarchy}
Let
\begin{equation}
 W_\sigma=\mathfrak D_+^\sigma\Up.
 \label{eq:W-sigma}
\end{equation}
For every complex $\sigma$ for which the terms are interpreted consistently
as tempered distributions,
\begin{equation}
 W_\sigma(x)=2^\sigma
 \bigl(W_{\sigma-1}(2x+1)-W_{\sigma-1}(2x-1)\bigr).
 \label{eq:Weyl-refinement}
\end{equation}
If $n-1<\beta<n$, this becomes
\begin{equation}
 \mathfrak D_+^\beta\Up(x)
 =2^\beta\left[
  (\mathfrak I_+^{n-\beta}\Up^{(n-1)})(2x+1)
 -(\mathfrak I_+^{n-\beta}\Up^{(n-1)})(2x-1)
 \right].
 \label{eq:Weyl-real-order}
\end{equation}
At $\beta=1$ it reduces to Rvachev's differential--difference
formula~\eqref{eq:up-dde}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Apply $\mathfrak D_+^{\sigma-1}$ to
\cref{eq:up-dde}.  Positive dilation obeys
\[
 \mathfrak D_+^\alpha[f(2x+a)]
 =2^\alpha(\mathfrak D_+^\alpha f)(2x+a).
\]
The prefactor $2$ in \cref{eq:up-dde} therefore produces $2^\sigma$.
Equivalently, multiply the Fourier refinement
\cref{eq:phi-refine} by $(\ii\xi)^\sigma$ and invert.  Finally,
$W_{\beta-1}=\mathfrak I_+^{n-\beta}\Up^{(n-1)}$ by equality of Fourier
multipliers.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[right hierarchy and reflection]
Because $\Up$ is even,
\begin{equation}
 \mathfrak D_-^\sigma\Up(x)
 =\mathfrak D_+^\sigma\Up(-x).
 \label{eq:Weyl-reflection}
\end{equation}
The corresponding right-sided refinement is obtained by reflecting
\cref{eq:Weyl-refinement}.
\end{corollary}

\subsection{Riesz symmetrization}

For real $0<\beta<2$, define the Riesz fractional Laplacian independently by
\begin{equation}
 \widehat{(-\Delta)^{\beta/2}f}(\xi)
 =|\xi|^\beta\widehat f(\xi).
 \label{eq:Riesz-Fourier}
\end{equation}
When $\cos(\pi\beta/2)\ne0$,
\begin{equation}
 (-\Delta)^{\beta/2}f
 =\frac{\mathfrak D_+^\beta f+\mathfrak D_-^\beta f}
 {2\cos(\pi\beta/2)}.
 \label{eq:Riesz-Weyl}
\end{equation}
At $\beta=1$ the quotient is understood by the Fourier definition (or via the
Hilbert-transform derivative), not by separately evaluating the vanishing
numerator and denominator.

\section{Generalized Stieltjes transforms of complex order}
\label{sec:Stieltjes}

The ordinary Cauchy transform is only the order-$1$ member of a much richer
family.  For real $z>1$ and $\lambda\in\C$, define
\begin{equation}
 S_\lambda(z)
 =\int_{-1}^1\frac{\Up(t)}{(z-t)^\lambda}\dd t
 =\E(z-Z)^{-\lambda},
 \label{eq:S-lambda}
\end{equation}
where the real logarithm fixes the power.  Analytic continuation in $z$ is
available on the slit plane after choosing a branch.

\subsection{Moment, derivative, and dyadic-order hierarchies}

\begin{theorem}[complete generalized Stieltjes hierarchy]
\label{thm:Stieltjes-hierarchy}
For fixed $z>1$, $S_\lambda(z)$ is entire in $\lambda$.  For $|z|>1$,
\begin{equation}
 S_\lambda(z)
 =z^{-\lambda}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}
   \frac{(\lambda)_{2k}}{(2k)!}\frac{c_k}{z^{2k}},
 \label{eq:S-moment-series}
\end{equation}
locally normally in $(\lambda,z)$.  Its $z$ derivatives satisfy
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\dd^n}{\dd z^n}S_\lambda(z)
 =(-1)^n(\lambda)_nS_{\lambda+n}(z).
 \label{eq:S-derivative-hierarchy}
\end{equation}
Moreover,
\begin{equation}
 S_\lambda(z)
 =2^{\lambda-1}\int_{-1}^{1}S_\lambda(2z-v)\dd v,
 \label{eq:S-integral-refinement}
\end{equation}
and, for $\lambda\ne1$,
\begin{equation}
 S_\lambda(z)
 =\frac{2^{\lambda-1}}{1-\lambda}
  \left(S_{\lambda-1}(2z+1)-S_{\lambda-1}(2z-1)\right).
 \label{eq:S-order-lowering}
\end{equation}
At $\lambda=1$, if
\begin{equation}
 L(z)=\E\log(z-Z),
 \label{eq:log-potential}
\end{equation}
then
\begin{equation}
 S_1(z)=L(2z+1)-L(2z-1).
 \label{eq:S1-log-difference}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For $z>1$, $\log(z-Z)$ is bounded, so
$S_\lambda(z)=\E\exp[-\lambda\log(z-Z)]$ is entire.  Expanding
$(1-Z/z)^{-\lambda}$ and using symmetry removes odd moments, proving
\cref{eq:S-moment-series}.  Differentiation under the integral gives
\cref{eq:S-derivative-hierarchy}.

The random series satisfies
\begin{equation}
 Z\overset d=\frac{V+Z'}2,
 \qquad V\sim\operatorname{Unif}[-1,1],
 \label{eq:Z-smoothing}
\end{equation}
with $Z'$ an independent copy.  Conditioning on $V$ gives
\cref{eq:S-integral-refinement}.  Changing variables turns its integral into
$\int_{2z-1}^{2z+1}S_\lambda(w)\dd w$.  Since
$S_{\lambda-1}'=(1-\lambda)S_\lambda$, this proves
\cref{eq:S-order-lowering}.  Taking the removable limit at $\lambda=1$ and
using $L'=S_1$ gives \cref{eq:S1-log-difference}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Interpolation between Appell polynomials and resolvents]
For $m\in\N_0$,
\begin{equation}
 S_{-m}(z)=\E(z-Z)^m
 \label{eq:S-negative-polynomial}
\end{equation}
is a rational-coefficient Appell polynomial.  Positive integer orders are
ordinary higher resolvents.  Thus \cref{eq:S-order-lowering} is a single
complex-order hierarchy connecting the polynomial and resolvent sides.
\end{remark}

\subsection{One-sided version}

Define, for $z>1$,
\begin{equation}
 R_\lambda(z)=\E(z-Y)^{-\lambda}.
 \label{eq:R-lambda}
\end{equation}
Since $Y\overset d=(U+Y')/2$ with $U$ uniform on $[0,1]$,
\begin{align}
 R_\lambda(z)
 &=2^\lambda\int_0^1R_\lambda(2z-u)\dd u,
 \label{eq:R-integral-refinement}\\
 &=\frac{2^\lambda}{1-\lambda}
   \left(R_{\lambda-1}(2z)-R_{\lambda-1}(2z-1)\right),
 \quad \lambda\ne1.
 \label{eq:R-order-lowering}
\end{align}
The centered and one-sided transforms are related by
\begin{equation}
 S_\lambda(z)=2^{-\lambda}
 R_\lambda\!\left(\frac{z+1}{2}\right).
 \label{eq:S-R-relation}
\end{equation}
For $\lambda>0$, $S_\lambda$ and $R_\lambda$ are completely monotone on their
real domains; all alternating derivatives are explicitly positive by
\cref{eq:S-derivative-hierarchy}.

\section{Complex-order resolvents involving \texorpdfstring{$F$}{F} and the inverse}
\label{sec:complex-resolvents}

\subsection{Distribution and survival resolvents}

For $z>1$ and $\lambda\ne1$, Fubini or integration by parts gives
\begin{align}
 \int_0^1\frac{F(x)}{(z-x)^\lambda}\dd x
 &=\frac{R_{\lambda-1}(z)-(z-1)^{1-\lambda}}{1-\lambda},
 \label{eq:F-complex-resolvent}\\
 \int_0^1\frac{1-F(x)}{(z-x)^\lambda}\dd x
 &=\frac{z^{1-\lambda}-R_{\lambda-1}(z)}{1-\lambda}.
 \label{eq:survival-complex-resolvent}
\end{align}
At $\lambda=1$, the removable limits are
\begin{align}
 \int_0^1\frac{F(x)}{z-x}\dd x
 &=\E\log(z-Y)-\log(z-1),
 \label{eq:F-log-resolvent}\\
 \int_0^1\frac{1-F(x)}{z-x}\dd x
 &=\log z-\E\log(z-Y).
 \label{eq:survival-log-resolvent}
\end{align}
These extend the integer resolvent formulas in the corpus to arbitrary complex
order.

\subsection{Inverse-composition resolvents}

\begin{theorem}[complex inverse-resolvent transmutation]
\label{thm:inverse-resolvent}
Let $H$ be absolutely continuous on $[0,1]$, $z>1$, and $\lambda\ne1$.  Then
\begin{align}
 T_{\lambda,H}(z)
 &:=\int_0^1(z-y)^{-\lambda}H(Q(y))\dd y
 \label{eq:T-def}\\
 &=\frac{H(0)z^{1-\lambda}-H(1)(z-1)^{1-\lambda}}
        {1-\lambda}
   +\frac1{1-\lambda}
    \int_0^1(z-F(x))^{1-\lambda}H'(x)\dd x.
 \label{eq:T-transmutation}
\end{align}
At $\lambda=1$,
\begin{equation}
 T_{1,H}(z)
 =H(0)\log z-H(1)\log(z-1)
  +\int_0^1H'(x)\log(z-F(x))\dd x.
 \label{eq:T-log-limit}
\end{equation}
Furthermore,
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\dd^n}{\dd z^n}T_{\lambda,H}(z)
 =(-1)^n(\lambda)_nT_{\lambda+n,H}(z).
 \label{eq:T-derivative-hierarchy}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Substitute $y=F(x)$:
\[
 T_{\lambda,H}(z)
 =\int_0^1(z-F(x))^{-\lambda}H(x)F'(x)\dd x.
\]
Since
$\dd(z-F(x))^{1-\lambda}=-(1-\lambda)
(z-F(x))^{-\lambda}F'(x)\dd x$, integration by parts proves
\cref{eq:T-transmutation}.  Expanding at $1-\lambda=0$ gives
\cref{eq:T-log-limit}; differentiation under the integral gives the hierarchy.
\end{proof}

For $H(x)=x^p$ with $p>0$,
\begin{equation}
 \int_0^1(z-y)^{-\lambda}Q(y)^p\dd y
 =\frac{-(z-1)^{1-\lambda}
  +p\int_0^1x^{p-1}(z-F(x))^{1-\lambda}\dd x}{1-\lambda}.
 \label{eq:Q-power-complex-resolvent}
\end{equation}
This is the complex-order extension of the inverse logarithmic resolvent found
in the latest corpus report.

\subsection{Large-\texorpdfstring{$z$}{z} mixed-moment series}

For $|z|>1$,
\begin{equation}
 T_{\lambda,H}(z)
 =z^{-\lambda}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}
  \frac{(\lambda)_n}{n!z^n}
  \int_0^1y^nH(Q(y))\dd y.
 \label{eq:T-moment-series}
\end{equation}
For $H(x)=x^p$, integration by parts after $y=F(x)$ gives
\begin{equation}
 \int_0^1y^nQ(y)^p\dd y
 =\frac1{n+1}
  -\frac{p}{n+1}\int_0^1x^{p-1}F(x)^{n+1}\dd x.
 \label{eq:mixed-inverse-moment}
\end{equation}
Thus every coefficient is reduced to a beta-type Fabius integral.  When $p$
and $n$ are nonnegative integers, finite dyadic formulas and rational moments
can be inserted wherever the existing corpus machinery applies.

\section{Riesz potentials and the fractional Laplacian of \texorpdfstring{$\Up$}{up}}
\label{sec:Riesz}

This section gives the cleanest new transform consequence.  Compact support
turns the nonlocal exterior fractional Laplacian into the generalized
Stieltjes hierarchy exactly, not merely asymptotically.

\subsection{Exact exterior fractional Laplacian}

For $0<\beta<2$, use the standard one-dimensional normalization
\cite{Kwasnicki2017}:
\begin{equation}
 C_{1,\beta}
 =\frac{\beta2^{\beta-1}\Gamma((1+\beta)/2)}
       {\sqrt\pi\,\Gamma(1-\beta/2)}.
 \label{eq:C1beta}
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
 (-\Delta)^{\beta/2}f(x)
 =C_{1,\beta}\PV\int_{\R}
   \frac{f(x)-f(t)}{|x-t|^{1+\beta}}\dd t.
 \label{eq:frac-lap-singular}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[exact exterior Stieltjes representation]
\label{thm:exterior-Riesz}
For $0<\beta<2$ and $|x|>1$,
\begin{equation}
 (-\Delta)^{\beta/2}\Up(x)
 =-C_{1,\beta}S_{1+\beta}(|x|).
 \label{eq:exterior-Riesz-Stieltjes}
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\begin{equation}
 (-\Delta)^{\beta/2}\Up(x)
 =-C_{1,\beta}|x|^{-1-\beta}
  \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}
   \frac{(1+\beta)_{2k}}{(2k)!}
   \frac{c_k}{|x|^{2k}}.
 \label{eq:exterior-Riesz-series}
\end{equation}
The first terms are
\begin{align}
 (-\Delta)^{\beta/2}\Up(x)
 ={}&-C_{1,\beta}|x|^{-1-\beta}
 \left[1+\frac{(1+\beta)(2+\beta)}{18x^2}\right.\nonumber\\
 &\left.\hspace{25mm}
 +\frac{19(1+\beta)(2+\beta)(3+\beta)(4+\beta)}{16200x^4}
 +O(x^{-6})\right].
 \label{eq:exterior-Riesz-first}
\end{align}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For $|x|>1$, $\Up(x)=0$ and the singularity is absent.  Thus
\[
 (-\Delta)^{\beta/2}\Up(x)
 =-C_{1,\beta}\int_{-1}^{1}
  \frac{\Up(t)}{|x-t|^{1+\beta}}\dd t.
\]
For $x>1$ this is $-C_{1,\beta}S_{1+\beta}(x)$.  Evenness gives the same formula
with $|x|$ for $x<-1$.  Insert \cref{eq:S-moment-series} and the exact moments
\cref{eq:first-c}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[sign and conservation]
The exterior fractional Laplacian is strictly negative.  At the center,
\begin{align}
 (-\Delta)^{\beta/2}\Up(0)
 &=C_{1,\beta}\int_{\R}\frac{1-\Up(t)}{|t|^{1+\beta}}\dd t\nonumber\\
 &=2C_{1,\beta}\left(
   \int_0^1\frac{1-\Up(t)}{t^{1+\beta}}\dd t+\frac1\beta
   \right)>0.
 \label{eq:Riesz-center-positive}
\end{align}
Moreover,
\begin{equation}
 \int_{\R}(-\Delta)^{\beta/2}\Up(x)\dd x=0.
 \label{eq:Riesz-zero-mass}
\end{equation}
Hence at least one zero occurs on each half-axis.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The exterior sign follows from \cref{eq:exterior-Riesz-Stieltjes}.  Since
$\Up(0)=1$, the singular-integral definition at $0$ gives the first line of
\cref{eq:Riesz-center-positive}; splitting at $|t|=1$ gives the second.  The
function $(-\Delta)^{\beta/2}\Up$ is integrable because it is smooth on compact
sets and has the exterior decay \cref{eq:exterior-Riesz-series}.  Its integral
is therefore its Fourier transform at $0$, namely
$|0|^\beta\widehat\Up(0)=0$.  Evenness and the opposite signs at $0$ and outside
the support force a zero on each half-axis.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Riesz potentials}

For $0<\alpha<1$, the Riesz potential with multiplier $|\xi|^{-\alpha}$ is
\begin{equation}
 (I_\alpha^{\mathrm R}f)(x)
 =\kappa_\alpha\int_{\R}|x-t|^{\alpha-1}f(t)\dd t,
 \qquad
 \kappa_\alpha=
 \frac{\Gamma((1-\alpha)/2)}
      {2^\alpha\sqrt\pi\,\Gamma(\alpha/2)}.
 \label{eq:Riesz-potential}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[exterior Riesz-potential expansion]
For $0<\alpha<1$ and $|x|>1$,
\begin{equation}
 I_\alpha^{\mathrm R}\Up(x)
 =\kappa_\alpha S_{1-\alpha}(|x|)
 =\kappa_\alpha|x|^{\alpha-1}
  \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}
   \frac{(1-\alpha)_{2k}}{(2k)!}
   \frac{c_k}{|x|^{2k}}.
 \label{eq:Riesz-potential-series}
\end{equation}
In particular, the leading far-field coefficient is exactly the unit mass of
$\Up$; all corrections are rational polynomials in $\alpha$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{conjecture}[one-node law]
\label{conj:one-node}
For every $0<\beta<2$, the even function
$(-\Delta)^{\beta/2}\Up$ has exactly two simple zeros $\pm a_\beta$.
It is positive on $(-a_\beta,a_\beta)$ and negative outside.  Moreover,
$a_\beta$ is strictly decreasing, with
\begin{equation}
 \lim_{\beta\downarrow0}a_\beta=1,
 \qquad
 \lim_{\beta\uparrow2}a_\beta=\frac12.
 \label{eq:one-node-limits}
\end{equation}
\end{conjecture}

The endpoint $1/2$ is natural: on $(0,1/2)$,
$-\Up''=F''=4F'(2x)>0$, while the integer second derivative changes its
Thue--Morse cell sign across $x=1/2$.  Proving uniqueness for noninteger order
will likely require a variation-diminishing or total-positivity principle for
the singular kernel combined with the special unimodality of $\Up$.

\section{Fractional energies and autocorrelation transforms}
\label{sec:fractional-energy}

The integer derivative norm law in the corpus samples a continuous spectral
Mellin transform at even integers.  Define
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal E(s)
 =\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\R}|\xi|^s|\phi(\xi)|^2\dd\xi,
 \qquad \Re s>-1.
 \label{eq:E-s}
\end{equation}
The sinc product decays faster than every power, so this is holomorphic in the
half-plane $\Re s>-1$.

\begin{proposition}[log-convex fractional energy]
For real $s>-1$, $\mathcal E(s)>0$ and $\log\mathcal E(s)$ is convex.  At even
integers,
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal E(2n)=\norm{\Up^{(n)}}_2^2
 =2^{n(n+1)}\mathcal E(0).
 \label{eq:E-even}
\end{equation}
Consequently, for $2n\le s\le2n+2$,
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal E(s)
 \le \mathcal E(0)\,2^{(n+1)(s-n)}.
 \label{eq:E-logconvex-bound}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Positivity is immediate.  Hölder's inequality applied to
$|\xi|^s|\phi|^2\dd\xi$ proves log-convexity.  Parseval gives the first equality
in \cref{eq:E-even}; the second is the existing derivative-equimeasurability
law.  Convex interpolation between the values at $2n$ and $2n+2$ gives
\cref{eq:E-logconvex-bound}.
\end{proof}

For a shift $a\in\R$, define the fractional autocorrelation
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal C_s(a)
 =\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\R}
  \e^{\ii\xi a}|\xi|^s|\phi(\xi)|^2\dd\xi.
 \label{eq:C-s-a}
\end{equation}
Since $\Up$ is even,
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal C_s(a)
 =(-\Delta_a)^{s/2}(\Up*\Up)(a),
 \qquad
 \mathcal C_s(0)=\mathcal E(s).
 \label{eq:C-fractional-correlation}
\end{equation}
Thus the entire family of fractional Sobolev energies and shifted correlations
is encoded by the same lacunary sinc product.  For $s=2n$, this reduces to
integer derivatives; for noninteger $s$, it is a new continuous interpolation
problem closely related to the shifted Stieltjes--Wigert spectral measure in
the derivative-norm report.

\section{Definite integrals and transform catalog}
\label{sec:definite-catalog}

This section gathers a practical set of exact reductions.  Most of the first
group is already in the corpus; several composition identities at the end are
new elementary consequences.

\subsection{CDF--survival master identities}

Let $G$ be absolutely continuous on $[0,1]$.  Then
\begin{align}
 \int_0^1G'(x)F(x)\dd x
 &=G(1)-\E G(Y),
 \label{eq:CDF-master}\\
 \int_0^1G'(x)(1-F(x))\dd x
 &=\E G(Y)-G(0).
 \label{eq:survival-master}
\end{align}
Examples include
\begin{align}
 \int_0^1x^{s-1}F(x)\dd x
 &=\frac{1-\Mell(s)}s,
 \label{eq:Mellin-F}\\
 \int_0^1x^{s-1}\Up(x)\dd x
 &=\frac{\Mell(s)}s,
 \label{eq:Mellin-half-up}\\
 \int_0^1\e^{tx}F(x)\dd x
 &=\frac{\e^t-P(t)}t,
 \label{eq:Laplace-F}\\
 \int_0^1\e^{tx}\Up(x)\dd x
 &=\frac{P(t)-1}t.
 \label{eq:Laplace-half-up}
\end{align}
The apparent singularities at $s=0$ or $t=0$ are removable.  Differentiating
in the parameters generates logarithmic and polynomially weighted families.

\subsection{Quantile transport and power duality}

For every integrable $H$,
\begin{equation}
 \int_0^1H(Q(y))\dd y
 =\int_0^1H(x)F'(x)\dd x
 =\E H(Y).
 \label{eq:quantile-transport}
\end{equation}
For $\Re p,\Re q>0$,
\begin{equation}
 p\int_0^1x^{p-1}F(x)^q\dd x
 +q\int_0^1y^{q-1}Q(y)^p\dd y=1.
 \label{eq:power-duality}
\end{equation}
The universal beta family is
\begin{equation}
 \int_0^1F(x)^a(1-F(x))^bF'(x)\dd x
 =B(a+1,b+1),
 \qquad \Re a,\Re b>-1.
 \label{eq:universal-beta}
\end{equation}
Using $F'=2\Up(2x-1)$,
\begin{equation}
 \int_0^1F(x)^a\Up(x)^b\Up(2x-1)\dd x
 =\frac12B(a+1,b+1).
 \label{eq:up-beta-combination}
\end{equation}
This gives, for example,
\begin{equation}
 \int_0^1F(x)(1-F(x))F'(x)\dd x=\frac16.
 \label{eq:F-survival-density}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Full-line analytic kernels and Fourier transforms}

If $h(t)=\sum_{n\ge0}a_nt^n$ is entire, then
\begin{equation}
 \int_{-1}^1h(t)\Up(t)\dd t
 =\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{2k}c_k.
 \label{eq:entire-kernel-up}
\end{equation}
The Fourier, bilateral Laplace, Bessel, and generalized Stieltjes transforms
are therefore
\begin{align}
 \int_{-1}^1\e^{\ii\xi t}\Up(t)\dd t
 &=\prod_{j\ge1}\sinc(\xi/2^j),
 \label{eq:Fourier-up-catalog}\\
 \int_{-1}^1\e^{st}\Up(t)\dd t
 &=\prod_{j\ge1}\frac{\sinh(s/2^j)}{s/2^j},
 \label{eq:bilateral-Laplace-up}\\
 \int_{-1}^1J_0(at)\Up(t)\dd t
 &=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}
   \frac{(-1)^k(a^2/4)^k}{(k!)^2}c_k,
 \label{eq:Bessel-up}\\
 \int_{-1}^1(z-t)^{-\lambda}\Up(t)\dd t
 &=S_\lambda(z).
 \label{eq:Stieltjes-catalog}
\end{align}

\subsection{Product and order-statistic integrals}

Let $m,n\ge1$, let $Y_1,Y_2,\ldots$ be independent copies of $Y$,
$A_m=\max(Y_1,\ldots,Y_m)$, and
$B_n=\min(Y_{m+1},\ldots,Y_{m+n})$.  Then
\begin{equation}
 F(x)^m(1-F(x))^n
 =\Prob(A_m\le x<B_n),
 \label{eq:order-gap-pointwise}
\end{equation}
and hence, for real $p>-1$,
\begin{equation}
 \int_0^1x^pF(x)^m(1-F(x))^n\dd x
 =\frac1{p+1}\E\left[(B_n^{p+1}-A_m^{p+1})_+\right].
 \label{eq:order-gap-integral}
\end{equation}
This is an exact probabilistic closed form even when no reduction to rational
moments is available.  The Lambert endpoint theory controls its large-$m$ or
large-$n$ regimes.

\subsection{Compositions with the inverse density}

Equation~\ref{eq:Qprime} yields a useful family that appears not to have been
isolated in the audited integral reports.  For $p>-1$,
\begin{align}
 \int_0^1\left(F'(Q(y))\right)^p\dd y
 &=\int_0^1F'(x)^{p+1}\dd x
 =2^p\int_{-1}^1\Up(t)^{p+1}\dd t,
 \label{eq:inverse-density-composition}\\
 \int_0^1\Up(2Q(y)-1)^p\dd y
 &=\int_{-1}^1\Up(t)^{p+1}\dd t,
 \label{eq:up-quantile-composition}\\
 \int_0^1Q'(y)^{-p}\dd y
 &=2^p\norm{\Up}_{p+1}^{p+1}.
 \label{eq:Qprime-negative-power}
\end{align}
The proof is the single substitution $y=F(x)$ followed by $t=2x-1$.
These formulas tie inverse-derivative integrals directly to the complete
$L^p$ spectrum of the compact density.

\section{Indefinite integral catalog}
\label{sec:indefinite-catalog}

To avoid ambiguity, an ``indefinite integral'' below means the normalized
primitive with zero initial data; arbitrary constants or lower-degree
polynomials can be restored afterward.

\subsection{Fabius and reflected Fabius functions}

For $0\le x\le1$,
\begin{align}
 \int_0^xF(t)\dd t
 &=F(x/2),
 \label{eq:first-F-antiderivative}\\
 J^nF(x)
 &=2^{\binom n2}F(x/2^n),
 \label{eq:nth-F-antiderivative}\\
 \int_0^x(1-F(t))\dd t
 &=x-F(x/2),
 \label{eq:first-survival-antiderivative}\\
 J^n(1-F)(x)
 &=\frac{x^n}{n!}-2^{\binom n2}F(x/2^n).
 \label{eq:nth-survival-antiderivative}
\end{align}
Because $F(1-x)=1-F(x)$, these are also primitives of the reflected Fabius
function.  Polynomial and arbitrary complex-power weights are supplied by
\cref{eq:weighted-newton-volterra}.

\subsection{Rvachev folds}

On $[-1,0]$, $\Up(x)=F(x+1)$, while on $[0,1]$,
$\Up(x)=1-F(x)$.  Thus a normalized first primitive based at $-1$ is
\begin{equation}
 \int_{-1}^x\Up(t)\dd t
 =\begin{cases}
 F((x+1)/2),&-1\le x\le0,\\[1mm]
 1-F((1-x)/2),&0\le x\le1,\\[1mm]
 1,&x\ge1.
 \end{cases}
 \label{eq:up-first-piecewise}
\end{equation}
Higher integer primitives follow either by repeated use of
\cref{eq:nth-F-antiderivative} on each fold or from the exact truncated moment
\begin{equation}
 I_{-1+}^n\Up(x)
 =\frac1{(n-1)!}\E[(x-Z)_+^{n-1}].
 \label{eq:up-n-incomplete-moment}
\end{equation}
For $x\ge1$ this becomes an Appell polynomial in $x$ with rational
coefficients.

\subsection{Inverse Fabius function}

The normalized first primitive is \cref{eq:Q-first-primitive}.  More generally,
for $\alpha>0$ and $p>0$,
\begin{equation}
 I_{0+}^\alpha Q^p(y)
 =\frac{p}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}
   \int_0^{Q(y)}x^{p-1}(y-F(x))^\alpha\dd x,
 \label{eq:inverse-fractional-antiderivative-catalog}
\end{equation}
while the Fabius-clock primitive is the elementary gamma law
\cref{eq:Q-clock-power-I}.  These two answers are complementary: the first
retains Euclidean probability time and has a Fabius kernel; the second uses
the inverse clock and becomes algebraic.

\section{Conjectures and frontier directions}
\label{sec:frontiers}

\subsection{Phase-resolved Caputo endpoint correction}

The proof of \cref{thm:Q-fractional-endpoint} discards the narrow layer
$t\approx1$ where $F(qt)/F(q)$ is non-negligible.  The width of that layer is
controlled by the same saddle parameter that appears in the inverse
Lambert--$W$ expansion.

\begin{conjecture}[Caputo endpoint phase]
\label{conj:Caputo-phase}
For fixed $r>0$ and $0<\beta<1$, there exists a bounded one-periodic function
$A_{r,\beta}$ of the repository's endpoint phase $\vartheta(y)$ such that
\begin{equation}
 \frac{{}^{\Ccap}D_{0+}^\beta Q(y)^r}
 {Q(y)^r y^{-\beta}/\Gamma(1-\beta)}
 =1+\frac{A_{r,\beta}(\vartheta(y))}{\rho(y)}
  +O\!\left(\rho(y)^{-2}\right),
 \label{eq:Caputo-phase-conjecture}
\end{equation}
where $\rho(y)$ is defined by \cref{eq:rho-y}.  The periodic term should be
nonconstant but extremely small, as in the endpoint density asymptotics.
\end{conjecture}

A route to proof is a boundary-layer rescaling of
\cref{eq:Caputo-endpoint-ratio}, using the all-orders ratio asymptotic for
$F(qt)/F(q)$ uniformly when $1-t=O(\rho(y)^{-1})$.

\subsection{The one-node law and total positivity}

Conjecture \ref{conj:one-node} is stronger than the sign information proved in
\cref{sec:Riesz}.  Three plausible approaches are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item represent the interior fractional Laplacian as a signed Stieltjes
      convolution and prove a variation-diminishing property;
\item use the infinite convolution construction of $\Up$ and preservation of
      single crossing under uniform averaging;
\item establish strict total positivity for the appropriate fractional kernel
      after subtracting the center value.
\end{enumerate}
A certified interval-arithmetic computation over a dense mesh in $\beta$ would
be a useful intermediate theorem, but not a substitute for uniqueness.

\subsection{Fractional spectral interpolation}

At the even integers,
\begin{equation}
 \log_2\frac{\mathcal E(2n)}{\mathcal E(0)}=n(n+1).
 \label{eq:E-quadratic-samples}
\end{equation}
The explicit spectral measure from the derivative-norm report is a
nonclassical representative of a shifted Stieltjes--Wigert moment problem.
Determining the full Mellin transform $\mathcal E(s)$ would interpolate those
quadratic samples and could reveal a small $2$-periodic correction to the
quadratic envelope
\begin{equation}
 \frac{s^2}{4}+\frac{s}{2}.
 \label{eq:E-quadratic-envelope}
\end{equation}
The exact integer samples alone do not force such periodicity; this is an
open spectral-analysis direction rather than a formal conjecture at present.

\subsection{General base and \texorpdfstring{$q$}{q}-deformation}

Replace $2^{-j}$ by normalized $b^{-j}$ weights.  The clock conjugacy,
inverse transmutation, generalized Stieltjes hierarchy, and exterior Riesz
expansion survive with minimal change.  The order-lowering factors become
$b$-dependent, while the rational moment coefficients become $q=1/b$
objects.  The resulting family should provide a direct fractional-calculus
bridge between the base-$b$ random series and Gaussian $q$-binomial
arithmetic.

\subsection{Arithmetic of fractional constants}

For rational $\beta$ and algebraic $z>1$, the values
\begin{equation}
 S_\beta(z),
 \qquad
 {}^{\Ccap}D_{0+}^\beta Q(y)^r,
 \qquad
 \mathcal E(\beta),
 \qquad
 a_\beta
 \label{eq:fractional-constants}
\end{equation}
are natural new constants.  The Stieltjes series expresses the first through
rational moments and gamma-polynomial coefficients.  Pad\'e approximation,
Mahler-type functional equations, and denominator estimates from the
$q$-binomial formulas may offer arithmetic information, although no
irrationality claim is currently justified.

\section{Lean formalization roadmap}
\label{sec:Lean}

The latest repository merge already formalizes the normalized integer
Volterra operator and the exact Fabius primitive ladder.  The new results can
be layered on top in increasing order of analytic difficulty.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Composition conjugacy.}  Define $\mathcal C_F$ and
      $\mathcal C_Q$ on suitable function spaces; prove they are inverse
      order isomorphisms and transport semigroups.
\item \textbf{Fabius-clock integrals.}  Formalize
      \cref{eq:IF-kernel} first for positive real orders and continuous
      functions using the substitution theorem.  The power law then reduces
      to Mathlib's beta integral.
\item \textbf{First-order Caputo transmutation.}  The proof of
      \cref{eq:inverse-Caputo-transmutation} requires only absolute
      continuity, inverse differentiation on $(0,1)$, and a monotone change of
      variables.  This is likely the most accessible genuinely new theorem.
\item \textbf{Endpoint asymptotics.}  Package rapid variation and convexity
      into a dominated-convergence lemma for kernels
      $t^{r-1}(1-F(qt)/F(q))^{-\beta}$.
\item \textbf{Generalized Stieltjes hierarchy.}  Formalize the bounded random
      variable $Z$, complex powers for real $z>1$, the moment series, and the
      smoothing equation.  The order-lowering identity is then elementary.
\item \textbf{Exterior Riesz theorem.}  Avoid principal values entirely by
      restricting first to $|x|>1$; compact support reduces the proof to an
      ordinary integral and the Stieltjes identity.
\item \textbf{Weyl and full Riesz operators.}  These require Fourier
      multiplier and tempered-distribution infrastructure and are a later
      target.
\end{enumerate}

The numerical script can be converted into certified rational interval checks
for selected transform identities and the conjectural zero locations, but the
formal statements should remain independent of floating-point evidence.

\section{Conclusion}

The ordinary integral theory of the Fabius--Rvachev system is already unusually
rigid: differentiation is conjugate to dyadic dilation, integer integration
runs backward along the same orbit, and inverse substitution converts many
areas into moments.  The present investigation shows that this rigidity
continues in several genuinely fractional directions.

The Fabius and inverse clocks give exact conjugate fractional calculi with
semigroups, gamma power laws, and Mittag--Leffler eigenfunctions.  In ordinary
time, the inverse-composition transmutation removes the singular derivative of
$Q$ and replaces it by a Fabius-kernel integral; this leads to rigorous
fractional endpoint laws.  Standard fractional derivatives of $F$ are exact
fractional primitives of $\Up$, while whole-line Weyl derivatives satisfy a
complex-order refinement hierarchy.  On the transform side, one entire
function $S_\lambda(z)$ unifies Appell polynomials, Cauchy resolvents, logarithmic
potentials, Riesz tails, and fractional-Laplacian exterior fields.

The strongest next targets are now sharply posed: prove the one-node law,
resolve the first phase-dependent correction to the inverse Caputo endpoint
asymptotic, determine the continuous fractional-energy interpolation of the
integer derivative norms, and formalize the first-order transmutation and
Stieltjes hierarchy in Lean.  None requires inventing a new ad hoc numerical
object; each is anchored in an exact identity proved above.
