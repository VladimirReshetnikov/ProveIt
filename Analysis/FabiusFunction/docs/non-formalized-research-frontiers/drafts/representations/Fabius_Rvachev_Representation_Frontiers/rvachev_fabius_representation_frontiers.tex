\begin{abstract}
This report audits the LaTeX corpus in the repository subtree
\path{Analysis/FabiusFunction/docs}, including its living expositions, consolidated frontier
volume, active draft families, archive lineages, and paper transcriptions \cite{Repository}.  The repository already contains an unusually
broad theory of the Fabius function, Rvachev's \(\Up\)-function, their dyadic values,
Thue--Morse expansions, Fourier products, moment and cumulant algebra, inverse asymptotics,
and spectral decay.  The objective here is therefore not to reproduce that material, but to
organize it as a representation atlas and then add constructions that are absent from the
audited corpus.

The principal new object is the Cauchy--Stieltjes transform
\[
 R(z)=\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\Up(x)}{z-x}\,\dd x.
\]
We derive its hyperbolic-product Laplace representation, its one-sided Fourier--Laplace
representation, an exact logarithmic fixed-point identity, the functional-differential law
\[
 R'(z)=2\bigl(R(2z+1)-R(2z-1)\bigr),
\]
and an all-orders Thue--Morse derivative tree.  Finite dyadic convolution laws yield an
explicit \(2^N\)-term logarithmic formula for \(R_N\), whose boundary jump is exactly the
finite Thue--Morse spline for the density.  This gives a new ``logarithm-to-spline'' bridge.

We also construct the Jacobi continued fraction of \(R\), equivalently the Stieltjes
continued fraction of the ordinary even-moment generating function.  Its coefficients are
positive rational numbers; the first forty are computed exactly.  The first coefficients are
\[
 \beta_1=\frac19,\quad \beta_2=\frac{32}{225},\quad
 \beta_3=\frac{619}{3675},\quad
 \beta_4=\frac{835232}{4640643}.
\]
The Denisov--Rakhmanov theorem proves \(\beta_n\to1/4\), while exact computation through
\(n=40\) supports strict monotonicity and a logarithmically corrected convergence law.
Further new representations include a trace-class Fredholm determinant for the Fourier
image, three equivalent meromorphic logarithmic-derivative expansions, a digamma/Hurwitz-
zeta hierarchy, exact one-sided Laplace and Fourier transforms of \(\Up\) and \(F\), and
pushforward transform identities for the inverse Fabius function.  A commented Python
program, exact CSV data, numerical checks, and figures accompany the report.
\end{abstract}

\section{Normalization and structural dictionary}

\subsection{The functions}

Let \(F:[0,1]\to[0,1]\) denote the bounded Fabius function \cite{Fabius1966,AriasTranslation}, extended by \(0\) on
\(( -\infty,0]\) and by \(1\) on \([1,\infty)\).  On the unit interval it satisfies
\begin{align}
 F(0)&=0, & F(1)&=1, & F(1-x)&=1-F(x), \label{eq:F-symmetry}\\
 F'(x)&=2F(2x) &&(0\le x\le\tfrac12). \label{eq:F-diff}
\end{align}
The Rvachev function is written
\begin{equation}
 \rho(x):=\Up(x)=
 \begin{cases}
 F(x+1),&-1\le x\le0,\\
 F(1-x),&0\le x\le1,\\
 0,&|x|\ge1.
 \end{cases}                                                \label{eq:rho-fold}
\end{equation}
Then \(\rho\) is even, strictly positive on \((-1,1)\), normalized by
\(\int\rho=1\), flat at \(\pm1\), and satisfies
\begin{equation}
 \rho'(x)=2\bigl(\rho(2x+1)-\rho(2x-1)\bigr).                \label{eq:rho-dde}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Fourier convention and basic transforms}

We use
\begin{equation}
 \widehat f(\xi)=\int_{\R}f(x)e^{-2\pi i\xi x}\,\dd x,
 \qquad
 f(x)=\int_{\R}\widehat f(\xi)e^{2\pi i\xi x}\,\dd\xi.       \label{eq:fourier-convention}
\end{equation}
Set
\begin{equation}
 \Phi(\xi):=\widehat\rho(\xi),
 \qquad
 \sincpi(z):=\frac{\sin(\pi z)}{\pi z},\quad \sincpi(0)=1. \label{eq:Phi-def}
\end{equation}
The classical products are
\begin{equation}
 \Phi(z)=\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}\sincpi\!\left(\frac{z}{2^n}\right)
 =\prod_{m=1}^{\infty}\cos\!\left(\frac{\pi z}{2^m}\right)^m. \label{eq:Phi-products}
\end{equation}
The scaling law is
\begin{equation}
 \Phi(z)=\sincpi(z)\Phi(z/2).                                \label{eq:Phi-scaling}
\end{equation}

\subsection{The probability model}

Let \(U_1,U_2,\dots\) be independent random variables, uniform on \([-1,1]\), and set
\begin{equation}
 X=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}2^{-k}U_k.                              \label{eq:X-series}
\end{equation}
Then \(X\) has density \(\rho\).  Equivalently,
\begin{equation}
 X\overset{\mathrm d}=\frac{U+X'}2,                           \label{eq:X-fixed-point}
\end{equation}
where \(U\sim\operatorname{Unif}[-1,1]\) and \(X'\) is an independent copy of \(X\).
The moment generating function is
\begin{equation}
 M(t):=\E e^{tX}
 =\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sinh(t/2^k)}{t/2^k}
 =\Phi\!\left(\frac{it}{2\pi}\right).                       \label{eq:M-product}
\end{equation}
The even moments are
\begin{equation}
 \mu_{2n}:=\E X^{2n}=\int_{-1}^{1}x^{2n}\rho(x)\,\dd x,
 \qquad \mu_{2n+1}=0.                                      \label{eq:moments}
\end{equation}
Finally, if
\begin{equation}
 Y=\frac{X+1}{2},                                             \label{eq:Y-affine}
\end{equation}
then \(F\) is the distribution function of \(Y\), and
\begin{equation}
 F'(x)=2\rho(2x-1),\qquad 0<x<1.                              \label{eq:F-density}
\end{equation}

\section{Audited representation atlas}

This section compresses the principal representation families already present in the
repository and places the new formulas beside them.  Proofs of the new entries begin in

\subsection{Real-space and probability representations of \(\rho\)}

\paragraph{Infinite convolution (\statusknown).}
Since \(2^{-k}U_k\) is uniform on \([-2^{-k},2^{-k}]\),
\begin{equation}
 \rho=\mathop{\ast}_{k=1}^{\infty}
 \left(2^{k-1}\one_{[-2^{-k},2^{-k}]}\right).                \label{eq:rho-convolution}
\end{equation}
This is the real-space counterpart of the sinc product.

\paragraph{Infinite cube integral (\statusderived).}
For every bounded continuous test function \(g\),
\begin{equation}
 \int_{-1}^{1}g(x)\rho(x)\,\dd x
 =\int_{[-1,1]^{\N}}g\!\left(\sum_{k\ge1}2^{-k}u_k\right)
   \prod_{k\ge1}\frac{\dd u_k}{2}.                           \label{eq:cube-pushforward}
\end{equation}
Formally, this may be written as a Dirac-delta integral for \(\rho(x)\).

\paragraph{Finite Thue--Morse splines (\statusknown).}
Put
\begin{equation}
 X_N=\sum_{k=1}^{N}2^{-k}U_k,
 \qquad
 a_{N,j}=\frac{2^N-1-2j}{2^N},
 \qquad
 \eps_j=(-1)^{\wt(j)}.                                      \label{eq:aNj}
\end{equation}
The density \(\rho_N\) of \(X_N\) is
\begin{equation}
 \rho_N(x)=\frac{2^{N(N-1)/2}}{(N-1)!}
 \sum_{j=0}^{2^N-1}\eps_j\,(x+a_{N,j})_+^{N-1}.             \label{eq:finite-spline}
\end{equation}
For each fixed derivative order, \(\rho_N\to\rho\) uniformly after \(N\) is sufficiently
large relative to that order.  The repository develops several centered and one-sided
variants with explicit error control.

\paragraph{Fourier inversion and cosine form (\statusknown).}
Because \(\Phi\) is real and even,
\begin{align}
 \rho(x)&=\int_{\R}\Phi(\xi)e^{2\pi i x\xi}\,\dd\xi,
                                                                  \label{eq:rho-fourier-inversion}\\
 \rho(x)&=2\int_0^{\infty}\Phi(\xi)\cos(2\pi x\xi)\,\dd\xi.
                                                                  \label{eq:rho-cosine}
\end{align}
The audited corpus also contains Poisson-summation formulas, Legendre expansions,
periodized cosine series, dyadic spline limits, and endpoint transseries.  They are not
rederived here.

\subsection{Product, zero, gamma, and exponential representations of \(\Phi\)}

\paragraph{Canonical zero product (\statusknown).}
Let \(\nu_2(n)\) be the exponent of \(2\) in \(n\).  The nonzero integer \(n\) is a zero of
multiplicity \(1+\nu_2(|n|)\), and
\begin{equation}
 \Phi(z)=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}
 \left(1-\frac{z^2}{n^2}\right)^{1+\nu_2(n)}.                \label{eq:canonical-product}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Gamma product (\statusderived).}
Euler's reflection identity gives
\begin{equation}
 \Phi(z)=\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}
 \frac{1}{\Gamma(1+z/2^k)\Gamma(1-z/2^k)}.                  \label{eq:gamma-product}
\end{equation}
This is the natural entry point for the Barnes and multiple-gamma compressions studied in
current repository drafts.

\paragraph{Spectral-zeta exponential (\statusknown).}
For \(|z|<1\),
\begin{equation}
 \log\Phi(z)
 =-\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}
 \frac{\zeta(2m)}{m(1-2^{-2m})}z^{2m}.                       \label{eq:logPhi-zeta}
\end{equation}
The coefficient sequence is the spectral zeta function
\begin{equation}
 Z_{\Phi}(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1+\nu_2(n)}{n^s}
 =\frac{\zeta(s)}{1-2^{-s}}.                                \label{eq:spectral-zeta}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Fredholm determinant (\statusnew).}
Section~\ref{sec:fredholm} upgrades \eqref{eq:canonical-product} to
\begin{equation}
 \Phi(z)=\det(I-z^2\cK),                                    \label{eq:fredholm-preview}
\end{equation}
for an explicit positive trace-class diagonal operator \(\cK\).  This makes
\eqref{eq:spectral-zeta} literally the trace zeta function of \(\cK\).

\subsection{Moment, cumulant, and Bell-polynomial representations}

The moment generating function has the convergent Taylor series
\begin{equation}
 M(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mu_{2n}\frac{t^{2n}}{(2n)!}.       \label{eq:M-moment-series}
\end{equation}
Its logarithm is
\begin{equation}
 \log M(t)=
 \sum_{m=1}^{\infty}
 \frac{2^{2m-1}B_{2m}}{m(2m)!(4^m-1)}t^{2m},                \label{eq:M-cumulant}
\end{equation}
so all moments are complete Bell polynomials in explicit Bernoulli/Mersenne cumulants.
These formulas, together with reciprocal Bell expansions for \(1/\Phi\), are developed
extensively in the repository.

\subsection{Transform representations of \(F\) and \(F^{-1}\)}

The CDF inversion formula gives
\begin{equation}
 F(x)=\frac12+\frac1\pi\int_0^{\infty}
 \Phi(\xi)\frac{\sin(2\pi(2x-1)\xi)}{\xi}\,\dd\xi,
 \qquad 0<x<1.                                               \label{eq:F-CDF-Fourier}
\end{equation}
Section~\ref{sec:fabius-transforms} adds closed Laplace and Fourier transforms of \(F\) as
a function, as well as transforms of its derivative measure and its inverse.

\section{The Cauchy--Stieltjes transform}\label{sec:cauchy}

\begin{definition}
For \(z\in\C\setminus[-1,1]\), define
\begin{equation}
 R(z)=\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\rho(x)}{z-x}\,\dd x=\E\frac1{z-X}. \label{eq:R-def}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

The transform \(R\) is odd because \(\rho\) is even.  It is an anti-Herglotz function:
when \(\operatorname{Im}z>0\),
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{Im}R(z)
 =-\operatorname{Im}z\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\rho(x)}{|z-x|^2}\,\dd x<0. \label{eq:R-anti-Herglotz}
\end{equation}
In particular, \(R\) has no zeros off the cut.  For real \(z>1\), \(R(z)>0\); for
\(z<-1\), \(R(z)<0\).

\begin{theorem}[Four complementary representations of \(R\)]\label{thm:R-representations}
The following formulas hold.

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item For \(|z|>1\),
\begin{equation}
 R(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\mu_{2n}}{z^{2n+1}}.          \label{eq:R-moment}
\end{equation}
\item For \(\operatorname{Re}z>1\),
\begin{equation}
 R(z)=\int_0^{\infty}e^{-zt}
 \prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sinh(t/2^k)}{t/2^k}\,\dd t.    \label{eq:R-Laplace}
\end{equation}
\item For \(\operatorname{Im}z>0\),
\begin{equation}
 R(z)=-2\pi i\int_0^{\infty}e^{2\pi i z\xi}\Phi(\xi)\,\dd\xi. \label{eq:R-Fourier-Laplace}
\end{equation}
For \(\operatorname{Im}z<0\), take the conjugate counterpart.
\item For \(-1<x<1\), the non-tangential boundary values satisfy
\begin{align}
 \rho(x)&=-\frac1\pi\lim_{y\downarrow0}\operatorname{Im}R(x+iy),
                                                               \label{eq:R-boundary-density}\\
 \PV\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\rho(t)}{x-t}\,\dd t
 &=\lim_{y\downarrow0}\operatorname{Re}R(x+iy)
 =2\pi\int_0^{\infty}\Phi(\xi)\sin(2\pi x\xi)\,\dd\xi.     \label{eq:R-boundary-Hilbert}
\end{align}
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For \(|z|>1\), expand \((z-X)^{-1}=z^{-1}\sum_{m\ge0}(X/z)^m\), use
\(|X|\le1\), and then symmetry.  This gives \eqref{eq:R-moment}.

For \(\operatorname{Re}z>1\),
\[
 \frac1{z-X}=\int_0^{\infty}e^{-(z-X)t}\,\dd t.
\]
Fubini's theorem is justified because \(X\le1\).  Taking expectation and using
\eqref{eq:M-product} yields \eqref{eq:R-Laplace}.

For \(\operatorname{Im}z>0\),
\[
 \frac1{z-x}=-2\pi i\int_0^{\infty}e^{2\pi i(z-x)\xi}\,\dd\xi.
\]
The exponential damping from \(\operatorname{Im}z>0\), together with the rapid decay of
\(\Phi\), permits Fubini and gives \eqref{eq:R-Fourier-Laplace}.  Finally,
Plemelj's boundary formula gives \eqref{eq:R-boundary-density}; taking real parts of
\eqref{eq:R-Fourier-Laplace} gives \eqref{eq:R-boundary-Hilbert}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The four formulas emphasize different structures.  The Laurent series exposes exact
rational moments; the Laplace integral exposes the hyperbolic product; the Fourier--Laplace
integral exposes spectral decay; and the boundary values recover both \(\rho\) and its
Hilbert transform.  Thus \(R\) is a single analytic object joining the moment, product,
and inversion theories.
\end{remark}

\section{A dyadic functional equation for the resolvent}

\subsection{Logarithmic fixed-point identity}

\begin{theorem}[Logarithmic fixed point]\label{thm:R-log-fixed}
For real \(z>1\),
\begin{equation}
 R(z)=\int_{-1}^{1}\rho(y)
 \log\!\left(\frac{2z+1-y}{2z-1-y}\right)\,\dd y.           \label{eq:R-log-fixed}
\end{equation}
The identity extends by analytic continuation to any simply connected subdomain of
\(\C\setminus[-1,1]\), with the logarithm chosen by continuation from large positive
\(z\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Use the fixed point \eqref{eq:X-fixed-point} and condition on \(X'\):
\begin{align*}
 R(z)
 &=\E\left[\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}
   \frac{\dd u}{z-u-X'/2}\right]\\
 &=\E\log\!\left(\frac{z+1/2-X'/2}{z-1/2-X'/2}\right),
\end{align*}
which is \eqref{eq:R-log-fixed} after multiplying numerator and denominator by \(2\).
\end{proof}

\subsection{Functional-differential equation}

\begin{theorem}[Resolvent equation]\label{thm:R-dde}
For every \(z\in\C\setminus[-1,1]\),
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{R'(z)=2\bigl(R(2z+1)-R(2z-1)\bigr).}                \label{eq:R-dde}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Differentiate \eqref{eq:R-log-fixed} first for \(z>1\):
\begin{align*}
 R'(z)
 &=\E\left(\frac{2}{2z+1-X'}-\frac{2}{2z-1-X'}\right)\\
 &=2\bigl(R(2z+1)-R(2z-1)\bigr).
\end{align*}
Both sides are holomorphic on \(\C\setminus[-1,1]\), so the identity theorem gives the
stated continuation.
\end{proof}

This is the transform-side analogue of the Rvachev equation \eqref{eq:rho-dde}.  It is
not merely a Fourier rephrasing: it acts on the spectral parameter of a Cauchy transform and
therefore interacts directly with moments, Pad\'e approximation, and orthogonal polynomials.

\subsection{All-order Thue--Morse derivative tree}

\begin{theorem}[Thue--Morse derivative orbit]\label{thm:R-derivative-tree}
For every integer \(n\ge0\),
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{
 R^{(n)}(z)=2^{n(n+1)/2}
 \sum_{j=0}^{2^n-1}\eps_j\,
 R\bigl(2^n z+2^n-1-2j\bigr).
 }                                                           \label{eq:R-derivative-tree}
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\begin{equation}
 \int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\rho(x)}{(z-x)^{n+1}}\,\dd x
 =\frac{(-1)^n2^{n(n+1)/2}}{n!}
 \sum_{j=0}^{2^n-1}\eps_j
 R\bigl(2^n z+2^n-1-2j\bigr).                               \label{eq:R-kernel-tree}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The case \(n=0\) is trivial and \(n=1\) is \eqref{eq:R-dde}.  Assume the formula at
order \(n\).  Differentiating contributes a factor \(2^n\), and then applying
\eqref{eq:R-dde} contributes another factor \(2\).  The exponent therefore changes by
\(n+1\), from \(n(n+1)/2\) to \((n+1)(n+2)/2\).

For the shifts, write \(a_{n,j}=2^n-1-2j\).  The two children are
\[
 2a_{n,j}+1=2^{n+1}-1-2(2j),
 \qquad
 2a_{n,j}-1=2^{n+1}-1-2(2j+1).
\]
The signs obey \(\eps_{2j}=\eps_j\) and \(\eps_{2j+1}=-\eps_j\), exactly matching the
subtraction in \eqref{eq:R-dde}.  This proves \eqref{eq:R-derivative-tree}.  Formula
\eqref{eq:R-kernel-tree} follows from
\[
 R^{(n)}(z)=(-1)^n n!\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\rho(x)}{(z-x)^{n+1}}\,\dd x.
\]
\end{proof}

Taking boundary imaginary parts in \eqref{eq:R-derivative-tree} recovers the familiar
iterated derivative formula for \(\rho\).  The new point is that the same finite
Thue--Morse orbit controls every higher Cauchy kernel, not only the density derivatives.

\subsection{Uniqueness in the Stieltjes class}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:R-uniqueness}
Among Cauchy transforms of probability measures supported on \([-1,1]\), \(R\) is the
unique solution of \eqref{eq:R-dde} satisfying
\begin{equation}
 R(z)=\frac1z+\bigO(z^{-3})\qquad(z\to\infty).               \label{eq:R-normalization}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let \(G(z)=\sum_{r\ge0}m_r z^{-r-1}\) be the Laurent expansion at infinity of any such
Cauchy transform.  The normalization gives \(m_0=1\) and \(m_1=0\).  Substitute this
expansion into \eqref{eq:R-dde}.  In the coefficient of \(z^{-r-2}\), the contribution
containing \(m_r\) is
\[
 -(r+1)m_r+\frac{r+1}{2^r}m_r
 =-(r+1)(1-2^{-r})m_r,
\]
while every other contribution contains only \(m_0,\ldots,m_{r-2}\).  Since the displayed
factor is nonzero for \(r\ge1\), the differential equation determines all moments
recursively.  The transform \(R\) is one solution, so \(G\) and \(R\) have identical
moments.  Probability measures on the compact interval \([-1,1]\) are determinate by their
moments (equivalently, polynomials are dense in \(C[-1,1]\)); hence the two measures and
their Cauchy transforms coincide.
\end{proof}

\section{Finite logarithmic resolvents and the spline boundary jump}

The finite random sum \(X_N\) has two exact representations: its density is a
Thue--Morse spline, while its Cauchy transform is a Thue--Morse sum of logarithms.  The
second formula appears to be absent from the audited corpus.

\begin{theorem}[Finite logarithmic formula]\label{thm:finite-log}
Let
\[
 R_N(z)=\E\frac1{z-X_N},
 \qquad X_N=\sum_{k=1}^{N}2^{-k}U_k.
\]
For \(z\notin[-1+2^{-N},1-2^{-N}]\), with logarithms continued consistently from
\(z=+\infty\),
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{
 R_N(z)=\frac{2^{N(N-1)/2}}{(N-1)!}
 \sum_{j=0}^{2^N-1}\eps_j
 (z+a_{N,j})^{N-1}\Log(z+a_{N,j}).
 }                                                           \label{eq:RN-log}
\end{equation}
Moreover, \(R_N\to R\) locally uniformly on \(\C\setminus[-1,1]\), so
\begin{equation}
 R(z)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{2^{N(N-1)/2}}{(N-1)!}
 \sum_{j=0}^{2^N-1}\eps_j
 (z+a_{N,j})^{N-1}\Log(z+a_{N,j}).                           \label{eq:R-log-limit}
\end{equation}
Finally, the upper boundary jump of \eqref{eq:RN-log} is exactly the spline
\eqref{eq:finite-spline}:
\begin{equation}
 \rho_N(x)=-\frac1\pi\operatorname{Im}R_N(x+i0).
                                                                    \label{eq:RN-boundary}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For \(a>0\), convolution with the uniform density on \([-a,a]\) sends a Cauchy transform
\(G'(z)\) to
\begin{equation}
 \frac1{2a}\int_{-a}^{a}G'(z-u)\,\dd u
 =\frac{G(z+a)-G(z-a)}{2a}.                                  \label{eq:uniform-difference}
\end{equation}
Start from the point mass at zero, whose Cauchy transform is \(1/z\).  An \(N\)-fold
antiderivative is
\[
 H_N(z)=\frac{z^{N-1}\Log z}{(N-1)!};
 \qquad H_N^{(N)}(z)=\frac1z.
\]
Applying \eqref{eq:uniform-difference} successively with \(a_k=2^{-k}\) gives
\[
 R_N(z)=\frac{1}{\prod_{k=1}^{N}2a_k}
 \sum_{\sigma\in\{\pm1\}^N}
 \left(\prod_{k=1}^{N}\sigma_k\right)
 H_N\!\left(z+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sigma_k a_k\right).
\]
Since
\[
 \frac1{\prod_{k=1}^{N}2a_k}=2^{N(N-1)/2},
\]
and binary subsets identify the shifts and signs with \(a_{N,j}\) and \(\eps_j\), this is
\eqref{eq:RN-log}.

The residual tail \(X-X_N\) has support in \([-2^{-N},2^{-N}]\), so \(X_N\to X\)
almost surely and uniformly in support.  The kernels \((z-x)^{-1}\) are uniformly
continuous on compact subsets away from \([-1,1]\); hence \(R_N\to R\) locally uniformly.

For the boundary value, \(\operatorname{Im}\Log(x+a+i0)=\pi\) when \(x+a<0\) and zero
when \(x+a>0\).  The alternating finite difference annihilates every polynomial of degree
less than \(N\), so the sum of the full powers \((x+a_{N,j})^{N-1}\) is zero.  Replacing the
negative-part sum by minus the positive-part sum yields exactly
\eqref{eq:finite-spline}.  This proves \eqref{eq:RN-boundary}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Two complementary approximation geometries]
The finite-convolution approximant \(R_N\) is a sum of logarithms and preserves the dyadic
construction.  The Jacobi approximants in Section~\ref{sec:jacobi} are rational functions
and preserve moments.  Both converge locally uniformly off the support, but their poles,
branch cuts, and error mechanisms are completely different.  This duality suggests hybrid
algorithms: use finite convolution to resolve endpoint geometry and a short Jacobi fraction
to compress the remaining moment error.
\end{remark}

\section{Exact transforms of \(F\), one-sided \(\Up\), and \(F^{-1}\)}\label{sec:fabius-transforms}

\subsection{The Laplace and Fourier transforms of \(F\)}

\begin{theorem}[Transform of the Fabius function]\label{thm:F-transform}
For complex \(s\), with the value at \(s=0\) understood by continuity,
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{
 \int_0^1 e^{-sx}F(x)\,\dd x
 =\frac{e^{-s/2}M(s/2)-e^{-s}}{s}.
 }                                                           \label{eq:F-Laplace}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, for real or complex \(\xi\),
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{
 \int_0^1F(x)e^{-2\pi i\xi x}\,\dd x
 =\frac{e^{-\pi i\xi}\Phi(\xi/2)-e^{-2\pi i\xi}}{2\pi i\xi}.
 }                                                           \label{eq:F-Fourier}
\end{equation}
At \(\xi=0\), the value is \(1/2\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The distributional Laplace transform of \(Y\) is
\begin{equation}
 \int_0^1 e^{-sx}\,\dd F(x)=\E e^{-sY}=e^{-s/2}M(s/2).       \label{eq:dF-Laplace}
\end{equation}
Integration by parts gives
\[
 \int_0^1e^{-sx}\,\dd F(x)
 =e^{-s}+s\int_0^1e^{-sx}F(x)\,\dd x,
\]
which is \eqref{eq:F-Laplace}.  Substitute \(s=2\pi i\xi\) and use
\(M(\pi i\xi)=\Phi(\xi/2)\).
\end{proof}

Separating real and imaginary parts gives the explicit sine and cosine transforms
\begin{align}
 \int_0^1F(x)\cos(2\pi\xi x)\,\dd x
 &=\frac{\sin(2\pi\xi)-\Phi(\xi/2)\sin(\pi\xi)}{2\pi\xi},    \label{eq:F-cosine-transform}\\
 \int_0^1F(x)\sin(2\pi\xi x)\,\dd x
 &=\frac{\Phi(\xi/2)\cos(\pi\xi)-\cos(2\pi\xi)}{2\pi\xi}. \label{eq:F-sine-transform}
\end{align}

\subsection{One-sided transforms of the up-function}

On \([0,1]\), \(\rho(x)=F(1-x)=1-F(x)\).  Therefore the previous theorem immediately
yields the following closed forms.

\begin{theorem}[One-sided transform package]\label{thm:one-sided}
For complex \(s\), with the removable value at zero,
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{
 \int_0^1e^{-sx}\rho(x)\,\dd x
 =\frac{1-e^{-s/2}M(s/2)}{s}.
 }                                                           \label{eq:rho-one-sided-Laplace}
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{
 \int_0^1e^{-2\pi i\xi x}\rho(x)\,\dd x
 =\frac{1-e^{-\pi i\xi}\Phi(\xi/2)}{2\pi i\xi}.
 }                                                           \label{eq:rho-one-sided-Fourier}
\end{equation}
The half-line cosine and sine transforms are
\begin{align}
 \int_0^1\rho(x)\cos(2\pi\xi x)\,\dd x
 &=\frac12\Phi(\xi),                                        \label{eq:rho-half-cos}\\
 \int_0^1\rho(x)\sin(2\pi\xi x)\,\dd x
 &=\frac{1-\Phi(\xi/2)\cos(\pi\xi)}{2\pi\xi}.             \label{eq:rho-half-sin}
\end{align}
\end{theorem}

The sum of \eqref{eq:rho-one-sided-Laplace} at \(s\) and \(-s\) recovers
\(M(s)\) precisely because
\begin{equation}
 M(s)=\frac{2\sinh(s/2)}{s}M(s/2),                           \label{eq:M-scaling}
\end{equation}
which is the hyperbolic continuation of \eqref{eq:Phi-scaling}.

\subsection{The inverse Fabius function as a transport map}

Let \(I=F^{-1}\) on \([0,1]\).  Since \(I\) pushes Lebesgue measure forward to the law
of \(Y\), every integral of a composition with \(I\) reduces to the up-law.

\begin{theorem}[Inverse pushforward master identity]\label{thm:inverse-pushforward}
For every Borel function \(g\) for which one side is integrable,
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{
 \int_0^1g(I(u))\,\dd u
 =\int_0^1g(x)\,\dd F(x)
 =\int_{-1}^{1}g\!\left(\frac{x+1}{2}\right)\rho(x)\,\dd x.
 }                                                           \label{eq:inverse-master}
\end{equation}
In particular,
\begin{align}
 \int_0^1 e^{-sI(u)}\,\dd u
 &=e^{-s/2}M(s/2),                                           \label{eq:inverse-Laplace}\\
 \int_0^1 e^{-2\pi i\xi I(u)}\,\dd u
 &=e^{-\pi i\xi}\Phi(\xi/2),                              \label{eq:inverse-Fourier}\\
 \int_0^1\frac{\dd u}{z-I(u)}
 &=2R(2z-1),\qquad z\in\C\setminus[0,1],                   \label{eq:inverse-Cauchy}\\
 \int_0^1I(u)^n\,\dd u
 &=2^{-n}\sum_{r=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
   \binom{n}{2r}\mu_{2r}.                                   \label{eq:inverse-moments}
\end{align}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
If \(U\) is uniform on \([0,1]\), then \(I(U)\) has CDF \(F\), hence the same law as
\(Y\).  This proves \eqref{eq:inverse-master}.  The remaining formulas follow by taking
\(g(x)=e^{-sx}\), \(e^{-2\pi i\xi x}\), \((z-x)^{-1}\), and \(x^n\), respectively,
and then using \eqref{eq:Y-affine} and symmetry of \(X\).
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Formula \eqref{eq:inverse-Cauchy} transports the entire Jacobi-fraction theory of \(R\) to
the inverse Fabius function.  Thus a function whose pointwise inverse asymptotics involve
Lambert \(W\) nevertheless has a globally simple Cauchy transform when sampled with
uniform measure in its range variable.
\end{remark}

\section{Logarithmic derivatives of the Fourier image}

\subsection{Three meromorphic expansions}

\begin{theorem}[Cotangent, tangent, and zero-set formulas]\label{thm:three-logder}
Away from the integer zero set of \(\Phi\),
\begin{align}
 \frac{\Phi'(z)}{\Phi(z)}
 &=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}
 \left[
 \frac{\pi}{2^k}\cot\!\left(\frac{\pi z}{2^k}\right)-\frac1z
 \right],                                                    \label{eq:logder-cot}\\
 &=-\pi\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{m}{2^m}
 \tan\!\left(\frac{\pi z}{2^m}\right),                    \label{eq:logder-tan}\\
 &=-2z\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1+\nu_2(n)}{n^2-z^2}.       \label{eq:logder-zero}
\end{align}
All three series converge locally normally on compact subsets avoiding the poles.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Differentiate the logarithm of each of the three products
\eqref{eq:Phi-products} and \eqref{eq:canonical-product}.  For large \(k\), the bracket
in \eqref{eq:logder-cot} and the term in \eqref{eq:logder-tan} are \(\bigO(4^{-k})\)
uniformly on compact sets.  The terms in \eqref{eq:logder-zero} are bounded by a constant
times \((1+\nu_2(n))/n^2\), which is summable by \eqref{eq:spectral-zeta} at \(s=2\).
\end{proof}

The three forms have different numerical regimes.  The tangent sum is usually the fastest
away from its poles; the cotangent sum tracks the original sinc factors; and the zero-set
sum exposes multiplicities and supports rigorous rational tail bounds.

\subsection{Digamma and Hurwitz-zeta hierarchy}

The valuation weight has the divisor decomposition
\begin{equation}
 1+\nu_2(n)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\one_{2^k\mid n}.             \label{eq:valuation-indicators}
\end{equation}
This turns the zero sum into a dyadic superposition of classical special functions.

\begin{theorem}[Digamma and Hurwitz-zeta representations]\label{thm:hurwitz}
Let \(\psi=\Gamma'/\Gamma\).  Then
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{
 \frac{\Phi'(z)}{\Phi(z)}
 =\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}2^{-k}
 \left[
 \psi\!\left(1-\frac{z}{2^k}\right)
 -\psi\!\left(1+\frac{z}{2^k}\right)
 \right].
 }                                                           \label{eq:logder-digamma}
\end{equation}
For every integer \(r\ge2\),
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{
 \frac{\dd^r}{\dd z^r}\log\Phi(z)
 =(r-1)!\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}2^{-kr}
 \left[
 -\zeta\!\left(r,1-\frac{z}{2^k}\right)
 +(-1)^{r-1}\zeta\!\left(r,1+\frac{z}{2^k}\right)
 \right].
 }                                                           \label{eq:logder-hurwitz}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
From \eqref{eq:canonical-product}, for \(r\ge1\), paired symmetrically when \(r=1\),
\begin{equation}
 (\log\Phi)^{(r)}(z)
 =(r-1)!\sum_{n\ge1}(1+\nu_2(n))
 \left[-\frac1{(n-z)^r}+\frac{(-1)^{r-1}}{(n+z)^r}\right].   \label{eq:logder-general-zero}
\end{equation}
Insert \eqref{eq:valuation-indicators}, write \(n=2^k m\), and sum over \(m\ge1\).
For \(r\ge2\), the inner sums are Hurwitz zeta functions.  For \(r=1\), the convergent
paired sum is the standard digamma difference.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Local jet data at nonzero zeros]
Let \(N\ne0\) be an integer and \(d_N=1+\nu_2(|N|)\).  Then
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\Phi'(z)}{\Phi(z)}=\frac{d_N}{z-N}+H_N(z),             \label{eq:local-logder-zero}
\end{equation}
where \(H_N\) is holomorphic near \(N\), and every derivative of \(H_N\) can be written
as an absolutely convergent valuation-weighted Hurwitz-zeta sum.  Thus the complete local
Taylor jet of \(\Phi(z)/(z-N)^{d_N}\) is algorithmically explicit.
\end{corollary}

\section{A Fredholm determinant for the Fourier product}\label{sec:fredholm}

The canonical product can be realized as an ordinary Fredholm determinant, not merely as a
formal spectral product.

\begin{definition}
Let
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal I=\{(n,r):n\ge1,\ 1\le r\le 1+\nu_2(n)\},
\end{equation}
and define the positive diagonal operator \(\cK\) on \(\ell^2(\mathcal I)\) by
\begin{equation}
 \cK e_{n,r}=\frac1{n^2}e_{n,r}.                             \label{eq:K-def}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Fredholm determinant representation]\label{thm:fredholm}
The operator \(\cK\) is trace class and
\begin{align}
 \boxed{\Phi(z)=\det(I-z^2\cK)},                             \label{eq:Phi-fredholm}\\
 \boxed{M(t)=\det\!\left(I+\frac{t^2}{4\pi^2}\cK\right)}.  \label{eq:M-fredholm}
\end{align}
Furthermore,
\begin{align}
 \tr(\cK^m)&=\frac{\zeta(2m)}{1-2^{-2m}},                   \label{eq:K-traces}\\
 \frac{\Phi'(z)}{\Phi(z)}
 &=-2z\,\tr\!\left(\cK(I-z^2\cK)^{-1}\right).            \label{eq:K-resolvent-trace}
\end{align}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By \eqref{eq:spectral-zeta},
\[
 \tr\cK=\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{1+\nu_2(n)}{n^2}
 =\frac{\zeta(2)}{1-2^{-2}}<\infty,
\]
so \(\cK\) is trace class.  Its Fredholm determinant is the product over its eigenvalues,
with multiplicity:
\[
 \det(I-z^2\cK)
 =\prod_{n\ge1}\left(1-\frac{z^2}{n^2}\right)^{1+\nu_2(n)}
 =\Phi(z).
\]
Substituting \(z=it/(2\pi)\) gives \eqref{eq:M-fredholm}.  Formula
\eqref{eq:K-traces} is again \eqref{eq:spectral-zeta}, and differentiating the Fredholm
logarithm gives \eqref{eq:K-resolvent-trace}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
This representation converts several scalar identities into operator identities.  For
example, the zeta expansion \eqref{eq:logPhi-zeta} is the standard trace expansion
\[
 \log\det(I-z^2\cK)=-\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{z^{2m}}m\tr(\cK^m).
\]
It also suggests perturbation theory for generalized exponent sequences: changing the
multiplicity sequence changes only the diagonal spectral multiplicities.
\end{remark}

\section{Jacobi continued fractions and exact rational coefficients}\label{sec:jacobi}

\subsection{Exact moment recurrence}

\begin{proposition}[Moment recurrence]\label{prop:moment-recurrence}
The even moments satisfy \(\mu_0=1\) and, for \(n\ge1\),
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{
 (4^n-1)\mu_{2n}
 =\sum_{j=1}^{n}\binom{2n}{2j}\frac{\mu_{2n-2j}}{2j+1}.
 }                                                           \label{eq:moment-recurrence}
\end{equation}
In particular, every \(\mu_{2n}\) is rational.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Raise \eqref{eq:X-fixed-point} to the \(2n\)-th power and take expectations:
\[
 4^n\mu_{2n}
 =\sum_{j=0}^{n}\binom{2n}{2j}\E U^{2j}\,\mu_{2n-2j}.
\]
Since \(\E U^{2j}=1/(2j+1)\), the \(j=0\) term is \(\mu_{2n}\).  Move it to the left.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Monic orthogonal polynomials}

Let \(P_n\) be the monic orthogonal polynomials for the probability measure
\(\rho(x)\dd x\), and let
\begin{equation}
 h_n=\int_{-1}^{1}P_n(x)^2\rho(x)\,\dd x.                    \label{eq:hn}
\end{equation}
Evenness forces the diagonal Jacobi parameters to vanish, so
\begin{equation}
 P_0(x)=1,\
 P_1(x)=x,
 \qquad
 P_{n+1}(x)=xP_n(x)-\beta_nP_{n-1}(x),
 \qquad
 \beta_n=\frac{h_n}{h_{n-1}}>0.                              \label{eq:monic-recurrence}
\end{equation}
The first polynomials are
\begin{align}
 P_0(x)&=1,\\
 P_1(x)&=x,\\
 P_2(x)&=x^2-\frac19,\\
 P_3(x)&=x^3-\frac{19}{75}x,\\
 P_4(x)&=x^4-\frac{62}{147}x^2+\frac{619}{33075}.
\end{align}

\subsection{The Jacobi and Stieltjes fractions}

\begin{theorem}[Jacobi continued fraction]\label{thm:J-fraction}
The Cauchy transform has the locally uniform continued fraction
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{
 R(z)=\cfrac{1}{z-\cfrac{\beta_1}{z-\cfrac{\beta_2}{z-
           \cfrac{\beta_3}{z-\ddots}}}}.
 }                                                           \label{eq:R-J-fraction}
\end{equation}
The coefficients are positive rational numbers.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Positivity follows from \(\beta_n=h_n/h_{n-1}\), and rationality follows because
Gram--Schmidt applied to the rational moment functional \eqref{eq:moment-recurrence}
uses only rational operations.  Markov's theorem for compactly supported positive measures
identifies the continued fraction generated by \eqref{eq:monic-recurrence} with the Cauchy
transform and gives local uniform convergence off the support \cite{VanAssche1993,SimonOPRL}.
\end{proof}

Define the ordinary even-moment generating function
\begin{equation}
 \cO(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mu_{2n}t^n
 =\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\rho(x)}{1-tx^2}\,\dd x,
 \qquad |t|<1.                                               \label{eq:O-def}
\end{equation}
Substituting \(t=z^{-2}\) into \eqref{eq:R-J-fraction} gives the contracted fraction.

\begin{corollary}[Stieltjes fraction and product integral]\label{cor:S-fraction}
For \(|t|<1\),
\begin{align}
 \boxed{
 \cO(t)=\cfrac{1}{1-\cfrac{\beta_1t}{1-\cfrac{\beta_2t}{1-
               \cfrac{\beta_3t}{1-\ddots}}}}
 }                                                           \label{eq:O-S-fraction}\\
 \cO(t)=\int_0^{\infty}e^{-u}
 \prod_{k=1}^{\infty}
 \frac{\sinh(\sqrt t\,u/2^k)}{\sqrt t\,u/2^k}\,\dd u.    \label{eq:O-product-integral}
\end{align}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The continued fraction follows from \(\cO(z^{-2})=zR(z)\).  For the integral, use
\[
 \frac1{1-tx^2}=\int_0^{\infty}e^{-u}\cosh(\sqrt t\,xu)\,\dd u
\]
and then average over \(X\), obtaining \(M(\sqrt t\,u)\).
\end{proof}

For \(s\ge0\), \(\cO(-s)\) is completely monotone:
\begin{equation}
 (-1)^m\frac{\dd^m}{\dd s^m}\cO(-s)
 =m!\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{x^{2m}\rho(x)}{(1+sx^2)^{m+1}}\,\dd x>0. \label{eq:O-complete-monotone}
\end{equation}
Thus the even moments form a Stieltjes moment sequence, and the fraction
\eqref{eq:O-S-fraction} belongs to the classical positive Stieltjes class.

\subsection{Hankel determinants}

Let
\begin{equation}
 \Delta_n=\det[\mu_{i+j}]_{i,j=0}^{n-1},\qquad \Delta_0=1.  \label{eq:Hankel}
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
 h_n=\frac{\Delta_{n+1}}{\Delta_n},
 \qquad
 \boxed{\beta_n=\frac{\Delta_{n+1}\Delta_{n-1}}{\Delta_n^2}}. \label{eq:beta-Hankel}
\end{equation}
Because odd moments vanish, each \(\Delta_n\) factors after a parity permutation into an
even-even and odd-odd moment determinant.  This parity factorization is useful for exact
arithmetic and should be exploited in future Lean formalization.

\subsection{The limiting coefficient}

\begin{theorem}[Nevai-class limit]\label{thm:beta-limit}
The Jacobi coefficients satisfy
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\lim_{n\to\infty}\beta_n=\frac14.}                  \label{eq:beta-limit}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The measure \(\rho(x)\dd x\) has essential support \([-1,1]\), and its density is strictly
positive for every \(x\in(-1,1)\).  The Denisov--Rakhmanov theorem therefore places its
orthonormal Jacobi matrix in the Nevai class of \([-1,1]\) \cite{Denisov2004,SimonOPRL}: the off-diagonal orthonormal
coefficients tend to \(1/2\), and the diagonal coefficients tend to zero.  In the monic
recurrence \eqref{eq:monic-recurrence}, \(\beta_n\) is the square of the off-diagonal
orthonormal coefficient, hence tends to \(1/4\).
\end{proof}

\subsection{Pad\'e and Gaussian-quadrature representations}

Let \(Q_{N-1}\) be the polynomial of the second kind
\begin{equation}
 Q_{N-1}(z)=\int_{-1}^{1}
 \frac{P_N(z)-P_N(x)}{z-x}\rho(x)\,\dd x.                    \label{eq:second-kind}
\end{equation}
The \(N\)-th Jacobi convergent is \(Q_{N-1}/P_N\).  If
\(x_{1,N},\dots,x_{N,N}\) are the zeros of \(P_N\), then
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{
 \frac{Q_{N-1}(z)}{P_N(z)}
 =\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{w_{j,N}}{z-x_{j,N}},
 \qquad w_{j,N}>0,\quad \sum_jw_{j,N}=1.
 }                                                           \label{eq:gauss-partial}
\end{equation}
This is the Cauchy transform of the \(N\)-node Gaussian quadrature measure for \(\rho\),
and it matches moments through degree \(2N-1\).  The exact error identity is
\begin{equation}
 R(z)-\frac{Q_{N-1}(z)}{P_N(z)}
 =\frac1{P_N(z)^2}\int_{-1}^{1}
 \frac{P_N(x)^2}{z-x}\rho(x)\,\dd x.                        \label{eq:Markov-error}
\end{equation}
Hence every Gaussian resolvent approximant is a strict lower bound for \(R(z)\) when
\(z>1\), and a strict upper bound when \(z<-1\).

The first nontrivial approximants are completely explicit:
\begin{align}
 R_1^{\mathrm G}(z)&=\frac1z,\\
 R_2^{\mathrm G}(z)&=\frac{z}{z^2-1/9}
 =\frac12\left(\frac1{z-1/3}+\frac1{z+1/3}\right),\\
 R_3^{\mathrm G}(z)&=
 \frac{32/57}{z}
 +\frac{25/114}{z-\sqrt{19/75}}
 +\frac{25/114}{z+\sqrt{19/75}}.
\end{align}
Thus the continued fraction gives a hierarchy of positive finite atomic models for the
up-law, complementary to the continuous finite-convolution models of Section~5.

\section{Further combined representations}

\subsection{The Cauchy transform of the Fabius measure}

Let
\begin{equation}
 S(z)=\int_0^1\frac{\dd F(x)}{z-x}=\E\frac1{z-Y}.            \label{eq:S-def}
\end{equation}
The affine relation \eqref{eq:Y-affine} gives
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{S(z)=2R(2z-1).}                                     \label{eq:S-R}
\end{equation}
Consequently, every representation of \(R\) immediately yields one for \(S\).  For example,
for \(|2z-1|>1\),
\begin{equation}
 S(z)=2\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\mu_{2n}}{(2z-1)^{2n+1}},   \label{eq:S-moment}
\end{equation}
and \(S\) has a Jacobi fraction obtained by the affine change of variable in
\eqref{eq:R-J-fraction}.

\subsection{A determinant form of the Fabius Laplace transform}

Combining \eqref{eq:F-Laplace} with \eqref{eq:M-fredholm} gives
\begin{equation}
 \int_0^1e^{-sx}F(x)\,\dd x
 =\frac{e^{-s/2}
 \det\!\left(I+\dfrac{s^2}{16\pi^2}\cK\right)-e^{-s}}{s}.  \label{eq:F-Laplace-determinant}
\end{equation}
Similarly,
\begin{equation}
 \int_0^1e^{-sx}\rho(x)\,\dd x
 =\frac{1-e^{-s/2}
 \det\!\left(I+\dfrac{s^2}{16\pi^2}\cK\right)}{s}.         \label{eq:rho-half-determinant}
\end{equation}
These are literal combinations of a compact-support transform with a trace-class spectral
determinant.

\subsection{A resolvent--product--continued-fraction triangle}

For \(|t|<1\), the same analytic function \(\cO(t)\) has three structurally different
representations:
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{
 \begin{aligned}
 \cO(t)
 &=\sum_{n\ge0}\mu_{2n}t^n\\
 &=\int_0^{\infty}e^{-u}
   \prod_{k\ge1}\frac{\sinh(\sqrt t\,u/2^k)}{\sqrt t\,u/2^k}\,\dd u\\
 &=\cfrac{1}{1-\cfrac{\beta_1t}{1-\cfrac{\beta_2t}{1-\ddots}}}.
 \end{aligned}
 }                                                           \label{eq:triangle}
\end{equation}
The first line is arithmetic, the second is multiplicative and analytic, and the third is
orthogonal-polynomial/geometric.  Equality of the three creates a direct bridge among the
Bernoulli--Bell moment formulas, the infinite hyperbolic product, and the exact rational
Jacobi parameters.

\section{Conjectures and frontier problems}

The following conjectures are deliberately separated from the proved results.

\begin{conjecture}[Strict Jacobi monotonicity]\label{conj:beta-monotone}
The exact Jacobi coefficients are strictly increasing:
\begin{equation}
 0<\beta_1<\beta_2<\cdots<\frac14.                            \label{eq:beta-monotone}
\end{equation}
\end{conjecture}

Exact arithmetic verifies the inequalities through \(n=40\).  The upper limit is proved by
Theorem~\ref{thm:beta-limit}, but monotonicity does not follow from the
Denisov--Rakhmanov theorem and appears to require a Fabius-specific argument.

\begin{conjecture}[Logarithmically corrected recurrence rate]\label{conj:beta-rate}
There exists a constant \(C_{\Up}\in(0,\infty)\) such that
\begin{equation}
 \frac14-\beta_n
 \sim C_{\Up}\frac{(\log n)^2}{n^2}.                         \label{eq:beta-rate}
\end{equation}
\end{conjecture}

The scaled quantity in Figure~\ref{fig:beta-scaled} rises from approximately \(0.674\) at
\(n=20\) to \(0.766\) at \(n=40\).  This is too short a range to determine a constant or
even to exclude a different slowly varying factor.  The conjecture is motivated jointly by
the data and by the squared-logarithmic endpoint scale of \(\rho\).  A Riemann--Hilbert or
strong Szeg\H{o} analysis adapted to the endpoint flatness is the natural next step.

\begin{conjecture}[Dyadically regularized Mittag--Leffler expansion]\label{conj:ML}
Let \(d_n=1+\nu_2(|n|)\), and let \(\operatorname{PP}_n(z)\) be the principal part of
\(1/\Phi(z)\) at the nonzero integer \(n\).  There exists a canonical dyadic grouping and a
finite genus of subtraction polynomials such that
\begin{equation}
 \frac1{\Phi(z)}
 =E(z)+\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z\setminus\{0\}}^{\mathrm{dyadic}}
 \left(\operatorname{PP}_n(z)-\text{canonical subtraction at }n\right), \label{eq:ML-conj}
\end{equation}
where \(E\) is an explicitly controlled entire function, ideally constant or of very low
order.
\end{conjecture}

The local principal parts are computable from Theorem~\ref{thm:hurwitz}; the unresolved
issue is a global summation scheme compatible with the unbounded dyadic multiplicities.

\begin{problem}[Arithmetic of \(\beta_n\)]
Determine sharp denominator-clearing factors for \(\beta_n\), \(h_n\), and the parity
Hankel determinants.  The first coefficients display repeated migration of prime factors
between consecutive norms, suggesting that factored norm variables may be arithmetically
simpler than reduced \(\beta_n\) themselves.
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}[Endpoint-sensitive Gaussian nodes]
Determine the asymptotic distance of the extreme zero of \(P_n\) from \(1\), and compare it
with the Chebyshev scale \(n^{-2}\).  The density is positive almost everywhere but flatter
than every power at the endpoint, so the leading equilibrium distribution is classical
while the first correction should retain the Lambert--\(W\)/log-square endpoint signature.
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}[A coupled resolvent hierarchy for exponent sequences]
The repository's Newton/Rvachev theory replaces the unit exponent sequence in the sinc
product by general integer sequences.  Construct the corresponding Cauchy transforms and
derive coupled affine functional equations when the exponent sequence is closed under a
Pascal or shift operator.  The Fredholm model of Section~\ref{sec:fredholm} suggests that
the Fourier side is easy; the real-space fixed-point law is the main obstacle.
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}[Fractional resolvent dynamics]
Equation \eqref{eq:R-dde} gives every integer derivative as a finite Thue--Morse orbit.
Seek a fractional-order interpolation
\[
 D_z^{\alpha}R(z)=\int_{\Gamma_\alpha}K_\alpha(j)
 R(2^\alpha z+\text{affine shift}(j))\,\dd j
\]
that reduces to \eqref{eq:R-derivative-tree} for \(\alpha\in\N\).  A semigroup built from
the two affine branches \(z\mapsto2z\pm1\) may provide the correct operator calculus.
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}[Formal verification roadmap]
A Lean development can be staged as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
\item define the Cauchy transform of the canonical up probability measure;
\item prove the logarithmic fixed-point identity first on \((1,\infty)\);
\item differentiate under the integral and formalize \eqref{eq:R-dde};
\item prove the Thue--Morse derivative tree by induction on binary children;
\item define rational moments and the monic Stieltjes procedure;
\item construct finite Jacobi convergents and prove the Gaussian error identity;
\item postpone the Denisov--Rakhmanov limit, which depends on substantial external
      orthogonal-polynomial theory.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}

\section{Promising research program}

\subsection{Analytic program}

The Cauchy transform should be treated as a primary Fabius--Rvachev object, on the same
level as \(F\), \(\rho\), and \(\Phi\).  Immediate goals are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item derive quantitative bounds for \(R\) near the cut from the sharp Fourier decay;
\item study the Hilbert transform \eqref{eq:R-boundary-Hilbert}, including its zeros and
      endpoint asymptotics;
\item use \eqref{eq:R-dde} to continue asymptotic expansions along dyadic affine rays;
\item compare finite-logarithmic and Jacobi rational approximants uniformly near the
      support.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Orthogonal-polynomial program}

The exact \(J\)-fraction opens a separate research direction:

\begin{enumerate}
\item prove or disprove Conjecture~\ref{conj:beta-monotone};
\item determine strong asymptotics of \(P_n\), \(h_n\), and \(\beta_n\);
\item construct Fabius-specific Gauss--Kronrod or nested positive rules;
\item investigate whether the dyadic moment recurrence induces a nonlinear recurrence or
      integrable system for \(\beta_n\);
\item study the arithmetic Galois structure of Gaussian nodes and exact weights.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Operator and special-function program}

The Fredholm determinant and Hurwitz-zeta hierarchy suggest:

\begin{enumerate}
\item regularized determinants and spectral shifts for deformed multiplicity sequences;
\item a Barnes-type compression of the determinant rather than only of the scalar product;
\item exact trace identities for logarithmic derivatives at rational and half-integer
      arguments;
\item meromorphic continuation of dyadically weighted spectral sums with local factors
      dictated by \(\nu_2(n)\).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Inverse-function program}

The pushforward formulas \eqref{eq:inverse-master}--\eqref{eq:inverse-Cauchy} show that
many global integral questions about \(F^{-1}\) are easier than pointwise questions.
Promising directions include:

\begin{enumerate}
\item exact orthogonal expansions of \(I(u)\) using its known pushforward moments;
\item entropy and transport functionals of \(I\), reduced to integrals against \(\rho\);
\item combining the global Cauchy transform with local Lambert--\(W\) endpoint inversions;
\item rational and Gaussian quadrature for inverse-function integrals without directly
      evaluating \(I\).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}

The audited repository already contains most of the classical and modern representation
theory one would first seek: convolution laws, sinc and cosine products, integer-zero
products, moment and cumulant series, Thue--Morse splines, q-binomial dyadic values,
Poisson and Legendre expansions, inverse asymptotics, and Fourier-decay envelopes.  The
most productive way to extend that corpus is therefore to introduce a new organizing
transform rather than another isolated formula.

The Cauchy transform \(R\) accomplishes this.  It joins the moment sequence to an analytic
function, converts the dyadic law into a functional-differential equation, turns finite
convolutions into logarithmic Thue--Morse sums, and generates a Jacobi continued fraction
with exact rational coefficients.  The Fourier image simultaneously acquires an operator
determinant and a special-function logarithmic-derivative calculus.  Closed one-sided
transforms and inverse pushforward identities complete a network in which real-space,
frequency-space, moment, orthogonal-polynomial, and operator representations are mutually
convertible.

The proved limit \(\beta_n\to1/4\) anchors the new continued-fraction theory.  The exact
order-40 data, the monotonicity conjecture, the endpoint-sensitive rate problem, and the
regularized Mittag--Leffler problem provide concrete frontier targets suitable for both
analysis and formalization.
