\begin{abstract}
This article is the human-readable exposition of the formally verified Fabius-function
development in the \texttt{ProveIt} repository.  It keeps three related functions separate:
the bounded distribution function $F$, Rvachev's even compactly supported bump $\Up$, and
the signed global extension $\mathcal F$.  Their construction and uniqueness, differential
and refinement laws, strict shape properties, optimal Lipschitz constants, derivative
bounds, effective flatness, and precisely formalized analytic and non-elementarity results
are presented together with direct pointers to their Lean proofs.

The probabilistic part identifies $F$ and $\Up$ with exact probability expressions for an
infinite weighted sum of independent uniform coordinates.  Finite atomic convolution laws,
weak convergence, half-weighted step approximants, and centered uniform splines give proved
finite models and quantitative approximation results.  The analytic part develops the
formal sinc and cosine products, Fourier inversion and Poisson summation, exact moment
recurrences, and the Fourier--Legendre expansion.  The arithmetic part gives reciprocal-power
values, Thue--Morse formulas for arbitrary dyadic arguments, executable binary reduction,
two-adic information, and the exact half-base $q$-binomial identities present in Lean.

The endpoint chapters report only the negative-Laplace decompositions, periodic correction,
lower-Lambert formulas, saddle estimates, and correction terms that have named proved
counterparts in the formal source.  Plausible extensions, numerical observations, generic
$q$-analogues, and other arguments not yet represented by an exact Lean theorem are kept in
the companion volume under \path{docs/non-formalized-research-frontiers/}, not in this
primary exposition.
\end{abstract}

\section{Historical note and scope}
\label{sec:history}

The probability distribution now attached to the name of Fabius is older than Fabius's
two-page note.  It occurs in the 1935 work of Jessen and Wintner on infinite convolutions
of independent random variables \cite{jessen1935}, was
rediscovered several times, and entered a different mathematical culture through the
compactly supported ``finite function'' of V.\,A.~Rvachev.  Fabius used it in 1966 as a
particularly economical probabilistic construction of a nowhere analytic
$C^\infty$ function \cite{fabius1966}.  Independently, and for entirely different reasons,
the Rvachevs introduced the same object in 1971 as the canonical compactly supported
solution of a functional--differential equation \cite{rvachev1971}, and V.\,A.~Rvachev
later developed it into the theory of \emph{atomic functions} used in approximation
theory and numerical analysis \cite{rvachev1973,rvachev1979,rvachev1990}.  Rvachev's
normalization emphasizes the even compactly
supported bump, whereas the Fabius normalization emphasizes its cumulative distribution
function.  Arias de Reyna's 1982 paper gave a systematic treatment of the compactly supported
function, including its Fourier product, probability interpretation, partitions of unity,
derivatives, and dyadic values; an English translation appeared in 2017
\cite{arias1982}.  His later arithmetic paper organized the exact rational sequences and
denominator questions \cite{arias2018}.  Haugland's note \cite{haugland2016} gives a
practical algorithm for evaluating $F$ at dyadic rationals, closely related to the
bit-recursion of \Cref{sec:dyadic}.

The present article is not merely a paraphrase of those papers.  It follows the proof
dependency graph of the Lean development in the \texttt{ProveIt} repository
\cite{proveit}.  That development first constructs the bounded distribution function, then
derives the Rvachev and signed-global forms, and finally builds the arithmetic and asymptotic
layers on top.  This order has two advantages.  It prevents identities valid for the signed
extension from being silently applied to the bounded distribution, and it makes the exact
dyadic algorithms independent of any numerical approximation.

The formalization also settles several questions that are only implicit, conjectural, or
incorrectly normalized in the source material.  Most importantly, the sharp endpoint
asymptotic contains a nonconstant periodic function of an exact lower-Lambert phase.  The
periodic term is not a cosmetic improvement: every nonzero Fourier mode is explicit and
nonzero, and an elementary formula without it fails at the error scale usually claimed.
The development further proves a full inverse-Lambert expansion, establishes an exact
bit-recursive evaluator for every dyadic rational, and supplies an absolutely and uniformly
convergent Fourier--Legendre expansion of $\Up$.

Arias de Reyna's arithmetic paper \cite{arias2018} enumerates the independent occurrences of
this function in chronological order, and two of them deserve mention beside Jessen--Wintner,
Fabius, and the Rvachevs.  In 1981 G.\,Kh.~Kirov and G.\,A.~Totkov arrived at it in a study
of the distribution of the zeros of the derivatives of the function they wrote $\lambda(x)$
(\emph{Godishnik Vissh. Uchebn. Zaved. Prilozhna Mat.} \textbf{17} (1981), no.~4, 157--165,
in Bulgarian with English and Russian summaries); related dyadic derivative phenomena are
represented in the formal Taylor and exact-evaluation APIs used below.  In
1985 R.~Schnabl gave a further independent construction, ``Über eine $C^\infty$-Funktion,''
in \emph{Zahlentheoretische Analysis}, Lecture Notes in Mathematics~1114, Springer, Berlin
(1985), 134--142.  Neither paper is used in the proofs below, which is why their data are
given here rather than in the bibliography; they are recorded because a reader tracing the
function through the literature will meet both names.

Our convention is fixed throughout:

\begin{itemize}
\item $F$ is the bounded cumulative distribution function, equal to $0$ on
      $(-\infty,0]$ and to $1$ on $[1,\infty)$;
\item $\Up$ is the even compactly supported bump on $[-1,1]$;
\item $\mathcal F$ is the signed global extension satisfying
      $\mathcal F'(x)=2\mathcal F(2x)$ on all of $\R$.
\end{itemize}

All logarithms are natural logarithms.  We write $L=\log2$ in the asymptotic sections,
$\wt(n)$ for the sum of the binary digits of $n$, and
$\sinc z=\sin z/z$ with the removable value $1$ at the origin.



\subsection{A guide to the main results}
\label{sec:aud-roadmap}

The exposition follows the dependency order of the formal development.  The opening chapters
separate the bounded function \(F\), the bump \(\Up\), and the signed extension
\(\mathcal F\); construct the canonical function; and record the exact uniqueness and shape
theorems.  The probability chapter then gives the weighted-uniform representation and its
three finite approximations: atomic convolution measures, half-weighted step functions, and
centered uniform splines.

The Fourier and global-extension chapters collect the proved product, inversion, Poisson,
derivative, bound, and flatness APIs.  They lead to the exact analytic locus of \(F\), the
proved nonanalyticity regions for \(\Up\) and \(\mathcal F\), the totalized inverse on
\([0,1]\), and the formal non-elementarity results.  The moment and arithmetic chapters then
derive the rational recurrences, reciprocal-power identities, path and composition formulas,
parity and two-adic statements, exact dyadic evaluation, binary reduction, and the specialized
half-base \(q\)-binomial formulas.

The later analytic chapters summarize the formal endpoint machinery: the exact
negative-Laplace product and logarithmic decomposition, the periodic Fourier correction,
the lower-Lambert coordinate, the proved saddle asymptotics and correction coefficients, and
the rigorous obstructions to deleting the periodic term.  The Fourier--Legendre, summation,
and computability chapters finish the proved story.  The final module guide names the Lean
declarations supporting each retained theorem.

A statement appears here only when its complete mathematical content has an exact proved Lean
counterpart.  Material removed from earlier drafts because that test is not yet met is
preserved in the research-frontier volume.  Thus the division between this document and
that volume is a proof-status boundary, not a judgment about mathematical plausibility
or importance.

\begin{boxedremark}
\textbf{One formal object, three global functions.}
The bounded \(F\) is the cumulative distribution function used for probabilities and
dyadic values.  The compactly supported \(\Up\) is obtained by folding \(F\), and its Fourier
transform has the proved sinc and cosine product descriptions.  The signed
\(\mathcal F\) is the global Thue--Morse continuation and satisfies the pure dilation
equation on all of \(\R\).  Domains and normalizations are stated explicitly whenever a
theorem passes from one presentation to another.
\end{boxedremark}

\section{Three functions that must not be conflated}
\label{sec:three-functions}

The literature often uses the same letter $F$ for three related, but globally different,
functions.  That economy of notation is harmless in a local calculation and dangerous in a
long proof.  We therefore begin by separating them.

\subsection{The bounded Fabius distribution function}

\begin{definition}[Bounded Fabius function]
A function $F:\R\to[0,1]$ is called a \emph{bounded Fabius function} if
\begin{align}
 F(x)&=0 &&(x\le 0), \label{eq:F-left}\\
 F(x)&=1 &&(x\ge 1), \label{eq:F-right}\\
 F(1-x)&=1-F(x) &&(0\le x\le 1), \label{eq:F-symmetry}\\
 F'(x)&=2F(2x) &&(0\le x\le \tfrac12), \label{eq:F-diffeq}
\end{align}
and $F$ is infinitely differentiable on $\R$.
\end{definition}

The derivative equation on the right half follows from symmetry:
\begin{equation}
 F'(x)=2F(2-2x),\qquad \tfrac12\le x\le 1.                 \label{eq:F-diffeq-right}
\end{equation}
In particular $F'(x)>0$ for $0<x<1$, once positivity in the interior is known.  Thus $F$
is a genuine cumulative distribution function, not merely a smooth interpolation between
$0$ and $1$.

Because $F$ is constant on both exterior half-lines and is $C^\infty$, every derivative of
positive order vanishes at both endpoints:
\begin{equation}
 F^{(m)}(0)=F^{(m)}(1)=0\qquad(m\ge1).                    \label{eq:flat-endpoints}
\end{equation}
This flatness is the source of both its extraordinary endpoint decay and its failure to be
analytic there.

\subsection{Rvachev's compactly supported bump}

\begin{definition}[Rvachev up-function]
Given $F$, define
\begin{equation}
 \Up(x)=
 \begin{cases}
   F(x+1),&x\le0,\\
   F(1-x),&x>0.
 \end{cases}                                               \label{eq:up-fold}
\end{equation}
Equivalently,
\begin{equation}
 \Up(x)=F(1-|x|).                                          \label{eq:up-absolute}
\end{equation}
Here the right side is interpreted using the globally bounded function $F$.
\end{definition}

It follows immediately that $\Up$ is even, nonnegative, supported on $[-1,1]$, and
$\Up(0)=1$.  On the unit interval the two functions are simply translates:
\begin{equation}
 F(x)=\Up(x-1),\qquad 0\le x\le1.                          \label{eq:F-up-translate}
\end{equation}
The graph of $F$ is an infinitely flat sigmoid; the graph of $\Up$ is its symmetric fold.

The primary exposition omits sampled plots: their decimal coordinates are not themselves
Lean declarations.  Exact point evaluation is developed below through rational dyadic
formulas and executable evaluators.

\subsection{The signed global extension}

Let $\wt(n)$ denote the sum of the binary digits of $n$, and put
\begin{equation}
 \varepsilon_n=(-1)^{\wt(n)}.                             \label{eq:tm-sign}
\end{equation}
The sequence $(\varepsilon_n)$ is the $\{\pm1\}$ form of the Thue--Morse sequence
\cite{allouche2003}.

\begin{definition}[Signed extension]
Define
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal F(x)=\sum_{n\ge0}\varepsilon_n\,
   \Up(x-2n-1).                                            \label{eq:global-extension}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

At a fixed $x$ only finitely many summands can be nonzero, so this is a locally finite sum.
On every block $[2b,2b+2]$ only the $b$-th translate can be nonzero
(and at the endpoints it too vanishes):
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal F(x)=\varepsilon_b\Up(x-2b-1),
 \qquad 2b\le x\le 2b+2.                                  \label{eq:single-block}
\end{equation}
It vanishes for $x\le0$, and it agrees with the bounded $F$ for every $x\le1$
(both functions vanish on $(-\infty,0]$).  It does
\emph{not} remain in $[0,1]$ to the right of that interval.  This is the global object that
satisfies the cleanest differential equation:
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal F'(x)=2\mathcal F(2x)\qquad(x\in\R).             \label{eq:global-diffeq}
\end{equation}
Arias de Reyna's arithmetic paper uses this extension.  Statements about its values outside
$[0,1]$ must therefore not be transferred blindly to the bounded cumulative distribution.

\begin{boxedremark}
\textbf{Notation used in this article.}
$F$ always means the bounded distribution function; $\Up$ always means the compactly
supported even bump; and $\mathcal F$ always means the signed global extension.  We have
$F=\mathcal F$ throughout $(-\infty,1]$ (hence on $[0,1]$), but globally they are different
functions.
\end{boxedremark}

\subsection{Exact shape: positivity, bijectivity, and unimodality}
\label{shape:basic}

The three defining equations \eqref{eq:F-left}--\eqref{eq:F-diffeq} already determine the
qualitative picture, but the statements that are actually used later are the sharp ones: not
$F\ge0$ but $F(x)>0$ exactly for $x>0$; not $\Up\le1$ but $\Up(x)=1$ exactly at the origin.
We record them here, in the form of equivalences, because the strict versions are what make
the convexity and Lipschitz theorems below sharp.

Everything rests on a single formula that merges \eqref{eq:F-diffeq} and
\eqref{eq:F-diffeq-right} into one identity valid on the whole line.

\begin{lemma}[Unified derivative formula]
\label{thm:shape-unified-derivative}
For every real $x$,
\begin{equation}
 F'(x)=2\Up(2x-1).                                        \label{eq:shape-unified-derivative}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By \eqref{eq:up-absolute}, $\Up(2x-1)=F\bigl(1-|2x-1|\bigr)$.  For $0\le x\le\tfrac12$ one has
$|2x-1|=1-2x$, so $\Up(2x-1)=F(2x)$ and \eqref{eq:shape-unified-derivative} is
\eqref{eq:F-diffeq}.  For $\tfrac12\le x\le1$ one has $|2x-1|=2x-1$, so $\Up(2x-1)=F(2-2x)$
and \eqref{eq:shape-unified-derivative} is \eqref{eq:F-diffeq-right}.  For $x<0$ or $x>1$ one
has $|2x-1|>1$, hence $1-|2x-1|<0$ and $\Up(2x-1)=0$ by \eqref{eq:F-left}, while $F'(x)=0$
because $F$ is constant on each exterior half-line by \eqref{eq:F-left} and
\eqref{eq:F-right}.
\end{proof}

Since $\Up\ge0$, formula \eqref{eq:shape-unified-derivative} shows at once that $F$ is
nondecreasing on all of $\R$.  It is also convenient to note that the reflection symmetry
\eqref{eq:F-symmetry}, stated on the unit interval, in fact holds globally:
\begin{equation}
 F(1-x)=1-F(x)\qquad(x\in\R).                             \label{eq:shape-symmetry-global}
\end{equation}
Indeed, for $x<0$ both sides equal $1$ by \eqref{eq:F-left} and \eqref{eq:F-right}, and for
$x>1$ both sides equal $0$.

\begin{theorem}[Positivity, strict monotonicity, bijectivity]
\label{thm:shape-bijection}
The bounded Fabius function satisfies the following.
\begin{enumerate}
\item $F(x)>0$ if and only if $x>0$.
\item $F(x)<1$ if and only if $x<1$.
\item $F'(x)>0$ for $0<x<1$, and $F$ is strictly increasing on $[0,1]$.
\item $F$ maps $[0,1]$ bijectively onto $[0,1]$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The heart of the matter is the following doubling step: if $0\le x\le\tfrac12$ and $F(x)=0$,
then $F(2x)=0$.  To see it, observe that $F\ge0$ everywhere, so a zero of $F$ is a global
minimum of a function differentiable on all of $\R$; Fermat's rule gives $F'(x)=0$, and
\eqref{eq:F-diffeq} turns this into $2F(2x)=0$.

Suppose now that $F(x)=0$ for some $x>0$.  Since $2^nx\to\infty$, there is a smallest
integer $n\ge0$ with $2^nx\ge\tfrac12$.  For $0\le m<n$ we have $2^mx<\tfrac12$, so the
doubling step applies at $2^mx$ and propagates the vanishing: from $F(x)=0$ we obtain
$F(2^mx)=0$ for every $m\le n$, and in particular $F(2^nx)=0$.  But $F$ is nondecreasing and
$2^nx\ge\tfrac12$, so $F(2^nx)\ge F(\tfrac12)=\tfrac12$, where the midpoint value comes from
\eqref{eq:F-symmetry} at $x=\tfrac12$.  This contradiction proves that $F$ has no zero on the
positive half-line; combined with \eqref{eq:F-left} it proves (i).

Assertion (ii) follows from (i) and \eqref{eq:shape-symmetry-global}: $F(x)<1$ if and only if
$F(1-x)>0$, that is, if and only if $1-x>0$.

For (iii), let $0<x<1$.  Then $2x-1\in(-1,1)$, so $1-|2x-1|\in(0,1]$, and (i) gives
$\Up(2x-1)=F(1-|2x-1|)>0$.  Now \eqref{eq:shape-unified-derivative} yields $F'(x)>0$.  A
continuous function on $[0,1]$ whose derivative is positive throughout the interior is
strictly increasing on $[0,1]$.

For (iv), $F$ is continuous with $F(0)=0$ and $F(1)=1$, so the intermediate value theorem
makes $F([0,1])$ contain $[0,1]$; since $F$ takes values in $[0,1]$, the image is exactly
$[0,1]$.  Strict monotonicity from (iii) gives injectivity.
\end{proof}

The fold \eqref{eq:up-absolute} now transfers the whole picture to Rvachev's bump, and it
does so in strict form.

\begin{theorem}[Strict unimodality of $\Up$]
\label{thm:shape-unimodal}
Rvachev's function satisfies the following.
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\Up$ is strictly increasing on $[-1,0]$ and strictly decreasing on $[0,1]$.
\item $\Up(x)=1$ if and only if $x=0$; the maximum value $1$ is attained at the origin and
      nowhere else.
\item $\Up(x)=0$ if and only if $x\notin(-1,1)$.  Thus $\Up$ is nonzero exactly on
      $(-1,1)$, and $\supp\Up=[-1,1]$.
\item $\Up'(x)>0$ for $-1<x<0$ and $\Up'(x)<0$ for $0<x<1$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By \eqref{eq:up-absolute}, $\Up(x)=F(1-x)$ for $x\ge0$.  As $x$ increases through $[0,1]$ the
argument $1-x$ decreases through $[0,1]$, on which $F$ is strictly increasing by
\cref{thm:shape-bijection}(iii); hence $\Up$ is strictly decreasing on $[0,1]$, and evenness
gives (i) on $[-1,0]$.

For (ii), $\Up(x)=F(1-|x|)$ and \cref{thm:shape-bijection}(ii) say that $\Up(x)<1$ unless
$1-|x|\ge1$, that is, unless $x=0$; and $\Up(0)=F(1)=1$.  For (iii), $\Up(x)>0$ if and only
if $1-|x|>0$ by \cref{thm:shape-bijection}(i), and $\Up\ge0$, so the zero set is exactly the
complement of $(-1,1)$; the closed support is the closure $[-1,1]$.

For (iv), the identity $\Up(x)=F(1-x)$ holds on the whole open half-line $x>0$, hence may be
differentiated there: $\Up'(x)=-F'(1-x)$, which is negative for $0<x<1$ by
\cref{thm:shape-bijection}(iii).  Evenness gives $\Up'(x)=-\Up'(-x)$, so $\Up'>0$ on
$(-1,0)$.
\end{proof}

Parts (i) and (iii) are the classical description of the bump \cite{rvachev1990,arias1982};
the strict forms, and the equivalences in \cref{thm:shape-bijection}, are exactly what the
formal development records.

\subsection{Convexity and the maximal slope}
\label{shape:convexity}

The derivative formula transfers the monotonicity of \(\Up\) to the derivative of \(F\).

\begin{theorem}[Convexity, concavity, and the maximal slope]
\label{thm:shape-convexity}
The derivative \(F'\) is nondecreasing on \((-\infty,\tfrac12]\) and nonincreasing on
\([\tfrac12,\infty)\).  It is strictly increasing on \([0,\tfrac12]\) and strictly decreasing
on \([\tfrac12,1]\).  Consequently \(F\) is convex on
\((-\infty,\tfrac12]\), concave on \([\tfrac12,\infty)\), strictly convex on
\([0,\tfrac12]\), and strictly concave on \([\tfrac12,1]\).  Moreover
\begin{equation}
 F'(\tfrac12)=2=\max_{x\in\R}F'(x),                       \label{eq:shape-max-slope}
\end{equation}
and equality \(F'(x)=2\) holds exactly at \(x=\tfrac12\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Formal proof]
The derivative monotonicity statements are
\lean{monotoneOn_deriv_fabiusReal},
\lean{antitoneOn_deriv_fabiusReal},
\lean{strictMonoOn_deriv_fabiusReal_Icc}, and
\lean{strictAntiOn_deriv_fabiusReal_Icc}.  The four convexity statements are
\lean{convexOn_fabiusReal_Iic_half},
\lean{concaveOn_fabiusReal_Ici_half},
\lean{strictConvexOn_fabiusReal_firstHalf}, and
\lean{strictConcaveOn_fabiusReal_secondHalf}.  Finally
\lean{deriv_fabiusReal_half},
\lean{deriv_fabiusReal_eq_two_iff}, and
\lean{isGreatest_deriv_fabiusReal} prove the exact maximal-slope statement.  All are in
\path{Convexity.lean}.
\end{proof}

The same module records the complete pointwise curvature profile.  For every
\(x\in\R\),
\begin{equation}
 F''(x)=8\bigl(\Up(4x-1)-\Up(4x-3)\bigr),                \label{eq:shape-second-derivative}
\end{equation}
and therefore
\begin{align}
 F''(x)>0&\iff 0<x<\tfrac12,\notag\\
 F''(x)<0&\iff \tfrac12<x<1,\notag\\
 F''(x)=0&\iff x\notin(0,1)\ \hbox{or}\ x=\tfrac12.    \label{eq:shape-curvature-loci}
\end{align}
The derivative-level statement and its pointwise corollary are
\lean{deriv_fabiusReal_hasDerivAt} and \lean{deriv_deriv_fabiusReal}; the
three equivalences are \lean{deriv_deriv_fabiusReal_pos_iff},
\lean{deriv_deriv_fabiusReal_neg_iff}, and
\lean{deriv_deriv_fabiusReal_eq_zero_iff}, with
\lean{deriv_deriv_fabiusReal_half} naming the midpoint specialization.

\subsection{The optimal Lipschitz constant}
\label{shape:lipschitz}

The bound $F'\le2$ of \eqref{eq:shape-max-slope} is a Lipschitz estimate, and because the
bound is attained the estimate cannot be improved.

\begin{theorem}[Two is the least Lipschitz constant]
\label{thm:shape-lipschitz}
Both $F$ and $\Up$ are $2$-Lipschitz on $\R$:
\begin{equation}
 |F(x)-F(y)|\le2|x-y|,\qquad |\Up(x)-\Up(y)|\le2|x-y|\qquad(x,y\in\R).
                                                          \label{eq:shape-lipschitz}
\end{equation}
Moreover $2$ is the least admissible constant for each: if $K\ge0$ is such that
$|F(x)-F(y)|\le K|x-y|$ for all $x,y$, or such that $|\Up(x)-\Up(y)|\le K|x-y|$ for all
$x,y$, then $K\ge2$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By \eqref{eq:shape-unified-derivative} and $0\le\Up\le1$ we have $0\le F'\le2$ on all of
$\R$, so the mean value theorem gives the first estimate in \eqref{eq:shape-lipschitz}.  The
map $x\mapsto1-|x|$ is $1$-Lipschitz, so the second estimate follows from the first through
the fold \eqref{eq:up-absolute}.

For optimality, let $K$ be admissible for $F$.  Dividing $|F(x)-F(\tfrac12)|\le K|x-\tfrac12|$
by $|x-\tfrac12|$ and letting $x\to\tfrac12$ gives $K\ge|F'(\tfrac12)|=2$ by
\eqref{eq:shape-max-slope}.  For $\Up$ the same limit at the same point works: the proof of
\cref{thm:shape-unimodal}(iv) gives $\Up'(\tfrac12)=-F'(\tfrac12)=-2$, so $K\ge2$.
\end{proof}

The two extremal points are the same point in the two pictures: $\tfrac12$ is the centre of
the sigmoid and the midpoint of the decreasing half of the bump, and the fold sends one to
the other.

\begin{corollary}[Linear majorants at the endpoints]
\label{thm:shape-majorants}
For all admissible arguments,
\begin{align}
 F(x)&\le2x                       &&(x\ge0),   \label{eq:shape-majorant-left}\\
 1-F(x)&\le2(1-x)                 &&(x\le1),   \label{eq:shape-majorant-right}\\
 \Up(x)&\le2\bigl(1-|x|\bigr)     &&(|x|\le1). \label{eq:shape-majorant-up}
\end{align}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Applying \eqref{eq:shape-lipschitz} with $y=0$ and using $F(0)=0$ gives
\eqref{eq:shape-majorant-left}.  Replacing $x$ by $1-x$ there and using
\eqref{eq:shape-symmetry-global} gives \eqref{eq:shape-majorant-right}.  Substituting
$1-|x|\ge0$ into \eqref{eq:shape-majorant-left} and using \eqref{eq:up-absolute} gives
\eqref{eq:shape-majorant-up}.
\end{proof}

The content of \eqref{eq:shape-majorant-left} lies on $[0,\tfrac12]$; for $x\ge\tfrac12$ it is
weaker than $F\le1$.  Its value is that it holds with no hypothesis at all on the size of
$x$, so it can be used as a linear majorant in any estimate near the left endpoint, and
\eqref{eq:shape-majorant-up} says that the bump vanishes at least linearly at both ends of
its support.  These majorants and the optimality of the constant $2$ are contributions of the
formal development; the plain $2$-Lipschitz bound is used implicitly throughout
\cite{arias1982,rvachev1990}.

\section{Existence and uniqueness by a contraction}
\label{sec:existence}

The most economical construction of $F$ is a Banach fixed-point argument.  It has an
additional advantage: the contraction estimate later gives a clean uniqueness theorem,
without presupposing Fourier analysis or probability.

\subsection{The admissible function space}

Let $I=[0,1]$ and let $C(I)$ carry the supremum norm.  Define $\mathcal A\subset C(I)$ by
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal A=\{f\in C(I):0\le f\le1,
   \ f(1-x)=1-f(x)\}.                                     \label{eq:admissible-space}
\end{equation}
The set $\mathcal A$ is closed in the Banach space $C(I)$, hence complete.  It is nonempty;
for example $f(x)=x$ belongs to it.

Extend $f\in\mathcal A$ to a continuous function $\widetilde f$ on $\R$ by clamping the
argument to $I$:
\begin{equation}
 \widetilde f(t)=
 \begin{cases}
  f(0),&t\le0,\\
  f(t),&0\le t\le1,\\
  f(1),&t\ge1.
 \end{cases}                                               \label{eq:clamped-extension}
\end{equation}
Symmetry gives
\begin{equation}
 \int_0^1 f(t)\dd t=\frac12.                              \label{eq:admissible-half-integral}
\end{equation}
Indeed, replacing $t$ by $1-t$ and adding the two integrals gives $2\int_0^1f=1$.

\subsection{The integral transform}

For $f\in\mathcal A$, define $Tf$ by
\begin{equation}
 (Tf)(x)=
 \begin{cases}
  \displaystyle\int_0^{2x}\widetilde f(t)\dd t,
       &0\le x\le\tfrac12,\\[1.2ex]
  \displaystyle 1-\int_0^{2-2x}\widetilde f(t)\dd t,
       &\tfrac12<x\le1.
 \end{cases}                                               \label{eq:fixed-transform}
\end{equation}
The two definitions agree at $x=1/2$ by
\eqref{eq:admissible-half-integral}.  Positivity and the upper bound $f\le1$ show that
$0\le Tf\le1$, and a direct substitution shows $(Tf)(1-x)=1-(Tf)(x)$.  Hence
$T:\mathcal A\to\mathcal A$.

The central estimate is stronger than the naive integral bound.

\begin{lemma}[Contraction estimate]
For $f,g\in\mathcal A$,
\begin{equation}
 \|Tf-Tg\|_\infty\le\frac12\|f-g\|_\infty.                \label{eq:contraction}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Write $h=f-g$.  Since both $f$ and $g$ have integral $1/2$,
$\int_0^1h=0$.  For $0\le y\le1$ we may therefore estimate the primitive in two ways:
\begin{align*}
 \left|\int_0^y h(t)\dd t\right|
 &\le y\|h\|_\infty,\\
 \left|\int_0^y h(t)\dd t\right|
 &=\left|\int_y^1h(t)\dd t\right|
 \le(1-y)\|h\|_\infty.
\end{align*}
Consequently
\begin{equation*}
 \left|\int_0^yh(t)\dd t\right|
 \le\min(y,1-y)\|h\|_\infty\le\frac12\|h\|_\infty.
\end{equation*}
Every value of $Tf-Tg$ is one of these primitives, possibly with a minus sign.  Taking the
supremum proves \eqref{eq:contraction}.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Existence of the bounded Fabius function]
The transform $T$ has a unique fixed point $f_\ast\in\mathcal A$.  Extending $f_\ast$ by
$0$ on $(-\infty,0]$ and by $1$ on $[1,\infty)$ produces the bounded Fabius function $F$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The space $\mathcal A$ is complete and $T$ is a strict contraction with constant $1/2$, so
Banach's fixed-point theorem gives a unique fixed point.  On $[0,1/2]$ its fixed-point
equation is
\begin{equation}
 F(x)=\int_0^{2x}F(t)\dd t.                               \label{eq:F-integral-equation}
\end{equation}
Differentiating gives $F'(x)=2F(2x)$.  The second half of
\eqref{eq:fixed-transform} supplies symmetry and the matching right-hand formula.  The
endpoint values are immediate.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The differential equation for $\Up$ and smoothness}

Folding \eqref{eq:F-diffeq} and \eqref{eq:F-diffeq-right} yields the refinement-type
differential equation
\begin{equation}
 \Up'(x)=2\bigl(\Up(2x+1)-\Up(2x-1)\bigr)\qquad(x\in\R).  \label{eq:up-diffeq}
\end{equation}
At $x=0$ the two one-sided derivatives glue because $\Up(1)=\Up(-1)=0$.  Once $\Up$ is
continuously differentiable, \eqref{eq:up-diffeq} bootstraps regularity: if $\Up\in C^r$,
then the right side is $C^r$, so $\Up\in C^{r+1}$.  Induction gives $\Up\in C^\infty$ and
then $F\in C^\infty$.

\begin{proposition}[Basic shape]
The functions $F$ and $\Up$ have the following properties.
\begin{enumerate}
\item $F$ is strictly increasing on $(0,1)$ and satisfies $0<F(x)<1$ there.
\item $\Up$ is even, strictly positive on $(-1,1)$, and zero outside $[-1,1]$.
\item $\int_{-1}^{1}\Up(x)\dd x=1$.
\item Every derivative of $\Up$ vanishes at $x=\pm1$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For $0<x\le1/2$, \eqref{eq:F-diffeq} gives $F'(x)=2F(2x)$.  Positivity follows first near
$1/2$ from $F(1/2)=1/2$, and then propagates toward zero by repeatedly doubling the
argument; symmetry handles the right half.  This proves strict monotonicity and, by
\eqref{eq:up-absolute}, positivity of $\Up$ in the interior of its support.  Finally,
\begin{equation*}
 \int_{-1}^{1}\Up(x)\dd x
 =2\int_0^1F(t)\dd t=1,
\end{equation*}
where the middle identity uses the fold and the last uses symmetry.  Flatness at the support
endpoints follows from smoothness and the fact that $\Up$ is identically zero just outside
the support.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The original Rvachev characterization}

The formal predicate \lean{IsOriginalFabius} packages the source characterization exactly.
For a function \(\varphi:\R\to\R\) and a scale \(k\), it requires \(C^\infty\)-smoothness,
topological support \([-1,1]\), strict positivity on \((-1,1)\), the normalization
\(\varphi(0)=1\), positivity of \(k\), and
\begin{equation}
 \varphi'(x)=k\bigl(\varphi(2x+1)-\varphi(2x-1)\bigr)
 \qquad(x\in\R).                                           \label{eq:formal-original-rvachev}
\end{equation}
The positivity of the scale is redundant: the smart constructor
\lean{IsOriginalFabius.mk_of_derivative_law} derives it from the other hypotheses.

\begin{theorem}[Original characterization]
\label{thm:formal-original-characterization}
If \(\lean{IsOriginalFabius}\ \varphi\ k\), then
\begin{equation}
 k=2,\qquad \varphi=\Up.                                   \label{eq:formal-characterization-iff}
\end{equation}
Conversely, the canonical \(\Up\) satisfies this predicate at scale \(2\); in particular
there is exactly one original solution.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Formal proof]
The scale is fixed by \lean{originalFabius_scale_eq_two}; the canonical witness is
\lean{canonical_isOriginalFabius}.  Uniqueness and the displayed characterization are
\lean{originalFabius_eq_canonical},
\lean{isOriginalFabius_iff_eq_canonical}, and
\lean{existsUnique_originalFabius} in
\path{OriginalUniqueness.lean}.
\end{proof}

The fold loses only the unused right tail of a bounded candidate.  Precisely,
\begin{equation}
 \Up_G=\Up_H
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 G(t)=H(t)\ \hbox{for every }t\le1.                        \label{eq:exact-kernel-fold}
\end{equation}
Thus a bounded candidate is canonical exactly when its fold satisfies the original
characterization at scale \(2\) and its right tail is constantly \(1\).
These are the exact declarations
\lean{rvachevUp_eq_iff_eqOn_Iic_one},
\lean{isFabius_iff_isOriginalFabius_rvachevUp_and_rightTail}, and
\lean{isOriginalFabius_iff_existsUnique_isFabius} in
\path{OriginalUniqueness.lean}.

\section{The probability law behind the functions}
\label{sec:probability}

The fixed-point construction is analytic.  A probabilistic construction explains the same
self-similarity almost without calculation and makes monotonicity and positivity transparent.

\subsection{An infinite weighted sum of uniforms}

Let $U_0,U_1,\ldots$ be independent random variables, each uniformly distributed on
$[0,1]$, and define
\begin{equation}
 X=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{U_n}{2^{n+1}}.                \label{eq:random-X}
\end{equation}
The series converges absolutely for every outcome and satisfies $0\le X\le1$.  Removing the
first coordinate gives an independent copy $X'$ of $X$, and
\begin{equation}
 X=\frac{U_0+X'}2.                                         \label{eq:random-split}
\end{equation}
Reflecting every coordinate, $U_n\mapsto1-U_n$, preserves the product measure and sends
$X$ to $1-X$.

Let $H(x)=\Prob(X\le x)$ be the cumulative distribution function.  Conditioning on $U_0$
in \eqref{eq:random-split} gives
\begin{equation}
 H(y)=\int_0^1 H(2y-u)\dd u.                               \label{eq:CDF-smoothing}
\end{equation}
For $0\le y\le1/2$, the integrand vanishes when $u>2y$, so
\begin{equation}
 H(y)=\int_0^{2y}H(t)\dd t.                                \label{eq:CDF-left}
\end{equation}
Reflection gives $H(1-y)=1-H(y)$.  Thus the restriction of $H$ to $[0,1]$ is a fixed point
of the contraction $T$ from \cref{sec:existence}.

\begin{theorem}[Global probabilistic representation]
\label{thm:probability-representation-global}
For every \(x,y\in\R\),
\begin{align}
 F(x)
 &=\Prob\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{U_n}{2^{n+1}}\le x\right),
                                                               \label{eq:F-CDF}\\
 \Up(y)
 &=\Prob\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{U_n}{2^{n+1}}\le1-|y|\right).
                                                               \label{eq:up-CDF-global}
\end{align}
The second identity includes both tails: if \(|y|\ge1\), both sides vanish.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Formal proof]
The product-space construction proves continuity and measurability of the uniformly
convergent coordinate sum, identifies its pushforward distribution, establishes the
head--tail self-similarity and the coordinate-reflection law, and invokes uniqueness of the
bounded Fabius fixed point.  The exact canonical specializations are
\lean{fabiusReal_eq_weightedSum_probability} and
\lean{rvachevUp_eq_weightedSum_probability_global} in
\path{ProbabilityRepresentation.lean}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Finite atomic laws and weak convergence}

There is also a completely discrete approximation theorem.  For \(k\ge0\), let
\(\beta_k\) be the probability measure assigning mass \(1/2\) to each of
\(\pm2^{-k-1}\).  Starting from \(\nu_0=\delta_0\), define
\begin{equation}
 \nu_{n+1}=\nu_n*\beta_{n+1}^{*(n+1)}.                         \label{eq:finite-atomic-law}
\end{equation}
Thus \(\nu_n\) is a finite atomic probability measure.  Its characteristic function is the
finite weighted cosine product
\begin{equation}
 \widehat\nu_n(t)
 =\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}
   \left(\cos\frac{t}{2^{k+2}}\right)^{k+1}.                   \label{eq:finite-atomic-char}
\end{equation}
The indexing in \eqref{eq:finite-atomic-law} is worth noticing: the new scale at stage
\(n+1\) is \(2^{-n-2}\), and it occurs with multiplicity \(n+1\).

Let \(\mu_{\Up}\) denote the formal measure \lean{rvachevMeasure}; by definition it is
Lebesgue measure with density \(\operatorname{ofReal}\!\circ\Up\).
It is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure; in particular, every
Lebesgue-null set is also \(\mu_{\Up}\)-null.  These are
\lean{rvachevMeasure_absolutelyContinuous} and
\lean{rvachevMeasure_eq_zero_of_volume_eq_zero} in \path{StepMeasureBridge.lean}.
Its characteristic function uses the positive-frequency normalization
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{charFun}(\mu_{\Up},t)
 =\widehat{\Up}\!\left(\frac{t}{2\pi}\right)\qquad(t\in\R),
                                                               \label{eq:rvachev-charfun}
\end{equation}
the exact identity \lean{rvachevMeasure_charFun_pos} in \path{WeakConvergence.lean}.
The finite laws converge weakly to \(\mu_{\Up}\):
\begin{equation}
 \nu_n\Longrightarrow\mu_{\Up}.                               \label{eq:finite-atomic-weak}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, for every bounded continuous real-valued map \(g\),
\begin{equation}
 \lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\R}g(x)\,\dd\nu_n(x)
 =\int_{\R}g(x)\,\dd\mu_{\Up}(x).                              \label{eq:finite-atomic-tests}
\end{equation}
The same test-function theorem is formalized for complex-valued, and more generally
real-or-complex-like, bounded continuous functions.

\begin{proof}[Formal proof]
The definitions are \lean{centeredBernoulliMeasure} and
\lean{finiteConvolutionMeasure}.  Their probability-measure instances and
\eqref{eq:finite-atomic-char} are proved in \path{EarlyApproximants.lean}.  Pointwise
convergence of the characteristic functions, weak convergence
\eqref{eq:finite-atomic-weak}, and the test-function formulations
\eqref{eq:finite-atomic-tests} are respectively
\lean{finiteConvolutionMeasure_charFun_tendsto},
\lean{finiteConvolutionProbability_tendsto_fabius}, and
\lean{integral_finiteConvolutionMeasure_tendsto_fabius} in
\path{WeakConvergence.lean}.
\end{proof}


\subsection{Combinatorial step functions and the half-weight convention}

A second finite model replaces each atom by a narrow interval.  Define polynomials with
nonnegative integer coefficients by
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal P_0(X)=1,\qquad
 \mathcal P_m(X)=\mathcal P_{m-1}(X^2)(1+X)^m\quad(m\ge1).     \label{eq:four-P-recursion}
\end{equation}
They also have the finite product form
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal P_m(X)=
 \prod_{j=1}^{m}\bigl(1+X+\cdots+X^{2^j-1}\bigr).             \label{eq:four-P-product}
\end{equation}
Writing \(\mathcal P_m(X)=\sum_r a_{m,r}X^r\), the coefficient \(a_{m,r}\)
counts the tuples
\begin{equation}
 (s_1,\ldots,s_m),\qquad
 0\le s_j\le2^j-1,\qquad s_1+\cdots+s_m=r.                    \label{eq:four-restricted-tuples}
\end{equation}
Moreover,
\begin{equation}
 g_m:=\deg\mathcal P_m=2^{m+1}-m-2,\qquad
 \mathcal P_m(1)=2^{\binom{m+1}{2}}.                          \label{eq:four-degree-mass}
\end{equation}

For \(\alpha<\beta\), let \(\mathbf 1^\ast_{[\alpha,\beta]}\) be \(1\) in the
interior, \(1/2\) at either endpoint, and \(0\) elsewhere.  Put
\begin{equation}
 I_{m,r}=
 \left[\frac{2r-1-g_m}{2^{m+1}},
       \frac{2r+1-g_m}{2^{m+1}}\right]                        \label{eq:four-cell}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal W_m(x)=
 \frac{2^m}{2^{\binom{m+1}{2}}}
 \sum_{r=0}^{g_m}a_{m,r}\,\mathbf 1^\ast_{I_{m,r}}(x).         \label{eq:four-step-approximant}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Corrected step approximation]
\label{thm:four-step-approx}
For every \(m\ge0\) and \(x\in\R\),
\[
 0\le\mathcal W_m(x)\le1,\qquad \mathcal W_m(0)=1,
 \qquad \int_{\R}\mathcal W_m(x)\dd x=1.
\]
The function \(\mathcal W_m\) is nondecreasing on \((-\infty,0)\) and
nonincreasing on \((0,\infty)\).  For every real \(x\),
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal W_m(x)\longrightarrow\Up(x).                        \label{eq:four-step-limit}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

The proof of pointwise convergence passes through a useful exact comparison.  Set
\(h_m=2^{-m-1}\), and use the finite atomic law \(\nu_m\) from
\eqref{eq:finite-atomic-law}.  Whenever \(a\le b\),
\begin{equation}
 \nu_m\bigl((a+h_m,b-h_m]\bigr)
 \ \le\ \int_a^b\mathcal W_m(x)\dd x
 \ \le\ \nu_m\bigl((a-h_m,b+h_m]\bigr).                       \label{eq:four-step-sandwich}
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\begin{equation}
 \lim_{m\to\infty}\int_a^b\mathcal W_m(x)\dd x
 =\int_a^b\Up(x)\dd x.                                       \label{eq:four-step-interval-limit}
\end{equation}
Monotonicity on the two open half-lines, the exact value at the origin, and continuity of
\(\Up\) then upgrade these interval limits to \eqref{eq:four-step-limit}.

\begin{proof}[Formal proof]
The polynomial recursion, product, degree, total coefficient mass, and bounded-partition
interpretation are respectively proved by
\lean{approximationPolynomial_succ},
the definition \lean{approximationPolynomial},
\lean{approximationPolynomial_natDegree},
\lean{approximationDegree_eq_sub},
\lean{approximationPolynomial_eval_one}, and
\lean{approximationPolynomial_coeff_eq_card} in
\path{EarlyApproximants.lean}.  The finite atomic description is identified with
\lean{finiteConvolutionMeasure} by
\lean{polynomialMeasure_eq_finiteConvolutionMeasure} in
\path{EarlyMeasureBridge.lean}.

For nonnegativity, see \lean{stepApproximant_nonneg} in
\path{EarlyApproximants.lean}.  The remaining analytic assertions are
\lean{stepApproximant_le_one},
\lean{stepApproximant_monotoneOn_Iio},
\lean{stepApproximant_antitoneOn_Ioi},
\lean{stepApproximant_apply_zero}, and
\lean{stepApproximant_tendsto_fabius} in
\path{StepApproximationLimit.lean}.  The normalization, the two sides of
\eqref{eq:four-step-sandwich}, and \eqref{eq:four-step-interval-limit} are
\lean{integral_stepApproximant},
\lean{finiteConvolutionMeasure_inner_le_intervalIntegral_stepApproximant},

\end{proof}

\subsection{Centered finite splines and an explicit error bound}
\label{sec:centered-splines}

The preceding finite random sums also give a completely explicit polynomial approximation
to $F$.  Put
\begin{equation}
 T_p=\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}\frac{U_i}{2^{i+1}},
 \qquad
 C_p(x)=\Prob\left(T_p\le x-2^{-p-1}\right),               \label{eq:centered-partial-cdf}
\end{equation}
where $p\ge1$.  The shift by half of the omitted tail is important: it centers the
one-sided approximation and improves its error by a factor of two.

Let
\begin{equation}
 N_p(x)=\max\left(0,\left\lfloor 2^p x+\frac12\right\rfloor\right),
 \qquad \varepsilon_r=(-1)^{\wt(r)},                       \label{eq:centered-cutoff}
\end{equation}
and interpret a sum with $N_p(x)=0$ as empty.  Define
\begin{equation}
 S_p(x)=
 \frac{(-1)^p}{2^{\binom p2}p!}
 \sum_{r=0}^{N_p(x)-1}
 \varepsilon_r
 \left(r-2^p x+\frac12\right)^p.                          \label{eq:centered-spline}
\end{equation}
Equivalently,
\begin{equation}
 S_p(x)=
 \frac{1}{2^{\binom p2}p!}
 \sum_{r\ge0}\varepsilon_r
 \left(2^p x-\frac12-r\right)_+^p,                        \label{eq:centered-spline-positive}
\end{equation}
where only the terms $r<N_p(x)$ can be nonzero and $t_+=\max(t,0)$.
The cutoff in \eqref{eq:centered-cutoff} is a safe natural-number form of the
inclusive upper limit
\[
 0\le r\le\left\lfloor2^p x-\frac12\right\rfloor.
\]
It handles the empty case without assigning a negative upper endpoint to a finite sum.

\begin{theorem}[Centered spline as a finite distribution function]
\label{thm:centered-spline-cdf}
For $p\ge1$ and $0\le x\le1$,
\begin{equation}
 S_p(x)=C_p(x).                                            \label{eq:spline-equals-cdf}
\end{equation}
Consequently $0\le S_p(x)\le1$ on the unit interval.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For $p=1$, $T_1=U_0/2$, and direct evaluation gives
\[
 C_1(x)=\max\left(0,\min\left(1,2x-\frac12\right)\right).
\]
Formula \eqref{eq:centered-spline-positive} gives exactly the same broken-linear
function: the term $r=0$ starts at $x=1/4$, and the term $r=1$ subtracts the excess
after $x=3/4$.

For the induction step, split off the first uniform coordinate.  The finite sums satisfy
\[
 T_{p+1}\ \overset{\mathrm d}=\ \frac{U_0+T_p'}2,
\]
where $T_p'$ is an independent copy of $T_p$.  Conditioning on $U_0$ and accounting for
the midpoint shifts gives the exact smoothing recurrence
\begin{equation}
 C_{p+1}(x)=\int_0^1 C_p(2x-u)\dd u.                      \label{eq:centered-cdf-smoothing}
\end{equation}
On the spline side, integrate each truncated power in
\eqref{eq:centered-spline-positive}.  The two binary children $2r$ and $2r+1$
have opposite Thue--Morse signs, because
\(\varepsilon_{2r}=\varepsilon_r\) and
\(\varepsilon_{2r+1}=-\varepsilon_r\).  After the change of scale this yields
\begin{equation}
 S_{p+1}(x)=\int_0^1 S_p(2x-u)\dd u.                      \label{eq:centered-spline-smoothing}
\end{equation}
The normalization is exactly the one required by
\[
 \sum_{r=0}^{2^p-1}\varepsilon_r r^p
 =(-1)^p2^{\binom p2}p!,
\]
while every lower power sum vanishes.  Thus the two recurrences and their common base
case prove \eqref{eq:spline-equals-cdf}.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Quantitative centered approximation]
\label{thm:centered-spline-error}
For $p\ge1$ and $0\le x\le1$,
\begin{equation}
 F(x-2^{-p-1})\le S_p(x)\le F(x+2^{-p-1}),                \label{eq:centered-sandwich}
\end{equation}
and hence
\begin{equation}
 |S_p(x)-F(x)|\le2^{-p}.                                  \label{eq:centered-error}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Write $X=T_p+R_p$, where the omitted tail satisfies
\[
 0\le R_p=\sum_{i=p}^{\infty}\frac{U_i}{2^{i+1}}\le2^{-p}.
\]
If $X\le x-2^{-p-1}$, then certainly
$T_p\le x-2^{-p-1}$.  Conversely, if
$T_p\le x-2^{-p-1}$, then
$X\le x+2^{-p-1}$.  Taking probabilities and using
\eqref{eq:F-CDF} and \eqref{eq:spline-equals-cdf} proves
\eqref{eq:centered-sandwich}.

The bounded Fabius function is globally $2$-Lipschitz.  Indeed, on the open unit interval
its derivative is a rescaled value of $\Up$ (or of the signed extension), whose absolute
value is at most $1$, and outside the unit interval $F$ is constant.  Thus
\[
 |F(x\pm2^{-p-1})-F(x)|\le2\cdot2^{-p-1}=2^{-p}.
\]
Combining these two inequalities with the sandwich gives
\eqref{eq:centered-error}.
\end{proof}

The bound is uniform on the closed interval and includes both endpoints.  It is stronger
than mere weak convergence of the finite distributions, and it is the quantitative input
for the computability proof in \cref{sec:effective-computability}.

The same finite formula also has the correct signed global behavior.  For $x\le0$ its safe
cutoff is empty, so $S_p(x)=\mathcal F(x)=0$.  On the nonnegative half-line, binary block
translation and midpoint reflection extend the local identification.  Consequently, for
every $p\ge1$ and every $x\in\R$,
\begin{equation}
 |S_p(x)-\mathcal F(x)|\le2^{-p}.                         \label{eq:centered-spline-global-limit}
\end{equation}
Indeed, every nonnegative two-unit block is a common signed copy of the first block, and
on the second unit cell both functions use the same complement.  Neither operation changes
the absolute error in \eqref{eq:centered-error}; the nonpositive half-line is exact.  This
pointwise all-real bound is
\lean{abs_fabiusUniformSpline_sub_extendedFabius_le}; it implies the separately proved
uniform convergence \lean{fabiusUniformSpline_tendstoUniformly_extendedFabius}.  This
global form strengthens and packages the analytic input used by the complex-shift and
discrete-limit results below.

\section{Fourier products, inversion, and Poisson summation}
\label{sec:fourier}

Use the convention
\begin{equation}
 \widehat f(z)=\int_\R f(x)\e^{-2\pi\ii xz}\dd x.          \label{eq:fourier-convention}
\end{equation}
The formal Fourier transform of \(\Up\) is entire and has two exact infinite-product
descriptions.

\begin{theorem}[Sinc and cosine products]
\label{thm:four-products}
For every \(z\in\C\),
\begin{align}
 \widehat{\Up}(z)
 &=\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}
   \sinc\!\left(\frac{\pi z}{2^n}\right),                 \label{eq:sinc-product}\\
 &=\prod_{m=1}^{\infty}
   \left(\cos\frac{\pi z}{2^m}\right)^m.                  \label{eq:four-product-cos}
\end{align}
The transform also satisfies
\begin{equation}
 \widehat{\Up}(z)=\sinc(\pi z)\widehat{\Up}(z/2).          \label{eq:fourier-refinement}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Formal proof]

\lean{rvachevFourier_eq_finite_product} and
\lean{rvachevFourier_eq_product} in \path{FourierProduct.lean}.
The refinement equation is \lean{rvachevFourier_scaling} in the same module.
Writing \(\Phi\) for the first product in \eqref{eq:sinc-product}, the
standalone one-step and \(n\)-fold
renormalizations
\[
 \Phi(z)=\sinc(\pi z)\Phi(z/2),\qquad
 \Phi(z)=\left(\prod_{j<n}\sinc(\pi z/2^j)\right)\Phi(z/2^n)
\]
are \lean{rvachevFourierProduct_scaling} and
\lean{rvachevFourierProduct_shell} in

The whole-plane analyticity theorem is

\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Dyadic shell factorization]
\label{thm:fourier-dyadic-shell}
For every \(k\in\N\) and \(z\in\C\), dilation by \(2^k\) extracts exactly the first
\(k\) factors of the sinc product:
\begin{equation}
 \widehat{\Up}(2^kz)
 =\left(\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}\sinc\!\left(\pi 2^{j+1}z\right)\right)
   \widehat{\Up}(z).                                      \label{eq:fourier-dyadic-shell}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Formal proof]
This is \lean{rvachevFourier_two_pow_mul} in
\path{RvachevFourierDecay.lean}.  Its standalone-product form is
\lean{rvachevFourierProduct_two_pow_mul} in \path{SincProductShells.lean}.
On the real axis the removable singularity can be cleared without excluding the zero ray:
for every \(k\in\N\) and \(y\in\R\),
\begin{equation}
 \begin{split}
 &2^{k(k+1)/2}(\pi|y|)^k
   \left|\widehat{\Up}(2^ky)\right|\\
 &\qquad={}
 \left(\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}\left|\sin(2^{j+1}\pi y)\right|\right)
   \left|\widehat{\Up}(y)\right|.
 \end{split}                                               \label{eq:fourier-dyadic-shell-cross}
\end{equation}
This total form includes \(y=0\), and at \(k=0\) both sides are the same Fourier modulus.
It is \lean{norm_rvachevFourier_two_pow_mul_cross} in
\path{RvachevFourierDecay.lean}; its product-level source is
\lean{norm_rvachevFourierProduct_two_pow_mul_cross} in
\path{SincProductShells.lean}.
\end{proof}

The mean-value input behind shell estimates is more general than the dyadic case.
Let \(m_0,m_1,\ldots\) be positive integer frequencies satisfying
\(2m_j\le m_{j+1}\).  Then, for every \(n\in\N\),
\begin{align}
 \int_0^1\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\sin^2(\pi m_jt)\,\dd t
   &=\left(\frac12\right)^n,                              \label{eq:lacunary-square-mean}\\
 \int_0^1\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}|\sin(\pi m_jt)|\,\dd t
   &\le\left(\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\right)^n.                  \label{eq:lacunary-abs-mean}
\end{align}
The exact square mean is \lean{integral_prod_sin_sq} in
\path{LacunaryRieszIntegral.lean}.  Applying Cauchy--Schwarz to it gives
\lean{integral_prod_abs_sin_le_sqrt_two} in
\path{LacunaryMeanSharp.lean}.  The geometric-base and dyadic specializations are
\lean{integral_prod_abs_sin_pow_le_sqrt_two} and
\lean{integral_prod_abs_sin_two_pow_le_sqrt_two}.

The logarithm of the normalized dyadic sine shell has an exact square mean as well.  For
every \(n\in\N\), including the empty product \(n=0\),
\begin{equation}
 \int_0^1
 \log^2\!\left|\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}2\sin(\pi2^jt)\right|\dd t
 =\frac{\pi^2}{4}n-\frac{\pi^2}{3}\left(1-2^{-n}\right).
                                                               \label{eq:dyadic-log-sine-variance}
\end{equation}
The product statement is \lean{integral_sq_log_prod_two_sin_all} in
\path{BirkhoffProductBridge.lean}.  Its doubling-cocycle form is
\lean{integral_sq_birkhoff_sum_all} in \path{BirkhoffVariance.lean}, and the finite
covariance summation is \lean{variance_closed_form_all} in
\path{VarianceBookkeeping.lean}.  The all-index declarations make the zero-length
boundary an identity rather than a side condition.

For a quantitative global form, set
\begin{equation}
 \kappa_\infty=\frac32+\frac{\log(\pi/\sqrt3)}{\log2},
 \qquad
 E_\kappa(x)=\exp\!\left(-\frac{(\log x)^2}{2\log2}\right)x^{-\kappa}
 \quad(x>0).                                               \label{eq:fourier-decay-gauge}
\end{equation}
There is a constant \(C>0\) such that the single two-sided estimate
\begin{equation}
 |\widehat{\Up}(x)|\le C E_{\kappa_\infty}(|x|)
 \qquad(|x|\ge1)                                          \label{eq:fourier-two-sided-envelope}
\end{equation}
holds on both real tails.  This is
\lean{norm_rvachevFourier_le_decayGauge_abs} in
\path{RvachevFourierDecay.lean}.  On the distinguished dyadic ray the same gauge occurs
in an exact identity:
\begin{equation}
 |\widehat{\Up}(2^k\,2/3)|
 =\frac{|\widehat{\Up}(2/3)|}{E_{\kappa_\infty}(2/3)}
   E_{\kappa_\infty}(2^k\,2/3),                           \label{eq:fourier-peak-ray-envelope}
\end{equation}
namely \lean{norm_rvachevFourier_peak_ray_envelope} in the same module.

The exponent \(\kappa_\infty\) is sharp within this family of gauges.  Write \(\Phi\) for
the standalone sinc product, which equals \(\widehat{\Up}\) by
\lean{rvachevFourier_eq_product}.  For every \(\kappa>\kappa_\infty\),
\begin{equation}
 \frac{|\Phi(2^k\,2/3)|}{E_\kappa(2^k\,2/3)}
 \longrightarrow+\infty
 \qquad(k\longrightarrow\infty).                            \label{eq:fourier-kappa-sharp}
\end{equation}
More explicitly, for every real \(C\) there is a \(k\in\N\) such that
\(x_k=2^k\,2/3\ge1\) and
\begin{equation}
 C E_\kappa(x_k)<|\Phi(x_k)|.                               \label{eq:fourier-kappa-obstruction}
\end{equation}
Consequently no \(C>0\) satisfies
\[
 |\Phi(x)|\le C E_\kappa(x)\qquad\text{for every real }x\ge1.
\]
The limit, explicit peak-ray obstruction, and positive-ray nonexistence theorem are
\lean{norm_rvachevFourierProduct_peak_ray_ratio_tendsto_atTop},
\lean{exists_peak_ray_norm_gt_mul_decayGauge}, and
\lean{not_exists_norm_rvachevFourierProduct_le_decayGauge_of_kappaInf_lt} in
\path{GlobalDecayEnvelope.lean}.

The transform also has the exact conjugation, parity, and real-axis identities
\begin{equation}
 \overline{\widehat{\Up}(z)}=\widehat{\Up}(-\overline z),
 \qquad \widehat{\Up}(-z)=\widehat{\Up}(z),
 \qquad \operatorname{Im}\widehat{\Up}(t)=0\quad(t\in\R).   \label{eq:fourier-symmetries}
\end{equation}
These are \lean{rvachevFourier_conj} in \path{FourierAnalytic.lean} and
\lean{rvachevFourier_neg}, \lean{rvachevFourier_ofReal_im_eq_zero} in
\path{FourierProduct.lean}.

\begin{theorem}[Exact Fourier zero set and positive-integer zero orders]
\label{thm:fourier-zeros}
The complex zeros of the Fourier transform are precisely the nonzero integers:
\begin{equation}
 \widehat{\Up}(z)=0
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \exists\,m\in\Z\setminus\{0\}\text{ such that }z=m,
 \qquad z\in\C.                                           \label{eq:fourier-integer-zeros}
\end{equation}
At every positive integer \(m\), its real-axis zero order is exactly
\(\vtwo(m)+1\), in the following quantitative sense: there is a constant
\(C_m>0\) such that
\begin{equation}
 \lim_{\substack{x\to m\\x\ne m}}
 \frac{\left|\widehat{\Up}(x)\right|}
      {|x-m|^{\vtwo(m)+1}}
 =C_m.                                                     \label{eq:fourier-zero-order}
\end{equation}
The corresponding canonical product records all of these orders at once:
\begin{equation}
 \widehat{\Up}(z)=\prod_{r=0}^{\infty}
 \left(1-\frac{z^2}{(r+1)^2}\right)^{1+\vtwo(r+1)}.       \label{eq:fourier-canonical-product}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Formal proof]
The complete complex zero-set equivalence is
\lean{rvachevFourier_eq_zero_iff} in \path{FourierProduct.lean}.
The positive-integer limit is
\lean{tendsto_norm_rvachevFourier_div_pow_zero_order} in
\path{RvachevFourierDecay.lean}; its limiting constant is exhibited there as
an exponential, and is therefore strictly positive.  The canonical product is
\lean{rvachevFourier_eq_canonical} in the same facade module, built from
\lean{rvachevFourierProduct_eq_canonical} in \path{SincCanonicalProduct.lean}.
\end{proof}

On the real axis the origin is the unique global point of unit modulus:
\begin{equation}
 |\widehat{\Up}(x)|=1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x=0,
 \qquad x\ne0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad |\widehat{\Up}(x)|<1.
                                                               \label{eq:fourier-global-peak}
\end{equation}
These are \lean{norm_rvachevFourier_eq_one_iff} and
\lean{norm_rvachevFourier_lt_one_of_ne_zero}.  On each positive real lobe
the sign and peak are also exact.  If \(m\in\N\) and \(m<x<m+1\), then
\begin{equation}
 \widehat{\Up}(x)=\varepsilon_m|\widehat{\Up}(x)|,        \label{eq:fourier-lobe-sign}
\end{equation}
by \lean{rvachevFourier_eq_thueMorse_sign_mul_norm}.  If moreover \(m\ge1\),
there is exactly one point of \((m,m+1)\) maximizing the modulus over that
open lobe and exactly one point of the reflected lobe \((-(m+1),-m)\)
maximizing it there.  These are
\lean{existsUnique_isMaxOn_rvachevFourier_lobe} and
\lean{existsUnique_isMaxOn_rvachevFourier_neg_lobe}.  All five facade
declarations are in \path{RvachevFourierDecay.lean}.

Globally, the origin is the unique real frequency of unit modulus:
\begin{equation}
 \left|\widehat{\Up}(x)\right|\le1,
 \qquad
 \left|\widehat{\Up}(x)\right|=1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x=0.
                                                               \label{eq:fourier-strict-global-peak}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, the first inequality is strict whenever \(x\ne0\).  These statements are
\lean{norm_rvachevFourier_le_one},
\lean{norm_rvachevFourier_lt_one_of_ne_zero}, and
\lean{norm_rvachevFourier_eq_one_iff} in \path{RvachevFourierDecay.lean}.

\subsection{Poisson summation and partitions of unity}

 \sum_{k\in\Z}\Up(t+uk)
 =\frac1u\sum_{k\in\Z}\widehat{\Up}(k/u)
   \e^{2\pi\ii kt/u}.                                     \label{eq:poisson-up}

\subsection{The totalized inverse}

Let \(I:\R\to[0,1]\) be the inverse order isomorphism on \([0,1]\), extended constantly by
\(0\) on the left and \(1\) on the right.  This is the formal \lean{fabiusInv} constructed
from \lean{fabiusOrderIso}.  It is continuous and nondecreasing; on \([0,1]\) it is a

\begin{equation}
 F(I(y))=y,\qquad I(F(x))=x\qquad(x,y\in[0,1]).            \label{eq:fabius-inverse-identities}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
 I'(y)
 =\frac{1}{F'(I(y))}
 =\frac{1}{2\,\Up(2I(y)-1)}

\end{equation}

\lean{deriv_fabiusInv_half}, \lean{deriv_deriv_fabiusInv_half}, and the
\lean{deriv_deriv_fabiusInv_neg_iff},
\lean{deriv_deriv_fabiusInv_pos_iff}, and
\lean{deriv_deriv_fabiusInv_eq_zero_iff} family.  The hypothesis \(0<y<1\)
is essential in the last three statements: the totalized inverse is not
differentiable at the clamping endpoints.

The inverse inherits the exact symmetry and midpoint
\begin{equation}
 I(1-y)=1-I(y),\qquad I(1/2)=1/2,                          \label{eq:fabius-inverse-symmetry}
\end{equation}
and for \(0\le a\le2^n\),
\begin{equation}
 I\!\left(F\!\left(\frac{a}{2^n}\right)\right)=\frac{a}{2^n}. \label{eq:fabius-inverse-dyadic}
\end{equation}
These are \lean{fabiusInv_one_sub}, \lean{fabiusInv_half}, and
\lean{fabiusInv_fabiusDyadicUnit_of_le}.  Its exact analytic locus is likewise
\begin{equation}
 I\text{ is analytic at }y
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad y\notin[0,1],               \label{eq:fabius-inverse-analytic-locus}
\end{equation}
by \lean{fabiusInv_analyticAt_iff}; in the open interval its derivative never vanishes by
\lean{deriv_fabiusInv_ne_zero}.

Flatness of \(F\) becomes infinite endpoint steepness of \(I\).  For every \(n\in\N\),
\begin{align}
 y&=o\bigl(I(y)^n\bigr) &&(y\downarrow0),                  \label{eq:fabius-inverse-left-steepness}\\
 1-y&=o\bigl((1-I(y))^n\bigr) &&(y\uparrow1).             \label{eq:fabius-inverse-right-steepness}
\end{align}
These are \lean{id_isLittleO_fabiusInv_pow_at_zero_right} and
\lean{one_sub_isLittleO_one_sub_fabiusInv_pow_at_one_left}.  In particular, the two
one-sided difference quotients diverge:
\begin{equation}
 \frac{I(y)}{y}\longrightarrow+\infty\quad(y\downarrow0),
 \qquad
 \frac{1-I(y)}{1-y}\longrightarrow+\infty\quad(y\uparrow1). \label{eq:fabius-inverse-infinite-slopes}
\end{equation}
The formally proved counterparts are \lean{tendsto_fabiusInv_div_atTop} and
\lean{tendsto_one_sub_fabiusInv_div_one_sub_atTop}.  Thus the totalized inverse is not
differentiable at either clamping point, exactly as stated by
\lean{fabiusInv_not_differentiableAt_zero} and
\lean{fabiusInv_not_differentiableAt_one}; no finite endpoint derivative is being smuggled
into the curvature statement above.  The stronger limits of the positive interior derivative
itself are also
formalized:
\begin{equation}
 I'(y)\longrightarrow+\infty\quad(y\downarrow0),\qquad
 I'(y)\longrightarrow+\infty\quad(y\uparrow1).           \label{eq:fabius-inverse-derivative-blowup}
\end{equation}
They are \lean{tendsto_deriv_fabiusInv_atTop_at_zero_right} and
\lean{tendsto_deriv_fabiusInv_atTop_at_one_left}; these are limits through
the open interval, not finite derivatives at \(0\) or \(1\).

\subsection{Precise non-elementarity}

The inductive predicate \lean{IsElementary} contains constants and the identity and is

\subsection{Even and half moments}

Retain the even moments \(c_n\) from \eqref{eq:c-def}; in particular, \(c_0=1\).  Define
\begin{equation}
 d_0=1,\qquad
 d_n=n\int_0^1t^{n-1}\Up(t)\dd t\quad(n\ge1).              \label{eq:d-integral}
\end{equation}
Both are positive rational sequences.  Their exact triangular recurrences, for \(n\ge1\),
are
\begin{align}
 (2n+1)(2^{2n}-1)c_n
 &=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\binom{2n+1}{2k}c_k,                   \label{eq:c-recurrence}\\
 (n+1)(2^n-1)d_n
 &=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\binom{n+1}{k}d_k.                     \label{eq:d-recurrence}
\end{align}
These are the rational declarations \lean{moment_succ} and
\lean{halfMoment_succ} in \path{Arithmetic.lean}; positivity is
\lean{moment_pos} in \path{Paper06487Supplement.lean} and
\lean{halfMoment_pos} in \path{Arithmetic.lean}.  The displayed integral definitions are
\lean{integral_even_pow_mul_rvachev_eq_moment},
\lean{moment_eq_integral_formula}, and
\lean{halfMoment_eq_integral_formula_all} in \path{AnalyticMoments.lean}.

The entire generating function
\begin{equation}
 G(z)=1+z\int_0^1\Up(t)\e^{zt}\dd t
     =\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}d_n\frac{z^n}{n!}                 \label{eq:G-def}
\end{equation}
satisfies
\begin{equation}
 G(2z)=\frac{\e^z-1}{z}\,G(z),                             \label{eq:G-functional}
\end{equation}
with the removable value at \(z=0\).  The exact formal statements are
\lean{proposition_two_formula} and
\lean{complexGeneratingFunction_eq_series_formula} in
\path{AnalyticMoments.lean}; entire differentiability is
\lean{differentiable_complexGeneratingFunction} in
\path{NegativeLaplaceVerticalLog.lean}.

The two sequences are linked by
\begin{equation}
 d_n=2^{-n}\sum_{0\le k\le n/2}\binom n{2k}c_k,            \label{eq:d-from-c}
\end{equation}
and, at odd indices,
\begin{equation}
 d_{2k+1}=\frac{2k+1}{2}\,c_k.                             \label{eq:mom-d-odd-c}
\end{equation}
These are \lean{halfMoment_eq_evenMomentSum} and
\lean{halfMoment_odd_eq_moment} in \path{MomentPowerSeries.lean}.  Division-free natural
numerators for \(c_n\) and \(d_n\), together with their exact recurrences and odd-index
bridge, are formalized by \lean{momentNumerator},
\lean{halfMomentNumerator}, \lean{halfMomentNumerator_succ},
\lean{halfMomentNumerator_odd_index_eq}, and
\lean{halfMomentNumerator_odd_index_formula}.  No unproved numerical table is needed.

More explicitly, if \(C_n=\lean{momentNumerator}\,(n)\), then \(C_n\) is a positive
integer and
\begin{equation}
 c_n=
 \frac{C_n}
 {(2n+1)!!\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{n}(2^{2j}-1)}.          \label{eq:moment-natural-normalization}
\end{equation}
The exact rational identity is \lean{moment_eq_momentNumerator_div}; the product
reindexing is \lean{evenMersenneProduct_eq_prod_Ico}, and positivity is
\lean{momentNumerator_pos}.  Thus \eqref{eq:moment-natural-normalization} records the
proved natural normalization, not a claim that the displayed denominator is already
reduced.

\subsection{Universal Bell and cumulant transforms}
\label{sec:moment-cumulant-algebra}

The algebra converting moments to cumulants is independent of the particular Fabius
law.  Let \(R\) be any commutative \(\Q\)-algebra and let \(a:\N\to R\).  Write
\begin{equation}
 (\mathcal Na)_n=\frac{a_n}{n!},
 \qquad
 (\mathcal Da)_n=n!a_n.                                  \label{eq:factorial-normalization}
\end{equation}
These changes of coefficient convention are mutually inverse.  For cumulants
\(\kappa:\N\to R\) and moments \(\mu:\N\to R\), define the complete Bell and
moment--cumulant transforms by the formal identities
\begin{align}
 \mathcal B(\kappa)_n
   &=n![X^n]\exp\!\left(\sum_{j\ge1}\kappa_j\frac{X^j}{j!}\right),
                                                               \label{eq:complete-bell-transform}\\
 \mathcal K(\mu)_n
   &=n![X^n]\log\!\left(1+\sum_{j\ge1}\mu_j\frac{X^j}{j!}\right).
                                                               \label{eq:moment-cumulant-transform}
\end{align}
Here the exponential and logarithm are formal power series implemented by finite
coefficient recurrences; no topology or field structure on \(R\) is being assumed.

\begin{proposition}[Bell recurrence and exact inversion]
\label{thm:moment-cumulant-algebra}
For every \(n\in\N\),
\begin{align}
 \mathcal B(\kappa)_0&=1,
 &\mathcal K(\mu)_0&=0,                                  \label{eq:bell-cumulant-zero}\\
 \mathcal B(\kappa)_{n+1}
   &=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\binom nj\kappa_{j+1}
       \mathcal B(\kappa)_{n-j}.                         \label{eq:complete-bell-recurrence}
\end{align}
At every index, including zero, the two composites are exactly
\begin{align}
 \mathcal B(\mathcal K(\mu))_n
   &=\begin{cases}1,&n=0,\\ \mu_n,&n>0,\end{cases}
 &
 \mathcal K(\mathcal B(\kappa))_n
   &=\begin{cases}0,&n=0,\\ \kappa_n,&n>0.\end{cases}    \label{eq:bell-cumulant-inverses}
\end{align}
Consequently the transforms are inverse as functions when \(\mu_0=1\) and
\(\kappa_0=0\), respectively.  More precisely, if \(f:R\to S\) is a morphism of
commutative \(\Q\)-algebras, then
\begin{align}
 f\!\left(\mathcal B_R(\kappa)_n\right)
   &=\mathcal B_S(f\circ\kappa)_n,
 &
 f\!\left(\mathcal K_R(\mu)_n\right)
   &=\mathcal K_S(f\circ\mu)_n.                           \label{eq:bell-cumulant-naturality}
\end{align}

More generally, if \(E\) and \(A\) are ordinary coefficient sequences for the formal
exponent and mass series, then the coefficient-convention bridge is
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal B(\mathcal D E)=\mathcal D(\lean{expCoeff}(E)),
 \qquad
 \mathcal K(\mathcal D A)=\mathcal D(\lean{logCoeff}(A)). \label{eq:bell-ordinary-bridge}
\end{equation}
Thus the same theorem applies without modification to polynomial rings, function rings,
and scalar extensions, not only to real- or rational-valued moment sequences.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Formal proof]
The operations \(\mathcal N,\mathcal D,\mathcal B,\mathcal K\) are respectively
\lean{factorialNormalize}, \lean{factorialDenormalize},
\lean{completeBellPolynomial}, and \lean{momentCumulant}.  The two identities following
\eqref{eq:factorial-normalization} are
\lean{factorialNormalize_factorialDenormalize} and
\lean{factorialDenormalize_factorialNormalize}.  The zero values and
\eqref{eq:complete-bell-recurrence} are \lean{completeBellPolynomial_zero},
\lean{momentCumulant_zero}, and \lean{completeBellPolynomial_succ}.

Equation \eqref{eq:bell-cumulant-inverses} is
\lean{completeBellPolynomial_momentCumulant} together with
\lean{momentCumulant_completeBellPolynomial}; its normalized function-level forms are
\lean{completeBellPolynomial_momentCumulant_of_zero_eq_one} and
\lean{momentCumulant_completeBellPolynomial_of_zero_eq_zero}.  The bridge
\eqref{eq:bell-ordinary-bridge} is
\lean{completeBellPolynomial_factorialDenormalize} and
\lean{momentCumulant_factorialDenormalize}.  Finally, functoriality is
\eqref{eq:bell-cumulant-naturality}, formalized by
\lean{map_completeBellPolynomial} and \lean{map_momentCumulant}.  All declarations are in
\path{MomentCumulantAlgebra.lean} and assume only a commutative \(\Q\)-algebra.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The probability--Laplace bridge}
\label{sec:probability-laplace-bridge}

Let \(\mu_X\) be the distribution of the weighted sum \(X\) from
\eqref{eq:random-X}.  Its survival function on the nonnegative half-line is exactly
\begin{equation}
 \mu_X((t,\infty))=\Up(t)\qquad(t\ge0).                    \label{eq:survival-up}
\end{equation}
If \(g,g':\R\to\R\) are continuous and \(g\) has derivative \(g'(t)\) at every
\(t\in[0,1]\), then
\begin{equation}
 \int_{[0,1]}g(x)\,\dd\mu_X(x)
 =g(0)+\int_0^1g'(t)\Up(t)\dd t.                           \label{eq:survival-fubini}
\end{equation}
For \(s\in\R\) and \(k\in\N\), put
\begin{equation}
 L_k(s)=\int_{[0,1]}\e^{-sx}x^k\,\dd\mu_X(x).              \label{eq:raw-laplace-moment}
\end{equation}
These raw tilted moments agree with the formal analytic Fabius Laplace moments, and at zero
tilt
\begin{equation}
 L_n(0)=\int_{[0,1]}(1-x)^n\,\dd\mu_X(x)=d_n.              \label{eq:probability-half-moment}
\end{equation}
On the real axis, every derivative of the generating function is a tilted moment:
\begin{equation}
 G^{(n)}(s)=L_n(-s),\qquad G^{(n)}(0)=d_n.                  \label{eq:G-derivative-laplace}
\end{equation}
Reflection of the law about \(1/2\) gives
\begin{equation}
 L_0(s)=\e^{-s}L_0(-s),\qquad
 \e^{s/2}L_0(s)=\e^{-s/2}L_0(-s),                          \label{eq:laplace-reflection}
\end{equation}
so \(s\mapsto\e^{s/2}L_0(s)\) is even.  Two useful quantitative forms, with all
variables real, are
\begin{align}
 L_0\!\left(\frac{a+b}{2}\right)^2
   &\le L_0(a)L_0(b) &&(a,b\in\R),                         \label{eq:laplace-midpoint}\\
 L_k(s)&\le (1/a)^k k!\,L_0(s-a) &&(a>0).                 \label{eq:laplace-shift}
\end{align}

\begin{proof}[Formal proof]
The survival identity and integration-by-parts formula are
\lean{weightedSumDistribution_real_Ioi_eq_rvachevUp_of_nonneg} and
\lean{integral_unit_eq_zero_add_integral_deriv_mul_rvachevUp}.  The tilted and zero-tilt
identities are
\lean{unitLaplaceMoment_weightedSumDistribution_eq_fabiusLaplaceMoment},
\lean{unitLaplaceMoment_weightedSumDistribution_zero_eq_halfMoment}, and
\lean{unitEndpointMoment_weightedSumDistribution_eq_halfMoment} in
\path{ProbabilityLaplaceMoments.lean}.  The derivative identities are
\lean{iteratedDeriv_generatingFunction} and
\lean{iteratedDeriv_generatingFunction_zero} in \path{LaplaceMoments.lean};
\eqref{eq:laplace-reflection} is
\lean{fabiusLaplaceMoment_zero_reflection} and
\lean{fabiusLaplaceMoment_zero_centered_even}.  Finally,
\eqref{eq:laplace-midpoint}--\eqref{eq:laplace-shift} are
\lean{fabiusLaplaceMoment_midpoint_sq_le_all} and
\lean{fabiusLaplaceMoment_le_shift} in \path{UnitLaplaceMomentBounds.lean}.
\end{proof}

\section{Exact evaluation at arbitrary dyadic rationals}
\label{sec:dyadic}

The rationality theorem for dyadic arguments comes with two complementary exact
presentations:
\begin{enumerate}
\item a closed finite formula involving Thue--Morse signs and the rational moments $c_k$;
\item an executable bit-recursive Taylor evaluator.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The closed Thue--Morse formula}

\begin{theorem}[Exact dyadic formula]
Let $n,a\in\N$ with $0\le a\le2^n$.  Then
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
 F\!\left(\frac{a}{2^n}\right)
 =\frac{2^{-\binom{n+1}{2}}}{n!}
 \sum_{h=0}^{a-1}(-1)^{\wt(h)}
 \sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
 \binom n{2k}c_k(2a-2h-1)^{n-2k}.}
                                                               \label{eq:dyadic-closed}
\end{equation}
An empty outer sum has value $0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Formal proof]
The rational expression on the right is the definition \lean{fabiusDyadic}.  Its equality,
after coercion to \(\R\), with \(F(a/2^n)\) under the displayed bound on \(a\) is
\lean{fabiusDyadic_cast} in \path{DyadicAnalytic.lean}.
\end{proof}

Every quantity on the right of \eqref{eq:dyadic-closed} is rational, so dyadic rationality is
immediate.  More importantly, the formula lives entirely in $\Q$ and can be evaluated without
roundoff.


\begin{theorem}[Global dyadic formula]
\label{thm:dy-global-closed}
For all $n,a\in\N$ the signed global extension satisfies
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
 \mathcal F\!\left(\frac{a}{2^n}\right)
 =\frac{2^{-\binom{n+1}{2}}}{n!}
 \sum_{h=0}^{a-1}\varepsilon_h
 \sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
 \binom n{2k}c_k(2a-2h-1)^{n-2k}.}
                                                               \label{eq:dy-global-closed}
\end{equation}
No upper bound on $a$ is imposed.  An empty outer sum has value $0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Formal proof]
The displayed rational identity is \lean{fabiusDyadic_cast_extended_nat} in
\path{GlobalDyadic.lean}, after unfolding the closed form \lean{fabiusDyadic}.
\end{proof}

This closed form depends only on the represented dyadic rational, not on the chosen
numerator--exponent pair.  Writing
\(\operatorname{Dyad}(n,m)=\lean{fabiusDyadic}\,n\,m\), Lean proves
\begin{align}
 \operatorname{Dyad}(n+k,2^km)&=\operatorname{Dyad}(n,m),
                                                               \label{eq:dyadic-refine-pow}\\
 \frac{m_1}{2^{n_1}}=\frac{m_2}{2^{n_2}}\ \text{in }\Q
 &\Longrightarrow
 \operatorname{Dyad}(n_1,m_1)=\operatorname{Dyad}(n_2,m_2).   \label{eq:dyadic-rat-invariance}
\end{align}
The exact declarations are \lean{fabiusDyadic_refine_pow} and
\lean{fabiusDyadic_eq_of_rat_eq} in \path{GlobalDyadic.lean}.

Two special cases are the ones used in practice.

\begin{corollary}[Bounded case]
\label{cor:dy-bounded-case}
If $0\le a\le2^n$ then $\mathcal F(a/2^n)=F(a/2^n)$, so \eqref{eq:dy-global-closed}
reduces to \eqref{eq:dyadic-closed}.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The two functions agree on $[0,1]$ and $a/2^n\in[0,1]$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Bump case]
\label{cor:dy-bump-case}
Let $n\in\N$ and $q\in\Z$.  If $|q|<2^n$, then
\begin{equation}
 \Up\!\left(\frac q{2^n}\right)
 =\frac{2^{-\binom{n+1}{2}}}{n!}
 \sum_{h=0}^{q+2^n-1}\varepsilon_h
 \sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
 \binom n{2k}c_k\bigl(2q+2^{n+1}-2h-1\bigr)^{n-2k},
                                                               \label{eq:dy-bump-case}
\end{equation}
that is, \eqref{eq:dy-global-closed} evaluated at $a=q+2^n$.  If $|q|\ge2^n$, then
$\Up(q/2^n)=0$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Taking $b=0$ in \eqref{eq:single-block} gives $\mathcal F(x)=\Up(x-1)$ for $0\le x\le2$.
With $x=1+q/2^n=(q+2^n)/2^n$ and $|q|<2^n$ we have $0<x<2$, so
$\Up(q/2^n)=\mathcal F\bigl((q+2^n)/2^n\bigr)$, and
\eqref{eq:dy-global-closed} with $a=q+2^n$ applies.  The last statement is the support
condition $\supp\Up=[-1,1]$.
\end{proof}

Corollary~\ref{cor:dy-bump-case} is the global form invoked in the proof of the uniform
denominator multiplier theorem below.  The point is that it genuinely requires
\Cref{thm:dy-global-closed} and not \eqref{eq:dyadic-closed}: for $0<q<2^n$ the numerator
$a=q+2^n$ exceeds $2^n$, so the bounded statement does not apply.  At the endpoints
$|q|=2^n$ the two clauses agree, both giving $0$; for $q=-2^n$ the outer sum is empty and
for $q=2^n$ the value is $\mathcal F(2)=0$.

\begin{remark}[Why the sum is finite]
\label{rem:dy-finiteness}
Both index sets in \eqref{eq:dy-global-closed} are finite: the outer range is
\(0\le h<a\), and the inner range is \(0\le k\le\lfloor n/2\rfloor\).
The moments \(c_k\) are rational by the proved recurrence \eqref{eq:c-recurrence}.
Thus the formula is an exact computation in \(\Q\), with no analytic truncation or
floating-point roundoff.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Exact evaluation without uncertified examples]
The finite sum is an exact rational algorithm, and its agreement with the analytic Fabius
function is \lean{fabiusDyadic_cast}.  Concrete numerical specializations that do not yet
have their own named Lean theorems are recorded only in the consolidated research-frontier
volume.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Thue--Morse cancellation}

The mechanism that makes the dyadic formulas simplify is the exact Prouhet cancellation
carried by the Thue--Morse signs.

\begin{lemma}[Affine power cancellation]
If \(d<b\), then for all \(X,Y\in\Q\),
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{h=0}^{2^b-1}(-1)^{\wt(h)}(X+Yh)^d=0.               \label{eq:thue-affine-cancel}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Formal proof]
This is the strict-degree case of
\lean{thueMorse_affine_power_sum_of_le} in \path{ThueMorsePrefix.lean};
the monomial statement used internally is
\lean{thueMorsePowerSum_eq_zero_of_lt}.
\end{proof}

The finite block polynomial packages the same signs without introducing an analytic
exponential identity:
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{h<2^b}\varepsilon_hz^h
 =\prod_{j=0}^{b-1}(1-z^{2^j}).                            \label{eq:thue-block-product}
\end{equation}
This is \lean{thueMorseBlockPolynomial_eq_product} in
\path{ThueMorseGenerating.lean}.  At the level of the centered formal exponential series,
the corresponding zero has exact order \(b\), by
\lean{order_thueMorseCenteredPowerSeries} and
\lean{thueMorseCenteredPowerSeries_eq_product} in
\path{ThueMorseExponential.lean}.

Writing \(P_b(z)\) for either side of \eqref{eq:thue-block-product}, its zero at \(z=1\)
has exact multiplicity \(b\) over \(\Z[z]\):
\begin{equation}
 (1-z)^b\mid P_b(z),
 \qquad
 (1-z)^{b+1}\nmid P_b(z).                                  \label{eq:thue-block-multiplicity}
\end{equation}
The divisibility and matching nondivisibility statements are respectively
\lean{one_sub_X_pow_dvd_thueMorseBlockPolynomial} and
\lean{one_sub_X_pow_succ_not_dvd_thueMorseBlockPolynomial} in
\path{ThueMorseBlockAlgebra.lean}.

The ordinary derivatives of the finite block polynomial make that exact order
visible without passing to formal power series.

\begin{proposition}[Block-polynomial derivatives at one]
\label{prop:thue-block-derivatives}
For every \(b,r\ge0\),
\begin{equation}
 \left.\frac{\dd^r}{\dd z^r}
   \sum_{h<2^b}\varepsilon_hz^h\right|_{z=1}
 =r!\sum_{h<2^b}\varepsilon_h\binom hr.                   \label{eq:thue-block-derivative-moment}
\end{equation}
Consequently this derivative is zero when \(r<b\), while the first surviving
derivative is
\begin{equation}
 \left.\frac{\dd^b}{\dd z^b}
   \prod_{j=0}^{b-1}(1-z^{2^j})\right|_{z=1}
 =(-1)^b b!\,2^{\binom b2}.                               \label{eq:thue-block-derivative-sharp}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Formal proof]
The derivative--binomial dictionary is
\lean{iterate_derivative_thueMorseBlockPolynomial_eval_one}; its strict
vanishing and first surviving cases are respectively
\lean{iterate_derivative_thueMorseBlockPolynomial_eval_one_of_lt} and
\lean{iterate_derivative_thueMorseBlockPolynomial_eval_one_self}, all in
\path{ThueMorseBlockAlgebra.lean}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[The first surviving Thue--Morse moment]
\label{prop:dy-tm-moment}
For every \(k\ge0\),
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\;
 \sum_{r=0}^{2^k-1}\varepsilon_r\,r^k
 =(-1)^k2^{\binom k2}k!.
 \;}                                                       \label{eq:dy-tm-moment}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Formal proof]
This is \lean{thueMorsePowerSum_self} in \path{ThueMorsePrefix.lean}.
Together with \lean{thueMorsePowerSum_eq_zero_of_lt}, it is also the coefficient
calculation behind the exact-order theorem above.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Translation invariance]
\label{cor:dy-tm-moment-shift}
For every \(k\ge0\) and \(Q\in\Q\),
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{r=0}^{2^k-1}\varepsilon_r\,(r+Q)^k
 =(-1)^k2^{\binom k2}k!.                                  \label{eq:dy-tm-moment-shift}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}[Formal proof]
Apply \lean{thueMorse_affine_power_sum_self} with \(X=Q\) and \(Y=1\).
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Centered reflection and the characteristic-two boundary]
\label{prop:thue-morse-centered-reflection}
Let \(R\) be a commutative ring, let \(c\in R\) satisfy
\(c+c=2^m-1\), and put
\begin{equation}
 S_{m,r}(c)=\sum_{n=0}^{2^m-1}\varepsilon_n(n-c)^r\in R.
                                                                    \label{eq:thue-centered-moment}
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
 S_{m,r}(c)=(-1)^{m+r}S_{m,r}(c).                         \label{eq:thue-centered-reflect}
\end{equation}
Consequently, if \(m+r\) is odd, then \(2S_{m,r}(c)=0\).  This is the
characteristic-free conclusion: in a ring with \(2\)-torsion one cannot in general cancel
the factor \(2\).  Over \(\Q\), taking \(c=(2^m-1)/2\) gives the familiar parity rule
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{n=0}^{2^m-1}\varepsilon_n
   \left(n-\frac{2^m-1}{2}\right)^r=0
 \qquad(m+r\ \text{odd}).                                \label{eq:thue-midpoint-parity}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Formal proof]
The fixed-point identity is
\lean{sum_thueMorseSign_mul_centered_pow_reflect}, and its universal
\(2\)-torsion consequence is
\lean{two_mul_sum_thueMorseSign_mul_centered_pow_eq_zero}.  The rational
specialization, including the legitimate cancellation of \(2\ne0\), is
\lean{sum_thueMorseSign_mul_midpoint_pow_eq_zero}.  All three declarations are in
\path{ThueMorseMoments.lean}.
\end{proof}

The centered specialization \(Q=-2^k\) is therefore
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{r=0}^{2^k-1}\varepsilon_r\,(r-2^k)^k
 =(-1)^k2^{\binom k2}k!,                                  \label{eq:dy-tm-moment-centered}
\end{equation}
the exact declaration \lean{thueMorseCenteredPowerSum_self}.

\subsection{The exact leading-bit recurrence}

Let \(e,a\in\N\) with \(0<a\le2^e\), and write
\begin{equation}
 b=\lfloor\log_2a\rfloor,\qquad
 r=e-b,\qquad q=a-2^b,\qquad y=\frac{q}{2^e}.              \label{eq:bit-split}
\end{equation}
Thus \(0\le q<a\): the leading binary power has been removed.  Put
\(V_m=F(2^{-m})\), whose exact half-moment form is \eqref{eq:F-inverse-power}, and define
\begin{equation}
 T_r(y)=\sum_{j=0}^{r}
 \frac{2^{\binom{j+1}{2}}V_{r-j}}{j!}\,y^j.               \label{eq:Horner-polynomial}
\end{equation}
Then the exact rational recurrence is
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\;
 F\!\left(\frac a{2^e}\right)
 =T_r\!\left(\frac q{2^e}\right)
  -F\!\left(\frac q{2^e}\right).
 \;}                                                       \label{eq:taylor-block}
\end{equation}

The formal route stays entirely inside the rational dyadic model:
\lean{fabiusTaylorHorner_eq_sum} identifies the Horner routine with
\eqref{eq:Horner-polynomial};
\lean{fabiusInversePowTwoTable_hasBlockTaylor} proves the block identity;
\lean{blockTaylor_implies_bitRecurrence} turns it into the leading-bit recurrence; and
\lean{fabiusInversePowTwoTable_hasBitRecurrence} instantiates that recurrence for the
precomputed inverse-power table.  Finally,
\lean{fabiusDyadicUnit_eq_fabiusDyadic} and
\lean{fabiusDyadic_cast} identify the executable rational result with the analytic
function.  These declarations are in \path{DyadicClosedForm.lean} and
\path{DyadicAnalytic.lean}.

\subsection{The bit-recursive evaluator}

Use \(b,r,q,y\), and \(T_r\) from
\eqref{eq:bit-split}--\eqref{eq:Horner-polynomial}.  Equation
\eqref{eq:taylor-block} reduces the numerator from \(a\) to the strictly smaller
\(q\).

\begin{algorithm}[Exact dyadic evaluator]
\label{alg:dyadic-evaluator}
Precompute $V_j=F(2^{-j})$ for $0\le j\le e$ from
\eqref{eq:F-inverse-power}.  Define $\operatorname{Eval}(e,a)$ recursively by
\begin{equation*}
 \operatorname{Eval}(e,a)=
 \begin{cases}
  0,&a\le0,\\
  1,&a\ge2^e,\\
  T_r(q/2^e)-\operatorname{Eval}(e,q),&0<a<2^e,
 \end{cases}
\end{equation*}
where \(b,r,q\) are given by \eqref{eq:bit-split}; evaluate \(T_r\) by Horner's rule.
\end{algorithm}

A literal pseudocode version is:
\begin{lstlisting}[style=pseudo]
Eval(e, a, V):
    if a <= 0:      return 0
    if a >= 2^e:    return 1

    b = floor_log2(a)
    r = e - b
    q = a - 2^b
    y = q / 2^e

    p = V[0]                       // highest Taylor coefficient, j = r
    for m = 1 .. r:
        j = r - m
        p = V[m] + (2^(j+1) * y / (j+1)) * p

    return p - Eval(e, q, V)
\end{lstlisting}
The displayed loop is one convenient Horner ordering; any exact evaluation of
\eqref{eq:Horner-polynomial} is equivalent.  In the formal source this evaluator is
\lean{fabiusDyadicValue}.

\begin{theorem}[Correctness and termination]
For every $e\in\N$ and $a\in\Z$,
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{Eval}(e,a)=F(a/2^e)                        \label{eq:evaluator-correct}
\end{equation}
under the bounded convention $F=0$ to the left and $F=1$ to the right.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Formal proof]
The pseudocode is the rational routine \lean{fabiusDyadicValue}, whose analytic correctness
is \lean{fabiusDyadicValue_cast} in \path{PaperStatements.lean}.  The interior recursion is
\lean{fabiusInversePowTwoTable_hasBitRecurrence}; the corresponding total recursive
correctness theorem is \lean{fabiusDyadicUnitAux_eq_of_bitRecurrence}.
\end{proof}


\subsection{Reduction of an arbitrary dyadic argument}
\label{subsec:dy-triangular}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:dyadic-evaluator} evaluates the bounded function $F$ on $[0,1]$.  A total
evaluator for the signed global extension first folds the argument back into the unit interval
and records the associated sign.  The fold is a reduction modulo $2$ followed by a reflection.

\begin{theorem}[Triangular reduction]
\label{thm:dy-triangular}
Let $n\in\N$ and put $s=2^n$.  Let $a\in\N$ and write
\begin{equation}
 a=2sB+R,\qquad B=\left\lfloor\frac a{2s}\right\rfloor,\qquad 0\le R<2s,
                                                               \label{eq:dy-triangular-split}
\end{equation}
and set
\begin{equation}
 C=\begin{cases}R,&R\le s,\\ 2s-R,&R>s.\end{cases}
                                                               \label{eq:dy-triangular-fold}
\end{equation}
Then $0\le C\le s$ and
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\ \mathcal F\!\left(\frac a{2^n}\right)=\varepsilon_B\,F\!\left(\frac C{2^n}\right).\ }
                                                               \label{eq:dy-triangular}
\end{equation}
For $a\in\Z$ with $a<0$ one has $\mathcal F(a/2^n)=0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Write $x=a/2^n=2B+R/s$.  Since $0\le R<2s$ we have $2B\le x<2B+2$, so $x$ lies in the
$B$th block and \eqref{eq:single-block} gives
\begin{equation*}
 \mathcal F(x)=\varepsilon_B\Up(x-2B-1)=\varepsilon_B\Up\!\left(\frac Rs-1\right).
\end{equation*}
Put $t=R/s-1$, so that $-1\le t<1$.  The fold relation \eqref{eq:up-fold} says
$\Up(t)=F(t+1)$ for $t\le0$ and $\Up(t)=F(1-t)$ for $t>0$; on $[-1,1]$ both clauses read
$\Up(t)=F(1-|t|)$.  If $R\le s$ then $|t|=1-R/s$ and $F(1-|t|)=F(R/s)$.  If $R>s$ then
$|t|=R/s-1$ and $F(1-|t|)=F(2-R/s)=F\bigl((2s-R)/2^n\bigr)$.  In both cases the argument is
$C/2^n$ with $C$ as in \eqref{eq:dy-triangular-fold}, and $0\le C\le s$ is immediate from
$0\le R<2s$.  The final statement is the vanishing of $\mathcal F$ on the negative half-line.
\end{proof}

Combining \eqref{eq:dy-triangular} with Algorithm~\ref{alg:dyadic-evaluator} gives a total exact
evaluator for $\mathcal F$ at every dyadic rational: reduce with
\eqref{eq:dy-triangular-split}--\eqref{eq:dy-triangular-fold}, compute the block sign
$\varepsilon_B=(-1)^{\wt(B)}$ from the binary digits of $B=\lfloor a/2^{n+1}\rfloor$, and call
$\operatorname{Eval}(n,C)$.

The reduction is compatible with unreduced presentations.  Replacing $(n,a)$ by $(n+1,2a)$
doubles $s$ and $R$, leaves $B$ unchanged, and doubles $C$; the right side of
\eqref{eq:dy-triangular} is therefore unchanged, as it must be.

\begin{remark}[Recognizing dyadic arguments exactly]
\label{rem:dy-rational-interface}
A reduced rational $p/q$ with $q>0$ is dyadic exactly when $q$ is a power of two, and in
that case $n=\log_2q$ and $a=p$ are read off directly.  An exact rational interface to
$F$, $\Up$, and $\mathcal F$ therefore tests a single integer condition on the reduced
denominator, returns ``not dyadic'' when it fails, and otherwise invokes the signed integer
evaluator above.  No floating-point recognition of powers of two is involved anywhere, so
the interface is exact on every input and cannot be defeated by rounding in the recognition
step.
\end{remark}


\subsection{Denominator bounds}

Let
\begin{equation}
 M_m=\prod_{j=1}^{m}(2^{2j}-1),
 \qquad (2m+1)!!=1\cdot3\cdots(2m+1).                     \label{eq:denominator-products}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Uniform denominator multiplier]
Let $n\ge1$ and $a\in\Z$ with $|a|<2^n$.  Then
\begin{equation}
 \Up(a/2^n)\;n!\,2^{\binom{n+1}{2}}
 \bigl(2\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1\bigr)!!\,
 M_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}                                  \label{eq:denominator-bound}
\end{equation}
is an integer.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Formal proof]
This is \lean{rvachevDyadic_mul_denominatorBound_isNatural_all} in
\path{DenominatorBound.lean}.  Its proof uses
\lean{moment_mul_common_denominator} for the moments and
\lean{fabiusDyadic_mul_denominatorBound_isInteger} for the bounded dyadic values.
\end{proof}

Let $\Delta_n$ be the least common multiple of the reduced denominators of the values
$\Up(a/2^n)$ with odd $a$ in the support.  The theorem gives the explicit divisibility bound
\begin{equation}
 \Delta_n\mid n!\,2^{\binom{n+1}{2}}
 \bigl(2\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1\bigr)!!M_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}. \label{eq:lcm-bound}
\end{equation}
A complementary lower statement is also formalized.

\begin{proposition}[The reciprocal power is one of the samples]
\label{prop:dy-delta-lower}
For every $n\ge1$ the reduced denominator of $F(2^{-n})$ divides $\Delta_n$.  Consequently
\begin{equation}
 \vtwo(\Delta_n)\ge\binom n2+1+\vtwo(n!).                  \label{eq:dy-delta-lower}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Formal proof]
The denominator divisibility is \lean{proposition_fifteen} in
\path{PaperStatements.lean}, after the bridge
\lean{dyadicDenominator_eq_fabiusDyadicDenominator}.  The exact two-adic exponent is
\lean{fabiusAtInverseTwoPow_padicVal_two_closed}.
\end{proof}

Together, \eqref{eq:lcm-bound} and \eqref{eq:dy-delta-lower} give the formally
proved denominator information used in this article.  Proposed exact factorizations of the
least common denominator, together with the associated numerical tables, are kept in the
consolidated research-frontier volume until their individual statements are formalized.

\subsection{The exact half-base \(q\)-binomial layer}
\label{subsec:q-binomial-dyadic}
\label{sec:qs-half-base-calculus}

The dyadic dilation forces the specialization \(q=1/2\).  For \(a,q\in\Q\), define
\begin{equation}
 (a;q)_n=\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}(1-aq^j),                          \label{eq:qpochhammer}
\end{equation}
and write \(\qbinom nk{1/2}\) for the Gaussian coefficient at that fixed base.  For
\(k\le n\), its exact Mersenne normalization is
\begin{equation}
 \qbinom nk{1/2}
 =
 \frac{\mathfrak m_n}
 {\mathfrak m_k\mathfrak m_{n-k}\,2^{k(n-k)}},
 \qquad
 \mathfrak m_j=\prod_{\ell=1}^{j}(2^\ell-1).                 \label{eq:qs-qbinom-def}
\end{equation}
In particular the row is symmetric under \(k\mapsto n-k\).

\begin{theorem}[Finite half-base \(q\)-binomial theorem]
\label{thm:qs-finite-q-binomial}
For every \(n\in\N\) and \(z\in\Q\),
\begin{equation}
 (z;1/2)_n=
 \sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^k2^{-\binom k2}
 \qbinom nk{1/2}z^k.                                        \label{eq:half-q-binomial-theorem}
\end{equation}
At every dyadic node this gives the single closed evaluation
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^k2^{-\binom k2}
 \qbinom nk{1/2}(2^m)^k
 =
 \begin{cases}
  0,&m<n,\\[2pt]
  (-1)^n\mathfrak m_m/\mathfrak m_{m-n},&n\le m.
 \end{cases}                                                \label{eq:qs-moment-annihilation}
\end{equation}
In particular, the sum vanishes if and only if \(m<n\).
Moreover,
\begin{equation}
 (1/2;1/2)_n=2^{-\binom{n+1}{2}}\mathfrak m_n.              \label{eq:half-q-mersenne}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Formal proof]
These are \lean{halfQBinomial_theorem},
\lean{qBinomial_half_two_pow_sum_eq_ite},
\lean{qBinomial_half_two_pow_sum_eq_zero_iff},
\lean{halfQPochhammer_eq_mersenne_div},
\lean{halfQBinomial_eq_mersenne}, and
\lean{halfQBinomial_symm} in \path{HalfQBinomial.lean}.
\end{proof}

The polynomial in \eqref{eq:half-q-binomial-theorem} has the complete rational root locus
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^k2^{-\binom k2}\qbinom nk{1/2}z^k=0
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \exists\,j<n\text{ such that }z=2^j,
 \qquad z\in\Q.                                             \label{eq:half-q-root-locus}
\end{equation}
This is \lean{qBinomial_half_sum_eq_zero_iff}.  The same statement is stable under scalar
extension at embedded rational arguments.  Let \(K\) be a field with a
\(\Q\)-algebra structure, and write \(\iota:\Q\to K\) for its canonical map.  Then, for
every \(z\in\Q\),
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{k=0}^{n}
 \iota\!\left((-1)^k2^{-\binom k2}\qbinom nk{1/2}\right)(\iota z)^k=0
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \exists\,j<n\text{ such that }\iota z=2^j\text{ in }K.     \label{eq:half-q-scalar-root-locus}
\end{equation}
This is \lean{qBinomial_half_sum_algebraMap_eq_zero_iff} in
\path{HalfQBinomial.lean}.  The scalar statement classifies the embedded rational
arguments \(\iota z\); it does not assert a root classification for arbitrary elements of
\(K\).

The same Gaussian coefficients form a geometric Prouhet functional.  Set
\[
 w_{n,k}=(-1)^k(1/2)^{\binom k2}\qbinom nk{1/2},
 \qquad \xi_k=-2^k.
\]
For every rational polynomial \(p\) with \(\deg p\le n\),
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{k=0}^{n}w_{n,k}p(\xi_k)
 =[X^n]p\;2^{\binom{n+1}{2}}(1/2;1/2)_n
 =[X^n]p\;\mathfrak m_n.                              \label{eq:half-q-gaussian-prouhet}
\end{equation}
Thus the functional annihilates every polynomial of degree below \(n\).
At \(n=0\), the degree-valued extractor covers all constant polynomials;
the strict-degree cancellation statement admits exactly the zero polynomial.
The two forms of \eqref{eq:half-q-gaussian-prouhet} are
\lean{halfQBinomial_negativeDyadic_polynomial_sum_eq_coeff} and
\lean{halfQBinomial_negativeDyadic_polynomial_sum_eq_mersenne}; strict-degree
cancellation is
\lean{halfQBinomial_negativeDyadic_polynomial_sum_eq_zero_of_degree_lt}, all in
\path{HalfQBinomial.lean}.

The inner cancellation in the dyadic formula is the exact Thue--Morse block identity
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{r=0}^{2^k-1}(-1)^{\wt(r)}z^r
 =\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}(1-z^{2^j}),                              \label{eq:thue-generating-product}
\end{equation}
formalized as \lean{thueMorseBlockPolynomial_eq_product}.  Its affine power-sum form
annihilates every degree below \(k\), while its first surviving moment is
\((-1)^k2^{\binom k2}k!\); see
\lean{thueMorse_affine_power_sum_of_le} and
\lean{thueMorse_affine_power_sum_self}.

For comparison with the moment recurrence, put
\begin{equation}
 a_n=\frac{d_n}{n!},\qquad
 \mathscr A(X)=\sum_{n\ge0}a_nX^n,\qquad
 E(X)=\frac{\e^X-1}{X},                                     \label{eq:qs-a-and-series}
\end{equation}
with the removable value \(E(0)=1\).  The refinement is
\begin{equation}
 \mathscr A(2X)=E(X)\mathscr A(X),                            \label{eq:q-moment-refinement}
\end{equation}
and its coefficient recurrence is
\begin{equation}
 (2^n-1)a_n=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{a_j}{(n-j+1)!}
 \quad(n\ge1),\qquad a_0=1.                                 \label{eq:qs-a-recurrence}
\end{equation}
The functional identity follows from \lean{proposition_two_formula} and
\lean{complexGeneratingFunction_eq_series_formula} in \path{AnalyticMoments.lean};
the coefficient recurrence is \lean{halfMoment_original_recurrence} in
\path{BernoulliRecurrences.lean}.  These are the formal-series bridge used by the
specialized dyadic theorem.

For \(m,k,d\in\N\) and \(Q\in\C\), write
\begin{equation}
 \sigma_{m;k,d}(Q)=
 \sum_{r=0}^{m2^k-1}(-1)^{\wt(r)}
       (r-m2^k+Q)^d.                                        \label{eq:qs-sigma-def}
\end{equation}
The exact result is the following finite identity.

\begin{theorem}[Translated global dyadic formula]
\label{thm:qs-translation}
For all \(m,n\in\N\) and \(Q\in\C\),
\begin{align}
 \mathcal F(m/2^n)
 &=\frac{1}{2^{n^2}(1/2;1/2)_n}
   \sum_{k=0}^{n}
   \frac{\qbinom nk{1/2}}
        {4^{\binom k2}(n+k)!} \notag\\
 &\qquad\qquad\times
   \sum_{r=0}^{m2^k-1}(-1)^{\wt(r)}
      (r-m2^k+Q)^{n+k}.                                    \label{eq:qbinomial-dyadic}
\end{align}
The real number on the left is embedded in \(\C\).  The entire right-hand side is
independent of the common translation \(Q\).  When \(m\le2^n\), the left side is the bounded
value \(F(m/2^n)\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Formal proof]
The complete normalized finite expression is packaged as a polynomial in the translation
and proved equal to the constant centered formula by
\lean{qBinomialThueMorseDyadicTranslatedFormulaPolynomial_eq_const}.
Its identification with the signed dyadic value over \(\C\) is
\lean{globalFabius_dyadic_eq_qBinomialThueMorseDyadic_translated_sum_complex};
the rational core and real, complex, and Gaussian-rational specializations are in
\path{FabiusQBinomialFormula.lean} and
\path{FabiusDyadicQBinomialScalar.lean}.
\end{proof}

The rational specialization is also independent of how the dyadic argument is written.
If
\[
 \frac{m_1}{2^{n_1}}=\frac{m_2}{2^{n_2}},
\]
then the two translated rational formulas agree even when their common translations
\(Q_1,Q_2\in\Q\) differ.  This is
\lean{qBinomialThueMorseDyadicTranslatedFormula_eq_of_rat_eq}; the one-step refinement

\lean{qBinomialThueMorseDyadicTranslatedFormula_refine}.  Over every field over \(\Q\),
both conclusions persist even when the scalar translations and the dyadic presentations
are changed simultaneously; these stronger wrappers are
\lean{qBinomialThueMorseDyadicTranslatedFormulaIn_eq_of_rat_eq} and
\lean{qBinomialThueMorseDyadicTranslatedFormulaIn_refine} in
\path{FabiusDyadicQBinomialScalar.lean}.

Binary complementation gives the raw inverse-dyadic form
\begin{equation}

 \frac{1}{(-2)^{n^2}(1/2;1/2)_n}

 \frac{\qbinom nk{1/2}}{4^{\binom k2}(n+k)!}

\end{equation}
Its translated numerator is constant by
\lean{qBinomialThueMorseRawTranslatedNumeratorPolynomial_eq_const}.  The

\section{The global binary-reduction telescope}
\label{sec:global-binary-reduction}

The Taylor-block recursion also applies to the signed global extension $\mathcal F$.  It can
therefore be iterated along the binary expansion of an arbitrary nonnegative real number,
rather than only along the finite expansion of a dyadic rational.  The reductions need not
terminate, but their residuals tend geometrically to zero.

Define the finite reduction polynomial
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal T_m(y)=
 \sum_{j=0}^{m}
 2^{\binom{j+1}{2}-\binom{m-j}{2}}
 \frac{d_{m-j}}{(m-j)!}\frac{y^j}{j!}.                   \label{eq:global-reduction-polynomial}
\end{equation}
All powers of $2$ here are integer powers.  In particular, negative exponents are allowed,
and $\mathcal T_0(y)=1$.  The signed Taylor-block identity is
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal F(2^{-m}+y)=-\mathcal F(y)+\mathcal T_m(y),
 \qquad 0\le y<2^{-m}.                                   \label{eq:global-taylor-reduction}
\end{equation}

Fix $x\ge0$.  At scale $m$, put
\begin{equation}
 p_m=\lfloor2^mx\rfloor,\qquad
 y_m=x-\frac{p_m}{2^m},\qquad 0\le y_m<2^{-m}.            \label{eq:global-prefix-tail}
\end{equation}
The previous prefix requires a genuine half-scale value at $m=0$:
\begin{equation}
 p^*_{m-1}=\begin{cases}
  \lfloor x/2\rfloor,&m=0,\\
  p_{m-1},&m\ge1.
 \end{cases}                                             \label{eq:global-previous-prefix}
\end{equation}
Uniformly, $p^*_{m-1}=\lfloor p_m/2\rfloor$.  Thus
$p_m=2p^*_{m-1}+\delta_m$ with $\delta_m\in\{0,1\}$.  Define
\begin{equation}
 \alpha_m(x)=(-1)^{\wt(p_m)}(2p^*_{m-1}-p_m),\qquad
 R_m(x)=(-1)^{\wt(p_m)}\mathcal F(y_m).                  \label{eq:global-reduction-coefficient}
\end{equation}
The coefficient $\alpha_m$ is zero or a sign, and it is nonzero precisely when the newly
exposed binary digit is $1$.

\begin{lemma}[One-step residual identity]
For $m\ge1$,
\begin{equation}
 \alpha_m(x)\mathcal T_m(y_m)=R_{m-1}(x)-R_m(x).          \label{eq:global-one-step-residual}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If $p_m=2p_{m-1}$, then $y_m=y_{m-1}$, the Thue--Morse signs agree, and
$\alpha_m=0$, so both sides vanish.  If $p_m=2p_{m-1}+1$, then
\begin{equation*}
 y_{m-1}=2^{-m}+y_m,\qquad
 (-1)^{\wt(p_m)}=-(-1)^{\wt(p_{m-1})},\qquad
 \alpha_m=(-1)^{\wt(p_{m-1})}.
\end{equation*}
Apply \eqref{eq:global-taylor-reduction} to $y_{m-1}$ and multiply by the old prefix sign;
the two copies of $\mathcal F(y_m)$ combine exactly into $R_{m-1}-R_m$.
\end{proof}

The scale-zero identity is separate and retains the parity of the integer block:
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal F(x)=\alpha_0(x)\mathcal T_0(y_0)+R_0(x).       \label{eq:global-scale-zero-step}
\end{equation}
Indeed, write $p_0=2p^*_{-1}+\delta_0$ and use the two-unit translation law
\eqref{eq:single-block}.  When $\delta_0=0$, the polynomial term vanishes.  When
$\delta_0=1$, the identity $\mathcal F(1+y_0)=-\mathcal F(y_0)+1$ supplies it.

\begin{theorem}[Exact finite binary telescope]
For $x\ge0$ and $N\in\N$,
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{
 \mathcal F(x)=
 \sum_{m=0}^{N}\alpha_m(x)\mathcal T_m(y_m(x))+R_N(x).}
                                                               \label{eq:global-finite-binary-telescope}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Start with \eqref{eq:global-scale-zero-step}.  Summing
\eqref{eq:global-one-step-residual} for $m=1,\ldots,N$ telescopes all intermediate residuals.
The case $N=0$ is exactly the scale-zero identity.
\end{proof}

For $N\ge1$, $0\le y_N<2^{-N}\le1/2$.  On this interval
$0\le\mathcal F(y)=F(y)\le2y$, so
\begin{equation}
 |R_N(x)|\le2^{1-N}.                                     \label{eq:global-residual-bound}
\end{equation}
The estimate is independent of $x$.  Therefore, if $B_N(x)$ denotes the finite sum in
\eqref{eq:global-finite-binary-telescope}, then for every $x\in\R$ and $N\ge1$,
\begin{equation}
 |B_N(x)-\mathcal F(x)|\le2^{1-N}.                       \label{eq:global-binary-uniform-bound}
\end{equation}
This is \lean{norm_globalBinaryReductionSum_sub_extendedFabius_le}.  Lean's natural-floor
convention extends every $B_N$ by zero on $x\le0$, where $\mathcal F$ also vanishes, and
\lean{globalBinaryReductionSum_tendstoUniformly_extendedFabius} proves that the finite
telescopes converge uniformly on the whole line.
The one-step identity then gives, for $m\ge2$,
\begin{equation}
 |\alpha_m(x)\mathcal T_m(y_m)|
 \le |R_{m-1}|+|R_m|\le4\,2^{-(m-1)}.                   \label{eq:global-summand-majorant}
\end{equation}
Thus the limiting series is absolutely, not merely conditionally, convergent.

\begin{theorem}[Absolutely convergent global binary series]
For every $x\ge0$,
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{
 \mathcal F(x)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}
 \alpha_m(x)\mathcal T_m(y_m(x)).}                       \label{eq:global-binary-reduction-series}
\end{equation}
On $0\le x\le1$, the sum is equivalently $F(x)$.
\end{theorem}

Lean's natural-floor definitions make the telescope genuinely all-real: when \(x\le0\),
every binary prefix, summand, residual, and \(\mathcal F(x)\) vanishes termwise.  Thus the
finite identity and the \(m=0\) infinite series hold for every \(x\in\R\), as
\lean{extendedFabius_eq_globalBinaryReductionSum_add_remainder_all} and
\lean{extendedFabius_eq_tsum_globalBinaryReductionSummand_all} in
\path{FabiusBinaryReductionSeries.lean}.  The fully expanded parity-power version has the
same all-real scope by \lean{globalFabius_eq_tsum_fabiusParityPower_literal_all} in
\path{FabiusParityPowerSeries.lean}.  The displayed floor formulas above remain ordinary
nonnegative-floor formulas on their stated domain.

The index $m=0$ is indispensable globally.  For $0\le x<1$ it vanishes, so a series
starting at $m=1$ happens to be valid there.  At $x=1$, however, the zeroth term is $1$ and
all later coefficients vanish.  More generally it is the zeroth term that distinguishes
even and odd integer blocks of the signed extension.

\subsection{The corrected parity-power presentation}
\label{subsec:corrected-parity-power}

The same summands have a more literal presentation in terms of prefix parity and inverse
dyadic values.  Define, using integer exponents,
\begin{equation}
 \kappa_j=\frac{((j:\Z)+2)((j:\Z)-1)}2
 =\binom{j+1}{2}-1.                                      \label{eq:parity-power-integer-exponent}
\end{equation}
The integer interpretation matters: $\kappa_0=-1$, hence $2^{\kappa_0}=1/2$; natural
subtraction would silently give the wrong term.  Put
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal I_m(y)=\sum_{j=0}^{m}
 2^{\kappa_j}\mathcal F\bigl(2^{(j:\Z)-(m:\Z)}\bigr)
 \frac{y^j}{j!}.                                         \label{eq:parity-power-inner}
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}
For every $m$ and $y$,
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal I_m(y)=\frac12\mathcal T_m(y).                 \label{eq:parity-power-half-reduction}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For $j\le m$, set $r=m-j$.  The inverse-dyadic identity
\eqref{eq:F-inverse-power} gives
\begin{equation*}
 \mathcal F(2^{j-m})=
 \frac{d_{m-j}}{(m-j)!\,2^{\binom{m-j}{2}}}.
\end{equation*}
Together with $\kappa_j=\binom{j+1}{2}-1$, this identifies each summand with one half of
the corresponding summand in \eqref{eq:global-reduction-polynomial}.
\end{proof}

Let $\tau(a)=\wt(a)\bmod2$.  Since
\begin{equation*}
 \frac{(-1)^a-1}{2}=2\left\lfloor\frac a2\right\rfloor-a,
\end{equation*}
the literal summand
\begin{align}
 \Lambda_m(x)
 &:=
 \bigl((-1)^{\lfloor2^mx\rfloor}-1\bigr)
 (-1)^{\tau(\lfloor2^mx\rfloor)}                         \notag\\[-2pt]
 &\quad\times
 \sum_{j=0}^{m}2^{((j+2)(j-1))/2}
 \mathcal F(2^{j-m})
 \frac{\left(x-\lfloor2^mx\rfloor/2^m\right)^j}{j!}     \label{eq:parity-power-literal-summand}
\end{align}
is exactly $\alpha_m(x)\mathcal T_m(y_m)$.  Both exponents
$((j+2)(j-1))/2$ and $j-m$ in this display are integer exponents, not truncated natural
subtractions.

\begin{warning}
\label{warn:blu-natural-subtraction}
In \eqref{eq:qs-sigma-def} and \eqref{eq:qbinomial-dyadic}, the summation indices are cast
into the ambient characteristic-zero field before the subtraction in $r-m2^k+Q$ is
performed.  This is the typing used by the formal theorem; the displayed subtraction is
therefore field subtraction, never truncated subtraction on natural numbers.
\end{warning}

\begin{theorem}[Corrected parity-power series]
For every $x\ge0$,
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\mathcal F(x)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\Lambda_m(x),}  \label{eq:corrected-parity-power-series}
\end{equation}
and the series converges absolutely.  On the half-open unit interval $0\le x<1$, the
zeroth summand vanishes and one may start at $m=1$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The elementary parity identity and \eqref{eq:parity-power-half-reduction} prove
$\Lambda_m=\alpha_m\mathcal T_m(y_m)$ term by term.  The claim follows from
\eqref{eq:global-binary-reduction-series}.  The half-open corollary follows from
$\lfloor x\rfloor=0$; the closed endpoint $x=1$ requires the restored zeroth term.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The global complex $q$-binomial series}
\label{subsec:global-q-series}

The finite constant-polynomial identity from \cref{subsec:q-binomial-dyadic} can be inserted
at every scale of the binary telescope.  For $Q\in\C$, define
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal N_n^{(Q)}=
 \sum_{k=0}^{n}
 \frac{\qbinom nk{1/2}}{4^{\binom k2}(n+k)!}
 \sum_{r=0}^{2^k-1}(-1)^{\wt(r)}
 (r-2^k+Q)^{n+k}.                                       \label{eq:q-global-numerator}
\end{equation}
Translation invariance and the inverse-dyadic normalization give
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\mathcal N_n^{(Q)}}
 {2^{\binom n2}(1/2;1/2)_n}
 =\frac{2^n d_n}{n!}.                                    \label{eq:q-taylor-normalization}
\end{equation}
The normalization differs from the $2^{n^2}$ in
\eqref{eq:qbinomial-dyadic}: here it produces a Taylor coefficient rather than a function
value.

For $m\in\N$, put
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal Q_m^{(Q)}(y)=
 2^{-\binom{m+1}{2}}
 \sum_{n=0}^{m}
 \frac{(2^{m+1}y)^{m-n}}{(m-n)!}
 \frac{\mathcal N_n^{(Q)}}
 {2^{\binom n2}(1/2;1/2)_n}.                             \label{eq:q-reduction-polynomial}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[$q$-binomial Taylor identity]
For every $m$, every $y\in\R$, and every $Q\in\C$,
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal Q_m^{(Q)}(y)=\mathcal T_m(y),                  \label{eq:q-reduction-equals-taylor}
\end{equation}
where the real polynomial on the right is embedded in $\C$.  In particular, each finite
summand is pointwise independent of $Q$ before any infinite sum is taken.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Insert \eqref{eq:q-taylor-normalization} into
\eqref{eq:q-reduction-polynomial} and set $j=m-n$.  Collecting the powers of $2$ gives
\begin{equation*}
 2^{-\binom{m+1}{2}}\,2^{(m+1)j}\,2^{m-j}
 =2^{\binom{j+1}{2}-\binom{m-j}{2}}.
\end{equation*}
The remaining factorials and moments are exactly those in
\eqref{eq:global-reduction-polynomial}.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Global complex $q$-binomial series]
For fixed $Q\in\C$ and every $x\ge0$,
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{
 \mathcal F(x)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}
 \alpha_m(x)\mathcal Q_m^{(Q)}(y_m(x)),}                 \label{eq:global-q-binomial-series}
\end{equation}
with the left side embedded in $\C$.  The series converges absolutely.
\end{theorem}

The displayed floor presentation is stated on the nonnegative half-line.  The literal Lean
summand has a stronger all-real scope: for every \(x\in\R\), its series is absolutely
summable and sums to the signed global extension, while for \(x\le0\) every term and the
target vanish.  These are
\lean{summable_norm_qBinomialFabiusGlobalSummand_all},
\lean{hasSum_qBinomialFabiusGlobalSummand_all},
\lean{extendedFabius_eq_tsum_qBinomialFabiusGlobalSummand_all},
\lean{globalFabius_eq_tsum_qBinomialFabiusGlobalSummand_all}, and
\lean{qBinomialFabiusGlobalSummand_eq_zero_of_nonpos} in
\path{FabiusGlobalQBinomialSeries.lean}.  The older nonnegative signatures are retained as
compatibility wrappers.

This is not a new infinite basic-hypergeometric identity.  The outer sum is the analytic
binary telescope, while at each scale a finite $q$-binomial identity presents the same
Taylor polynomial.  Expanding one summand makes the architecture explicit:
\begin{align}
 &\alpha_m(x)\,2^{-\binom{m+1}{2}}
 \sum_{n=0}^{m}
 \frac{(2^{m+1}x-2p_m(x))^{m-n}}{(m-n)!}
 \frac1{2^{\binom n2}(1/2;1/2)_n}                       \notag\\
 &\qquad\times
 \sum_{k=0}^{n}
 \frac{\qbinom nk{1/2}}{4^{\binom k2}(n+k)!}
 \sum_{r=0}^{2^k-1}(-1)^{\wt(r)}
 (r-2^k+Q)^{n+k}.                                       \label{eq:global-q-literal-summand}
\end{align}
Convergence comes entirely from the binary-tail majorant
\eqref{eq:global-summand-majorant}; the special-function manipulations are finite and
algebraic.

\section{A complex $q$-binomial discrete limit}
\label{sec:q-discrete-limit}

There is a second way in which the same Gaussian row produces $\mathcal F$.  It is a genuine
limit of finite shifted splines rather than a sequence of partial sums of
\eqref{eq:global-q-binomial-series}.  Reuse the safe midpoint cutoff $N_p(x)$ from
\eqref{eq:centered-cutoff}.  For $x\ge0$ it is equivalent to the inclusive source range
\begin{equation}
 0\le r\le\left\lfloor2^px-\frac12\right\rfloor,          \label{eq:discrete-cutoff-inclusive}
\end{equation}
including the empty case where the upper endpoint is $-1$.

For fixed $Q\in\C$ and $x\in\R$, define
\begin{align}
 \mathcal D_n^{(Q)}(x)
 &=
 \frac1{2^{n^2}(1/2;1/2)_n}
 \sum_{k=0}^{n}
 \frac{\qbinom nk{1/2}}{4^{\binom k2}(n+k)!}             \notag\\
 &\quad\times
 \sum_{r=0}^{N_{n+k}(x)-1}(-1)^{\wt(r)}
 \bigl(r-2^{n+k}x+Q\bigr)^{n+k}.                         \label{eq:discrete-q-approximant}
\end{align}

\begin{theorem}[Complex discrete-limit formula]
For every fixed $Q\in\C$ and every $x\in\R$,
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\mathcal D_n^{(Q)}(x)\longrightarrow\mathcal F(x)}
                                                               \label{eq:discrete-q-limit}
\end{equation}
in $\C$.  If $0\le x\le1$, the limit is equivalently $F(x)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
If $x\le0$, every safe cutoff $N_p(x)$ is zero.  Hence every shifted spline and every
approximant \eqref{eq:discrete-q-approximant} is zero, as is $\mathcal F(x)$.  It remains
to treat $x\ge0$.

For a degree $p$ and complex shift $Q$, introduce the finite shifted spline
\begin{equation}
 S_p^{(Q)}(x)=
 \frac{(-1)^p}{2^{\binom p2}p!}
 \sum_{r=0}^{N_p(x)-1}(-1)^{\wt(r)}
 (r-2^px+Q)^p.                                           \label{eq:complex-shift-spline}
\end{equation}
At $Q=1/2$ this is exactly the centered spline
\eqref{eq:centered-spline}, so by \eqref{eq:centered-spline-global-limit},
\begin{equation*}
 S_p^{(1/2)}(x)\longrightarrow\mathcal F(x).
\end{equation*}
\begin{lemma}[Fixed-cutoff Taylor expansion of a complex shift]
\label{lem:res-spline-taylor}
Read \eqref{eq:centered-spline} at $p=0$ as
\begin{equation}
 S_0(x)=\sum_{r=0}^{N_0(x)-1}\varepsilon_r,               \label{eq:res-degree-zero-spline}
\end{equation}
so that a centered branch $S_d$ is defined for every integer $d\ge0$.  Fix $p\ge1$ and
$x\ge0$, and abbreviate $M=N_p(x)$.  For every $d$ with $0\le d\le p$, the degree-$d$ branch
carried by that one fixed cutoff satisfies
\begin{equation}
 \frac1{2^{\binom d2}d!}\sum_{r=0}^{M-1}\varepsilon_r
 \left(2^px-\frac12-r\right)^{d}
 =S_d\!\left(2^{\,p-d}x\right),                           \label{eq:res-fixed-cutoff-branch}
\end{equation}
and in particular $\left|S_d\!\left(2^{\,p-d}x\right)\right|\le1$.  Moreover, for every
$Q\in\C$,
\begin{equation}
 S_p^{(Q)}(x)-S_p^{(1/2)}(x)
 =\sum_{d=0}^{p-1}
 \frac{2^{\binom d2}}{2^{\binom p2}(p-d)!}
 \left(\frac12-Q\right)^{p-d}
 S_d\!\left(2^{\,p-d}x\right).                            \label{eq:res-spline-taylor}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Write the shifted spline in its descending orientation.  Since
$(-1)^p(r-2^px+Q)^p=(2^px-Q-r)^p$, definition \eqref{eq:complex-shift-spline} reads
\begin{equation}
 S_p^{(Q)}(x)=\frac1{2^{\binom p2}p!}
 \sum_{r=0}^{M-1}\varepsilon_r\left(2^px-Q-r\right)^{p},
 \qquad M=N_p(x),                                         \label{eq:res-descending-spline}
\end{equation}
and at $Q=1/2$ this is \eqref{eq:centered-spline}.  The cutoff $M$ depends on $p$ and $x$
only.  It is unaffected by $Q$, and it is held fixed in what follows while the degree of the
summand varies; that is the whole point of \eqref{eq:res-fixed-cutoff-branch}.

We prove \eqref{eq:res-fixed-cutoff-branch}.  The dilation $x\mapsto2^{\,p-d}x$ compensates
exactly for the drop in degree, in the sense that
\begin{equation}
 N_d\!\left(2^{\,p-d}x\right)
 =\max\left(0,\left\lfloor2^{p}x+\frac12\right\rfloor\right)
 =N_p(x)=M                                                \label{eq:res-cutoff-invariance}
\end{equation}
by \eqref{eq:centered-cutoff}.  If $M=0$, both sides of
\eqref{eq:res-fixed-cutoff-branch} are empty sums and there is nothing to prove.  Assume
$M\ge1$.  Every index in the range satisfies
\[
 r\le M-1=\left\lfloor2^px+\frac12\right\rfloor-1\le2^px-\frac12,
\]
so every base $2^px-\frac12-r$ occurring in \eqref{eq:res-fixed-cutoff-branch} is
nonnegative and the truncation in \eqref{eq:centered-spline-positive} is inactive.  For
$d\ge1$, formula \eqref{eq:centered-spline-positive} evaluated at the point $2^{\,p-d}x$,
whose own cutoff is $M$ by \eqref{eq:res-cutoff-invariance}, is therefore literally the left
side of \eqref{eq:res-fixed-cutoff-branch}.  For $d=0$ both sides equal
$\sum_{r=0}^{M-1}\varepsilon_r$, by \eqref{eq:res-degree-zero-spline} and
\eqref{eq:res-cutoff-invariance}.

For the uniform bound, the rescaled argument \(2^{p-d}x\) is nonnegative.
The all-degree declaration \lean{abs_fabiusUniformSpline_le_one} therefore gives the
claim directly, including \(d=0\).

Now expand.  Put $w=2^px-\frac12$ for the descending argument at $Q=1/2$ and
$t=\frac12-Q$ for its increment, so that $2^px-Q=w+t$.  The binomial theorem gives
\[
 (w+t-r)^{p}=\sum_{d=0}^{p}\binom pd\,t^{\,p-d}(w-r)^{d}.
\]
Multiply by $\varepsilon_r\bigl/\bigl(2^{\binom p2}p!\bigr)$, sum over $0\le r\le M-1$, and
interchange the two finite sums.  Using \eqref{eq:res-descending-spline} on the left and
\eqref{eq:res-fixed-cutoff-branch} on each inner sum,
\begin{align}
 S_p^{(Q)}(x)
 &=\sum_{d=0}^{p}\frac{t^{\,p-d}}{2^{\binom p2}p!}\binom pd
   \sum_{r=0}^{M-1}\varepsilon_r(w-r)^{d}                 \notag\\
 &=\sum_{d=0}^{p}\frac{2^{\binom d2}}{2^{\binom p2}(p-d)!}\,t^{\,p-d}\,
   S_d\!\left(2^{\,p-d}x\right),                          \label{eq:res-full-expansion}
\end{align}
because $\binom pd\bigl/p!=1\bigl/\bigl(d!\,(p-d)!\bigr)$ and the inner sum is
$2^{\binom d2}d!\,S_d(2^{\,p-d}x)$.  The term $d=p$ is $S_p(x)=S_p^{(1/2)}(x)$; subtracting
it leaves \eqref{eq:res-spline-taylor}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Which convention makes the sign disappear]
\label{rem:res-sign-convention}
The expansion \eqref{eq:res-spline-taylor} is free of alternating signs only because the
increment is measured in the \emph{descending} variable $2^px-Q$ of
\eqref{eq:res-descending-spline}, that is, because the coefficient carries
$\left(\tfrac12-Q\right)^{p-d}$ and not $\left(Q-\tfrac12\right)^{p-d}$.  If one instead
measures the shift by the ascending increment $q=Q-\tfrac12$, which is the natural reading
when the branch is written as a function of the ascending argument $r-2^px+Q$ of
\eqref{eq:complex-shift-spline}, then the identity acquires exactly the factor
$(-1)^{p+d}$:
\begin{equation}
 S_p^{(Q)}(x)-S_p^{(1/2)}(x)
 =\sum_{d=0}^{p-1}(-1)^{p+d}
 \frac{2^{\binom d2}}{2^{\binom p2}(p-d)!}\,
 q^{\,p-d}\,S_d\!\left(2^{\,p-d}x\right),
 \qquad q=Q-\frac12.                                      \label{eq:res-ascending-form}
\end{equation}
The two displays are the same statement, since $(-1)^{p+d}=(-1)^{p-d}$ and therefore
$(-1)^{p+d}q^{\,p-d}=\left(\tfrac12-Q\right)^{p-d}$.  It is worth being explicit about where
the sign does \emph{not} live: it is not a matter of renormalizing the lower branches.  The
alternating prefactor $(-1)^d$ of \eqref{eq:centered-spline} is cancelled exactly by the
$(-1)^d$ produced when the ascending argument is reversed, so the branch values
$S_d(2^{\,p-d}x)$ appearing in \eqref{eq:res-spline-taylor} and in
\eqref{eq:res-ascending-form} are the same numbers.  The sign belongs to the increment
alone, and the convention that removes it is the descending one.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Formal bound]
Only the exponential bound \eqref{eq:complex-shift-spline-bound} is asserted here; it is
\lean{norm_fabiusComplexShiftSpline_sub_center_le_half_pow_mul_exp} in
\path{FabiusComplexShiftSpline.lean}.  A proposed sharper constant is recorded in the
research-frontier gap register pending a corrected named theorem.
\end{remark}

Expanding a fixed cutoff branch about $Q=1/2$ gives an exact finite Taylor combination of
lower-degree centered branches.  The normalized Thue--Morse bounds then give, for $p\ge1$
and $x\ge0$,
\begin{equation}
 \left|S_p^{(Q)}(x)-S_p^{(1/2)}(x)\right|
 \le2^{-(p-1)}\exp\!\left(|Q-\tfrac12|\right).            \label{eq:complex-shift-spline-bound}
\end{equation}
The nonpositive half-line is handled separately by exact vanishing, and
\lean{fabiusComplexShiftSpline_tendsto_globalFabius_all} proves that every fixed complex
shift has the same pointwise spline limit for every real $x$.

Now reindex the outer sum in \eqref{eq:discrete-q-approximant} by $j=n-k$.  Direct
collection of signs, powers, and factorials gives the exact finite identity
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal D_n^{(Q)}(x)=
 \sum_{j=0}^{n}\omega_{n,j}S_{2n-j}^{(Q)}(x),             \label{eq:discrete-toeplitz-reindex}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
 \omega_{n,j}=
 \frac{(-1)^j\qbinom nj{1/2}\,2^{-\binom{j+1}{2}}}
 {(1/2;1/2)_n}.                                          \label{eq:discrete-toeplitz-weight}
\end{equation}
The finite $q$-binomial theorem gives the row normalization
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{j=0}^{n}\omega_{n,j}=1.                            \label{eq:discrete-row-sum}
\end{equation}
The same theorem identifies the entire row rather than only its value at one.

\begin{proposition}[Toeplitz row polynomial and module-valued $q$-Richardson cancellation]
\label{prop:discrete-row-polynomial}
For $z\in\Q$, put $W_n(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\omega_{n,j}z^j$.  Then
\begin{equation}
 W_n(z)=\frac{(z/2;1/2)_n}{(1/2;1/2)_n}.                 \label{eq:discrete-row-polynomial}
\end{equation}
Consequently its rational zeros are exactly
\begin{equation}
 W_n(z)=0
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 z\in\{2,2^2,\ldots,2^n\}.                              \label{eq:discrete-row-roots}
\end{equation}
Moreover, let $M$ be any additive commutative monoid carrying a $\Q$-module
structure.  If an $M$-valued sequence has the finite geometric-error model
$u_p=L+\sum_{r=1}^{n}2^{-rp}\mathbin{\cdot}a_r$, where
$\cdot$ denotes scalar multiplication, then
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{j=0}^{n}\omega_{n,j}\mathbin{\cdot}u_{2n-j}=L.
                                                               \label{eq:discrete-row-richardson}
\end{equation}
Thus the cancellation needs no multiplication, topology, order, or norm on
the target.  Taking $M=\Q$ recovers the rational formula
$u_p=L+\sum_{r=1}^{n}a_r2^{-rp}$.  This module-valued extension concerns
cancellation only; the root classification above remains a statement for
rational $z$.  All statements include $n=0$: the row polynomial is then $1$,
the root set is empty, and the error sum is empty.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Formal proof]
The full generating identity and its root classification are
\lean{sum_range_discreteLimitWeight_mul_pow} and
\lean{sum_range_discreteLimitWeight_mul_pow_eq_zero_iff}.  The reusable
single-mode identity is
\lean{sum_range_discreteLimitWeight_mul_geometricMode_smul_eq_zero},
and the module-valued cancellation theorem is
\lean{discreteLimitWeight_qRichardson_exact_module}; its $M=\Q$
specialization is \lean{discreteLimitWeight_qRichardson_exact}.  All are in
\path{FabiusDiscreteLimitToeplitz.lean}.  The former proof of
\eqref{eq:discrete-row-sum} is now its specialization at $z=1$.
\end{proof}

Applying it with the opposite sign also gives the uniform variation bound
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{j=0}^{n}|\omega_{n,j}|
 =\frac{(-1/2;1/2)_n}{(1/2;1/2)_n}\le16.                 \label{eq:discrete-row-variation}
\end{equation}
Finally, every sampled degree satisfies $2n-j\ge n$.  A finite-row Toeplitz lemma therefore
carries the convergence of $S_p^{(Q)}(x)$ to the weighted rows
\eqref{eq:discrete-toeplitz-reindex}.  Explicitly, given $\epsilon>0$, choose $P$ so that
$|S_p^{(Q)}(x)-\mathcal F(x)|<\epsilon/16$ for $p\ge P$.  If $n\ge P$, then every
$2n-j\ge n\ge P$, and the row-sum identity together with
\eqref{eq:discrete-row-variation} gives
\begin{equation*}
 \left|\sum_{j=0}^{n}\omega_{n,j}S_{2n-j}^{(Q)}(x)-\mathcal F(x)\right|
 \le\sum_{j=0}^{n}|\omega_{n,j}|\,
 |S_{2n-j}^{(Q)}(x)-\mathcal F(x)|<\epsilon.
\end{equation*}
This proves \eqref{eq:discrete-q-limit}.
\end{proof}

Two distinctions prevent common misreadings of this theorem.  First, unlike the complete
finite dyadic numerator and each summand of the global $q$-series, a finite value
$\mathcal D_n^{(Q)}(x)$ can depend on $Q$; it is the limit that is independent of a fixed
shift.  Second, the Toeplitz row \eqref{eq:discrete-toeplitz-reindex} is not a finite binary
telescope or a partial sum of \eqref{eq:global-q-binomial-series}.  These finite expressions
share a limit but are not equal term by term.

The two properties of the reindexed row \eqref{eq:discrete-toeplitz-weight} used above are
both evaluations of the finite $q$-binomial theorem \eqref{eq:half-q-binomial-theorem}, at
$z=1/2$ and at $z=-1/2$ respectively.  Both use the exponent identity
$\binom j2+j=\binom{j+1}2$.

For the row sum, evaluate \eqref{eq:half-q-binomial-theorem} at $z=1/2$.  The left side is
$(1/2;1/2)_n$ by definition, and the right side is
\begin{equation}
 (1/2;1/2)_n
 =\sum_{j=0}^{n}(-1)^j2^{-\binom j2}\qbinom nj{1/2}\left(\frac12\right)^{j}
 =\sum_{j=0}^{n}(-1)^j\qbinom nj{1/2}\,2^{-\binom{j+1}2}.  \label{eq:qs-row-sum-derivation}
\end{equation}
Dividing by $(1/2;1/2)_n$ gives exactly $\sum_{j=0}^{n}\omega_{n,j}=1$, which is
\eqref{eq:discrete-row-sum}.

For the total variation, evaluate \eqref{eq:half-q-binomial-theorem} at $z=-1/2$ instead.
Each factor $(-1)^j(-1/2)^j=2^{-j}$ is now positive, so the alternating signs disappear:
\begin{equation}
 (-1/2;1/2)_n
 =\sum_{j=0}^{n}(-1)^j2^{-\binom j2}\qbinom nj{1/2}\left(-\frac12\right)^{j}
 =\sum_{j=0}^{n}\qbinom nj{1/2}\,2^{-\binom{j+1}2}.        \label{eq:qs-row-variation-derivation}
\end{equation}
Since $\qbinom nj{1/2}>0$, the summands of \eqref{eq:qs-row-variation-derivation} are the
absolute values of those of \eqref{eq:qs-row-sum-derivation}.  Dividing by $(1/2;1/2)_n$
gives the exact evaluation
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{j=0}^{n}|\omega_{n,j}|=\frac{(-1/2;1/2)_n}{(1/2;1/2)_n}, \label{eq:qs-row-variation-exact}
\end{equation}
which is the first equality in \eqref{eq:discrete-row-variation}.  The formally proved
finite-product estimate supplies the bound by $16$ used in the Toeplitz argument above.

\section{Iterated prefix sums of the signed Thue--Morse sequence}
\label{tm:sec-prefix}

The signed Thue--Morse sequence $\varepsilon_n=(-1)^{\wt(n)}$ has already appeared twice in
this article: once as the coefficient sequence of the locally finite global expansion
\eqref{eq:global-extension}, and once as the sign pattern that makes the finite dyadic sums
of \cref{sec:dyadic} collapse.  There is a third, entirely discrete, way in which
the same signs produce the Fabius function.  Sum the sequence, sum the result, and iterate.
After $k$ summations the sequence has been convolved with an explicit binomial kernel, and
after a dyadic normalization the resulting integer grid reproduces, in the
limit, the Rvachev bump and hence $F$.

This section develops that construction: the convolution law and its explicit binomial
kernel, the signed dyadic reflection preceding each terminal zero run, the zero runs
themselves and their exact boundary values, the two generating-series identities, their
analytic realization and natural-boundary theorem, the discrete functional equation
satisfied by the normalized grid, and the
precise sense in which the normalized grid converges.  The convergence statement requires
one shift of the prefix order and the centered reading of the grid cells; the literal
normalization does not converge, and that failure is recorded separately in
\cref{tm:sub-literal-endpoint}.  Two further pieces of foundational material are attached at
the end: the lower branch of Lambert's $W$-function, which the asymptotic sections of this
article use but never construct, and an exact binomial--logarithm formula for the
Thue--Morse bit.

The two binary recurrences used throughout this section are
\begin{align}
 \varepsilon_{2m}&=\varepsilon_m,&
 \varepsilon_{2m+1}&=-\varepsilon_m,                       \label{eq:tm-even-odd}\\
 \wt(2m)&=\wt(m),&
 \wt(2m+1)&=\wt(m)+1.                                     \label{eq:weight-even-odd}
\end{align}
They are exactly
\lean{thueMorseSign_two_mul},
\lean{thueMorseSign_two_mul_add_one},
\lean{binaryWeight_two_mul}, and
\lean{binaryWeight_two_mul_add_one} in \path{DyadicClosedForm.lean}.

\subsection{Iterated prefix sums and the binomial convolution law}
\label{tm:sub-convolution}

\begin{definition}[Iterated prefix sums]
\label{thm:tm-prefix-def}
Set $\sigma_0(n)=\varepsilon_n$ and, for $k\ge0$,
\begin{equation}
 \sigma_{k+1}(n)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\sigma_k(j).                \label{eq:tm-prefix-def}
\end{equation}
All sums are \emph{inclusive}: the index $n$ itself is summed.
\end{definition}

Every $\sigma_k(n)$ is an integer, and $\sigma_k(0)=1$ for all $k$.  The inclusive
convention has one consequence that must be kept in view throughout, because it is the
source of an index shift later:
\begin{equation}
 \sigma_{k+1}(n+1)-\sigma_{k+1}(n)=\sigma_k(n+1).          \label{eq:tm-forward-diff}
\end{equation}
The increment of a prefix sum is the lower-order value at the \emph{new} index, not at the
old one.

The first prefix sum nevertheless vanishes at every odd index:
\[
 \sigma_1(2m+1)=0\qquad(m\in\N).
\]
This is exactly \lean{iteratedPrefix_one_two_mul_add_one} in
\path{ThueMorsePrefix.lean}.

The general structure is a discrete convolution.  Write
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal B_k(i)=\binom{i+k-1}{k-1}\qquad(k\ge1,\ i\ge0)   \label{eq:tm-kernel}
\end{equation}
which is \lean{iteratedPrefixKernel}.  Because the argument \(i=n-m\) is
nonnegative in the sums below, no separate indicator is needed.

\begin{theorem}[Binomial convolution law]
\label{thm:tm-convolution}
For every $k\ge1$ and every $n\ge0$,
\begin{equation}
 \sigma_k(n)=\sum_{m=0}^{n}\mathcal B_k(n-m)\,\varepsilon_m
 =\sum_{m=0}^{n}\binom{n-m+k-1}{k-1}\varepsilon_m.         \label{eq:tm-kernel-sum}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, in a form valid for every $k\ge0$,
\begin{equation}
 \sigma_{k+1}(n)=\sum_{m=0}^{n}\binom{n-m+k}{k}\varepsilon_m. \label{eq:tm-kernel-sum-shifted}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Induct on $k$ in the form \eqref{eq:tm-kernel-sum-shifted}.  For $k=0$ the binomial
coefficient is $1$ and the assertion is the definition of $\sigma_1$.  Assume
\eqref{eq:tm-kernel-sum-shifted} for $k$.  Then
\begin{align*}
 \sigma_{k+2}(n)
 &=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\sigma_{k+1}(j)
  =\sum_{j=0}^{n}\sum_{m=0}^{j}\binom{j-m+k}{k}\varepsilon_m\\
 &=\sum_{m=0}^{n}\varepsilon_m\sum_{i=0}^{n-m}\binom{i+k}{k}
  =\sum_{m=0}^{n}\binom{n-m+k+1}{k+1}\varepsilon_m,
\end{align*}
where the interchange is the triangular reindexing $i=j-m$ and the inner evaluation is the
hockey-stick identity $\sum_{i=0}^{N}\binom{i+k}{k}=\binom{N+k+1}{k+1}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The kernel in \eqref{eq:tm-kernel-sum} is exactly the coefficient sequence of
$(1-z)^{-k}$.  \Cref{thm:tm-convolution} is therefore the coefficient form of the series
identity proved in \cref{thm:tm-generating} below, and either statement may be taken as the
definition of the other.  Both are recorded here because the coefficient form is what one
actually evaluates, while the series form is what one manipulates.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Prouhet annihilation, signed dyadic reciprocity, and exact zero runs}
\label{tm:sub-zero-runs}

\Cref{eq:thue-affine-cancel} already records that a complete dyadic block of Thue--Morse
signs annihilates every polynomial of degree below the block exponent.  For the prefix sums
we also need the sharp boundary case, which identifies the first power that survives.

\begin{lemma}[Sharp Prouhet extraction]
\label{thm:tm-prouhet-extract}
Write $S_{r,d}=\sum_{h<2^r}\varepsilon_h h^d$.  Then $S_{r,d}=0$ for $0\le d<r$, and
\begin{equation}
 S_{r,r}=(-1)^r2^{\binom r2}r!.                            \label{eq:tm-power-sum-sharp}
\end{equation}
Consequently, for all rational numbers $a_0,\ldots,a_r$,
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{h<2^r}\varepsilon_h\sum_{d=0}^{r}a_dh^d
 =a_r\,(-1)^r2^{\binom r2}r!,                              \label{eq:tm-polynomial-extract}
\end{equation}
so summation against a complete dyadic block of Thue--Morse signs annihilates every
polynomial of degree below $r$ and, on polynomials of degree at most $r$, extracts the
leading coefficient up to the explicit nonzero factor $(-1)^r2^{\binom r2}r!$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The vanishing statement is \eqref{eq:thue-affine-cancel} with $X=0$, $Y=1$.  For the
boundary case, split the block by parity rather than by halves.  Since
$\varepsilon_{2j}=\varepsilon_j$ and $\varepsilon_{2j+1}=-\varepsilon_j$ by
\eqref{eq:tm-even-odd},
\begin{equation*}
 S_{r+1,d}
 =\sum_{j<2^r}\varepsilon_j\bigl[(2j)^d-(2j+1)^d\bigr]
 =-\sum_{c=0}^{d-1}\binom dc2^{c}S_{r,c},
\end{equation*}
because $(2j+1)^d-(2j)^d=\sum_{c<d}\binom dc(2j)^c$.  Take $d=r+1$.  All terms with $c<r$
vanish by the first assertion, leaving only $c=r$:
\begin{equation*}
 S_{r+1,r+1}=-\binom{r+1}{r}2^rS_{r,r}=-(r+1)2^rS_{r,r}.
\end{equation*}
Since $S_{0,0}=\varepsilon_0=1$, induction gives
$S_{r,r}=(-1)^rr!\,2^{0+1+\cdots+(r-1)}=(-1)^rr!\,2^{\binom r2}$, which is
\eqref{eq:tm-power-sum-sharp}.  Formula \eqref{eq:tm-polynomial-extract} follows by
exchanging the two sums: every index $d<r$ contributes $a_dS_{r,d}=0$, and the index $d=r$
contributes $a_rS_{r,r}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Affine sharp case]
\label{thm:tm-affine-sharp}
For all $X,Y\in\Q$,
\begin{equation*}
 \sum_{h<2^r}\varepsilon_h(X+Yh)^r=(-1)^rY^r2^{\binom r2}r!.
\end{equation*}
In particular the sum does not depend on the translation $X$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Expand $(X+Yh)^r$ by the binomial theorem and apply \eqref{eq:tm-polynomial-extract}: the
coefficient of $h^r$ is $Y^r$.
\end{proof}

The public Boolean-cube theorem uses the genuine degree
\(\deg p\in\N\cup\{-\infty\}\), rather than the natural degree that assigns the zero
polynomial degree zero.  Over any commutative ring, for a finite set \(S\), weights
\(w_j\), and a polynomial satisfying \(\deg p<|S|\), it states
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{T\subseteq S}(-1)^{|T|}
 p\!\left(x+\sum_{j\in T}w_j\right)=0.                    \label{eq:tm-powerset-degree-cancel}
\end{equation}
This is \lean{sum_powerset_neg_one_pow_eval_of_degree_lt} in
\path{ThueMorseSparseProuhet.lean}.  The degree convention matters at the boundary
\(S=\varnothing\): the hypothesis then forces \(p=0\), so the theorem correctly includes
the empty-support case instead of declaring it vacuous.  With \(S\) the bit support of
\(n\) and \(w_j=2^jh\), the corresponding sparse submask parametrization is
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{T\subseteq\operatorname{bits}(n)}(-1)^{|T|}
 p\!\left(x+h\sum_{j\in T}2^j\right)=0
 \quad\text{when }\deg p<\wt(n),                           \label{eq:tm-submask-degree-cancel}
\end{equation}
formalized as \lean{sum_submask_neg_one_pow_eval_of_degree_lt}.  It is valid also at
\(n=0\), where again the only polynomial satisfying the strict degree bound is zero.

At the level of the centered formal exponential series, the corresponding zero has exact
order \(r\); this is \lean{order_thueMorseCenteredPowerSeries}.  For the binomial kernel
\eqref{eq:tm-kernel}, the directly applicable formally proved form is instead the
coefficient-extraction theorem \lean{thueMorse_polynomial_sum_eq_coeff}, displayed in
\eqref{eq:tm-polynomial-extract}.

\begin{theorem}[Signed dyadic reciprocity]
\label{thm:tm-dyadic-reciprocity}
Let $k,r,d\in\N$ satisfy $k\le r$ and $k+d<2^r$.  Then the part of the
order-$k$ prefix row immediately preceding its final $k$ positions is the
initial part of that row, read backwards and multiplied by one fixed sign:
\begin{equation}
 \sigma_k\bigl(2^r-k-1-d\bigr)
   =(-1)^{r-k}\sigma_k(d).                                \label{eq:tm-dyadic-reciprocity}
\end{equation}
Consequently reflection preserves, rather than excludes, its zero locus:
\begin{equation}
 \sigma_k\bigl(2^r-k-1-d\bigr)=0
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \sigma_k(d)=0.                                           \label{eq:tm-dyadic-zero-iff}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

The identity \eqref{eq:tm-dyadic-reciprocity} and equivalence
\eqref{eq:tm-dyadic-zero-iff} are formalized respectively as
\lean{iteratedPrefix_dyadic_reverse_window} and
\lean{iteratedPrefix_dyadic_reverse_window_eq_zero_iff}.  At $k=0$ the first
identity is precisely the dyadic bit-complement law
\lean{thueMorseSign_dyadic_complement}.  Its proof below uses the independently
established left boundary in \cref{thm:tm-zero-run} as the seed for the prefix
recurrence.

\begin{theorem}[Exact dyadic zero runs]
\label{thm:tm-zero-run}
Let $1\le k\le r$.  Then
\begin{align}
 \sigma_k(n)&=0 &&\text{for } 2^r-k\le n\le2^r-1,          \label{eq:tm-zero-run}\\
 \sigma_k\bigl(2^r-k-1\bigr)&=(-1)^{r-k},                  \label{eq:tm-run-left}\\
 \sigma_k\bigl(2^r\bigr)&=-1.                              \label{eq:tm-run-right}
\end{align}
Formula \eqref{eq:tm-run-left} also holds for $k=0$, where it reduces to
$\varepsilon_{2^r-1}=(-1)^r$, and \eqref{eq:tm-run-right} also holds for $k=0$.  Hence the
zero run terminating at $2^r-1$ has length exactly $k$: it is bounded on the left by a value
of modulus one and on the right by the value $-1$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Formal proof and Lean correspondence]
The terminal block is formalized by
\lean{iteratedPrefix_dyadic_zero_run}; its endpoint and reverse-indexed forms
are \lean{iteratedPrefix_dyadic_endpoint} and
\lean{iteratedPrefix_dyadic_zero_reverse}.  Its left and right boundaries are
\lean{iteratedPrefix_before_dyadic_run} and
\lean{iteratedPrefix_at_dyadic}.  All five declarations are in
\path{ThueMorsePrefix.lean}.  The mathematical argument behind them is as
follows.

\emph{Endpoint.}  Put $q=k-1$, so $0\le q<r$.  By \eqref{eq:tm-kernel-sum},
\begin{equation*}
 \sigma_k(2^r-1)
 =\sum_{m=0}^{2^r-1}\binom{2^r-1-m+q}{q}\varepsilon_m
 =\frac1{q!}\sum_{m<2^r}\varepsilon_m\prod_{i=1}^{q}\bigl(2^r-1-m+i\bigr).
\end{equation*}
The product is a polynomial in $m$ of degree $q<r$, so the sum vanishes by
\eqref{eq:tm-polynomial-extract}.

\emph{Rest of the run.}  We prove, by induction on $d$, that $\sigma_k(2^r-1-d)=0$ whenever
$1\le k\le r$ and $0\le d<k$.  The case $d=0$ is the endpoint.  Suppose the claim holds for
$d$ and that $d+1<k$, so $k\ge2$ and $1\le k-1\le r$.  The induction hypothesis applied at
order $k$ gives $\sigma_k(2^r-1-d)=0$, and applied at order $k-1$, which is legitimate
because $d<k-1$, gives $\sigma_{k-1}(2^r-1-d)=0$.  Now use \eqref{eq:tm-forward-diff} with
$n=2^r-1-(d+1)$, so that $n+1=2^r-1-d$:
\begin{equation*}
 0-\sigma_k(n)=\sigma_k(n+1)-\sigma_k(n)=\sigma_{k-1}(n+1)=0 .
\end{equation*}
This proves \eqref{eq:tm-zero-run}, since the indices $2^r-k,\ldots,2^r-1$ are exactly the
$2^r-1-d$ with $0\le d<k$.

\emph{Left boundary.}  Induct on $k$.  For $k=0$ the number $2^r-1$ has $r$ binary digits,
all equal to one, so $\varepsilon_{2^r-1}=(-1)^r$.  Assume \eqref{eq:tm-run-left} at order
$k$ and let $k+1\le r$.  Put $n=2^r-(k+1)-1$, so $n+1=2^r-k-1$.  By the induction hypothesis
$\sigma_k(n+1)=(-1)^{r-k}$, and by \eqref{eq:tm-zero-run} at order $k+1$ we have
$\sigma_{k+1}(n+1)=\sigma_{k+1}(2^r-(k+1))=0$.  Then \eqref{eq:tm-forward-diff} gives
$-\sigma_{k+1}(n)=(-1)^{r-k}$, that is, $\sigma_{k+1}(n)=(-1)^{r-(k+1)}$.

\emph{Right boundary.}  Induct on $k$.  For $k=0$, $\wt(2^r)=1$, so
$\varepsilon_{2^r}=-1$.  If \eqref{eq:tm-run-right} holds at order $k$ and $k+1\le r$, then
by \eqref{eq:tm-forward-diff} and \eqref{eq:tm-zero-run},
$\sigma_{k+1}(2^r)=\sigma_{k+1}(2^r-1)+\sigma_k(2^r)=0-1=-1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{thm:tm-dyadic-reciprocity}]
Induct first on the prefix order $k$.  At order zero, $\sigma_0=\varepsilon$, and
reflecting $d$ in the block $0,\ldots,2^r-1$ complements its first $r$ binary
digits.  Thus
$\varepsilon_{2^r-1-d}=(-1)^r\varepsilon_d$, which is
\eqref{eq:tm-dyadic-reciprocity} for $k=0$.

Now suppose the identity is known at order $k$ and prove it at order $k+1$ by
induction on $d$.  For $d=0$, equation \eqref{eq:tm-run-left} and
$\sigma_{k+1}(0)=1$ give exactly the required identity.  For the induction
step, put
\[
 n=2^r-(k+1)-1-(d+1),
 \qquad s=(-1)^{r-(k+1)}.
\]
The same-order induction hypothesis at offset $d$ and the lower-order
hypothesis at offset $d+1$ have the common reflected index $n+1$ and give
\[
 \sigma_{k+1}(n+1)=s\sigma_{k+1}(d),
 \qquad
 \sigma_k(n+1)=(-1)^{r-k}\sigma_k(d+1)=-s\sigma_k(d+1).
\]
Applying the forward-difference law \eqref{eq:tm-forward-diff} first at $n$
and then at $d$ therefore yields
\begin{align*}
 \sigma_{k+1}(n)
 &=\sigma_{k+1}(n+1)-\sigma_k(n+1)\\
 &=s\bigl(\sigma_{k+1}(d)+\sigma_k(d+1)\bigr)
  =s\sigma_{k+1}(d+1),
\end{align*}
which closes both inductions.  Finally, the multiplier $(-1)^{r-k}$ is
nonzero, so \eqref{eq:tm-dyadic-reciprocity} is equivalent to
\eqref{eq:tm-dyadic-zero-iff}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
At the level of statements, setting $d=0$ in
\eqref{eq:tm-dyadic-reciprocity} and using
\lean{iteratedPrefix_at_zero}, namely $\sigma_k(0)=1$, recovers the left
boundary \eqref{eq:tm-run-left}.  Reciprocity pairs any zeros that occur in
the pre-run window; it does not claim that window is zero-free.  Nor does it
imply the terminal zero run \eqref{eq:tm-zero-run} or the separate right
boundary \eqref{eq:tm-run-right}, both of which were proved independently
above.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
The right boundary value is not $+1$.  Every iterated prefix sum, at every order
$k\le r$, takes the value $-1$ at the index $2^r$.  This single sign is what makes the
literal normalization of \cref{tm:sub-grid-equation} fail at the right endpoint; see
\cref{tm:sub-literal-endpoint}.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Two generating-series identities}
\label{tm:sub-generating}

All series in this subsection are formal power series over $\Z$; no convergence hypothesis
on a complex variable is used or needed.

Let
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal K_r(z)=\sum_{n<2^r}\varepsilon_nz^n,
 \qquad
 \mathcal K(z)=\sum_{n\ge0}\varepsilon_nz^n,
 \qquad
 \mathcal S_k(z)=\sum_{n\ge0}\sigma_k(n)z^n.               \label{eq:tm-series-def}
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Block product and coefficient stabilization]
\label{thm:tm-block-product}
For every \(r\ge0\),
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal K_r(z)=\prod_{j=0}^{r-1}\bigl(1-z^{2^j}\bigr),   \label{eq:tm-block-product}
\end{equation}
and, whenever \(n<2^r\), the coefficient of \(z^n\) in this finite product is
\(\varepsilon_n\).  Hence each coefficient stabilizes once the finite product reaches a
large enough dyadic block.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Formal proof]
The finite product is \lean{thueMorseBlockPolynomial_eq_product}; the stable coefficient
identity is \lean{coeff_finite_thueMorse_product}, both in
\path{ThueMorseGenerating.lean}.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Prefix generating series]
\label{thm:tm-generating}
For every \(k\ge0\),
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal S_k(z)=\frac{\mathcal K(z)}{(1-z)^k},            \label{eq:tm-prefix-series}
\end{equation}
or equivalently
\begin{equation}
 (1-z)^k\,\mathcal S_k(z)=\mathcal K(z).                  \label{eq:tm-prefix-series-cleared}
\end{equation}
For each fixed coefficient index, either side can be computed from a sufficiently long
finite product \eqref{eq:tm-block-product}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Formal proof]
The two identities are \lean{iteratedPrefixSeries_eq} and
\lean{one_sub_X_pow_mul_iteratedPrefixSeries}; their finite coefficient bridge is
\lean{coeff_eq6_finite} in \path{ThueMorseGenerating.lean}.
\end{proof}

We use \(\prod_{j\ge0}(1-z^{2^j})\) only as shorthand for the coefficientwise stabilization
of the finite products \eqref{eq:tm-block-product}; no separate infinite-product object is
asserted.

The two identities of \cref{thm:tm-generating} connect the prefix sums to the polynomial
approximants used in \cref{tm:sub-corrected}.  These are the combinatorial step polynomials
already introduced in \eqref{eq:four-P-product}; their coefficient interpretation and exact
degree were proved there.  In the index convention of this section,
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal P_n(z)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(1+z+\cdots+z^{2^i-1}\bigr),
 \qquad
 g_n=\deg \mathcal P_n=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(2^i-1\bigr)=2^{n+1}-n-2.
                                                          \label{eq:tm-approx-poly}
\end{equation}
Since $1-z^{2^j}=(1-z)(1+z+\cdots+z^{2^j-1})$ and the factor with $j=0$ is trivial,
\eqref{eq:tm-block-product} factors as
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal K_{n+1}(z)=(1-z)^{n+1}\mathcal P_n(z).                    \label{eq:tm-block-vs-approx}
\end{equation}
Comparing \eqref{eq:tm-block-vs-approx} with \eqref{eq:tm-prefix-series-cleared} at order
$k=n+1$ and using stabilization below $2^{n+1}$ gives the exact coefficient identification
\begin{equation}
 \sigma_{n+1}(m)=\bigl[z^m\bigr]\mathcal P_n(z)\qquad(0\le m<2^{n+1}).
                                                          \label{eq:tm-prefix-eq-coeff}
\end{equation}
Note that \eqref{eq:tm-prefix-eq-coeff} must fail at $m=2^{n+1}$, because
$g_n<2^{n+1}$ makes the right side zero while the left side is $-1$ by
\eqref{eq:tm-run-right}.  That single index is the exceptional right endpoint of
\cref{thm:tm-corrected-convergence}.

\subsection{The analytic Thue--Morse series and its natural boundary}
\label{tm:sub-analytic-series}

The series \(\mathcal K\) in \eqref{eq:tm-series-def} was interpreted there as a formal
power series.  On the open unit disc the same coefficients define an analytic function,
and we retain the notation \(\mathcal K\):
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal K(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\varepsilon_nz^n,
 \qquad |z|<1.                                                \label{eq:tm-disc-series}
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Analytic product and Mahler equation]
\label{thm:tm-disc-series}
For every \(z\in\C\) with \(|z|<1\), the series \eqref{eq:tm-disc-series} converges and
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal K(z)=\prod_{j=0}^{\infty}\bigl(1-z^{2^j}\bigr).     \label{eq:tm-analytic-product}
\end{equation}
The function \(\mathcal K\) is holomorphic on \(|z|<1\) and satisfies
\begin{align}
 \mathcal K(z)&=(1-z)\mathcal K(z^2),                         \label{eq:tm-mahler}\\
 \mathcal K(z)&=\left(\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}(1-z^{2^j})\right)
                 \mathcal K(z^{2^k}) \qquad(k\in\N).        \label{eq:tm-mahler-iterate}
\end{align}
On the real radius,
\begin{equation}
 |\mathcal K(x)|\le 1-x\qquad(0\le x<1),                    \label{eq:tm-real-bound}
\end{equation}
and \(\mathcal K(0)=1\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Formal proof]
The analytic function is \lean{thueMorseDiscSeries}.  Convergence, the product identity,
and holomorphy are respectively \lean{summable_thueMorseDiscSeries},
\lean{thueMorseDiscSeries_eq_tprod}, and
\lean{thueMorseDiscSeries_differentiableOn}.  Convergence of the product itself is
\lean{multipliable_one_sub_pow_two_pow_complex} in
\path{ThueMorseInfiniteProduct.lean}.  Equations \eqref{eq:tm-mahler} and
\eqref{eq:tm-mahler-iterate} are \lean{thueMorseDiscSeries_mahler} and
\lean{thueMorseDiscSeries_iterate}; \eqref{eq:tm-real-bound} and the value at zero are
\lean{thueMorseDiscSeries_real_le} and \lean{thueMorseDiscSeries_zero}.  Except for the
product-convergence declaration just cited, all these declarations are in
\path{ThueMorseDiscSeries.lean}.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Dyadic radial zeros and the natural boundary]
\label{thm:natural-boundary}
If \(k\in\N\) and \(\zeta\in\C\) satisfy \(\zeta^{2^k}=1\), then
\begin{equation}
 \lim_{\rho\to1^-}\mathcal K(\rho\zeta)=0.                  \label{eq:tm-radial-zero}
\end{equation}
Moreover, for every \(z_0\in\C\) with \(|z_0|=1\) and every \(R>0\), there is no function
\(g:\C\to\C\) which is holomorphic on \(\{z:|z-z_0|<R\}\) and satisfies
\begin{equation}
 g(z)=\mathcal K(z)
 \quad\text{whenever}\quad |z-z_0|<R\ \text{ and }\ |z|<1. \label{eq:tm-no-continuation}
\end{equation}
Thus, in precisely this local-disc sense, every point of the unit circle is singular and
the unit circle is a natural boundary for \(\mathcal K\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Formal proof]
The radial limit is \lean{tendsto_thueMorseDiscSeries_radial_zero}.  More generally, if
\(n\in\N\) is nonzero and \(\zeta^n=1\), then \(|\zeta|=1\); this is
\lean{norm_eq_one_of_pow_eq_one}.  The exact quantified nonextension statement
\eqref{eq:tm-no-continuation}, and hence the
natural-boundary conclusion in the stated local-disc sense, is
\lean{thueMorse_natural_boundary}.  All three declarations are in
\path{ThueMorseNaturalBoundary.lean}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The functional equation of the normalized prefix grid}
\label{tm:sub-grid-equation}

The natural dyadic normalization of the prefix sums is
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal V_k(j)=\frac{\sigma_k(j)}{2^{\binom k2}},
 \qquad
 x_{k,j}=\frac j{2^k},                                     \label{eq:tm-grid-def}
\end{equation}
so that $\mathcal V_k$ is a rational-valued function on the level-$k$ dyadic grid.  The
exponent $\binom k2$ is forced: it is the exponent that appears in the sharp Prouhet value
\eqref{eq:tm-power-sum-sharp}, in the centered spline \eqref{eq:centered-spline}, and in the
derivative scaling \eqref{eq:global-derivatives}.

\begin{theorem}[Discrete functional equation]
\label{thm:tm-grid-equation}
Let $q\ge0$ and $0\le j<2^q$, and put $h=2^{-(q+1)}$ and $x=x_{q+1,j}$.  Then
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\mathcal V_{q+1}(j+1)-\mathcal V_{q+1}(j)}{h}
 =2\,\mathcal V_q(j+1),                                    \label{eq:tm-grid-equation}
\end{equation}
and the abscissa attached to the right-hand value is
\begin{equation}
 x_{q,j+1}=2(x+h)=2x_{q+1,j+1}\in[0,1].                   \label{eq:tm-grid-argument}
\end{equation}
The same identity holds verbatim for the shifted grid
$\widetilde{\mathcal V}_k(j)=\sigma_{k+1}(j)/2^{\binom k2}$ of
\cref{tm:sub-corrected}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Because $\binom{q+1}2=\binom q2+q$, the forward difference \eqref{eq:tm-forward-diff} gives
\begin{equation*}
 \mathcal V_{q+1}(j+1)-\mathcal V_{q+1}(j)
 =\frac{\sigma_{q+1}(j+1)-\sigma_{q+1}(j)}{2^{\binom q2+q}}
 =\frac{\sigma_q(j+1)}{2^{\binom q2}}\cdot\frac1{2^q}
 =\frac{\mathcal V_q(j+1)}{2^q}.
\end{equation*}
Dividing by $h=2^{-(q+1)}$ multiplies by $2^{q+1}$ and gives \eqref{eq:tm-grid-equation}.
For \eqref{eq:tm-grid-argument}, $2(x+h)=2(j+1)/2^{q+1}=(j+1)/2^q=x_{q,j+1}$, and the
hypothesis $j<2^q$ is exactly the condition $j+1\le2^q$ that keeps this abscissa in the unit
interval.  The shifted grid satisfies the same computation with $\sigma$ replaced by
$\sigma_{\bullet+1}$ throughout.
\end{proof}

\Cref{eq:tm-grid-equation} is the discrete counterpart of the global differential equation
\eqref{eq:global-diffeq-again}: a difference quotient at level $q+1$ equals twice a
level-$q$ value at the doubled abscissa.  Two features of the discrete statement have no
continuous analogue and are easy to lose.  First, the right-hand side is evaluated at the
\emph{forward} node $2(x+h)$ rather than at $2x$; this is the inclusive-prefix convention
\eqref{eq:tm-forward-diff}.  Second, the identity is an assertion about a level-$q$ value,
so it is meaningful as a statement about $F$ or $\Up$ only when the doubled abscissa lies in
the relevant interval, that is, only when $j<2^q$.  Both features are used in
\cref{tm:sub-corrected}.

\subsection{The literal right endpoint does not converge}
\label{tm:sub-literal-endpoint}

The unshifted normalization printed in the source formula cannot converge to a function
whose right-endpoint value is \(1\): the sequence of literal values at the index \(2^k\)
does not tend to \(1\).  This exact negative statement is
\lean{paperPrefixGridValue_endpoint_not_tendsto_one} in
\path{ThueMorseGenerating.lean}.  The corrected normalization below is therefore a
mathematical requirement, not a cosmetic reindexing.

\subsection{Corrected normalization and pointwise convergence}
\label{tm:sub-corrected}

We now identify the normalized prefix grid with the histogram approximants of the
probabilistic construction, and deduce convergence.  Two adjustments to the naive reading
are required, and both are forced by identities already proved: the prefix order must be
shifted by one, because \eqref{eq:tm-prefix-eq-coeff} matches $\sigma_{n+1}$ with $\mathcal P_n$; and
the grid index $m$ must be read as labelling the \emph{center} of a cell of width $2^{-n}$,
not the left endpoint of a cell of width $2^{-(n+1)}$.

For the polynomial $\mathcal P_n$ of \eqref{eq:tm-approx-poly}, place the coefficient of
the monomial $z^m$ at
\begin{equation}
 \xi_{n,m}=\frac{2m-g_n}{2^{n+1}},                    \label{eq:tm-center}
\end{equation}
and spread it uniformly over the cell $I_{n,m}$ of width $2^{-n}$ centered at $\xi_{n,m}$.
The consecutive centers are spaced $2^{-n}$ apart, so these finitely many cells tile their
support interval
\[
 \left[-1+\frac{n+1}{2^{n+1}},\ 1-\frac{n+1}{2^{n+1}}\right].
\]
The resulting step approximant is
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal W_n(x)=\frac{2^n}{2^{\binom{n+1}2}}
 \sum_{m=0}^{g_n}\bigl[z^m\bigr]\mathcal P_n(z)\cdot
 \mathbf 1_{I_{n,m}}(x),                                   \label{eq:tm-histogram}
\end{equation}
where a point shared by two adjacent cells is given the weight $\tfrac12$ in each, so that
the two contributions add to the single value the formula is intended to have.  This
endpoint convention is not cosmetic: with ordinary closed-interval indicators the value at
$x=0$ would be counted twice already for $n=1$.

\begin{theorem}[Histogram approximants]
\label{thm:tm-histogram-limit}
For every $n\ge0$ the function $\mathcal W_n$ satisfies $0\le\mathcal W_n\le1$, is nondecreasing on
$(-\infty,0)$, is nonincreasing on $(0,\infty)$, and satisfies $\mathcal W_n(0)=1$.  Moreover
$\mathcal W_n(x)\to\Up(x)$ for every real $x$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For $n\ge1$, this is exactly \cref{thm:four-step-approx}, since
\eqref{eq:tm-approx-poly} and \eqref{eq:four-P-product} define the same polynomial, while
\eqref{eq:tm-center} and \eqref{eq:tm-histogram} give the same cells and normalization as
\eqref{eq:four-cell} and \eqref{eq:four-step-approximant}.  At $n=0$ the empty product is
$\mathcal P_0=1$, $g_0=0$, and $\mathcal W_0$ is the half-weighted indicator of
$[-1/2,1/2]$, so the stated order and normalization properties are immediate.  The
pointwise convergence of the sequence is already the final assertion of
\cref{thm:four-step-approx}.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Exact identification of the corrected grid]
\label{thm:tm-histogram-identity}
Put $\widetilde{\mathcal V}_n(m)=\sigma_{n+1}(m)/2^{\binom n2}$.  For every $n\ge0$ and
every $m$ with $0\le m<2^{n+1}$,
\begin{equation}
 \widetilde{\mathcal V}_n(m)=\mathcal W_n(\xi_{n,m}).          \label{eq:tm-grid-eq-histogram}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
If $m\le g_n$, the point $\xi_{n,m}$ is the center of $I_{n,m}$ and lies in no other cell,
so \eqref{eq:tm-histogram} evaluates there to
\begin{equation*}
 \frac{2^{n}}{2^{\binom{n+1}2}}\bigl[z^m\bigr]\mathcal P_n(z).
\end{equation*}
Since $\binom{n+1}2=\binom n2+n$, this is
$\bigl[z^m\bigr]\mathcal P_n/2^{\binom n2}=\widetilde{\mathcal V}_n(m)$ by
\eqref{eq:tm-prefix-eq-coeff}.  If $g_n<m<2^{n+1}$, then $\xi_{n,m}$ lies strictly to the
right of the finite support interval above, so $\mathcal W_n(\xi_{n,m})=0$; meanwhile
$[z^m]\mathcal P_n=\sigma_{n+1}(m)=0$ because $\deg\mathcal P_n=g_n$.  Thus both sides
again agree.
\end{proof}

Sampling at the dyadic floor now gives an approximation scheme.  For $x\in\R$ set
\begin{equation}
 j_n(x)=\max\!\left(0,\bigl\lfloor2^{n+1}x\bigr\rfloor\right),
 \qquad
 \mathcal J_n(x)=\widetilde{\mathcal V}_n\bigl(j_n(x)\bigr)
 =\frac{\sigma_{n+1}\bigl(j_n(x)\bigr)}{2^{\binom n2}}.    \label{eq:tm-sample}
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}[Moving cell centers]
\label{thm:tm-moving-center}
For every $x\in[0,1)$, $\xi_{n,j_n(x)}\to2x-1$ as $n\to\infty$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By \eqref{eq:tm-center},
$\xi_{n,j_n(x)}=2\cdot j_n(x)2^{-(n+1)}-g_n2^{-(n+1)}$.  The first term tends to
$2x$ because $0\le2^{n+1}x-j_n(x)<1$, and
$g_n2^{-(n+1)}=1-(n+2)2^{-(n+1)}\to1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Stability under moving arguments]
\label{thm:tm-moving}
If $u_n\to y$ in $\R$, then $\mathcal W_n(u_n)\to\Up(y)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $\varepsilon>0$.  Suppose first $y>0$.  By continuity of $\Up$ choose
$d\in(0,y/2]$ with $|\Up(y\pm d)-\Up(y)|<\varepsilon/2$.  For all large $n$ we have
$u_n\in[y-d,y+d]\subset(0,\infty)$, so the monotonicity of \cref{thm:tm-histogram-limit} on
$(0,\infty)$ gives
\begin{equation*}
 \mathcal W_n(y+d)\le\mathcal W_n(u_n)\le\mathcal W_n(y-d).
\end{equation*}
By pointwise convergence at the two fixed points $y\pm d$, for all large $n$ both outer
terms lie within $\varepsilon/2$ of $\Up(y\pm d)$, hence within $\varepsilon$ of $\Up(y)$.
The case $y<0$ is identical using monotonicity on $(-\infty,0)$.  For $y=0$ the upper bound
is $\mathcal W_n(u_n)\le1=\Up(0)$, and the lower bound is $\mathcal W_n(-d)$, $\mathcal W_n(0)=1$, or
$\mathcal W_n(d)$ according to the sign of $u_n$; each of the three converges to a value within
$\varepsilon/2$ of $\Up(0)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Corrected prefix approximation]
\label{thm:tm-corrected-convergence}
For every \(x\le1\),
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal J_n(x)\longrightarrow\Up(2x-1),                      \label{eq:tm-corrected-limit}
\end{equation}
and on the whole nonpositive half-line the finite-stage values are exactly
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal J_n(x)=\mathcal J_n(0)=2^{-\binom n2}\qquad(x\le0).  \label{eq:tm-left-tail-exact}
\end{equation}
and consequently, for every $x\in[0,1]$,
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal J_n\!\left(\frac x2\right)\longrightarrow F(x).      \label{eq:tm-corrected-fabius}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For \(x\le0\), the natural-floor clamp is exact:
\lean{correctedPrefixGridSample_eq_at_zero_of_nonpos} and
\lean{correctedPrefixGridSample_zero} give \eqref{eq:tm-left-tail-exact}, while
\lean{correctedPrefixGridSample_tendsto_of_nonpos} gives the asserted limit.  Now let
$0<x<1$.  Then $2^{n+1}x<2^{n+1}$, so $j_n(x)<2^{n+1}$ and
\cref{thm:tm-histogram-identity} applies:
$\mathcal J_n(x)=\mathcal W_n(\xi_{n,j_n(x)})$.  Combining
\cref{thm:tm-moving-center} with \cref{thm:tm-moving} gives
$\mathcal J_n(x)\to\Up(2x-1)$.

At $x=1$ the identification fails, because $j_n(1)=2^{n+1}$ is exactly the first index not
covered by \eqref{eq:tm-prefix-eq-coeff}.  Here the value is computed instead from
\eqref{eq:tm-run-right} with $k=r=n+1$:
\begin{equation*}
 \mathcal J_n(1)=\frac{\sigma_{n+1}(2^{n+1})}{2^{\binom n2}}=-\frac1{2^{\binom n2}}
 \longrightarrow0=\Up(1),
\end{equation*}
since $\binom n2\to\infty$ and $\Up(1)=F(0)=0$.  Altogether this is the public theorem
\lean{correctedPrefixGridSample_tendsto_rvachevUp_of_le_one} in
\path{ThueMorseApproximation.lean}.  For \eqref{eq:tm-corrected-fabius}, apply
it at $x/2\in[0,1]$ and use $2(x/2)-1=x-1\le0$, so that $\Up(x-1)=F(x)$ by
\eqref{eq:up-fold}.
\end{proof}

\begin{warning}
\Cref{thm:tm-corrected-convergence} is a pointwise statement and nothing more.  No uniform
rate is asserted, and none is proved.  The proof passes through
\cref{thm:tm-moving}, whose only quantitative inputs are the shared monotonicity of the
$\mathcal W_n$ and the modulus of continuity of the limit $\Up$ at the single point $2x-1$; the
sandwich it uses degrades as that modulus degrades.  A uniform bound of the kind supplied
for the centered splines by \eqref{eq:centered-error} is available from
\cref{sec:centered-splines}, but it is a different approximation scheme, and the two should
not be conflated.
\end{warning}

\begin{remark}
The two corrections are independent of one another and both are necessary.  Shifting the
prefix order from $k$ to $k+1$ is what makes \eqref{eq:tm-prefix-eq-coeff} available;
reading the index $m$ as the center \eqref{eq:tm-center} of a cell of width $2^{-n}$ rather
than as an abscissa $m2^{-k}$ is what supplies the affine change of variable $x\mapsto2x-1$
between the unit interval and the support of $\Up$.  In particular the corrected grid is a
sample of the compactly supported bump, not of the distribution function; this is why its
right-endpoint limit is $0$ and not $1$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The lower Lambert branch}
\label{tm:sub-lambert}

The asymptotic analysis of \cref{sec:sharp-asymptotic} is organized around the coordinate
\eqref{eq:W-def}, where $W_{-1}$ denotes the lower real branch of Lambert's
$W$-function \cite{corless1996}.  That section, and the elementary expansions derived from
it in \cref{sec:elementary-asymptotic}, use three facts about $W_{-1}$ without proving any
of them: that the branch exists on $(-\e^{-1},0)$, that it is characterized by being the
solution below $-1$, and that it has the two-term expansion recorded below.  We supply them
here, since they are the foundational input for \eqref{eq:lambda-equation} and for every
subsequent estimate in the Lambert variable.

\begin{proposition}[Existence, defining equation, and uniqueness]
\label{thm:tm-lambert-branch}
For \(z\in(-\e^{-1},0)\), let \(W_{-1}(z)\) denote the totalized lower branch
\lean{lowerLambertW}.  Then
\begin{equation}
 W_{-1}(z)<-1,
 \qquad
 W_{-1}(z)\,\e^{W_{-1}(z)}=z,                              \label{eq:tm-lambert-eq}
\end{equation}
and $W_{-1}(z)$ is the unique real $w<-1$ with $w\e^w=z$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Formal proof]
These are respectively \lean{lowerLambertW_lt_neg_one},
\lean{lowerLambertW_mul_exp}, and \lean{lowerLambertW_unique} in
\path{LowerLambertW.lean}.
\end{proof}

On its natural domain, \(W_{-1}\) is continuous and strictly decreasing, and its image is
exactly \((-\infty,-1)\).  At every \(z\in(-\e^{-1},0)\), it is differentiable with
\begin{equation}
 \frac{d}{dz}W_{-1}(z)
 =\frac{W_{-1}(z)}{z\bigl(1+W_{-1}(z)\bigr)}<0.              \label{eq:lower-lambert-derivative}
\end{equation}
These four assertions are \lean{lowerLambertW_continuousOn},
\lean{lowerLambertW_strictAntiOn}, \lean{lowerLambertW_image},
\lean{deriv_lowerLambertW}, and \lean{deriv_lowerLambertW_neg}; the underlying derivative

\subsection{A binomial--logarithm formula for the Thue--Morse bit}
\label{tm:sub-binomial-log}

The zero-one Thue--Morse bit is \(\tau(n)=\wt(n)\bmod2\), so that
\(\varepsilon_n=1-2\tau(n)\).  Its primitive-recursive evaluator is
\lean{tmBitPR}; the theorems \lean{tmBitPR_of_pos} and
\lean{tmBitPR_eq_thueMorseBit} prove its agreement with the binary-weight definition.
The bit also admits a closed formula in which the binary structure is hidden inside a
single row of Pascal's triangle.  Set
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal X(n)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{\binom nk}\in\Z.        \label{eq:tm-parity-sum}
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}[Glaisher count]
\label{thm:tm-glaisher}
The number of odd entries in the $n$\/th row of Pascal's triangle is $2^{\wt(n)}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $\nu(n)$ denote that number, so that $\nu(n)$ is the number of nonzero coefficients of
$(1+z)^n$ in $\mathbb F_2[z]$.  There $(1+z)^2=1+z^2$, hence
\begin{equation*}
 (1+z)^{2n}=\bigl(1+z^2\bigr)^n,
 \qquad
 (1+z)^{2n+1}=\bigl(1+z^2\bigr)^n(1+z).
\end{equation*}
The substitution $z\mapsto z^2$ does not change the number of nonzero coefficients, so
$\nu(2n)=\nu(n)$; and multiplying a polynomial in $z^2$ by $1+z$ produces two summands with
disjoint supports, one even and one odd, so $\nu(2n+1)=2\nu(n)$.  Since $\wt(2n)=\wt(n)$
and $\wt(2n+1)=\wt(n)+1$ by \eqref{eq:weight-even-odd}, and $\nu(0)=1=2^{\wt(0)}$,
induction on the binary representation gives $\nu(n)=2^{\wt(n)}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[The logarithm is exact]
\label{thm:tm-log-exact}
For every $n\ge0$,
\begin{equation}
 n+1-\mathcal X(n)=2^{\wt(n)+1}.                           \label{eq:tm-log-exact}
\end{equation}
In particular $n+1-\mathcal X(n)$ is a positive power of two, and
$\log_2\bigl(n+1-\mathcal X(n)\bigr)=\wt(n)+1$ is an integer.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Each summand of \eqref{eq:tm-parity-sum} is $-1$ at an odd entry and $+1$ at an even entry,
and the row has $n+1$ entries.  Hence $\mathcal X(n)=(n+1)-2\nu(n)$ with $\nu$ as in
\cref{thm:tm-glaisher}, so $n+1-\mathcal X(n)=2\nu(n)=2^{\wt(n)+1}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Binomial--logarithm formula]
\label{thm:tm-bit-formula}
For every $n\ge0$,
\begin{equation}
 \tau(n)=\frac{1+(-1)^{\,\log_2\!\left(n+1-\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{\binom nk}\right)}}{2},
 \qquad
 \varepsilon_n=1-2\tau(n).                                 \label{eq:tm-bit-formula}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By \cref{thm:tm-log-exact} the exponent equals $\wt(n)+1$.  If $\wt(n)$ is even then
$\tau(n)=0$ and $(-1)^{\wt(n)+1}=-1$, so the right side is $0$; if $\wt(n)$ is odd then
$\tau(n)=1$ and $(-1)^{\wt(n)+1}=1$, so the right side is $1$.  The second identity is
$\varepsilon_n=(-1)^{\wt(n)}=1-2\bigl(\wt(n)\bmod2\bigr)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The order of the two steps matters.  As printed, \eqref{eq:tm-bit-formula} raises $-1$ to a
logarithm, an operation that is meaningful only because the argument of that logarithm has
first been proved to be exactly a power of two.  The formal development therefore proves
\eqref{eq:tm-log-exact} before it interprets the outer expression at all, evaluates the
logarithm on the natural numbers, and reads the final division in $\Q$.  A floating-point
evaluation of the same display, or an interpretation of the logarithm as a real-valued
function whose output is then rounded, is an approximation to a formula rather than the
formula.
\end{remark}

\section{Endpoint asymptotics: the exact formal hierarchy}
\label{sec:asymptotic-decomposition}
\sectionmark{Endpoint asymptotics}

The behavior of \(F(x)\) as \(x\downarrow0\) is governed by three nested structures: an
exact dyadic product on the negative Laplace axis, a genuinely nonconstant periodic
correction on logarithmic scale, and a lower-Lambert saddle coordinate.  This section
states the hierarchy in the form proved in Lean.  Longer heuristic derivations, numerical
experiments, and proposed refinements without exact theorem counterparts are collected in
\path{docs/non-formalized-research-frontiers/non-formalized-research-frontiers.tex}.

Throughout, put
\begin{equation}
 L=\log 2.                                                   \label{eq:L-def}
\end{equation}

\subsection{The negative-Laplace product and its exact decomposition}

For \(s>0\), the entire generating function \(G\) from \eqref{eq:G-def} satisfies
\begin{equation}
 G(-s)=\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}
 \frac{1-\exp(-s/2^j)}{s/2^j}.                              \label{eq:negative-Laplace-product}
\end{equation}
This is the real specialization of
\lean{complexGeneratingFunction_neg_eq_tprod} in
\path{NegativeLaplaceVertical.lean}.  If
\[
 \kappa(u)=\log\!\left(\frac{1-\exp(-u)}{u}\right),
 \qquad
 \Lambda(s)=\log G(-s)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\kappa(s/2^j),
\]
then the series is absolutely summable for every \(s>0\); the precise declarations are
\lean{summable_negativeLaplaceTerm},
\lean{exp_negativeLaplaceLog_eq_generatingFunction_neg}, and
\lean{negativeLaplaceLog_eq_log_laplaceMoment}.

Let \(P\) denote \lean{negativeLaplacePeriodicCorrection} and let \(E\) denote
\lean{negativeLaplaceTailError}.  The exact decomposition is
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\;
 \Lambda(s)=
 -\frac{(\log s)^2}{2L}+\frac{\log s}{2}
 +P(\log_2 s)+E(s)
 \;}\qquad(s>0).                                            \label{eq:Lambda-exact-periodic}
\end{equation}
Moreover,
\begin{equation}
 0\le E(s)\le
 \frac{\exp(-s)}{(1-\exp(-s))^2},\qquad
 E(s)\le4\exp(-s)\quad(s\ge L).                             \label{eq:negative-Laplace-tail-bound}
\end{equation}
These are exactly
\lean{negativeLaplaceLog_exact_periodic_decomposition},
\lean{negativeLaplaceTailError_nonneg},
\lean{abs_negativeLaplaceTailError_le}, and
\lean{abs_negativeLaplaceTailError_le_four_exp} in
\path{NegativeLaplace.lean}.  The correction \(P\) is \(C^\infty\) and one-periodic by
\lean{contDiff_infty_negativeLaplacePeriodicCorrection} and
\lean{negativeLaplacePeriodicCorrection_add_one}.

Write
\begin{equation}
 \overline P=\int_0^1P(u)\,\dd u,\qquad
 \Psi(t)=P(t)-\overline P.                                  \label{eq:Psi-def}
\end{equation}
Then \(\Psi\) is \(C^\infty\), one-periodic, and has mean zero:
\[
 \Psi(t+1)=\Psi(t),\qquad \int_0^1\Psi(t)\,\dd t=0.
\]
The formal counterparts are
\lean{contDiff_infty_negativeLaplacePsi},
\lean{negativeLaplacePsi_add_one}, and
\lean{integral_negativeLaplacePsi_zero}.

The first logarithmic derivative has a correspondingly sharp full-ray expansion.  As
\(s\to+\infty\),
\begin{equation}
 \Lambda'(s)=
 \frac{-\log s/L+1/2+\Psi'(\log_2s)/L}{s}
 +\bigO(s^{-2}).                                            \label{eq:negative-Laplace-first-derivative}
\end{equation}
Here \(\Lambda'\) is the formal \lean{negativeLaplaceLogFirst}, identified with the
derivative of \(\Lambda\) by \lean{negativeLaplaceLog_hasDerivAt}.  The exact
\(\bigO(s^{-2})\) statement is
\lean{negativeLaplaceLogFirst_sub_periodic_main_isBigO_inv_sq_real} in
\path{LaplacePeriodicSecondOrder.lean}.  This promotes the proved derivative estimate
without importing the longer unformalized contour and Mellin derivations from the frontier
volume.

\subsection{The Gamma--zeta Fourier series}

For \(k\in\Z\), use the Fourier convention
\begin{equation}
 \widehat\Psi(k)=\int_0^1\Psi(t)\exp(-2\pi\ii kt)\,\dd t,
 \qquad
 \chi_k=\frac{2\pi\ii k}{L}.                                \label{eq:Psi-Fourier-def}
\end{equation}
The zero mode vanishes, and every nonzero mode has the closed form
\begin{equation}
 \widehat\Psi(0)=0,\qquad
 \widehat\Psi(k)=
 -\frac{\Gamma(-\chi_k)\zeta(1-\chi_k)}{L}
 \quad(k\ne0).                                              \label{eq:Psi-Fourier}
\end{equation}
Every coefficient with \(k\ne0\) is nonzero, so \(\Psi\) is genuinely nonconstant.
The coefficient sequence is absolutely summable and reconstructs \(\Psi\) pointwise:
\begin{equation}
 \Psi(t)=\sum_{k\in\Z}\widehat\Psi(k)\exp(2\pi\ii kt).
                                                                  \label{eq:Psi-Fourier-series}
\end{equation}
These assertions are
\lean{negativeLaplacePsiFourierCoeff_zero},
\lean{negativeLaplacePsiFourierCoeff_eq_neg_gamma_zeta},
\lean{negativeLaplacePsiFourierCoeff_ne_zero},
\lean{negativeLaplacePsi_not_constant},
\lean{summable_negativeLaplacePsiGammaZetaFourierCoeff}, and
\lean{hasSum_negativeLaplacePsi_gammaZeta_fourierSeries} in
\path{PeriodicFourier.lean}.  No numerical truncation or stronger coefficient-decay
claim is needed here.

\subsection{The evaluated constant}

Let \(\gamma\) be Euler's constant and let \(\gamma_1\) denote the formally defined first
Stieltjes constant \lean{firstStieltjesConstant}.  The regularized Gamma--zeta
combination
\[
 A_0=\frac{\gamma^2}{2}+\gamma_1-\frac{\pi^2}{12}
\]
is \lean{gammaZetaConstant}; its equivalent common-denominator form is proved by
\lean{gammaZetaConstant_eq_div_twelve}.  The split, convergent finite-part Bose integral
is evaluated as this same constant by
\lean{boseFinitePartIntegral_eq_gammaZetaConstant}.

The formal finite part is independent of where the convergent integral is split.  More
precisely, put
\[
 \kappa_0(x)=\frac1x\log\!\left(\frac{1-\exp(-x)}x\right),
 \qquad
 \kappa_\infty(x)=\frac1x\log(1-\exp(-x)).
\]
For every \(c>0\), both integrals below are convergent and
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{
 \int_{(0,c]}\kappa_0(x)\,dx+
 \int_{(c,\infty)}\kappa_\infty(x)\,dx+
 \frac{(\log c)^2}{2}=A_0.}                                  \label{eq:bose-finite-part-cutoff}
\end{equation}
If \(0<a\le b\), changing the splitting point is governed by the exact transition identity
\begin{equation}
 \int_{(a,b]}\bigl(\kappa_\infty(x)-\kappa_0(x)\bigr)\,dx
 =\frac{(\log b)^2-(\log a)^2}{2}.                           \label{eq:bose-cutoff-transition}
\end{equation}
Here the two kernels are \lean{boseFinitePartSmallKernel} and
\lean{boseFinitePartLargeKernel}; their integrability is
\lean{integrableOn_boseFinitePartSmallKernel_Ioc} and
\lean{integrableOn_boseFinitePartLargeKernel_Ioi_of_pos}.  Equations
\eqref{eq:bose-finite-part-cutoff} and \eqref{eq:bose-cutoff-transition} are respectively
\lean{boseFinitePartIntegral_eq_gammaZetaConstant_of_pos} and
\lean{integral_boseFinitePartLargeKernel_sub_small_Ioc} in
\path{BoseFinitePartIntegral.lean}.  Thus the constant is represented by ordinary convergent
real integrals, without assigning a value to a divergent integral.

In turn,
\lean{negativeLaplacePeriodicMean_eq} gives
\[
 \overline P=\frac{A_0}{L}-\frac{L}{12}.
\]
After the Gaussian normalization, the sharp Fabius constant is
\begin{equation}
 C_F=
 \frac{6\gamma^2+12\gamma_1-\pi^2}{12L}
 -\frac{7L}{12}-\frac12\log\pi.                             \label{eq:sharp-constant}
\end{equation}
This is the exact identity
\lean{fabiusSharpAsymptoticConstant_eq} in
\path{FabiusSharpConstant.lean}.

\subsection{A fully effective dyadic bound}

The dyadic specialization admits a proved numerical threshold and error constant.  For

 D_n={}&-\frac L2n^2-n\log n+\left(1+\frac L2\right)n

Here the final term is exactly \lean{dyadicEndpointSecondOrder}; the derivative

                                                               \label{eq:dyadic-effective-bound}
\end{equation}
This is \lean{abs_log_fabius_dyadic_sub_explicitCumulantMain_le} in
\path{FabiusDyadicSharpCumulant.lean}.  The threshold is a sufficient certified threshold,

\subsection{The lower-Lambert saddle and exact reduction}
\label{sec:sharp-asymptotic}

On the endpoint-inclusive lower-branch domain
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal D_L=\left(0,\frac{\e^{-1}}L\right],                \label{eq:lambert-phase-domain}
\end{equation}
let
\begin{equation}
 W=W_{-1}(-Lx),                                             \label{eq:W-def}
\end{equation}
and define
\begin{equation}
 \lambda=\lambda(x)=-\frac{W}{L},
 \qquad r=r(x)=2^\lambda.                                  \label{eq:lambda-def}
\end{equation}
The global Lean definitions are \lean{fabiusLambertPhase} and
\lean{fabiusLambertRadius}; here we use them on \(\mathcal D_L\).  For every
\(x\in\mathcal D_L\), their exact identities are
\begin{equation}
 \lambda2^{-\lambda}=x,\qquad
 \frac{\lambda}{r}=x,\qquad
 rx=\lambda,\qquad
 \log r=L\lambda.                                          \label{eq:lambda-equation}
\end{equation}
The endpoint-inclusive quotient and product identities are
\lean{fabiusLambertPhase_div_radius_of_le} and
\lean{fabiusLambertRadius_mul_argument_of_le}; the first equality in
\eqref{eq:lambda-equation} is the quotient identity rewritten using \(r=2^\lambda\).
The logarithmic identity in fact holds for every real \(x\), not only on
\(\mathcal D_L\); this is \lean{log_fabiusLambertRadius}.  The corresponding
strict-interior wrappers are \lean{fabiusLambertPhase_div_radius} and
\lean{fabiusLambertRadius_mul_argument}.  All five declarations are in
\path{FabiusLambertSaddle.lean}.

On the same closed-upper domain, the phase has the sharp lower bound and endpoint
characterization
\begin{equation}
 \lambda(x)\ge\frac1L,
 \qquad
 \lambda(x)=\frac1L
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 x=\frac{\e^{-1}}L.                                        \label{eq:lambert-phase-closed-bound}
\end{equation}
In particular,
\begin{equation}
 \lambda\!\left(\frac{\e^{-1}}L\right)=\frac1L.             \label{eq:lambert-phase-branch-point}
\end{equation}
Moreover, \(\lambda\) is strictly decreasing on \(\mathcal D_L\), and its exact image is
\begin{equation}
 \lambda(\mathcal D_L)=\left[\frac1L,\infty\right).         \label{eq:lambert-phase-closed-image}
\end{equation}
The bound, equality characterization, endpoint value, strict antitonicity, and image are
respectively \lean{fabiusLambertPhase_ge_inv_log_two},
\lean{fabiusLambertPhase_eq_inv_log_two_iff},
\lean{fabiusLambertPhase_branchPoint},
\lean{fabiusLambertPhase_strictAntiOn_Ioc}, and
\lean{fabiusLambertPhase_image_Ioc} in \path{FabiusLambertSaddle.lean}.

Equivalently, under the hypotheses \(x>0\) and \(Lx\le\e^{-1}\), the same
lower bound and the strict positivity \(0<\lambda(x)\) are
\lean{one_div_log_two_le_fabiusLambertPhase} and
\lean{fabiusLambertPhase_pos_of_le}.  Thus the algebraic saddle identities
extend to the finite branch point even though the smooth formulas below do not.

The smooth API is deliberately restricted to the open interior
\(0<x<\e^{-1}/L\).  There \(\lambda\) is continuous and strictly decreasing, its image is
\((1/L,\infty)\), and it is differentiable with
\begin{equation}
 \lambda'(x)=
 \frac{\lambda(x)}{x\bigl(1-L\lambda(x)\bigr)}<0.            \label{eq:lambert-phase-derivative}
\end{equation}
These are \lean{fabiusLambertPhase_continuousOn},
\lean{fabiusLambertPhase_strictAntiOn}, \lean{fabiusLambertPhase_image},
\lean{deriv_fabiusLambertPhase}, and \lean{deriv_fabiusLambertPhase_neg} in
\path{FabiusLambertSaddle.lean}; the derivative witness is
\lean{fabiusLambertPhase_hasDerivAt}, and pointwise continuity is
\lean{fabiusLambertPhase_continuousAt}.
At the small-argument endpoint \(x=0\), the exact phase and its reciprocal have the limits
\begin{equation}
 \lambda(x)\longrightarrow+\infty,
 \qquad \lambda(x)^{-1}\longrightarrow0
 \qquad(x\downarrow0).                                    \label{eq:lambert-phase-endpoint-limits}
\end{equation}
These are \lean{tendsto_fabiusLambertPhase_nhdsGT_zero_atTop} and
\lean{tendsto_inv_fabiusLambertPhase_nhdsGT_zero} in
\path{FabiusLambertRates.lean}.

The corrected main term is
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal M(x)=
 -\frac L2\lambda(x)^2+
 \left(1+\frac L2\right)\lambda(x)
 -\frac12\log\lambda(x)+C_F+\Psi(\lambda(x)).
                                                               \label{eq:sharp-main-lambda}
\end{equation}
This is definition \lean{fabiusSharpLambertMain}.  The exact saddle action
itself is endpoint-inclusive: whenever \(x>0\) and \(Lx\le\e^{-1}\),
\begin{equation}
 r(x)x+\Lambda(r(x))-\frac12\log\bigl(2\pi\lambda(x)\bigr)
 =\mathcal M(x)+E(r(x)).                                  \label{eq:lambert-saddle-action}
\end{equation}
This is \lean{fabiusLambertSaddleAction_eq_of_le} in
\path{FabiusSharpLambertMain.lean}.  If
\(\mathcal R(x)\) denotes the normalized saddle ratio
\lean{fabiusSaddleRatio} at \(r(x)\) and \(\lambda(x)\), then Lean proves the exact
error separation
\begin{equation}
 \log F(x)-\mathcal M(x)=\log\mathcal R(x)+E(r(x)).
                                                               \label{eq:sharp-exact-reduction}
\end{equation}
The declaration is

This is \lean{negativeLaplaceTailError_dyadicLambert_isLittleO_inv_pow} in

\end{equation}
This is \lean{fabius_bromwich}; the dimensionless, Gaussian-normalized version used by

\subsection{The compact online formula and its missing periodic factor}
\label{sec:compact-online-lambert}

 \mathcal W_{\mathrm{MSE}}(x)=
 -\frac{7L}{12}-\frac12\log(\pi x)
 +\frac1L\left(
   \gamma_1+\frac{\gamma^2}{2}-\frac{\pi^2}{12}

                                                               \label{eq:mse-lambert-log-main}
\end{equation}
This is the human-readable form of definition \lean{fabiusWikipediaLambertMain} in

\begin{equation}
 A_{\mathrm{MSE}}(x)=

  \frac1L\left(
   \gamma_1+\frac{\gamma^2}{2}-\frac{\pi^2}{12}

For \(x>0\), this factor is strictly positive.  Lean names it
\lean{fabiusWikipediaLambertFactor};
\lean{exp_fabiusWikipediaLambertMain_eq_WikipediaLambertFactor} identifies it

That ratio-one claim is false.  The answer on the cited page first derives a Gamma--zeta

\section{Effective computability}
\label{sec:computation}

\section{Formalization boundary and audit conventions}
\label{sec:audit}

This exposition follows a fail-closed rule.  A mathematical assertion remains in the main
document only when an exact proved declaration can be identified in the Lean development.
A nearby theorem, an executable computation, a numerical check, or an argument that appears
straightforward is not treated as a substitute.  When the formal theorem has stricter
hypotheses, narrower domains, or different endpoint conventions, the prose uses those exact
conditions.

That rule explains several choices made above:

\begin{itemize}
\item The finite probability approximation uses atomic convolution measures and the exact
      weak-convergence API, not an unformalized continuous box-convolution density.
\item Step cells use the formal half-endpoint indicator, including its point value at a
      shared boundary.
\item The Fourier chapter states the exact complex zero classification and canonical product,
      and the exact positive-integer zero order in its proved real punctured-limit form; it
      does not promote that real limit to an unstated complex local-multiplicity theorem.
\item Flatness uses natural powers, and the analytic-locus claims use precisely the domains
      proved in \path{NowhereAnalytic.lean}.
\item The \(q\)-calculus is restricted to the half-base identities actually present in Lean.
\item Endpoint asymptotics retain exact phases and the proved remainder scopes; numerical
      fits and unproved coefficient formulas are absent.
\end{itemize}

The source drafts have been discharged into two destinations.  Proved material was rewritten
into the theorem-backed narrative here.  The remaining research material is preserved under

\begin{quote}
\path{Analysis/FabiusFunction/docs/non-formalized-research-frontiers/}.
\end{quote}

In particular, the inverse/saddle, repeated-integration, small-argument, generic
\(q\)-calculus, and other deferred claims remain available there with an explicit warning
that they are not part of the formal corpus.
