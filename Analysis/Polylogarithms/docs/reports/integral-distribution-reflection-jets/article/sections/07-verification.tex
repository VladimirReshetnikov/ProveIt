\section{Reproducible verification and what the computations establish}
\label{ir:sec:verification}

\subsection{The exact core and its independent certificate replay}
The package's core is ordinary Python integer arithmetic; it has no
third-party dependency. A sparse polynomial is encoded as integer
coefficients indexed by exponent tuples in the ordered prime variables.
No source string is passed to an evaluator, and no floating-point number
enters a polynomial equality check.

The file \code{distribution.py} constructs the basis, terminates the
straightener, and produces raw-row certificates. The independent
\code{verify\_certificates.py} does not call the basis selector,
straightener, or certificate generator. It reads the frozen JSON,
reconstructs each prime fiber directly in the original $q$-point grid,
expands the polynomial row combination, and checks exact coefficient
cancellation. It shares only the sparse integer-polynomial arithmetic.
A deliberate one-coefficient corruption is also tested and rejected.
This controls a simple failure mode of the replay code; it is not a
transcendental-independence test.

The three certified identities use $3$, $4$, and $5$ raw rows at
levels $12$, $15$, and $30$, respectively. Their selected universal
unit minors have sizes $8$, $7$, and $22$. The coefficients and row
indices are preserved in
\src{data/identity_certificates.json}; the exact replay result is in
\src{logs/certificate_replay.json}.

The resolution tests construct its matrices from
\eqref{ir:eq:chain-differential}, verify $d^2=0$, and check that
adjacent boundary ranks add to the chain dimension in every positive
degree. They use $2\le q\le40$ and $q=60,105$, in each of the three
specified characteristics. Their degree-zero quotient dimension is
checked against $\varphi(q)$.

The jet tests work with bit-encoded polynomials in
$\F_2[t]/(t^L)$, for $1\le L\le7$, eleven levels, and five weight
profiles. They expand the reflection operator as a binary matrix on
all basis-point/jet-degree pairs and compute its exact rank. The
profiles include a curve entirely on the exceptional locus, different
positive contact orders, the $a_2=0$ branch, and a generic point.
The predicted contact order is computed directly from the active
scalars, not fitted to the matrix rank.

The optional integer Smith tests use SymPy's integer Smith algorithm,
independently of the binary-rank formula. At fifteen specified levels,
they use all combinations of weights in $\{-1,0,1,2\}$ and both
reflection signs. They verify $c^2=1$ as an integer matrix and compare
every invariant factor, including the zeros. Exact logs record the
versions used: Python 3.13.5 and SymPy 1.14.0. These version strings
report the executed environment, not requirements for a future run.

\subsection{Numerical diagnostics use a separate analytic representation}
The analytic equalities already have exact proofs. Numerical work is
included to expose convention and branch errors, not to upgrade a
small residual into a proof. The level-$15$ spectral identity was
checked at $s=2,3,1/2,1.2+0.4i,-2,1$, at both 65 and 95 decimal
working digits. At 95 digits, the largest recorded absolute residual
in these six checks is approximately $1.82\times10^{-94}$.

For an independent mathematical representation, use
\begin{equation}\label{ir:eq:mellin}
 \Li_s(z)=\frac1{\Gamma(s)}\int_0^\infty
 u^{s-1}\frac{ze^{-u}}{1-ze^{-u}}\,du,
 \qquad\Re s>0,\quad |z|=1,\ z\ne1.
\end{equation}
This is the standard Mellin representation \cite{dlmf-polylog}; it
also follows by first working with $|z|<1$ and passing to the boundary
away from $z=1$. Its integral is locally uniformly convergent in
$\Re s>0$. Near zero, the kernel is bounded, and
$u^{\sigma-1}|\log u|^N$ is integrable for $\sigma>0$;
at infinity there is exponential decay. These bounds justify spectral
differentiation under the integral on compact subsets.

Instead of numerically differencing polylogarithm order at an integer,
we differentiate \eqref{ir:eq:mellin} analytically. The first four
polynomial multipliers are
\begin{align*}
 P_0(X)&=1,\\
 P_1(X)&=X+\gamma_0,\\
 P_2(X)&=(X+\gamma_0)^2-\zeta(2),\\
 P_3(X)&=(X+\gamma_0)^3-3\zeta(2)(X+\gamma_0)+2\zeta(3).
\end{align*}
They are the derivatives of $e^{tX}/\Gamma(1+t)$ at zero.
A factor $p^{-t}$ replaces $X$ by $X-\log p$. Integrating the
corresponding combined level-$15$ kernels tests
\eqref{ir:eq:jets15} for $0\le N\le3$. At 65 working digits, the
largest recorded residual among these four checks is approximately
$5.95\times10^{-63}$. Separate Mellin checks at $s=1,2$ also agree
with the direct polylogarithm evaluation.

These are floating-point quadrature diagnostics. No interval enclosure
or rigorous numerical error bound is claimed. A recorded zero residual
means zero at the working precision, not a newly certified exact zero.
All 18 diagnostics, their precisions, and their residuals are preserved
in \src{logs/numerical_diagnostics.json}. The exact equality certificates
remain the polynomial identities and the analytic argument translating
them.

\subsection{Replay commands}
From the package root, the exact core is replayed by
\begin{verbatim}
python code/verify_certificates.py
python code/test_exact.py
\end{verbatim}
The optional independent Smith and numerical diagnostics are
\begin{verbatim}
python code/test_smith.py
python code/numerical_diagnostics.py
\end{verbatim}
The first optional command requires SymPy; the second requires mpmath.
The delivered versions are pinned in an optional requirements file.
The certificate generator is provided separately, so replay does not
silently regenerate a claimed certificate before checking it.

A Makefile builds the article with three LaTeX passes and exposes the
same checks. The supplied PDF has been compiled and visually inspected.
Neither these programs nor the prose have been checked in Lean or another
proof assistant. The theorem statements and algebraic interfaces are
suitable future formalization targets, not already formalized results.
