\begin{abstract}
The sextic radical-solvability development in \repo\ uses terminating but
unbounded search to specialize two block-partition descriptors. We replace
that search, at the level of an ordinary mathematical proof, by a single
explicit parameter range. For every separable sextic in characteristic zero,
one of the integers $1,\ldots,196$ separates all fifteen pair partitions and
all ten triple partitions simultaneously. The underlying descriptors compress
to quadratics and linears. Their collisions are controlled by three invariant
polynomials $Q_f,U_f,L_f$, with degrees at most $30,120,45$, and we prove
\[
 \disc_Z\Rtwo=\disc(f)^6Q_f^6U_f^2,
 \qquad
 \disc_Z\Rthree=\disc(f)^3L_f^2.
\]
For the generic sextic, the three factors are irreducible and have the stated
exact degrees. Consequently 196 is optimal for a guarantee valid for every
set of distinct nonzero complex test parameters, although optimality of the
initial integer interval is not established. A split integer example gives a
lower bound of eight for that latter problem. We derive a coefficient-only
primitive-recursive decision architecture, a height-only separator requiring
no search, a precise repository integration plan, and further research
questions. Exact symbolic checks and a modular B\'ezout certificate accompany
the proofs.
\end{abstract}

\section{The repository question and the contribution}
\label{sbr:sec:scope}

The starting point is a concrete implementation boundary, not an assertion
that sextic solvability is an unsolved problem. In the pinned \repo\ snapshot,
\src{SexticRadicalDecision.lean} proves a total recursive criterion for integer
sextics. Its introductory comment explicitly does not claim primitive
recursiveness, because the separating-invariant search uses unbounded
minimization. The supporting file \src{SexticIrreducibleDecision.lean}
constructs pair and triple parameters using \src{Nat.find}. The file
\src{SexticSeparatingInvariants.lean} proves that suitable parameters exist by
representing each block by its root polynomial and each partition by the
polynomial of those block polynomials \cite{repo,repo-decision,repo-search,repo-invariants}.

That is a particularly useful research target: the existing semantic
architecture already identifies the right invariant and its correctness
condition. What is missing from this route is a small explicit bound and a
sufficiently transparent explanation of the exceptional specializations.
Our answer is stronger than merely bounding an enumeration of pairs of
natural numbers. A one-dimensional curve works for every distinct root
tuple, its failure set has three explicit components, and its generic number
of failures can be determined exactly.

\subsection{What is classical, inherited, and proved here}

The equivalence between radical solvability and solvability of the Galois
group is classical. So is the sextic criterion using the two imprimitive
wreath-product groups of degrees fifteen and ten in the corresponding
coset actions; see, for example, Boswell and Glasser \cite{boswell-glasser}.
Landau and Miller proved polynomial-time decidability of radical solvability
in 1985 \cite{landau-miller}. Thus even a primitive-recursive decision for
sextics is not, as an abstract existence theorem, new.

The partition descriptors and the strategy of separating their specialized
values are inherited from the inspected \repo\ source. More broadly, the
construction and use of relative invariants are central to computational
Galois theory; Fieker and Kl\"uners discuss general methods and the practical
importance of structured invariants \cite{fieker-klueners}.

The claims established in this article are the explicit compression,
the special curve $(u,v)=(t,2t^2)$, the $196$-point uniform bound, the
three-factor description of its obstruction, the exact discriminant
identities, and generic irreducibility and sharpness. Their proofs are given
below rather than inferred from computations. The bounded decision
architecture is an application to the repository's mathematical gap, not a
replacement claim for the stronger classical complexity theorem.

The literature check consulted the primary sources just cited and the
pinned source files. It does not establish that no equivalent identity has
appeared elsewhere under different coordinates or resolvent conventions.
Accordingly, ``contribution'' here means a result developed and proved for
this report, with a demonstrated use relative to this repository; it does
not mean a certified first-in-literature result.

\subsection{Main results at a glance}

The root tuple is always assumed distinct. The polynomials $h_P$ and $k_T$
are defined explicitly in \cref{sbr:sec:compression}; their resolvents have degrees
fifteen and ten in $Z$.

\begin{resultbox}
\textbf{Uniform separator.} Among any 196 distinct nonzero elements of a
characteristic-zero field containing the roots, there is a parameter $t$
for which both maps $P\mapsto h_P(t)$ and $T\mapsto k_T(t)$ are injective.
In particular, testing $1,\ldots,196$ always succeeds.

\textbf{Exact obstruction.} There is a nonzero polynomial
$E_f=Q_fU_fL_f$ of degree at most 195, with coefficients polynomially
expressible in the sextic coefficients, such that $E_f(t)\ne0$ is exactly
simultaneous separation. Its three factors have generic degrees $30,120,45$
and are generically irreducible.

\textbf{Sharpness and implementation.} The number 196 is optimal for
arbitrary complex test sets. For the initial integer interval the proven
bounds are $8\le N_{\mathrm{int}}\le196$. The explicit finite range removes
unbounded minimization from a coefficient-only sextic decision architecture.
\end{resultbox}

\begin{writenote}[Scope of the last sentence]
The finite range removes unbounded minimization from the architecture of
\cref{sbr:sec:algorithm}, on paper. It removes nothing from the Lean
development yet: none of this report is formalized, and the Lean search it
bounds is still an unbounded \src{Nat.find}; see \cref{sbr:sec:formal}.
\end{writenote}

\section{Notation and complete partition descriptors}
\label{sbr:sec:descriptors}

Let $K$ be a field of characteristic zero, let
$\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_6\in K$ be distinct, and put
\begin{equation}
 f(X)=\prod_{i=1}^6(X-\alpha_i)
     =X^6-e_1X^5+e_2X^4-e_3X^3+e_4X^2-e_5X+e_6.
 \label{sbr:eq:sextic}
\end{equation}
If the coefficients originally lie in a smaller field, take $K$ to contain
a splitting field. Our arguments are algebraic and do not depend on an
ordering, numerical approximation, or choice of complex branches.

Write $[6]=\{1,\ldots,6\}$ and
$\Edges=\binom{[6]}2$. Let $\Ptwo$ be the set of partitions of $[6]$ into
three unordered two-element blocks, and let $\Pthree$ be the set of
partitions into two unordered three-element blocks. Their cardinalities are
\begin{equation}
 |\Ptwo|=\frac{6!}{(2!)^3 3!}=15,
 \qquad |\Pthree|=\frac{\binom63}{2}=10.
\end{equation}
We call members of $\Ptwo$ \emph{matchings}. When two matchings have no common
block, we call them \emph{block-disjoint}; they still use the same six roots.

For a block $B\subseteq[6]$, define its root polynomial and the complete
partition descriptor by
\begin{equation}
 p_B(u)=\prod_{i\in B}(u-\alpha_i),
 \qquad D_{\mathcal B}(u,v)=\prod_{B\in\mathcal B}(v-p_B(u)).
 \label{sbr:eq:descriptor}
\end{equation}
These are the ordinary polynomial counterparts of the descriptors in
\src{SexticSeparatingInvariants.lean} \cite{repo-invariants}. The distinction
between the variables $u$ and $v$ matters: $u$ enters the root polynomials,
whereas $v$ records the unordered collection of blocks.

\begin{lemma}[A descriptor determines the partition]
\label{sbr:lem:complete}
For distinct roots, the map $\mathcal B\mapsto D_{\mathcal B}(u,v)$ is
injective on either partition family.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
First, the map $B\mapsto p_B(u)$ is injective. Indeed, the roots of $p_B$
among $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_6$ are precisely the roots indexed by $B$.
Next regard $D_{\mathcal B}$ as a monic polynomial in $v$ over the field
$K(u)$. Its distinct linear factors are $v-p_B(u)$. Equality of two such
products implies equality of their sets of roots in $K(u)$, so it implies
equality of the sets of block polynomials. Their injectivity recovers the
blocks and hence the partition. This proof also explains why repeated
sextic roots must be excluded at this stage.
\end{proof}

The descriptors therefore solve the information-loss problem before
specialization. The remaining task is to evaluate them at a pair $(u,v)$
without causing two distinct descriptors to become equal. Arbitrary fixed
evaluations need not preserve injectivity. We next expose the unusually
small amount of partition-dependent data in the sextic case.

\section{Compression to quadratics and linears}
\label{sbr:sec:compression}

\subsection{Pair partitions}

For a pair $b=\{i,j\}$, write
$s_b=\alpha_i+\alpha_j$ and $p_b=\alpha_i\alpha_j$. For a matching $P$, set
\begin{align}
 A_P&=\sum_{b\in P}p_b,\notag\\
 B_P&=\sum_{\substack{b,c\in P\\b\ne c}}s_b p_c,\notag\\
 C_P&=\sum_{\{b,c\}\in\binom P2}p_b p_c,
 &h_P(t)&=A_Pt^2+B_Pt-C_P.
 \label{sbr:eq:h}
\end{align}
The sum defining $B_P$ is over ordered pairs of different blocks, so it has
six summands before expanding the $s_b$.

\begin{proposition}[Pair descriptor expansion]
\label{sbr:prop:pair-expansion}
For every matching $P$,
\begin{align}
 D_P(u,v)={}&v^3-(3u^2-e_1u+A_P)v^2\notag\\
 &+\bigl(3u^4-2e_1u^3+(e_2+A_P)u^2-B_Pu+C_P\bigr)v-f(u).
 \label{sbr:eq:pair-descriptor}
\end{align}
Consequently, if $K_2(t)=2t^6+2e_2t^4-f(t)$, then
\begin{equation}
 D_P(t,2t^2)=K_2(t)-2t^2h_P(t).
 \label{sbr:eq:pair-curve}
\end{equation}
The map $P\mapsto h_P(t)$ is injective as a map into $K[t]$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Each block polynomial is $u^2-s_bu+p_b$. Their sum is
$3u^2-e_1u+A_P$. The sum of their pairwise products has leading coefficients
$3,-2e_1$, and its coefficient of $u^2$ is
$2A_P+\sum_{b<c}s_bs_c$. Since the global $e_2$ is
$A_P+\sum_{b<c}s_bs_c$, that coefficient is $e_2+A_P$.
The remaining two coefficients are $-B_P$ and $C_P$. The product of all
three block polynomials is $f(u)$. Expanding the monic cubic in $v$ gives
\cref{sbr:eq:pair-descriptor}; substituting $u=t,v=2t^2$ gives
\cref{sbr:eq:pair-curve}.

If two $h$ polynomials agree, their coefficients give equality of $A,B,C$.
The displayed descriptor formula then gives equal descriptors. Apply
\cref{sbr:lem:complete}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Triple partitions}

For a three-element block $B$, let $e_j(B)$ denote the elementary symmetric
polynomial of degree $j$ in its three roots. For $T\in\Pthree$, define
\begin{equation}
 a_T=\sum_{B\in T}e_2(B),\qquad
 b_T=\sum_{B\in T}e_3(B),\qquad
 k_T(t)=a_Tt-b_T.
 \label{sbr:eq:k}
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Triple descriptor expansion]
\label{sbr:prop:triple-expansion}
For every triple partition $T$,
\begin{equation}
 D_T(u,v)=v^2-(2u^3-e_1u^2+a_Tu-b_T)v+f(u).
 \label{sbr:eq:triple-descriptor}
\end{equation}
On $(u,v)=(t,2t^2)$ this has the form
\begin{equation}
 D_T(t,2t^2)=K_3(t)-2t^2k_T(t),
 \quad K_3(t)=4t^4-4t^5+2e_1t^4+f(t).
 \label{sbr:eq:triple-curve}
\end{equation}
The map $T\mapsto k_T(t)$ into $K[t]$ is injective.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The sum of the two monic cubic block polynomials is
$2u^3-e_1u^2+a_Tu-b_T$, and their product is $f(u)$. Expanding their
quadratic descriptor proves the formula. Equality of $k$ polynomials gives
equality of the two descriptor-dependent coefficients, and then
\cref{sbr:lem:complete} proves injectivity.
\end{proof}

In particular every difference $h_P-h_Q$ for $P\ne Q$ is a nonzero
polynomial of degree at most two; every difference $k_T-k_{T'}$ is nonzero
of degree at most one. A first root-counting bound would therefore allow
$2\binom{15}{2}+\binom{10}{2}=255$ bad parameters. The next two sections
remove repeated collision factors and reduce this to 195.

\begin{remark}[The zero parameter and the source coordinate order]
\label{zpt:rem:sbr-zero}
The compressed families make sense and may separate at $t=0$, but the
original descriptors on the curve all collapse within each family there,
because the multiplier $2t^2$ vanishes. We use positive integer tests when

Thus the required parameter is $x(0)=2t^2$, $x(1)=t$, not the reverse

\end{remark}

\section{The two types of pair-partition collision}
\label{sbr:sec:pair-collisions}

Two different matchings can share at most one block, since two common pairs
force the remaining pair to agree. There are exactly 45 unordered pairs of
matchings sharing a block: choose the common pair in 15 ways, then choose
two of the three matchings on the four remaining indices. The other
$\binom{15}{2}-45=60$ pairs are block-disjoint. Their union is a single
alternating six-cycle, since a cycle of length two would be a common block.

For a root pair $e=\{i,j\}$, define
\begin{equation}
 q_e(t)=t^2+s_et-p_e
       =t^2+(\alpha_i+\alpha_j)t-\alpha_i\alpha_j.
 \label{sbr:eq:q}
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}[Common-block collision factor]
\label{sbr:lem:shared}
If distinct matchings $P,Q$ share the pair $e$, then
\begin{equation}
 h_P(t)-h_Q(t)=(A_P-A_Q)q_e(t),\qquad A_P-A_Q\ne0.
 \label{sbr:eq:shared}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Let the four remaining roots be $a,b,c,d$. Their two-block pairing affects
$A$ through one of
\[
 z_1=ab+cd,\qquad z_2=ac+bd,\qquad z_3=ad+bc.
\]
The products of the two remaining pair products are always $abcd$.
The contribution to $B$ involving only those two blocks is always the
third elementary symmetric function of $a,b,c,d$. Therefore changing the
pairing changes $B$ by $s_e$ times the change in $A$, and changes $C$ by
$p_e$ times the change in $A$. Substitution in \cref{sbr:eq:h} proves the
factorization.

The three differences are
\begin{equation}
 z_1-z_2=(a-d)(b-c),\quad
 z_1-z_3=(a-c)(b-d),\quad
 z_2-z_3=(a-b)(c-d).
 \label{sbr:eq:four-root-differences}
\end{equation}
Distinctness of the roots makes every one nonzero.
\end{proof}

Thus the 45 shared-block comparisons do not contribute 90 unrelated
exceptional parameters: they contribute roots of only fifteen quadratics.
The 60 block-disjoint comparisons each still contribute a quadratic.
There are at most $2(15+60)=150$ pair-family failures. Some of these
quadratics can have lower degree or share roots after specializing the
sextic, so this is a uniform upper bound rather than a claim about every
input.

\section{Triple collisions as exchanges of two roots}
\label{sbr:sec:triple-collisions}

For disjoint root pairs $e,e'$, set
\begin{equation}
 \ell_{e,e'}(t)=(s_e-s_{e'})t-(p_e-p_{e'}).
 \label{sbr:eq:ell}
\end{equation}
Interchanging $e,e'$ changes only the sign. There are
$\binom64\cdot3=45$ unordered choices of two disjoint pairs.

\begin{lemma}[Exchange identity]
\label{sbr:lem:exchange}
For the triple partitions
\[
T=\{\{a,b,c\},\{d,e,f\}\},\qquad
T'=\{\{a,b,d\},\{c,e,f\}\},
\]
where letters now denote root values,
\begin{equation}
 k_T(t)-k_{T'}(t)
   =(c-d)\bigl((a+b-e-f)t-(ab-ef)\bigr).
 \label{sbr:eq:exchange}
\end{equation}
Every unordered pair of distinct triple partitions occurs exactly once in
this manner, up to the irrelevant orientations, from an unordered pair of
disjoint root pairs $\{\{a,b\},\{e,f\}\}$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Subtracting the internal pair sums gives
$a_T-a_{T'}=(c-d)(a+b-e-f)$, while subtracting the two triple products gives
$b_T-b_{T'}=(c-d)(ab-ef)$. This proves the formula.

For the combinatorial assertion, two distinct partitions can be oriented so
that one block from each has two elements in common. Call these $a,b$;
call the exchanged elements $c,d$; the other two elements are $e,f$.
Conversely, two disjoint pairs and their two complementary elements specify
exactly the two partitions in the display. Simultaneously switching the
chosen blocks or reversing the exchange does not change the unordered data.
This establishes the bijection, with 45 elements on either side.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[No identically vanishing exchange factor]
\label{sbr:lem:ell-nonzero}
For disjoint pairs of distinct roots, $\ell_{e,e'}$ is not the zero
polynomial.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
If it vanished identically, the two pair sums and the two pair products
would agree. The two monic quadratics $X^2-s_eX+p_e$ and
$X^2-s_{e'}X+p_{e'}$ would agree, contradicting their disjoint root sets.
\end{proof}

The triple-family failure set is therefore the union of the roots of 45
nonzero linears, and has cardinality at most 45. A nonzero constant factor
simply contributes no root.

\section{Three invariant obstruction polynomials}
\label{sbr:sec:obstruction}

Fix total orders on the matchings and on the root pairs, solely to orient
differences in products. Define
\begin{align}
 Q_f(t)&=\prod_{e\in\Edges}q_e(t),\label{sbr:eq:Q}\\
 U_f(t)&=\prod_{\substack{P<Q\\P\cap Q=\varnothing}}(h_P(t)-h_Q(t)),
 \label{sbr:eq:U}\\
 L_f(t)&=\prod_{\substack{e<e'\\e\cap e'=\varnothing}}
             \ell_{e,e'}(t),\label{sbr:eq:L}\\
 E_f(t)&=Q_f(t)U_f(t)L_f(t).\label{sbr:eq:E}
\end{align}
Here $P\cap Q=\varnothing$ refers to their block collections. Reversing a
chosen orientation can multiply $U_f$ or $L_f$ by $-1$, which changes
neither the zero set nor any squared identity below.

It is not automatic that an oriented product of differences is symmetric;
the ordinary Vandermonde product is the familiar warning. The following
simple sign argument proves what is needed here.

\begin{lemma}[Graph-product sign test]
\label{sbr:lem:graph-sign}
Let a finite group act on a finite graph's vertices, permuting quantities
$w_v$. Form an oriented product $\prod_{\{v,w\}\in E}(w_v-w_w)$, with one
orientation for each edge. For a group element $\tau$ of order two, its
sign on this product is $(-1)^r$, where $r$ is the number of edges whose
endpoints are interchanged by $\tau$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Edges exchanged with other edges occur in two-element orbits. The two
orientation signs in each such orbit multiply to $+1$, since applying
$\tau$ twice fixes each oriented difference. A fixed edge contributes $+1$
if its endpoints are individually fixed, and $-1$ if they are interchanged.
Multiplying these contributions gives the claim.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Symmetry, integrality, and exact failure test]
\label{sbr:prop:obstruction}
The expressions $Q_f,U_f,L_f$ are symmetric polynomials with integer
coefficients in the six roots and $t$. They are nonzero for every distinct
root tuple and satisfy
\begin{equation}
 \deg Q_f=30,\qquad \deg U_f\le120,\qquad \deg L_f\le45.
 \label{sbr:eq:degree-bounds}
\end{equation}
Their coefficients are universal integer polynomials in $e_1,\ldots,e_6$.
For every $t\in K$,
\begin{equation}
 E_f(t)\ne0
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \bigl(P\mapsto h_P(t)\bigr)\text{ and }
 \bigl(T\mapsto k_T(t)\bigr)\text{ are both injective}.
 \label{sbr:eq:exact-test}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The product $Q_f$ is visibly symmetric. For $U_f$, use the graph with
matchings as vertices and block-disjoint pairs as edges. A transposition
of two root labels sends any matching either to itself or to a matching
sharing its untouched third block. Hence it interchanges the endpoints of
no edge in this graph. By \cref{sbr:lem:graph-sign}, it fixes $U_f$. Root
transpositions generate $S_6$, so $U_f$ is symmetric.

For $L_f$ use the graph on two-element subsets, joined when they are
disjoint, with vertex quantity $s_et-p_e$. A root transposition sends a
pair either to itself or to a pair sharing one root. It therefore reverses
no disjointness edge, so the same argument proves symmetry of $L_f$.
The fundamental theorem of symmetric polynomials over $\ZZ[t]$ now yields
the integer coefficient expressions in the $e_i$.

Every factor of $Q_f$ is monic quadratic. Every factor of $U_f$ is nonzero
by \cref{sbr:prop:pair-expansion}, and every factor of $L_f$ is nonzero by
\cref{sbr:lem:ell-nonzero}. This proves nonvanishing and the degree bounds.
Finally, \cref{sbr:lem:shared} accounts for all shared-block collisions,
\cref{sbr:eq:U} accounts for all block-disjoint ones, and
\cref{sbr:lem:exchange} accounts for all triple collisions. Their scalar
root-difference factors are nonzero. This proves \cref{sbr:eq:exact-test}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Homogeneity and scaling]
Assign weight one to every root and to $t$. Then $h_P,k_T,q_e,\ell_{e,e'}$
have respective total degrees $4,3,2,2$. Consequently $Q_f,U_f,L_f,E_f$
have total degrees $30,240,90,360$. Scaling all roots and $t$ by $c$
multiplies $E_f(t)$ by $c^{360}$. These are homogeneous degrees in the
roots and parameter together, not the parameter degrees in
\cref{sbr:eq:degree-bounds}.
\end{remark}

\section{Exact discriminant factorizations}
\label{sbr:sec:discriminants}

Define the compressed resolvents
\begin{equation}
 \Rtwo(Z,t)=\prod_{P\in\Ptwo}(Z-h_P(t)),\qquad
 \Rthree(Z,t)=\prod_{T\in\Pthree}(Z-k_T(t)).
 \label{sbr:eq:resolvents}
\end{equation}
Their coefficients are symmetric integer polynomials in the roots, so they
are coefficient-only constructions just as $E_f$ is. Let
\begin{equation}
 \Delta_f=\disc(f)=\prod_{1\le i<j\le6}(\alpha_i-\alpha_j)^2.
\end{equation}
For a monic polynomial with roots $z_1,\ldots,z_m$, we use the convention
$\disc=\prod_{i<j}(z_i-z_j)^2$.

\begin{theorem}[Discriminant identities]
\label{sbr:thm:discriminants}
As polynomial identities in the roots and $t$,
\begin{align}
 \disc_Z\Rtwo(Z,t)&=\Delta_f^6Q_f(t)^6U_f(t)^2,
 \label{sbr:eq:disc-pair}\\
 \disc_Z\Rthree(Z,t)&=\Delta_f^3L_f(t)^2.
 \label{sbr:eq:disc-triple}
\end{align}
In particular the formulas remain valid at nonseparating parameter values.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The pair-resolvent discriminant is the product of all 105 squared matching
differences. The 60 block-disjoint differences give $U_f^2$ directly.
Fix a common pair $e$. The three unordered comparisons between matchings
containing $e$ give, by \cref{sbr:lem:shared},
\[
 q_e(t)^6\prod_{1\le i<j\le3}(z_i-z_j)^2.
\]
By \cref{sbr:eq:four-root-differences}, the second factor is exactly the
discriminant of the monic quartic whose roots are the other four roots.
Multiplying over the fifteen choices of $e$ gives $Q_f^6$ times the
product of these fifteen quartic discriminants. A fixed root difference
$(\alpha_i-\alpha_j)^2$ occurs in precisely six of those quartics: choose
the excluded pair among the four other roots. Their product is therefore
$\Delta_f^6$, proving \cref{sbr:eq:disc-pair}.

For triples, \cref{sbr:lem:exchange} gives one squared linear factor for each
unordered pair of disjoint root pairs, producing $L_f^2$. The two leftover
roots are the exchanged roots. For a fixed exchanged pair there are three
pairings of the other four roots, so its squared difference appears three
times. The product of the root-difference factors is $\Delta_f^3$.
This proves \cref{sbr:eq:disc-triple}. No division was used, so these are
polynomial identities, not merely identities on the open set of good
parameters.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Discriminant square classes]
\label{sbr:cor:squareclass}
Over any characteristic-zero coefficient field, the pair-resolvent
discriminant is a square. If both $f$ and the triple resolvent are
separable, the triple-resolvent discriminant has the same square class
as $\Delta_f$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The two formulas read
\[
 \disc_Z\Rtwo=(\Delta_f^3Q_f^3U_f)^2,
 \qquad
 \disc_Z\Rthree=\Delta_f(\Delta_f L_f)^2.
\]
In the second assertion all displayed factors are nonzero.
\end{proof}

There is a useful independent permutation explanation. A transposition of
root labels fixes three of the fifteen matchings and interchanges the
other twelve in six pairs; it acts evenly. It fixes four triple partitions
and interchanges the other six in three pairs; it acts oddly. Thus the
fifteen-point action has trivial sign character and the ten-point action
has the ordinary six-point sign character. The explicit factorizations
identify much more than this parity information: they also isolate every
collision factor and its multiplicity.

\section{Generic irreducibility of the three components}
\label{sbr:sec:irreducibility}

The degree bound is not merely the result of an inefficient factor count.
Generically all its factors are needed. In this section take
$x_1,\ldots,x_6,t$ to be algebraically independent over $\QQ$ and put
$S=\QQ[x_1,\ldots,x_6,t]$. Its symmetric subring is
\[
 S^{S_6}=\QQ[e_1,\ldots,e_6,t].
\]
The symbols $Q,U,L$ now denote the universal polynomials in this ring,
prior to any specialization of the sextic coefficients.

\subsection{Irreducibility of the individual root-coordinate factors}

\begin{lemma}[Primitive linear-polynomial test]
\label{sbr:lem:primitive}
Let $R$ be a unique factorization domain. If $A,B\in R$ are coprime and
$A\ne0$, then $Ay+B$ is irreducible in $R[y]$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The polynomial is primitive, and is linear, hence irreducible, over the
fraction field of $R$. Gauss's lemma gives irreducibility over $R$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[The three kinds of factor are irreducible]
\label{sbr:lem:individual-irred}
In $S$, each $q_e$, each $\ell_{e,e'}$ for disjoint pairs, and each
$h_P-h_Q$ for block-disjoint matchings is irreducible.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For $q_e$, rename its two roots $a,b$ and write
\[
 q_e=a(t-b)+t(t+b).
\]
The coefficients as a linear polynomial in $a$ are coprime: substituting
$t=b$ in the second gives $2b^2\ne0$. Apply \cref{sbr:lem:primitive}.
For a linear exchange factor, rename its disjoint pairs $\{a,b\}$ and
$\{c,d\}$. Then
\[
 \ell=a(t-b)+(b-c-d)t+cd.
\]
Substituting $t=b$ in its constant coefficient gives
$(b-c)(b-d)\ne0$, so the same test applies. Adjoining unused independent
variables preserves these irreducibilities.

For the third type, all block-disjoint matching pairs are related by a
root permutation, because their union is an alternating six-cycle. It
suffices to consider
\[
 P=(ab)(cd)(ef),\qquad Q=(ac)(be)(df).
\]
Write $h_P-h_Q=aA+B$, where $A,B\in\QQ[b,c,d,e,f,t]$. This is linear in
$a$ because every monomial in \cref{sbr:eq:h} uses each root at most once.
It is homogeneous of total degree four; hence $A$ is homogeneous of
degree three and $B$ of degree four. At $t=0$, direct expansion gives
\begin{align}
 A_0&=-bcd+bce-bef+cdf,\notag\\
 B_0&=def(b-c).
 \label{sbr:eq:primitive-specialization}
\end{align}
The polynomial $A_0$ is divisible by none of $d,e,f,b-c$. Indeed its
respective substitutions at zero or at $b=c$ are
\[
 be(c-f),\quad cd(f-b),\quad bc(e-d),\quad c(e-d)(c-f),
\]
all nonzero. Therefore $\gcd(A_0,B_0)=1$.

Suppose an irreducible nonconstant polynomial $H$ divided both $A$ and
$B$. A factor of a homogeneous polynomial can be chosen homogeneous, so
$H$ is homogeneous. If $H|_{t=0}$ is nonzero, it has the same positive
total degree and divides both $A_0$ and $B_0$, a contradiction. If it is
zero, then $t$ divides $H$, so irreducibility makes $H$ associated to $t$;
but $A_0\ne0$. Thus $A,B$ are coprime. Apply
\cref{sbr:lem:primitive} with variable $a$ to finish the proof.
\end{proof}

The homogeneity in the last argument is essential: without it, coprime
specializations need not imply coprimality of the original polynomials.
Here it prevents a nonconstant common homogeneous factor from specializing
to a nonzero constant.

\subsection{Descent from root coordinates to coefficients}

\begin{lemma}[One-orbit product lemma]
\label{sbr:lem:orbit-product}
Let a finite group act on a polynomial ring over a field. Suppose a finite
collection of pairwise nonassociate irreducible polynomials forms a single
orbit up to units, and its product $F$ is invariant. Then $F$ is
irreducible in the invariant subring.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
A factorization $F=AB$ in the invariant subring is also a factorization in
the ambient unique factorization domain. The irreducible factors of $A$
therefore form a subset of the factors of $F$. Invariance makes this subset
stable under the group. Transitivity forces it to be empty or the whole
set. In the first case $A$ is a unit, and in the second $B$ is a unit.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Generic three-factor theorem]
\label{sbr:thm:generic}
The universal polynomials $Q,U,L$ are pairwise nonassociate irreducible
polynomials in $\QQ[e_1,\ldots,e_6,t]$, and are irreducible in
$\QQ(e_1,\ldots,e_6)[t]$. Their degrees in $t$ are respectively
$30,120,45$. Their product $E$ is squarefree over the generic coefficient
field and has degree 195 and nonzero constant coefficient.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The individual factors are irreducible by \cref{sbr:lem:individual-irred}.
The symmetric group is transitive on root pairs, on unordered pairs of
disjoint root pairs, and on unordered pairs of block-disjoint matchings.
In each collection the individual factors are pairwise nonassociate.
For $q_e$, this follows from its monic leading term and its two root
indices. For $\ell_{e,e'}$, the coefficient of $t$ specifies the two
pairs with opposite signs, up to their interchange. For $h_P-h_Q$, the
coefficient of $t^2$ is $A_P-A_Q$: its six distinct root-product monomials,
with coefficients $+1$ or $-1$, specify the two block-disjoint matchings
up to interchange. Thus no further associations occur.

Each product is invariant by \cref{sbr:prop:obstruction}. The one-orbit
product lemma proves irreducibility in the symmetric subring. The leading
coefficient in $t$ of each individual $q$ is one, of each individual $h$
difference is a nonzero $A_P-A_Q$, and of each individual $\ell$ is a
nonzero difference of pair sums. The ambient ring is a domain, so the
three parameter degrees are exactly $30,120,45$. They are consequently
pairwise nonassociate. Their irreducibility and positive parameter degree
also imply that they are primitive over $\QQ[e_1,\ldots,e_6]$.
Gauss's lemma proves irreducibility over its fraction field.

In characteristic zero every irreducible polynomial of positive degree is
separable. The three are pairwise coprime there, so $E$ is squarefree and
has degree 195. Its constant coefficient is nonzero: every $q_e(0)$ and
$\ell_{e,e'}(0)$ is a nonzero polynomial, and every block-disjoint $h$
difference at zero is nonzero by the representative calculation
\cref{sbr:eq:primitive-specialization} and root permutation.
\end{proof}

This theorem is a statement about the generic coefficient field, not about
every numerical sextic. After specialization a factor can become
reducible, lose degree, or share roots with another factor. None of these
possibilities threatens the uniform separator theorem; they only reduce
the number of bad parameters. On the open coefficient locus
$\Delta_f\ne0$, the three polynomials describe the three irreducible
families of bad specializations over $\QQ$. The constructive factor proofs
also work after extending the constant field to any characteristic-zero
field.

\section{The finite separator theorem and its sharpness}
\label{sbr:sec:bound}

\begin{theorem}[Uniform simultaneous separation]
\label{sbr:thm:196}
For any six distinct roots in a characteristic-zero field, at most 195
parameters cause a collision in either compressed family. Therefore any
196 distinct nonzero test parameters contain a simultaneous separator.
The integers $1,\ldots,196$ always contain one, and this same parameter
separates both original descriptors on $(u,v)=(t,2t^2)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
By \cref{sbr:prop:obstruction}, the bad parameters are exactly the roots of the
nonzero polynomial $E_f$, which has degree at most $30+120+45=195$.
A nonzero polynomial over a field has at most its degree many distinct
roots. Characteristic zero makes the positive integers $1,\ldots,196$
distinct and nonzero. Finally use \cref{sbr:eq:pair-curve,sbr:eq:triple-curve},
whose common multiplier is nonzero at these parameters.
\end{proof}

The same proof gives 151 test values for the pair family alone and 46 for
the triple family alone. A common parameter is preferable when a single
uniform interface is wanted; separate searches can stop sooner on some
inputs. There is no requirement that the least good pair parameter agree
with the least good triple parameter.

\begin{theorem}[Sharpness for arbitrary test sets]
\label{sbr:thm:sharp}
The constant 196 in the assertion about \emph{every} set of distinct
nonzero complex test parameters cannot be lowered. There are separable
sextics with exactly 195 distinct nonzero bad parameters. Such a sextic
can even be chosen split with integer roots.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Over the generic coefficient field, \cref{sbr:thm:generic} gives a degree-195
squarefree polynomial with nonzero constant coefficient. Thus its leading
coefficient, constant coefficient, and discriminant in $t$ are all nonzero.
Multiply these coefficient polynomials by $\Delta_f$ and substitute the
elementary symmetric functions of six independent root variables. The
result is still a nonzero polynomial in those variables, because the
symmetric-polynomial substitution is injective.

The integer lattice is Zariski dense in affine six-space: a nonzero
polynomial over $\QQ$ cannot vanish on all integer tuples, as follows by
induction on the number of variables and the one-variable root bound.
Choose an integer tuple where the displayed product is nonzero. Its roots
are distinct, and its $E_f$ has degree 195, no zero root, and no repeated
root. Over $\CC$ it has exactly 195 distinct nonzero roots. These are all
bad by \cref{sbr:eq:exact-test}; taking them as the test set disproves any
195-point guarantee of the stated kind.
\end{proof}

\section{Why the separated resolvents decide an irreducible sextic}
\label{sbr:sec:galois}

The group-theoretic criterion in this section is classical. We include its
proof to show exactly what the new separator supplies and what it does not.
It is the criterion underlying the degree-fifteen and degree-ten sextic
resolvents in the literature and the repository
\cite{boswell-glasser,repo-search}. Let $f\in\QQ[X]$ now be irreducible of
degree six, let $M$ be its splitting field, and view
$G=\Gal(M/\QQ)$ as a transitive subgroup of $S_6$.

\begin{lemma}[Solvable primitive groups have prime-power degree]
\label{sbr:lem:primitive-group}
A finite solvable group acting faithfully and primitively on a set with
more than one element has degree $p^r$ for some prime $p$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Choose a minimal nontrivial normal subgroup $N$ of $G$. Because $G$ is
solvable, $N$ is elementary abelian. Here is the standard short justification:
a last nontrivial term of the derived series of $N$ is an abelian
characteristic subgroup of $N$, hence normal in $G$, so minimality makes
$N$ abelian. Its primary components are characteristic, so only one prime
$p$ occurs. The characteristic subgroup of elements killed by $p$ is
nontrivial and hence all of $N$.

The orbits of a normal subgroup form a $G$-invariant partition. Primitivity
and faithfulness force $N$ to be transitive: otherwise its orbits are
singletons, making $N$ act trivially. An abelian transitive permutation
group is regular. Indeed, an element fixing one point fixes every point
by commuting with all elements that carry it to another point. Faithfulness
then makes the stabilizer trivial. Thus the degree is $|N|=p^r$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[The two block systems are complete]
\label{sbr:prop:blocks-galois}
For a transitive subgroup $G\le S_6$, the following are equivalent:
$G$ is solvable; $G$ is imprimitive; $G$ preserves a partition in $\Ptwo$
or in $\Pthree$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
A solvable primitive group cannot have degree six by
\cref{sbr:lem:primitive-group}. Thus solvability implies imprimitivity.
For a transitive action, the blocks in a nontrivial block system have a
common size properly dividing six, so that size is two or three.
This proves the next implication.

The full stabilizer of a pair partition is $S_2\wr S_3$, an extension of
$S_2^3$ by $S_3$. The full stabilizer of a triple partition is
$S_3\wr S_2$, an extension of $S_3^2$ by $S_2$. Both are solvable,
since finite direct products and extensions of solvable groups are
solvable. Any subgroup preserving the corresponding partition is a
subgroup of its stabilizer and is therefore solvable.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Separated rational-root criterion]
\label{sbr:thm:criterion}
Let $f\in\QQ[X]$ be an irreducible monic sextic, and let $t\in\QQ$ be a
simultaneous separator. Then $f$ is solvable by radicals if and only if
at least one of $\Rtwo(Z,t)$ and $\Rthree(Z,t)$ has a rational root.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For a matching $P$ and $\sigma\in G$, coefficient equivariance gives
$\sigma(h_P(t))=h_{\sigma P}(t)$, since $t$ is rational. If $P$ is fixed
by $G$, then $h_P(t)$ is fixed by $G$ and lies in $\QQ$. Conversely,
if $h_P(t)$ is rational, then
$h_{\sigma P}(t)=h_P(t)$ for every $\sigma$, and separation gives
$\sigma P=P$. A rational root of the pair resolvent is one of its
explicit values, so it is equivalent to a $G$-fixed pair partition.
The same proof applies to $k_T$ and the triple resolvent.
Now use \cref{sbr:prop:blocks-galois} and the classical equivalence between
solvability by radicals and solvability of the Galois group.
\end{proof}

Separation is not a cosmetic condition in the converse. Without it,
$h_{\sigma P}(t)=h_P(t)$ would not imply $\sigma P=P$. A specialized value
could be fixed while its underlying partition moves among colliding
partitions. This is precisely the logical failure prevented by the
nonvanishing certificate $E_f(t)\ne0$.

\section{A bounded coefficient-only decision architecture}
\label{sbr:sec:algorithm}

\subsection{Computing the invariant coefficients without roots}

A root-coordinate definition can hide a computational oracle unless the
coefficient bridge is made explicit. Here the bridge is the constructive
fundamental theorem of symmetric polynomials. For each fixed power of $Z$
in \cref{sbr:eq:resolvents}, its coefficient is a fixed symmetric polynomial
in six roots with coefficients in $\ZZ[t]$. It can be reduced to a fixed
polynomial in $e_1,\ldots,e_6,t$ using integer arithmetic only.

For completeness, the reduction is finite in a particularly elementary
sense. Order root monomials lexicographically with
$x_1>\cdots>x_6$. The leading exponent vector of a symmetric polynomial
has the form $\lambda_1\ge\cdots\ge\lambda_6$. The product
\begin{equation}
 e_1^{\lambda_1-\lambda_2}e_2^{\lambda_2-\lambda_3}
 \cdots e_5^{\lambda_5-\lambda_6}e_6^{\lambda_6}
 \label{sbr:eq:symmetric-reduction}
\end{equation}
has exactly that leading root monomial, with coefficient one. Subtract its
multiple by the leading coefficient, which is a polynomial in $t$.
The leading monomial strictly decreases and the total root degree never
increases. There are only finitely many root monomials within the original
degree bound, so the process terminates with an expression over
$\ZZ[e_1,\ldots,e_6,t]$. This proof gives a finite symbolic construction,
not a choice of an unspecified algebraic root.

For the coefficient of $Z^{15-j}$ in $\Rtwo$, the root degree is at most
$4j$ and parameter degree at most $2j$. For the coefficient of $Z^{10-j}$
in $\Rthree$, they are at most $3j$ and $j$. Hence degrees at most 60 and
30 in the root variables suffice for the two complete coefficient tables.
These are fixed bounds, independent of the input height. The analogous
bound for $E$ is 360 in the root variables and 195 in $t$.

The article does not distribute expanded versions of these potentially
large universal tables. Their construction and their evaluation are
mathematically specified by the fixed finite products and the reduction
above. The supplied executable code is instead a verifier on exact root
tuples. This distinction is important when assessing what has and has not
been implemented.

\subsection{Monicization and bounded factor search}

Let the input be an integer polynomial
$g(X)=a_6X^6+\cdots+a_0$, with $a_6\ne0$. Define
\begin{equation}
 f(Y)=a_6^5g(Y/a_6)
     =Y^6+\sum_{i=0}^5 a_i a_6^{5-i}Y^i.
 \label{sbr:eq:monicization}
\end{equation}
This is monic integral, and its roots are $a_6$ times those of $g$.
Multiplication and division by the nonzero rational $a_6$ preserve the
radical closure of $\QQ$, so this transformation preserves all-root
radical solvability. The repository already provides this semantic bridge
\cite{repo-semantics}.

Write $H$ for the maximum absolute value of the six lower coefficients of
$f$, and let $R=1+H$. Every complex root of $f$ has absolute value at most
$R$. For example, if $|z|>1+H$, the geometric sum estimate gives
\[
 H\sum_{i=0}^5|z|^i<|z|^6,
\]
so the leading term cannot be canceled by the others.
If a monic degree-$d$ integer polynomial divides $f$, its roots are a
submultiset of these roots, and the coefficient at distance $j$ below its
leading term has absolute value at most
\begin{equation}
 \binom dj R^j.\label{sbr:eq:factor-bound}
\end{equation}
Gauss's lemma implies that rational reducibility of a monic integer
polynomial has a monic integer factor. Any proper factorization of a
sextic has a factor of degree at most three. Thus enumeration of the
finite coefficient boxes in \cref{sbr:eq:factor-bound}, for $d=1,2,3$,
completely decides reducibility.

A degree-two or degree-three factor leaves a quotient of degree at most
four; then every root is radical. A degree-one factor leaves a quintic,
whose radical solvability must actually be tested. In particular, one
must not accept every reducible sextic: a nonsolvable quintic multiplied
by a linear factor is the basic counterexample. The existing
primitive-recursive quintic decision can be reused
\cite{repo,repo-reducible}. Repeated factors are handled by these reducible
branches; the irreducible branch is automatically separable in
characteristic zero.

\subsection{The algorithm}

The following is a mathematical specification of a finite coefficient
algorithm. A default return on malformed input makes it a total Boolean
on seven encoded integers; the target predicate includes exact degree six.

\begin{resultbox}
\textbf{Bounded sextic radical criterion.}

\textbf{1.} Reject if $a_6=0$. Otherwise form the monic integer polynomial
$f$ in \cref{sbr:eq:monicization} and the root bound $R$.

\textbf{2.} Search the explicit boxes for a monic factor of degree two or
three. If one exists, accept.

\textbf{3.} Search the explicit interval for a linear factor. If one
exists, choose the first in a fixed order, form its quintic quotient,
and return the existing primitive-recursive quintic criterion.

\textbf{4.} The remaining input is irreducible. For $t=1,\ldots,196$,
evaluate the universal coefficient polynomials of the two compressed
resolvents. Choose the first $t$ for which both are squarefree, or,
equivalently, for which $E_f(t)\ne0$.

\textbf{5.} Accept exactly when one of the two resulting monic integer
resolvents has an integer root. For each resolvent, test all integers in
its Cauchy interval $[-(1+H_R),1+H_R]$, where $H_R$ is the maximum absolute
value of its lower coefficients.
\end{resultbox}

Squarefreeness in step 4 can be tested by a rational polynomial gcd with
the derivative, by a fixed-size resultant determinant, or by the
obstruction value itself. For an especially direct primitive-recursive
implementation, the fixed-size determinant avoids any concern about a
hidden unbounded gcd loop. The resolvent degrees are always fifteen and
ten.

\begin{theorem}[Bounded architecture and correctness]
\label{sbr:thm:primrec}
The coefficient procedure above has a primitive-recursive Boolean
realization and accepts exactly the integer inputs of degree six whose
complex roots are all expressible by radicals over $\QQ$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Monicization is integer arithmetic with fixed exponents. The factor boxes
have primitive-recursive bounds, and polynomial division for their fixed
degrees is a finite coefficient computation. The correctness of their
branches follows from \cref{sbr:eq:factor-bound} and the degree arguments
above. The quintic subroutine is the established primitive-recursive
criterion, not an unbounded search for radical expressions.

On the irreducible branch, \cref{sbr:thm:196} guarantees that step 4 finds a
parameter. Each coefficient is the evaluation of a fixed integer
polynomial. Evaluation, fixed-size determinants, a loop of length 196,
and bounded selection are primitive recursive under the usual effective
encodings of integers and finite tuples. Step 5 is a bounded integer
search, and is complete because the resolvents are monic and the Cauchy
bound includes all their integer roots. Its correctness is
\cref{sbr:thm:criterion}. Composing these primitive-recursive operations proves
the claim.
\end{proof}

This theorem concerns a mathematical realization. It does not assert that
the existing Lean function has already been changed, that a \src{Primrec}
proof has been accepted by the kernel, or that the attached verifier is
this coefficient solver. A source-level integration must additionally
supply the coefficient-evaluation lemmas and bounded-selector code
discussed in \cref{sbr:sec:integration}.

Nor does the naive factor and integer-root enumeration above claim
polynomial bit complexity. Its bounds depend polynomially on coefficient
\emph{magnitudes}, which can still give exponential running time in their
bit lengths. The stronger general polynomial-time theorem is classical
\cite{landau-miller}; our objective is a small, transparent invariant
search with a tractable verification boundary.

\section{A separator determined by coefficient height alone}
\label{sbr:sec:height}

The finite search has only 196 candidates, but it is also possible to give
one coefficient-dependent parameter that is guaranteed to work without
any separation test. It is useful as a termination certificate or a
comparison construction, not as a recommendation for practical evaluation.

For a polynomial $P(t)=\sum c_it^i$ over $\CC$, write
$\|P\|_1=\sum|c_i|$. This norm is submultiplicative. Let $f\in\ZZ[X]$ be
monic and separable, put $R=1+H$ as above, and use $|\alpha_i|\le R$.
Then
\begin{align}
 \|q_e\|_1&\le1+2R+R^2\le4R^2,\notag\\
 \|h_P-h_Q\|_1&\le6R^2+24R^3+6R^4\le36R^4,\notag\\
 \|\ell_{e,e'}\|_1&\le4R+2R^2\le6R^2.
 \label{sbr:eq:norms}
\end{align}
For the middle estimate, $|A_P|\le3R^2$, $|B_P|\le12R^3$, and
$|C_P|\le3R^4$; subtracting two such quantities doubles each bound.
Multiplying the bounds for fifteen, sixty, and forty-five factors gives
\begin{equation}
 \|E_f\|_1\le4^{15}6^{165}R^{360}=:M(H).
 \label{sbr:eq:height-bound}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[A search-free height separator]
\label{sbr:thm:height}
The positive integer
\begin{equation}
 t_\ast=4^{15}6^{165}(1+H)^{360}+2
 \label{sbr:eq:height-separator}
\end{equation}
separates both compressed families and both original descriptors on the
curve.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The nonzero polynomial $E_f$ has integer coefficients, so its leading
coefficient has absolute value at least one. Its Cauchy root bound is at
most $1+M(H)$ by \cref{sbr:eq:height-bound}. Hence $E_f(t_\ast)\ne0$.
Apply \cref{sbr:eq:exact-test} and the nonzero-multiplier identities.
\end{proof}

Although $t_\ast$ is numerically enormous, its bit length is $O(1+\log(1+H))$
with a large absolute constant. Its role is to show that even separator
selection itself can be replaced by an explicit arithmetic expression.
The first successful candidate in $1,\ldots,196$ will generally be much
smaller and avoid unnecessary coefficient growth.

\section{Exact checks, certificates, and their limits}
\label{sbr:sec:checks}

The attached computations are designed to test local algebra, combinatorial
counts, discriminant multiplicities, symmetry signs, and explicit examples.
They are not used as evidence that an unproved general statement should be

\section{A reusable general finite-separation principle}
\label{sbr:sec:general}

The sextic construction is special in its low degrees and factorization,
but the idea of converting polynomial descriptors to a finite hitting set
has an elementary general form. The following statement explains why an
existence proof by avoiding finitely many polynomial equalities often
admits an explicit bounded counterpart.

\begin{proposition}[Kronecker-curve separation]
\label{sbr:prop:kronecker}
Let $F_1(u,v),\ldots,F_m(u,v)$ be distinct polynomials over a
characteristic-zero field, each having $u$-degree at most $a$ and
$v$-degree at most $b$. Put
\[
 G_i(t)=F_i(t,t^{a+1}),\qquad D=a+b(a+1).
\]
Then the $G_i$ are distinct, have degree at most $D$, and among any
$D\binom m2+1$ distinct parameter values there is one at which all their
values are different.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
A monomial $u^iv^j$ becomes $t^{i+(a+1)j}$. For $0\le i\le a$, the
exponent uniquely determines $i,j$ by division with remainder by $a+1$.
Thus the substitution is injective on the stated polynomial space and
preserves nonzero differences. The product of the $\binom m2$ differences
is nonzero of degree at most $D\binom m2$, and the root bound applies.
\end{proof}

This is a standard monomial-encoding argument, not a novelty claim. Its
contrast with the sextic result is instructive. A general curve must
preserve all descriptor coefficients. Here we first subtract the
partition-independent part, use the coefficient relations to compress
the family, and then recognize common collision factors. The result is a
quadratic curve, a low-degree obstruction, and an exact generic factor
count rather than a large undifferentiated degree estimate.

For other resolvent actions the promising order of work is therefore:
identify a complete descriptor; isolate its varying coefficients; find a
low-degree curve faithful to those coefficients; organize collision
pairs by group orbits; and only then use a root bound. The graph-product
sign test and the one-orbit product lemma can be reused independently of
the sextic setting.

\section{Further research questions and priorities}
\label{sbr:sec:questions}

The results leave several concrete problems. They fall into three groups:
sharp arithmetic separation, the geometry of the obstruction, and
certified implementation. The distinction between the solved arbitrary-set
bound and the unresolved integer-interval bound should be retained in
all follow-up work.

\begin{question}[The optimal initial integer interval]
Determine $N_{\mathrm{int}}$, currently bracketed by $8\le
N_{\mathrm{int}}\le196$ in this report. A proof that eight suffices would
require new control over simultaneous integer roots of the three factors,
not just an improvement in generic complex root counting. Conversely,
construct a distinct root tuple for which $1,\ldots,8$ all fail.
\end{question}
A practical first experiment is an exact search over larger integer and
rational root sets, recording which factor accounts for each failed
parameter. The combinatorial pattern of overlaps may be more informative
than the maximum alone. Any proposed universal improvement should be
proved independently of a finite sample.

\begin{question}[Short fixed test sets not forming an interval]
What is the smallest cardinality of a fixed set
$S\subset\ZZ_{>0}$ that separates every distinct complex root tuple?
The sharpness theorem for arbitrary input-dependent complex test sets
does not answer this question. Can a sparse integer set outperform an
initial interval substantially?
\end{question}

\begin{question}[Irreducible rational inputs]
\label{tpo:q:sbr-irreducible}
Do the best uniform bounds decrease when $f$ is required to be
irreducible over $\QQ$? For a prescribed positive integer $t_0$, can one
construct an irreducible rational sextic with $E_f(t_0)=0$? The split
counterexamples in this article deliberately do not settle this.
\end{question}
A route through rational points on the three collision hypersurfaces
would have to preserve irreducibility of the sextic, rather than merely
produce a generic point in root coordinates.

\begin{writenote}[Answered for the decision in Part~II, 29 September 2026 (batch~57)]
For deciding radical solvability, as opposed to separating, irreducible
inputs need no uniform range at all: the four fixed tests $u=1$ and
$t=1,2,3$ suffice (\cref{tpo:thm:main}), over any characteristic-zero base
field. Whether the uniform separation bounds decrease under irreducibility,
and whether $E_f(t_0)=0$ is possible for an irreducible rational sextic,
remain open. Part~II's \cref{tpo:thm:triple,tpo:lem:disjoint} give a partial
constraint, noted at the end of \cref{tpo:sec:nonclaims}: for a nonsolvable
irreducible sextic, $E_f(t_0)=0$ at a base-field $t_0$ forces
$U_f(t_0)=0$.
\end{writenote}

\begin{writenote}[Added 30 September 2026, batch~70]
Part~III reduces the four fixed tests to two: one triple test and the
compressed matching test at $t=0$ (\cref{zpt:thm:main}). The open parts
named above are not affected.
\end{writenote}

\begin{question}[Alternative low-degree curves]
Can a different scalar compression or a different curve in descriptor
space lower the maximum number of exceptional parameters below 195?
The proof of sharpness applies to the particular $h,k$ parameterization,
not to every possible separating invariant.
\end{question}
The common-block factorization suggests looking for a curve that collapses
more repeated comparisons without making an entire family identical.
Any such proposal must still distinguish all partitions for every distinct
root tuple, including special symmetric configurations.

\begin{question}[Geometry of the three components]
Describe the intersections, singularities, and branching of the three
irreducible hypersurfaces $Q=0$, $U=0$, and $L=0$ over the separable-sextic
coefficient locus. Which specializations cause degree loss, repeated
parameter roots, or intersections between the components?
\end{question}
The generic irreducibility theorem reduces this from an unspecified
collision locus to three explicit orbit products. Their normalizations
and projection monodromy are natural next invariants.

\begin{question}[Galois groups of the obstruction factors]
Determine the Galois groups over $\QQ(e_1,\ldots,e_6)$ of the degree-30,
degree-120, and degree-45 parameter polynomials. Their factorizations into
quadratics and linears over the generic sextic splitting field provide
strong constraints, but do not by themselves determine the full groups.
\end{question}

\begin{question}[Compact coefficient circuits]
\label{tpo:q:sbr-circuits}
Construct certified straight-line programs for the two resolvents or for
$E_f(t)$ that avoid expanding large symmetric-polynomial tables. Compare
Newton identities, traces in finite algebras, and modular reconstruction,
with exact operation counts and coefficient-height estimates.
\end{question}
A useful first target is not the entire final solver but an independently
checkable evaluator for the obstruction at a supplied integer parameter.

\begin{writenote}[Partly addressed in Part~II, 29 September 2026 (batch~57)]
Part~II gives coefficient-only formulas for both resolvents from the
$45$- and $20$-dimensional compound matrices of the companion matrix
(\cref{tpo:thm:pairmatrix,tpo:thm:triplematrix}), with exact power
extraction, and an implementation. It gives no operation counts or height
estimates and no evaluator for $E_f$; its Research
question~\ref{tpo:q:smaller} asks for smaller realizations.
\end{writenote}

\begin{writenote}[Added 30 September 2026, batch~70]
Part~III adds a denominator-free Pfaffian of order~$20$ for the constant
triple resolvent $T_f$ and a $15\times15$ cubic matrix polynomial with a
trace recurrence for the constant matching resolvent $M_f$
(\cref{zpt:thm:pf,zpt:thm:cubic-matrix}); again without operation counts or
height estimates, and with no evaluator for $E_f$.
\end{writenote}

\begin{question}[End-to-end formal primitive recursiveness]
\label{tpo:q:sbr-primrec}
Implement the module sequence in \cref{sbr:sec:integration}, close the bounded
linear-selector issue, and obtain a kernel-checked \src{Primrec} theorem
for the complete coefficient criterion. Port the same bounded search to
the independent Rocq development.
\end{question}
The decisive artifact would be a verified executable Boolean and its
all-complex-roots specification, not only a classical existence theorem
for a suitable natural number.

\begin{writenote}[Added 29 September 2026, batch~57]
On the irreducible branch Part~II's \cref{tpo:prop:primrec} gives a
primitive-recursive realization with no separating search at all, as a
written argument. The kernel-checked \src{Primrec} theorem asked for here
remains open, and nothing in Part~II is formalized
(\cref{tpo:sec:formal}).
\end{writenote}

\begin{question}[Modular certificates and practical performance]
Can a small collection of finite-field evaluations certify good
parameters and rational-root decisions substantially faster than direct
integer expansion? Establish explicit reconstruction bounds and fallback
conditions so that modular success is a certificate rather than a
probabilistic guess.
\end{question}

\begin{question}[Positive characteristic and field size]
Determine the precise characteristics and finite-field sizes for which
analogues hold. The compressed formulas and many collision identities
survive algebraically, but the multiplier $2t^2$, the distinctness of
integer parameters, and generic irreducibility arguments must be
revisited. Extension-field parameters may be necessary.
\end{question}

\begin{question}[Higher-degree block systems]
For partitions into equal-sized blocks in higher degrees, classify
collision orbits and search for compressed descriptors with similarly
small invariant products. Which actions admit a graph-product
factorization and irreducible orbit factors of manageable degree?
\end{question}

\begin{question}[From a decision to explicit radical expressions]
Use a certified rational resolvent root to recover a fixed block system
and then construct branch-compatible radical expressions. A Boolean
solvability certificate is not yet an explicit all-roots formula; the
recovery and radical-tower interfaces require separate proofs.
\end{question}

\section{Conclusion}

The repository's unbounded separating search can be replaced by a
mathematically explicit finite mechanism. The central reason is not a
general computability theorem, but a small amount of partition-dependent
information: three coefficients for pair partitions, two for triple
partitions, and a quadratic curve that preserves them. The resulting
collision factors have enough structure to yield an exact discriminant
factorization and a generic irreducibility theorem.

This resolves the bounded-search obligation at the written-proof level
and identifies a precise next formalization target. It also settles a
sharpness question for arbitrary complex test sets while leaving the more
arithmetic positive-integer problem genuinely open. Keeping these scopes
separate is necessary both for mathematical accuracy and for a useful
research program.

\begin{writenote}[Status of the obligation in ProveIt]
In the Lean and Rocq developments the obligation stays open: the
separating parameters are still found by unbounded minimization, and
formalizing \cref{sbr:thm:196} would bound only the parameter selector
(\cref{sbr:sec:formal}).
\end{writenote}

\section{The question: must a decision algorithm separate everything?}
\label{tpo:sec:scope}

The formal sextic project in ProveIt already proves a correct total-recursive
radical-solvability decision for integer sextics. Its final decision file
explicitly does not claim primitive recursiveness, because the construction
uses unbounded search for separating invariant evaluations
\cite{repo-decision}. A subsequent research report compresses the relevant
partition descriptors and proves that one of $1,\ldots,196$ simultaneously
separates all fifteen matching values and all ten triple-partition values
\cite{tpo:separators}. That report is not a formal theorem of the Lean project.

\begin{writenote}[The report cited here is Part~I]
The separator report \cite{tpo:separators} is Part~I of this report, printed
above; its text at the manuscript's pin is Part~I without the dated notes
of this write. Its 196-point bound is \cref{sbr:thm:196}.
\end{writenote}

The present article asks a different question. Is full separation actually
necessary for the \emph{Boolean radical-solvability decision}? A collision
between two nonrational invariant values may be harmless. Even a rational
value with several antecedents may be harmless if its occurrence at a few
independent parameters forces a genuine block system.

Our answer is that, for irreducible sextics, the stronger separation problem
can be bypassed. The decision only needs three fixed matching evaluations and
one fixed triple evaluation. The important work is not the final root-counting
step; it is the proof that every potentially misleading rational matching
fiber must be the same five-element configuration.

\subsection{A precise statement of the main theorem}
Let $K$ have characteristic zero and let
\begin{equation}
 f(X)=X^6-e_1X^5+e_2X^4-e_3X^3+e_4X^2-e_5X+e_6\in K[X]
 \label{tpo:eq:f}
\end{equation}
be irreducible. Section~\ref{tpo:sec:descriptors} defines monic polynomials
$\Rtwo(t,Z)$ and $\Rthree(t,Z)$ of $Z$-degrees $15$ and $10$, respectively.
Write $\operatorname{Root}_K(P)$ when $P$ has a root in $K$.

\begin{theorem}[Three plus one criterion]
\label{tpo:thm:main}
For any distinct $t_1,t_2,t_3\in K$ and any $u\in K$,
\begin{align}
 f\text{ is solvable by radicals over }K
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad&
 \operatorname{Root}_K\bigl(\Rthree(u,Z)\bigr)\notag\\
 &\lor\ \bigwedge_{j=1}^3
 \operatorname{Root}_K\bigl(\Rtwo(t_j,Z)\bigr).
 \label{tpo:eq:main}
\end{align}
In particular $t_1=1,t_2=2,t_3=3,u=1$ works for every irreducible rational
sextic. Repeated roots of the specialized resolvents are allowed.
\end{theorem}

The proof is completed in Section~\ref{tpo:sec:decision}. The formula has an
\emph{and} between the three matching tests, and an \emph{or} between the
matching branch and the triple branch. Replacing the former by an or is not
justified by this article.

\begin{writenote}[Added 30 September 2026, batch~70]

\subsection{What is classical, inherited, and proposed here}
Solvability by radicals is equivalent to solvability of the Galois group in
characteristic zero. The sextic block-system criterion using the two solvable
wreath products is classical; Boswell and Glasser give sextic resolvent
criteria in this setting \cite{boswell-glasser}. More generally, Landau and Miller
proved polynomial-time decidability of radical solvability in 1985
\cite{landau-miller}. Modern computational Galois theory provides much broader
splitting-field and radical-tower algorithms \cite{tpo:fieker}.

The descriptors $D_{\mathcal B}$ and their compression into $h_P(t)$ and
$k_T(t)$ are inherited from the ProveIt formal project and its separator report
\cite{repo-invariants,tpo:separators}. We reprove the algebraic facts needed here,
so the new argument does not assume the correctness of every claim in an
unrefereed predecessor.

The contributions developed in this article are the nonsolvable-case triple
rigidity, the pentad classification of rational matching fibers, the explicit
forced rational value and multiplicity, the two-point exceptional-set theorem,
the three-plus-one decision criterion, and the displayed coefficient-only
matrix realizations with a tested implementation. They are proposed
contributions relative to the inspected source, not certified claims of first
publication. In particular we do not infer novelty merely from the absence of
a statement in the repository.

\subsection{Scope and non-claims}
Irreducibility is essential to the proof: it supplies transitivity and ensures
that a base-field parameter is not a root of $f$. The matrix identities, in

The new theorem neither improves the predecessor's $196$-point
\emph{simultaneous-separation bound} nor contradicts its sharpness statement
for arbitrary complex test sets. It solves a weaker decision problem under a
stronger irreducibility hypothesis. We do not determine the optimal number of
matching tests. In particular, no example in this package is claimed to show
that two, or even one, must fail.

\section{Complete descriptors and the compressed resolvents}
\label{tpo:sec:descriptors}

Let $L$ be the splitting field of $f$ over $K$, and label its distinct roots
$\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_6$. Let $G=\Gal(L/K)\le S_6$. Put
$\Edges=\binom{[6]}2$. Let $\tpoM$ be the fifteen perfect matchings of $[6]$, and let
$\tpoT$ be the ten partitions of $[6]$ into two unordered triples. The action of
$G$ on roots induces actions on all three sets.

For a block $B$, set
\[
 p_B(u)=\prod_{i\in B}(u-\alpha_i),\qquad
 D_{\mathcal B}(u,v)=\prod_{B\in\mathcal B}(v-p_B(u)).
\]
These are complete descriptions of the block partitions, before evaluation.

\begin{lemma}[Completeness]
\label{tpo:lem:complete}
Distinct partitions in either $\tpoM$ or $\tpoT$ have distinct descriptors.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The roots of $p_B$ among the distinct $\alpha_i$ recover exactly the set $B$.
Thus different blocks have different root polynomials. Regard $D_{\mathcal B}$
as a polynomial in $v$ over $L(u)$. Its monic linear factors recover the set
of polynomials $p_B$, and hence the blocks. Unique factorization in $L(u)[v]$
proves the assertion.
\end{proof}

For a pair $b=\{i,j\}$, write $s_b=\alpha_i+\alpha_j$ and
$p_b=\alpha_i\alpha_j$. For $P\in\tpoM$, define
\begin{align}
 A_P&=\sum_{b\in P}p_b,&
 B_P&=\sum_{\substack{b,c\in P\\b\ne c}}s_b p_c,&
 C_P&=\sum_{\{b,c\}\in\binom P2}p_b p_c,\notag\\[-3pt]
 h_P(t)&=A_Pt^2+B_Pt-C_P.&&&
 \label{tpo:eq:h}
\end{align}
The sum in $B_P$ is ordered: it contains six products $s_b p_c$, not three.
For $T\in\tpoT$, put
\begin{equation}
 a_T=\sum_{B\in T}e_2(B),\qquad
 b_T=\sum_{B\in T}e_3(B),\qquad k_T(t)=a_Tt-b_T.
 \label{tpo:eq:k}
\end{equation}
Here $e_j(B)$ denotes the $j$th elementary symmetric function of the roots in
$B$. Define
\begin{equation}
 \Rtwo(t,Z)=\prod_{P\in\tpoM}(Z-h_P(t)),\qquad
 \Rthree(t,Z)=\prod_{T\in\tpoT}(Z-k_T(t)).
 \label{tpo:eq:resolvents}
\end{equation}
Their coefficients lie in $K[t]$ by Galois invariance. More strongly, their
coefficients are integer polynomials in $t,e_1,\ldots,e_6$, because the root
expressions are symmetric integer polynomials.

\begin{proposition}[Compression identities]
\label{tpo:prop:compression}
For every $P\in\tpoM$ and $T\in\tpoT$,
\begin{align}
 D_P(u,v)={}&v^3-(3u^2-e_1u+A_P)v^2\notag\\
 &+\bigl(3u^4-2e_1u^3+(e_2+A_P)u^2-B_Pu+C_P\bigr)v-f(u),
 \label{tpo:eq:DP}\\
 D_T(u,v)={}&v^2-(2u^3-e_1u^2+a_Tu-b_T)v+f(u).
 \label{tpo:eq:DT}
\end{align}
Consequently $P\mapsto h_P$ is injective into $L[t]$, and $T\mapsto k_T$ is
injective into $L[t]$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The three pair-block polynomials are $u^2-s_bu+p_b$. Their sum is
$3u^2-e_1u+A_P$. Their pairwise product sum is
$3u^4-2e_1u^3+(e_2+A_P)u^2-B_Pu+C_P$: for the coefficient of $u^2$, use
$e_2=A_P+\sum_{b<c}s_bs_c$. Their total product is $f(u)$. Expanding the
cubic in $v$ proves \eqref{tpo:eq:DP}. The sum of the two cubic block polynomials
is $2u^3-e_1u^2+a_Tu-b_T$, and their product is $f(u)$, proving
\eqref{tpo:eq:DT}. Equality of two compressed polynomials gives equality of all
the partition-dependent coefficients in the corresponding displayed
descriptor. Lemma~\ref{tpo:lem:complete} then proves injectivity.
\end{proof}

On the curve used by the predecessor report,
\begin{align}
 D_P(t,2t^2)&=K_2(t)-2t^2h_P(t),&K_2(t)&=2t^6+2e_2t^4-f(t),\notag\\
 D_T(t,2t^2)&=K_3(t)-2t^2k_T(t),&K_3(t)&=4t^4-4t^5+2e_1t^4+f(t).
 \label{tpo:eq:curve}
\end{align}
For $t\ne0$, these are invertible affine changes of the specialized values.
Thus root existence and multiplicity transfer to the original descriptor
resolvents. The compressed polynomials also make sense at $t=0$, whereas the
original descriptors on this curve collapse there.

\section{Two group lemmas on six vertices}
\label{tpo:sec:groups}

We use no list of the sixteen transitive subgroups of $S_6$ in the proof.
The first lemma is classical; the second reduces the relevant degree-six
obstruction to very small graphs.

\begin{lemma}[The block criterion]
\label{tpo:lem:block}
A transitive subgroup $G\le S_6$ is solvable if and only if it fixes an element
of $\tpoM$ or of $\tpoT$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
A group fixing a matching embeds in $S_2\wr S_3$, and a group fixing a
partition into two triples embeds in $S_3\wr S_2$. Both wreath products are
solvable, since their base groups and quotient groups are solvable. Subgroups
of solvable groups are solvable.

Conversely, a finite solvable primitive permutation group has prime-power
degree. Here is the needed argument. Choose a minimal nontrivial normal
subgroup $N$. Its commutator subgroup is characteristic in $N$ and normal in
$G$; minimality and solvability imply that $N$ is abelian. For a prime $p$
dividing $|N|$, the subgroup $N[p]$ is characteristic and nontrivial, so
$N=N[p]$ is elementary abelian. The $N$-orbits form a block system. Primitivity
and faithfulness therefore make $N$ transitive. An abelian transitive
permutation group is regular: all point stabilizers in it agree, and their
intersection is the kernel, which is trivial. The degree is then $|N|=p^r$.
Since six is not a prime power, a solvable transitive $G\le S_6$ is
imprimitive. The possible sizes of its nontrivial blocks are two and three,
so it fixes an element of $\tpoM$ or $\tpoT$.
\end{proof}

\begin{writenote}[Part~I proves the same criterion]
\Cref{tpo:lem:block} is \cref{sbr:lem:primitive-group} together with the
equivalence of solvability and a fixed pair or triple partition in
\cref{sbr:prop:blocks-galois}, proved there by the same
minimal-normal-subgroup argument.
\end{writenote}

\begin{lemma}[Invariant regular graphs]
\label{tpo:lem:graphs}
If a transitive subgroup of $S_6$ preserves a simple regular graph of degree
$r\in\{1,2,3,4\}$, then the subgroup is solvable. Consequently every
nonsolvable transitive subgroup of $S_6$ is transitive on $\Edges$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
A $1$-regular graph is a perfect matching; its automorphism group is
$S_2\wr S_3$. A $2$-regular graph on six vertices is either a six-cycle or
two disjoint triangles. Their automorphism groups are a dihedral group of
order twelve and $S_3\wr S_2$, respectively. All are solvable. Taking
complements handles degrees four and three.

Now take an orbit of $G$ on edges. Its union is a $G$-invariant graph, and
vertex transitivity makes that graph regular. A nonempty proper edge orbit
has degree between one and four: degree five would be the entire $K_6$.
The first part rules out a proper edge orbit for nonsolvable $G$.
\end{proof}

The proof of Lemma~\ref{tpo:lem:graphs} also shows why degree six is special.
A proper invariant graph in a higher degree need not have a solvable
automorphism group. The rest of the argument uses this small-degree rigidity
rather than an enumeration of Galois groups.

\section{Multiplicative rigidity and a safe triple test}
\label{tpo:sec:triple}

Throughout this section assume that $G$ is nonsolvable. For a base-field
parameter $u\in K$, set $\beta_i=u-\alpha_i$. The $\beta_i$ are distinct and
nonzero: $f$ is irreducible of degree six, hence has no root in $K$.

\begin{lemma}[Distinct shifted pair products]
\label{tpo:lem:products}
The fifteen products $\beta_i\beta_j$, $1\le i<j\le6$, are pairwise distinct.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Equality of two pair products defines a $G$-invariant equivalence relation
on $\Edges$. By the edge transitivity of Lemma~\ref{tpo:lem:graphs}, all equivalence
classes have the same size $m$. Two distinct edges with equal products cannot
share a vertex: cancellation of its nonzero $\beta$ would equate two other
$\beta$'s. Thus each class is a matching and $m\le3$. Also $m$ divides
$|\Edges|=15$, so $m=1$ or $m=3$.

If $m=3$, there are five classes, each a perfect matching. The common value
$c$ of a class satisfies
\[
 c^3=\prod_{i=1}^6\beta_i=f(u).
\]
Different classes give five distinct values $c$, impossible for a cubic
polynomial. Hence $m=1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Triple rigidity at every base-field point]
\label{tpo:thm:triple}
If $G$ is nonsolvable, then for every $u\in K$ the ten values $k_T(u)$ are
pairwise distinct, and $\Rthree(u,Z)$ has no root in $K$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Any two distinct partitions into two triples can be relabeled
\[
 T=abc\mid def,\qquad T'=abd\mid cef.
\]
Indeed, after replacing a block by its complement when necessary, two chosen
three-element blocks have intersection size two. The difference of the
sums of their shifted block products is
\begin{align*}
 &\beta_a\beta_b\beta_c+\beta_d\beta_e\beta_f
   -\beta_a\beta_b\beta_d-\beta_c\beta_e\beta_f\\
 &\hspace{2em}=(\beta_c-\beta_d)
                  (\beta_a\beta_b-\beta_e\beta_f),
\end{align*}
which is nonzero by distinctness of the roots and Lemma~\ref{tpo:lem:products}.
By \eqref{tpo:eq:DT}, the sum of the shifted block products is
$2u^3-e_1u^2+k_T(u)$. Hence the $k_T(u)$ are distinct.

If one value were in $K$, its unique antecedent $T$ would be fixed by every
element of $G$, by equivariance and injectivity. Lemma~\ref{tpo:lem:block} would
then make $G$ solvable, a contradiction.
\end{proof}

Thus a single triple test is unconditionally safe as a \emph{positive}
solvability certificate for irreducible sextics. It is not a complete test:
a solvable group can preserve three pairs without preserving two triples.
Those groups require the matching branch.

\section{Matching collisions, pentads, and rational fibers}
\label{tpo:sec:pentads}

\subsection{Shared-edge collisions cannot occur in the nonsolvable case}
For an edge $e=\{i,j\}$ define
\begin{equation}
 q_e(t)=t^2+(\alpha_i+\alpha_j)t-\alpha_i\alpha_j.
 \label{tpo:eq:qedge}
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}[Shared-edge factorization]
\label{tpo:lem:shared}
If distinct matchings $P,Q$ share an edge $e$, then
\begin{equation}
 h_P(t)-h_Q(t)=(A_P-A_Q)q_e(t),\qquad A_P-A_Q\ne0.
 \label{tpo:eq:shared}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Let the four remaining roots be $a,b,c,d$. Changing their pairing changes
its product sum among $ab+cd,ac+bd,ad+bc$. The differences are products of
differences of distinct roots, for example
$ab+cd-ac-bd=(a-d)(b-c)$, so $\Delta A\ne0$.

The contribution of the two changing blocks to $B$ is always the third
elementary symmetric polynomial of $a,b,c,d$; the common block contributes
$s_e\Delta A$ to the change in $B$. The product of the two changing pair
products is always $abcd$, so the change in $C$ is $p_e\Delta A$. Therefore
$\Delta h=\Delta A(t^2+s_et-p_e)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{writenote}[Also in Part~I]
This is \cref{sbr:lem:shared}, with $q_e$ as in \eqref{sbr:eq:q}, proved
there by the same argument.
\end{writenote}

\begin{lemma}[No shared edge in an equal-value fiber]
\label{tpo:lem:disjoint}
Suppose $G$ is nonsolvable and $t\in K$. Then $q_e(t)\ne0$ for every edge
$e$. In particular, distinct matchings with equal $h$-values at $t$ are
edge-disjoint.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The edges satisfying $q_e(t)=0$ form a $G$-invariant graph. For a fixed vertex
$i$, the equation is
\[
 (t-\alpha_i)\alpha_j=-t^2-t\alpha_i.
\]
Since $t\ne\alpha_i$, there is at most one possible partner $j$. The graph
has maximum degree one. Vertex transitivity makes it either empty or a
perfect matching. The latter is impossible by Lemma~\ref{tpo:lem:block}.
The final assertion follows from Lemma~\ref{tpo:lem:shared}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The six pentads}
\begin{definition}
A \emph{pentad} is a set of five pairwise edge-disjoint perfect matchings of
$K_6$. Equivalently it is a one-factorization of $K_6$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[The small incidence geometry]
\label{tpo:lem:pentads}
There are exactly six pentads. Each matching belongs to exactly two pentads,
and any two distinct pentads intersect in exactly one matching.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The fifteen matchings and all six pentads are listed in
Appendix~\ref{tpo:app:pentads}. Here is a completeness argument behind the table.
Fix $P_0=12\mid34\mid56$. A pentad containing $P_0$ must contain exactly one
matching using the edge $13$. The matchings containing $13$ and disjoint
from $P_0$ are $13\mid25\mid46$ and $13\mid26\mid45$.

For either choice, the union of the two chosen matchings is a six-cycle, and
its complement in $K_6$ is a triangular prism. That prism has exactly one
one-factorization. To see this, call its three edges between the triangles
vertical. A perfect matching contains either all three vertical edges, or
one vertical edge together with the opposite edge in each triangle. The
all-vertical matching cannot occur in a one-factorization, because the
remaining two triangles have no perfect matching. The other three matchings
must therefore all occur, and they do partition the prism's edges.
Thus $P_0$ lies in exactly two pentads. Relabeling proves the same for every
matching, and incidence counting gives $15\cdot2/5=6$ pentads.

Two pentads sharing two matchings would give two one-factorizations of the
same complementary triangular prism, so they would coincide. Hence distinct
pentads share at most one matching. The fifteen matchings, each belonging to
two pentads, account for fifteen distinct unordered pairs of pentads; there
are exactly $\binom62=15$ such pairs. Every pair therefore shares one.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Classification of the rational fibers}
\begin{theorem}[The pentad obstruction]
\label{tpo:thm:fiber}
Assume $G$ is nonsolvable and $t\in K$. If $c\in K$ is a root of
$\Rtwo(t,Z)$, then its fiber
\[
 \Sigma=\{P\in\tpoM:h_P(t)=c\}
\]
is a $G$-invariant pentad. The root $c$ has multiplicity exactly five.
There is at most one root in $K$ at this parameter. Moreover, $G$ fixes at
most one pentad, independently of the parameter.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The fiber is nonempty and $G$-invariant because $t,c\in K$. Its matchings
are pairwise edge-disjoint by Lemma~\ref{tpo:lem:disjoint}; let its size be $r$.
Their union is a $G$-invariant $r$-regular graph, with $1\le r\le5$.
Lemma~\ref{tpo:lem:graphs} excludes $r=1,2,3,4$. Hence $r=5$, giving a pentad
and multiplicity exactly five in the product \eqref{tpo:eq:resolvents}.

Two different rational roots would have disjoint fibers, whereas two
pentads always meet. Thus there is at most one rational root. Finally, if
$G$ fixed two distinct pentads as sets, their singleton intersection would
be a $G$-fixed matching. Lemma~\ref{tpo:lem:block} again gives a contradiction.
\end{proof}

A useful feature is that the proof excludes all possible fiber sizes without
using orbit tables. In particular, the number five is forced by the geometry
of $K_6$, rather than guessed from an exceptional subgroup of $S_6$.

\begin{lemma}[Pentad averages]
\label{tpo:lem:averages}
For every pentad $\Sigma$,
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{P\in\Sigma}A_P=e_2,\qquad
 \sum_{P\in\Sigma}B_P=3e_3,\qquad
 \sum_{P\in\Sigma}C_P=e_4.
 \label{tpo:eq:averages}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Every edge occurs exactly once in a pentad, proving the first identity.
For a triple of roots $\alpha_i,\alpha_j,\alpha_k$, each of its three internal
edges occurs in one matching of the pentad. In the corresponding $B_P$, the
remaining root lies in another block and contributes the triple monomial
exactly once. This accounts for coefficient three in $3e_3$.

For a four-element set of roots, its complementary edge occurs in exactly
one matching of the pentad. That matching pairs the four remaining roots
in exactly one way, and contributes their product once to $C_P$. Conversely,
every term of every $C_P$ arises this way. This proves the third identity.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[The forced value]
\label{tpo:cor:mu}
Under the nonsolvable hypothesis, every base-field root of $\Rtwo(t,Z)$ is
\begin{equation}
 \mu_f(t)=\frac{e_2t^2+3e_3t-e_4}{5}.
 \label{tpo:eq:mu}
\end{equation}
Consequently any base-field root with another value, or with multiplicity
other than five, certifies solvability of an irreducible sextic.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Sum $h_P(t)=c$ over the five members of its pentad fiber and apply
Lemma~\ref{tpo:lem:averages}. The certificate statement is the contrapositive of
this calculation and Theorem~\ref{tpo:thm:fiber}.
\end{proof}

\section{The exceptional polynomial and the two-point theorem}
\label{tpo:sec:exceptional}

Let
\[
 \mathcal B_f=\{t\in K:\Rtwo(t,Z)\text{ has a root in }K\}
\]
when $G$ is nonsolvable. These are precisely the parameters at which the
matching test alone gives a false positive.

\begin{theorem}[A canonical exceptional polynomial]
\label{tpo:thm:exceptional}
If $G$ has no invariant pentad, then $\mathcal B_f=\varnothing$. Otherwise
let $\Sigma$ be its unique invariant pentad and define
\begin{equation}
 J_f(t)=\operatorname{monic\ gcd}_{P\in\Sigma}
                  \bigl(h_P(t)-\mu_f(t)\bigr)\quad\text{in }L[t].
 \label{tpo:eq:J}
\end{equation}
Then $J_f\in K[t]$, $J_f\ne0$, and $\deg J_f\le2$. Moreover,
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal B_f=\{t\in K:J_f(t)=0\}.
 \label{tpo:eq:badJ}
\end{equation}
In particular $|\mathcal B_f|\le2$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
If a rational root occurs, Theorem~\ref{tpo:thm:fiber} supplies an invariant
pentad. This proves the first assertion. Now suppose $\Sigma$ exists. Not
all the polynomials $h_P-\mu_f$, $P\in\Sigma$, are zero, because the $h_P$
are pairwise distinct polynomials by Proposition~\ref{tpo:prop:compression}.
Thus their monic gcd is defined and nonzero, and its degree is at most two.

Every $\sigma\in G$ permutes the family whose gcd is being taken and fixes
$\mu_f$. It follows that $\sigma(J_f)$ is the same monic gcd, hence equals
$J_f$. Its coefficients belong to $L^G=K$.

If $t\in\mathcal B_f$, its fiber is the unique invariant pentad $\Sigma$,
and Corollary~\ref{tpo:cor:mu} makes each $h_P(t)-\mu_f(t)$ zero. Therefore
$t$ is a common zero, hence a zero of the gcd. Conversely a zero of the
gcd makes all five values equal to $\mu_f(t)\in K$, so the matching
resolvent has a base-field root. This proves \eqref{tpo:eq:badJ}. A nonzero
polynomial of degree at most two has at most two roots in a field.
\end{proof}

There is also a shorter proof of the cardinality bound alone. Any two
false-positive parameters use the same invariant pentad. Choose two
members $P\ne Q$ of that pentad. Every false-positive parameter is a zero
of the single nonzero polynomial $h_P-h_Q$, of degree at most two.
The gcd formulation additionally identifies a canonical exceptional
polynomial over the base field.

\begin{remark}[Existence versus an upper bound]
The theorem is valid if $J_f=1$, if $J_f$ has no root in $K$, or even if
$\mathcal B_f$ is empty for every nonsolvable irreducible sextic. We have
not proved that any nonempty $\mathcal B_f$ occurs over $\QQ$, and the
verification sample below contains no such example. Accordingly, the number
two is a proved upper bound, not a proved maximum.
\end{remark}

$\deg J_f\le1$ and $J_f(0)\ne0$, so $|\mathcal B_f|\le1$ and
$0\notin\mathcal B_f$, and a nonconstant $J_f$ is $t-\tau$ with $\tau\ne0$.
The degree bound comes from the affine rank of a pentad (\cref{zpt:thm:rank}),

\end{writenote}

\section{Decision certificates and the proof of three plus one}
\label{tpo:sec:decision}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{tpo:thm:main}]
If $f$ is solvable by radicals, its transitive Galois group is solvable.
By Lemma~\ref{tpo:lem:block}, it fixes a matching $P$ or a triple partition
$T$. In the first case $h_P\in K[t]$, so every matching test succeeds.
In the second case $k_T\in K[t]$, so the chosen triple test succeeds.

Conversely, suppose $G$ is nonsolvable. Theorem~\ref{tpo:thm:triple} rules out
the triple branch at every $u\in K$. Theorem~\ref{tpo:thm:exceptional} allows
at most two matching parameters with a base-field root. Three distinct
matching parameters therefore cannot all succeed. Thus neither branch of
\eqref{tpo:eq:main} can hold.
\end{proof}

\begin{writenote}[Added 30 September 2026, batch~70]
With Part~III's bound $|\mathcal B_f|\le1$ (\cref{zpt:thm:exceptional}) the
same argument gives \cref{zpt:cor:two}.
\end{writenote}

\subsection{Positive and negative certificates}
There are several ways for exact computations to terminate early. A root of
$\Rthree(u,Z)$ in $K$ is a positive certificate. A root $c$ of
$\Rtwo(t,Z)$ with $c\ne\mu_f(t)$, or with multiplicity different from five,
is another positive certificate. Without that shortcut, roots at all three
distinct matching parameters give a positive certificate. A root-free triple
resolvent together with a root-free matching resolvent at just one of the
three chosen parameters gives a negative certificate.

A negative certificate still needs an exact certificate that the two
polynomials have no base-field roots; numerical root approximations do not
supply that fact. Over $\QQ$, rational polynomial factorization or a bounded
integer-root search after an integral construction suffices. The delivered
prototype uses exact rational root extraction and records the roots and
multiplicities; it does not implement the multiplicity shortcut, to keep the
baseline rule transparent.

\subsection{A general orbit-collapse bound}
The elementary principle behind the parameter count applies more widely.
Its role is explanatory, not a claim that finite root counting is new.

\begin{proposition}[Orbit budget]
\label{tpo:prop:orbit}
Let $L/K$ be finite Galois, with group $G$, and let $X$ be a finite $G$-set
with no fixed point. Suppose $x\mapsto p_x(t)\in L[t]$ is equivariant and
injective, with $\deg p_x\le d$. If $X$ has $r$ orbits, then
\[
 \left|\left\{t\in K:\prod_{x\in X}(Z-p_x(t))\text{ has a root in }K\right\}\right|
 \le dr.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
A base-field value has a $G$-invariant nonempty fiber, which contains an
entire orbit. In each orbit choose two different points $x,y$. If that
orbit collapses to one value at $t$, then $(p_x-p_y)(t)=0$. This nonzero
polynomial has at most $d$ roots. Taking the union over the $r$ orbits gives
the bound.
\end{proof}

For the matching problem, the stronger argument identifies every possible
rational fiber with the same pentad, rather than budgeting for every orbit.
That is why its bound is two rather than a multiple of two. This suggests a
useful research strategy: classify invariant equal-value fibers before
counting pairwise collisions.

\section{Coefficient-only resolvents from compound matrices}
\label{tpo:sec:matrices}

The decision theorem would be less useful if evaluating its resolvents
required already knowing the roots or the splitting field. This section gives
explicit rational matrix formulas using only the sextic coefficients. The
formulas also avoid a search for an elementary-symmetric representation of a
large invariant polynomial.

All matrices below are over $K$. Let $C$ be the $6\times6$ companion matrix
representing multiplication by $X$ on $K[X]/(f)$ in the basis
$1,X,\ldots,X^5$. For a linear map $A$, write $A^{\wedge k}$ for its
multiplicative compound on the $k$th exterior power; its entries are $k\times k$
minors of $A$. Write $A^{[k]}$ for its additive compound, defined by
\[
 A^{[k]}(v_1\wedge\cdots\wedge v_k)
 =\sum_{j=1}^k v_1\wedge\cdots\wedge Av_j\wedge\cdots\wedge v_k.
\]
If $A$ has eigenvalues $\lambda_i$ and is diagonalizable, the two compounds
have eigenvalues $\prod_{i\in I}\lambda_i$ and $\sum_{i\in I}\lambda_i$,
respectively, in the same exterior basis. This follows immediately by
applying the displayed definitions to wedges of eigenvectors.

Since $f$ is separable, $C$ diagonalizes over $L$. Consequently
\begin{equation}
 S=C^{[2]},\qquad P=C^{\wedge2}
 \label{tpo:eq:SP}
\end{equation}
are commuting $15\times15$ matrices with joint eigenvalues
$(s_e,p_e)$ indexed by the edges $e\in\Edges$. Repetition of some sums or products
causes no problem; a common eigenbasis still exists.

\subsection{The quartic data attached to an edge}
Let $I=I_{15}$ and define
\begin{align}
 r_1&=e_1I-S,\notag\\
 r_2&=e_2I-P-Sr_1,\notag\\
 r_3&=e_3I-Pr_1-Sr_2,\notag\\
 r_4&=e_4I-Pr_2-Sr_3.
 \label{tpo:eq:rmat}
\end{align}
On the eigenspace indexed by $e=\{i,j\}$, the scalar eigenvalue of $r_k$ is
the $k$th elementary symmetric function of the four roots outside $e$.
Indeed, if those four roots have elementary coefficients $\rho_k$, then
\[
 \sum_{k=0}^6 e_k z^k=(1+s_ez+p_ez^2)
                         \sum_{k=0}^4\rho_kz^k.
\]
Equating coefficients gives exactly the recurrence \eqref{tpo:eq:rmat}.

For four roots $a,b,c,d$, the three pairing sums
\[
 ab+cd,\qquad ac+bd,\qquad ad+bc
\]
are roots of the classical quartic cubic resolvent
\begin{equation}
 z^3-\rho_2z^2+(\rho_1\rho_3-4\rho_4)z
 -(\rho_3^2+\rho_1^2\rho_4-4\rho_2\rho_4).
 \label{tpo:eq:quartic}
\end{equation}
This identity can be proved by multiplying its three linear factors and
collecting elementary symmetric terms; it is also checked symbolically by the
supplied script. Define the commuting matrices
\begin{equation}
 U=r_1r_3-4r_4,\qquad V=r_3^2+r_1^2r_4-4r_2r_4.
 \label{tpo:eq:UV}
\end{equation}
Here $U,V$ are matrices, not the descriptor variables $u,v$.

\subsection{A $45$-dimensional matching realization}
For the chosen parameter $t$, put
\begin{equation}
 Q=t^2I+tS-P,\qquad
 H=t^2P+t(r_3+Pr_1)-r_4.
 \label{tpo:eq:QH}
\end{equation}
For a matching containing an edge $e$, let $z$ be the pairing sum of its
remaining four roots. A direct use of \eqref{tpo:eq:h} gives
\begin{equation}
 h_P(t)=H_e+Q_e z.
 \label{tpo:eq:affinez}
\end{equation}
In fact $A=p_e+z$, $B=s_ez+p_e\rho_1+\rho_3$, and $C=p_ez+\rho_4$.
Substituting these three identities proves \eqref{tpo:eq:affinez}.

Define a monic cubic matrix polynomial
\begin{align}
 N_t(Z)={}&(ZI-H)^3-r_2Q(ZI-H)^2+UQ^2(ZI-H)-VQ^3\notag\\
        ={}&Z^3I+N_2Z^2+N_1Z+N_0,
 \label{tpo:eq:N}\\
 N_2={}&-3H-r_2Q,\notag\\
 N_1={}&3H^2+2r_2QH+UQ^2,\notag\\
 N_0={}&-H^3-r_2QH^2-UQ^2H-VQ^3.\notag
\end{align}
All products commute, since every matrix is a polynomial expression in the
commuting $S,P$. Set
\begin{equation}
 B_2(t)=
 \begin{pmatrix}
 0&0&-N_0\\ I&0&-N_1\\0&I&-N_2
 \end{pmatrix}\in\operatorname{Mat}_{45}(K).
 \label{tpo:eq:B2}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Matching characteristic-polynomial identity]
\label{tpo:thm:pairmatrix}
For every separable monic sextic and every $t\in K$,
\begin{equation}
 \det(ZI_{45}-B_2(t))=\Rtwo(t,Z)^3.
 \label{tpo:eq:pairmatrix}
\end{equation}
No nonvanishing assumption on any $q_e(t)$ is required.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Use the common eigenbasis of $S,P$ over $L$. In that basis the three block
coefficients of $B_2(t)$ are diagonal. Reordering the $45$ basis vectors
therefore decomposes $B_2(t)$ into fifteen scalar cubic companion matrices,
one for each edge $e$. Their characteristic polynomials are
\[
 (Z-H_e)^3-\rho_2Q_e(Z-H_e)^2
    +(\rho_1\rho_3-4\rho_4)Q_e^2(Z-H_e)
    -(\rho_3^2+\rho_1^2\rho_4-4\rho_2\rho_4)Q_e^3.
\]
By \eqref{tpo:eq:quartic}, this is the product of the three factors
$Z-H_e-Q_e z$, over the three pairings of the remaining four roots.
The identity is polynomial in $Q_e$; in particular it remains true when
$Q_e=0$, when all three factors coincide.

By \eqref{tpo:eq:affinez}, these factors are precisely $Z-h_P(t)$ for matchings
containing $e$. Each matching contains three edges, so the product over all
fifteen edges counts each of the fifteen matching factors exactly three
times. This proves \eqref{tpo:eq:pairmatrix} over $L$, hence over $K$.
\end{proof}

The absence of division by $Q_e$ is important. A construction that first solves
$z=(Z-H_e)/Q_e$ would require special handling at shared-edge collisions. The
homogeneous cubic \eqref{tpo:eq:N} includes those collisions automatically.

\subsection{A $20$-dimensional triple realization}
For $u\in K$ with $F=f(u)\ne0$, set
\begin{equation}
 D=(uI_6-C)^{\wedge3},\qquad
 B_3(u)=D+FD^{-1}-(2u^3-e_1u^2)I_{20}.
 \label{tpo:eq:B3}
\end{equation}
The inverse exists because $uI_6-C$ is invertible. This condition is automatic
for irreducible $f$ of degree six and $u\in K$.

\begin{theorem}[Triple characteristic-polynomial identity]
\label{tpo:thm:triplematrix}
For every separable monic sextic with $f(u)\ne0$,
\begin{equation}
 \det(ZI_{20}-B_3(u))=\Rthree(u,Z)^2.
 \label{tpo:eq:triplematrix}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
On a wedge eigenvector indexed by a triple $B$, the eigenvalue of $D$ is
$z_B=\prod_{i\in B}(u-\alpha_i)$. The eigenvalue of $FD^{-1}$ is
$F/z_B=z_{B^c}$. The resulting eigenvalue of $B_3(u)$ is
\[
 z_B+z_{B^c}-(2u^3-e_1u^2)=k_{\{B,B^c\}}(u).
\]
Each unordered triple partition is counted twice, once for each complementary
triple, proving \eqref{tpo:eq:triplematrix}.
\end{proof}

Although $D^{-1}$ is computed over the rational field in the prototype, the
resulting resolvent has integer coefficients for a monic integral $f$ and
integral $u$. That integrality follows from the symmetric root formula, not
from a numerical cancellation assumption.

\subsection{Exact perfect-power extraction without factoring}
The characteristic polynomials have degrees $45$ and $20$, but the desired
resolvents have degrees $15$ and $10$. Their monic cube and square roots can
be extracted by a triangular coefficient recurrence.

Suppose $A(Z)$ is monic of degree $md$ and known to be $Q(Z)^m$, with
\[
 Q(Z)=Z^d+q_1Z^{d-1}+\cdots+q_d.
\]
At degree $md-k$, the coefficient of $Q^m$ is
\[
 m q_k+F_k(q_1,\ldots,q_{k-1}),\qquad 1\le k\le d.
\]
Exactly one factor can contribute $q_k$ to that coefficient, and there are
$m$ choices of factor; every other contribution uses earlier coefficients.
Thus $q_k$ is determined by division by $m$. In characteristic zero this
recovers the unique monic power root. The implementation then checks the full
identity $Q^m=A$ exactly and raises an error if it fails.

This extra check guards the implementation against a wrong matrix formula or
an indexing error. It is not a substitute for the mathematical proof of
Theorems~\ref{tpo:thm:pairmatrix} and~\ref{tpo:thm:triplematrix}.

\section{An explicit bounded arithmetic architecture}
\label{tpo:sec:algorithm}

\subsection{The delivered decision function}
Over $\QQ$ the algorithm takes a polynomial, makes it monic, verifies that it
is an irreducible sextic, and proceeds as follows.
\begin{lstlisting}
Build the companion matrix and the two compound matrices.
Compute R3(1, Z) by the 20-dimensional characteristic-polynomial identity.
If R3(1, Z) has a rational root, return SOLVABLE.
For t in (1, 2, 3):
    Compute R2(t, Z) by the 45-dimensional identity.
    If R2(t, Z) has no rational root, return NONSOLVABLE.
Return SOLVABLE.
\end{lstlisting}
Every characteristic-polynomial power identity is checked during extraction.
The function records the rational roots and multiplicities used in its
conclusion. The separate verification program invokes an independent Galois
classifier, but the decision implementation itself never does.

The test values are fixed independently of the coefficient heights. This
is a bounded number of resolvent evaluations, not a claim that each evaluation
has unit cost or that factorization is free.

\subsection{Root bounds and a primitive-recursive realization}
For completeness one can avoid an abstract root-finding oracle entirely.
Suppose first that $f$ is monic integral, and let $H$ be the maximum absolute
value of its six nonleading coefficients. Put $R=1+H$. Every complex root
satisfies $|\alpha_i|\le R$: if $|z|>1+H$, then
\[
 H\sum_{j=0}^5|z|^j<|z|^6,
\]
so the leading term of $f(z)$ cannot be canceled by the others.
From their definitions,
\begin{align*}
 |A_P|&\le3R^2,& |B_P|&\le12R^3,& |C_P|&\le3R^4,\\
 |a_T|&\le6R^2,& |b_T|&\le2R^3.
\end{align*}
Consequently, for $t=1,2,3$,
\begin{equation}
 |h_P(t)|\le27R^2+36R^3+3R^4\le66R^4,
 \qquad |k_T(1)|\le6R^2+2R^3\le8R^3.
 \label{tpo:eq:bounds}
\end{equation}
Both specialized resolvents are monic integer polynomials. Their rational
roots must therefore be integers. Exact evaluation on
\begin{equation}
 [-66R^4,66R^4]\cap\ZZ\quad\text{and}\quad[-8R^3,8R^3]\cap\ZZ
 \label{tpo:eq:boxes}
\end{equation}
decides the respective root-existence predicates. This is a coarse termination
bound, not a recommended practical root-finding method.

\begin{proposition}[Bounded realization on the irreducible branch]
\label{tpo:prop:primrec}
The three-plus-one criterion for monic irreducible integer sextics has a
primitive-recursive realization using bounded integer and rational arithmetic.
Irreducibility of an integral input can also be checked by a bounded search.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The matrices have fixed dimensions. Their entries are obtained by a fixed
number of rational operations; the triple inverse can be evaluated by the
adjugate formula, whose denominator is nonzero on this branch. Determinants,
characteristic polynomials, and the power-root recurrence are finite
coefficient algorithms in fixed dimensions. Integer encoding of rational
arithmetic, including reduction by the Euclidean algorithm or bounded gcd
search, has a primitive-recursive realization. The final searches are bounded
by \eqref{tpo:eq:boxes}.

For the last assertion, a proper monic factor of a monic integral sextic has
integer coefficients, and its roots are a subset of the six roots. Each of
its coefficients is bounded in absolute value by $\binom{d}{j}R^j$, hence by
$64R^6$, for a factor of degree $d\le5$. Enumerate the finitely many monic
polynomials of degrees one through five in that coefficient box and test
exact divisibility. A proper factor exists exactly when one test succeeds.
All bounds are explicit integer expressions in the input coefficients.
\end{proof}

For an integral sextic with leading coefficient $a\ne0$, monicization
\[
 F(Y)=a^5 f(Y/a)
\]
produces a monic integral sextic and scales every root by $a$. It preserves
the splitting field and radical solvability. Rational input coefficients can
first be cleared of denominators. The actual prototype instead works directly
over $\QQ$ and normalizes the leading coefficient; it uses exact rational
factorization rather than the deliberately coarse boxes.

\subsection{Why this is not a new general decidability theorem}
Landau--Miller already gives a stronger general complexity theorem
\cite{landau-miller}. Proposition~\ref{tpo:prop:primrec} isolates a direct, bounded
arithmetic architecture for these particular ProveIt descriptors. It does not
claim a new abstract decidability result, a polynomial bit-complexity bound for
the supplied code, or a speed advantage over production Galois algorithms.

The prototype rejects reducible input. A full sextic solver can factor first
and handle the irreducible factors; degrees at most four are classically
solvable, while a degree-five factor needs a quintic criterion. That extension
is not silently included in the advertised three-plus-one theorem.

For $f(X)=X^6-3X^2-1$, the exact outputs are
\[

 \qquad

\]
The matching branch therefore certifies radical solvability even though the
triple branch fails. In fact the displayed matching roots have multiplicity
one, so Corollary~\ref{tpo:cor:mu} would already certify it at one parameter.

For $f(X)=X^6+X+1$, the coefficient-only triple formula yields
\begin{align*}
 \Rthree(1,Z)={}&Z^{10}-24Z^8+66Z^7+195Z^6-1136Z^5-489Z^4\\

\end{align*}
It has no rational root, and neither does $\Rtwo(1,Z)$. The algorithm
therefore returns nonsolvable without evaluating the remaining two matching
resolvents. The supplied exact root routine, rather than numerical inspection
of this displayed polynomial, establishes the root-free assertions.

\subsection{What the checks do and do not establish}
The independent classifier comparison checks the implementation against a
separate computational route. It still shares the SymPy arithmetic library,
and forty-eight examples are not an exhaustive proof. Likewise, symbolic
identities test formulas, not every group-theoretic hypothesis in an infinite
family. The logical justification is the written chain of lemmas above.

The implementation is a usable coefficient-only prototype rather than the
root-level demonstration accompanying the predecessor separator report. No
benchmark comparison with Magma, PARI/GP, or another production Galois package
was performed. The package's running-time record is an environment observation,
not a performance theorem.

All these files live under

A sensible formalization order begins with finite combinatorics. Encode the
fifteen matchings and six pentads and prove the incidence statements, either
structurally or by reflected finite computation. Next prove the invariant
regular-graph lemma and its consequences for nonsolvable transitive actions.
Then formalize the rational-fiber and exceptional-parameter arguments using
Galois invariance and polynomial root bounds. Only after that is the exact
matrix implementation needed for a fully coefficient-level decision.

The matrix path has separate obligations: the companion/exterior-power
spectral identities, the quartic resolvent identity, exact characteristic
polynomial powering, and the power-root recurrence. A verification-friendly
alternative is to prove the matrix identities first for independent
indeterminates, then specialize. Either route must distinguish formal
identities from tested numerical instances.

To interface with the existing bivariate descriptor, Lean's coordinate order
is $x(0)=v$ and $x(1)=u$, so the curve is represented by
$x=(2t^2,t)$, not $(t,2t^2)$ \cite{repo-invariants,tpo:separators}.
The fixed points $1,2,3$ therefore correspond to three fixed encoded
parameters for the matching \emph{decision tests}. They are not guaranteed
separators of the full value set.

\section{Further research questions}
\label{tpo:sec:questions}

The following questions are deliberately narrower than a general request for
better sextic algorithms. Each is tied to a specific remaining obstruction
or implementation boundary. They are open within this article; no claim is
made that each is an unrecorded open problem in the literature.

\begin{question}[Can the exceptional set ever be nonempty?]
\label{zpt:q:tpo-nonempty}
Does there exist an irreducible nonsolvable $f\in\QQ[X]$ and $t\in\QQ$ such
that $\Rtwo(t,Z)$ has a rational root? If yes, produce an exact example with
its multiplicity-five factor and nonsolvable Galois certificate. If no, prove
that the pentad collapse equations have no admissible rational Galois
specialization. The present tests do not decide this question.
\end{question}
Theorem~\ref{tpo:thm:fiber} turns this from an unrestricted resolvent search into
a constrained problem: the five matching values in one pentad must equal
$\mu_f(t)$, while the original sextic remains irreducible and nonsolvable.

\begin{writenote}[Narrowed in Part~III, 30 September 2026 (batch~70)]
By \cref{zpt:thm:exceptional} an example would have $J_f=t-\tau$ with
$\tau\ne0$: a single nonzero parameter, never $t=0$. The question stays open
and continues as Question~\ref{zpt:q:nonzero}.
\end{writenote}

\begin{question}[How many matching tests are really necessary?]
\label{zpt:q:tpo-fewer}
Can three matching evaluations in Theorem~\ref{tpo:thm:main} be reduced to two,
or to one? There are distinct versions: a universal choice of one or two
rational parameters; every choice of distinct parameters; and an adaptive
choice depending on the sextic coefficients. The two-point upper bound does
not prove optimality in any of these versions.
\end{question}

\begin{writenote}[Answered in two versions in Part~III, 30 September 2026 (batch~70)]
For a universal choice, one matching test suffices: the compressed test at
$t=0$ (\cref{zpt:thm:main}). For every choice of distinct parameters, two
suffice (\cref{zpt:cor:two}). Open is whether \emph{every} single
prescribed parameter, in particular every nonzero one, suffices
(Question~\ref{zpt:q:nonzero-safe}). The manuscript does not discuss the
adaptive version; a universal choice is in particular an adaptive one, so
one test suffices there too (an observation of this write). Whether the
matching branch could be dropped altogether is not asked: the triple test is
not complete (\cref{tpo:sec:triple}).
\end{writenote}

\begin{question}[Classify the exceptional polynomial]
\label{zpt:q:tpo-classify}
For the unique invariant pentad, which degrees and factorization types can
$J_f$ have under the nonsolvable hypothesis? Determine whether degree two can
occur, whether its discriminant is constrained by $f$, and whether a linear
factor forces an additional block system. An answer could strengthen the
criterion without changing the resolvent evaluators.
\end{question}

\begin{writenote}[Answered as to the degree in Part~III, 30 September 2026 (batch~70)]
Degree two cannot occur: $J_f$ is constant or $t-\tau$ with $\tau\ne0$
(\cref{zpt:thm:exceptional}, from \cref{zpt:thm:rank}), so the question about
its discriminant no longer arises. Whether a linear $J_f$ occurs at all is
Question~\ref{zpt:q:tpo-nonempty}, and the last clause of this question is
not addressed.
\end{writenote}

\begin{question}[Eliminate the pentad from the coefficient description]
Can $J_f$, or just its rational zero set, be recovered directly from
$\Rtwo(t,Z)$ and $\mu_f(t)$ using low-degree subresultants, without constructing
a splitting field or choosing a pentad? A necessary and, in the nonsolvable
case, sufficient condition is that $(Z-\mu_f(t))^5$ divide the specialized
matching resolvent. The challenge is to distinguish that exceptional
condition from genuine solvability and to keep coefficient growth controlled.
\end{question}

\begin{question}[Extract useful witnesses, not only a Boolean answer]
From a successful three-test certificate, can one recover a block system,
a low-degree subfield, or the initial data of a radical tower efficiently?
The decision proof does not identify which matching is fixed, and a rational
root alone need not distinguish it from its colliding companions.
A witness-producing refinement would connect more directly to radical
expression construction.
\end{question}

\begin{question}[Smaller coefficient-only realizations]
\label{tpo:q:smaller}
Can the degree-$15$ and degree-$10$ resolvents be computed by rational matrices
of those dimensions, or by structured determinant identities that avoid the
$45$- and $20$-dimensional auxiliary characteristic polynomials? The present
construction intentionally counts each matching three times and each triple
partition twice. Removing that redundant incidence information without a
coefficient-table search is a concrete algorithmic target.
\end{question}

\begin{writenote}[Partly addressed in Part~III, 30 September 2026 (batch~70)]
For the constant resolvents, Part~III gives a $15\times15$ cubic matrix
polynomial with $\det Q(Z)=M_f(Z)^3$ and a trace recurrence
(\cref{zpt:thm:cubic-matrix}), and an order-$20$ Pfaffian equal to $T_f$
itself, without the condition $f(u)\ne0$ (\cref{zpt:thm:pf}). Neither is a
linear matrix of size $15$ or $10$; Question~\ref{zpt:q:smaller} asks for
that.
\end{writenote}

\begin{question}[Certified modular acceleration]
Develop modular characteristic-polynomial evaluation, rational reconstruction,
and independent certificates for the two exact power identities. Determine
usable coefficient bounds and compare bit complexity and actual running time
with standard Galois-group algorithms. The fixed matrix dimensions make this
plausible, but the present article does not supply such a comparison.
\end{question}

\begin{question}[Formalize the collision-aware criterion]
Formalize Theorems~\ref{tpo:thm:triple}, \ref{tpo:thm:fiber},
\ref{tpo:thm:exceptional}, and~\ref{tpo:thm:main} in Lean against the existing
descriptor definitions. Which route is shortest and most reusable: abstract
finite-group action arguments, a verified classification of the tiny regular
graphs, or reflection over the explicit pentad table? Then verify the
coefficient-only evaluator and connect the result to the semantic predicate.
\end{question}

\begin{question}[Higher-degree collision geometry]
For block resolvents of degrees eight, nine, or ten, which invariant fiber
configurations can mimic a rational fixed point? Seek analogues of the unique
pentad mechanism, or prove that several different invariant configurations
force a larger parameter budget. Proposition~\ref{tpo:prop:orbit} gives a
baseline, but not the exceptional rigidity needed for a small bound.
\end{question}

\begin{question}[Other characteristics and base fields]
Which parts of the fiber classification survive for separable sextics in
positive characteristic? The graph and cancellation arguments are largely
characteristic-free, but averaging by five, power extraction by two or three,
and the relation between radical towers and separability require separate
treatment at small primes. Over characteristic-zero fields other than $\QQ$,
identify practical root-existence procedures compatible with the same
three-plus-one theorem.
\end{question}

\section{Conclusion}

The central gain is a change in what has to be certified. To decide radical
solvability of an irreducible sextic, it is unnecessary to prove that every
specialized invariant value is distinct. In the nonsolvable case, the triple
values separate automatically at base-field points. The only possible
rational matching fiber is a pentad, its value is forced, its multiplicity is
five, and the invariant pentad is unique. Polynomial injectivity then bounds
all false-positive parameters by two.

This yields a universal three-plus-one decision rule and an exact,
coefficient-only implementation. It leaves meaningful questions about the
existence and optimality of the exceptional set, rather than assuming that
the root-counting bound is sharp. The proof and implementation together provide
a concrete new branch to formalize in ProveIt, without claiming that the
repository already contains it or that classical decidability has been
rediscovered as a new theorem.

\begin{writenote}[Added 30 September 2026, batch~70]
The bound ``two'' in this conclusion and in the chain of
\cref{tpo:app:audit} (``at most two bad parameters'') is Part~II's; Part~III
lowers it to one and excludes $t=0$ (\cref{zpt:thm:exceptional}), and
reduces the three matching tests to one at $t=0$ or two at any distinct
parameters.
\end{writenote}

\section{Proof dependency and audit checklist}
\label{tpo:app:audit}

The main logical chain is
\[
 \text{small regular graphs}
 \Longrightarrow \text{edge transitivity}
 \Longrightarrow \text{shifted-product rigidity}
 \Longrightarrow \text{safe triple test},
\]
and, separately,
\[
 \text{shared-edge factorization}
 \Longrightarrow \text{disjoint matching fibers}
 \Longrightarrow \text{unique pentad}
 \Longrightarrow \text{at most two bad parameters}.
\]
The classical block criterion joins those two chains into
Theorem~\ref{tpo:thm:main}. The coefficient-only matrix theorems then implement
the already-proved resolvents; they do not enter the group-theoretic argument.

An independent reviewer should particularly check the use of irreducibility
at every cancellation, the classification of $2$-regular graphs on six
vertices, completeness of the pentad list, the uniqueness of a $G$-invariant
pentad, and the distinction between equality of specialized values and
equality of their underlying polynomials. For the code, the exterior-basis
sign convention, the signs in $N_0$, and exact square/cube-root reconstruction
are the most sensitive points. All receive either explicit proofs or separate
identity checks above; none is justified only by floating-point evidence.

\subsection{Answers and remaining limits}
Our answers have three logically distinct levels.

\textbf{Decision.} The compressed matching test at $t=0$ is safe for every
irreducible sextic over a characteristic-zero field. Together with one
triple test, it decides radical solvability. The original bivariate
\emph{descriptor evaluated on the repository's curve} still collapses at
$t=0$. The improvement therefore uses the compressed polynomial, not a
forbidden substitution into a formula whose affine multiplier vanishes.

\textbf{Collision geometry.} In every pentad, the five vectors
$(A_P,B_P,C_P)$ have affine dimension at least two. Consequently a common
quadratic factor of their differences is impossible. Under the nonsolvable
hypothesis, the exceptional polynomial is constant or linear, and zero is
not a root. This also reduces three arbitrary matching parameters to two.

\textbf{Coefficient realization.} The triple resolvent is a Pfaffian of
order twenty rather than an unspecified square root of a characteristic
polynomial. Its formula has polynomial entries even when the companion
matrix is singular. The simpler matching resolvent is the cube root of a
$15\times15$ \emph{cubic matrix polynomial} determinant; a trace recurrence
computes it without forming a $45\times45$ characteristic polynomial.
This is a structured realization, not a claimed $15\times15$ linear matrix
pencil or an optimal arithmetic circuit.

The older $196$-point simultaneous-separation theorem is neither refuted nor
improved by the decision result. Its task is stronger and its hypotheses
allow arbitrary separable sextics. Nor do we prove that any nonsolvable
false positive occurs at a nonzero parameter: an upper bound of one is not
an existence theorem.

\subsection{Classical overlap and what is not a discovery claim}
Boswell and Glasser already employ degree-fifteen and degree-ten sextic
resolvents \cite{boswell-glasser}. Their displayed matching invariant is,
after relabeling, exactly $e_6 A_P$. Thus the pair-product matching invariant,
and the general idea of a two-resolvent sextic criterion, are not new.
Their triple invariant is different from the product-sum invariant used
below. Our contribution is an explicit treatment of specialized collision
fibers, the zero-parameter consequence for the inspected ProveIt family,
the affine-rank obstruction, and the stated matrix realizations. An
exhaustive literature-priority assessment has not been made.

All group-theoretic steps needed below are proved, rather than inferred
from a generic stabilizer calculation. This matters: a rational value of an
invariant need not label a single Galois-fixed partition. Section
\ref{zpt:sec:example} gives a concrete example of that failure, while preserving
the correctness of the solvability decision.

\section{Definitions and the main theorem}
\label{zpt:sec:definitions}
Let $K$ be a field of characteristic zero, and let
\begin{equation}
 f(X)=X^6-e_1X^5+e_2X^4-e_3X^3+e_4X^2-e_5X+e_6
 \label{zpt:eq:f}
\end{equation}
be irreducible in $K[X]$. Let $L$ be its splitting field, let
$\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_6$ be its distinct roots, and write
$G=\Gal(L/K)\le S_6$. Irreducibility makes this action transitive and implies
$\alpha_i\notin K$, in particular $e_6\ne0$.

Let $\tpoM$ be the fifteen perfect matchings of $[6]$, and let $\tpoT$ be the ten
partitions into two unordered triples. For an edge $b=\{i,j\}$ put
$s_b=\alpha_i+\alpha_j$ and $p_b=\alpha_i\alpha_j$. For $P\in\tpoM$ set
\begin{align}
 A_P&=\sum_{b\in P}p_b, &
 B_P&=\sum_{\substack{b,c\in P\\b\ne c}}s_b p_c, &
 C_P&=\sum_{\{b,c\}\subset P}p_b p_c,\label{zpt:eq:ABC}\\
 h_P(t)&=A_Pt^2+B_Pt-C_P.\label{zpt:eq:h}
\end{align}
The sum defining $B_P$ is ordered and has six summands. For $T\in\tpoT$ define
\begin{equation}
 a_T=\sum_{B\in T}\sum_{\{i,j\}\subset B}\alpha_i\alpha_j,
 \qquad b_T=\sum_{B\in T}\prod_{i\in B}\alpha_i,
 \qquad k_T(u)=a_Tu-b_T.\label{zpt:eq:k}
\end{equation}
These agree with the predecessor's compressed invariants. Its resolvents are
\begin{equation}
 \Rtwo(t,Z)=\prod_{P\in\tpoM}(Z-h_P(t)),\qquad
 \Rthree(u,Z)=\prod_{T\in\tpoT}(Z-k_T(u)).\label{zpt:eq:R}
\end{equation}
We shall also use the simpler constant resolvents
\begin{equation}
 \Ares(Z)=\prod_{P\in\tpoM}(Z-A_P),\qquad
 \Tres(Z)=\prod_{T\in\tpoT}(Z-b_T).\label{zpt:eq:MT}
\end{equation}
All coefficients belong to $K$; in fact they are integer polynomials in the
$e_j$ (and the parameter where applicable). This follows from symmetry in
six independent root indeterminates and the fundamental theorem of
symmetric polynomials.

Write $\roots{Q}\ne\varnothing$ to mean that $Q\in K[Z]$ has a root in $K$.
This notation does not assume an algorithm for arbitrary $K$.

\begin{theorem}[Two resolvents, with a safe zero parameter]
\label{zpt:thm:main}
For an irreducible sextic \eqref{zpt:eq:f}, the following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)},itemsep=1pt]
\item all roots of $f$ are expressible by radicals over $K$;
\item $\roots{\Ares}\ne\varnothing$ or $\roots{\Tres}\ne\varnothing$;
\item for any prescribed $u\in K$,
\[
 \roots{\Rtwo(0,Z)}\ne\varnothing
 \quad\text{or}\quad
 \roots{\Rthree(u,Z)}\ne\varnothing.
\]
\end{enumerate}
No squarefreeness test or separating-parameter search is required.
Each positive root-existence test in (ii) or (iii) is individually safe as a
solvability certificate, but need not identify a fixed partition of its own
type.
\end{theorem}

collision arguments. It concerns a Boolean decision, not a radical
expression, a subfield generator, or a witness to a particular block system.

\section{Small permutation groups and pentads}
\label{zpt:sec:groups}
\begin{lemma}[The sextic block criterion]
\label{zpt:lem:block}
A transitive subgroup of $S_6$ is solvable if and only if it fixes a matching
in $\tpoM$ or a triple partition in $\tpoT$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The stabilizers of these partitions are $S_2\wr S_3$ and $S_3\wr S_2$.
Both are solvable: each is an extension of a product of solvable groups by
a solvable group. Their subgroups are solvable.

For the converse, a faithful finite solvable primitive permutation group
has prime-power degree. To see this, choose a minimal nontrivial normal
subgroup $N$. Its commutator subgroup is characteristic, hence normal in
the whole group. Minimality and solvability make $N$ abelian. Its primary
components and its subgroup of elements killed by a prime show, again by
minimality, that $N$ is elementary abelian. The $N$-orbits form a block
system. Primitivity and faithfulness force $N$ to be transitive. A
transitive abelian permutation group is regular: its point stabilizers
coincide, and their common intersection is the trivial kernel. Thus the
degree equals $|N|$, a prime power.

Six is not a prime power. A solvable transitive subgroup of $S_6$ is
therefore imprimitive. A nontrivial block system has blocks of size two or
three, exactly the two possibilities in the assertion.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Invariant graphs]
\label{zpt:lem:graphs}
A transitive subgroup of $S_6$ preserving a simple regular graph of degree
$1$, $2$, $3$, or $4$ is solvable. Consequently a nonsolvable transitive
subgroup is transitive on the fifteen unordered pairs of vertices.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
A $1$-regular graph is a perfect matching. A $2$-regular graph on six
vertices is a six-cycle or two triangles. Their automorphism groups are,
respectively, a solvable wreath product, a dihedral group of order twelve,
and $S_3\wr S_2$. Taking graph complements handles degrees four and three.
For the final assertion, the union of an edge orbit is regular by vertex
transitivity. A nonempty proper edge orbit would give one of the four
forbidden degrees.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}
A \emph{pentad} is a set of five pairwise edge-disjoint perfect matchings of
$K_6$; equivalently, it is a one-factorization of $K_6$.
\end{definition}

One example, used throughout the polynomial calculation, is
\begin{equation}
\begin{array}{lll}
 P_0=12|34|56,&P_1=13|25|46,&P_2=14|26|35,\\
 P_3=15|24|36,&P_4=16|23|45.&
\end{array}\label{zpt:eq:pentad}
\end{equation}
Here $12$ denotes the edge $\{1,2\}$, not a product or an integer label.

\begin{lemma}[Pentad incidence geometry]
\label{zpt:lem:pentads}
There are six pentads. Each matching belongs to two pentads. Distinct
pentads intersect in one matching. All pentads are related by a relabeling
of the vertices.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Fix $12|34|56$. A pentad containing it must use an edge $13$, and the
matching containing that edge must be either $13|25|46$ or $13|26|45$.
In either case the union of these two matchings is a six-cycle; its
complement is a triangular prism. That prism has a unique
one-factorization. Indeed, a perfect matching in the prism uses either
all three vertical edges or one vertical edge and one edge in each
triangle. The all-vertical choice leaves two triangles, which cannot be
partitioned into perfect matchings. The other three choices partition all
prism edges and are forced.

Thus a fixed matching lies in exactly two pentads; the two choices are
interchanged by a relabeling preserving the fixed matching. Relabeling also
moves any matching to any other. This proves transitivity on pentads, and
incidence counting gives $15\cdot2/5=6$ pentads.

Two pentads cannot share two matchings, since the complementary triangular
prism would then have two different one-factorizations. Each of the fifteen
matchings accounts for one pair of pentads, and these pairs are all
different. There are $\binom62=15$ pairs, so every pair of pentads intersects.
\end{proof}

The following sums will be useful:
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{P\in\Sigma}A_P=e_2,\qquad
 \sum_{P\in\Sigma}B_P=3e_3,\qquad
 \sum_{P\in\Sigma}C_P=e_4
 \quad\text{for every pentad }\Sigma.\label{zpt:eq:sums}
\end{equation}
For the first, each edge appears once. For the second, a fixed triple of
vertices contains three edges, each in a different matching of the pentad;
these give its three appearances in the ordered sums defining $B$.
For the third, a fixed four-set contributes precisely in the matching
containing its complementary edge. These arguments prove the identities
with independent indeterminates, so they hold under every specialization.

\section{An explicit certificate excluding pentad collapse}
\label{zpt:sec:certificate}
This is the first new algebraic ingredient. It does not assume that the
roots belong to an irreducible polynomial.

\begin{theorem}[Pair-product sums do not collapse on a pentad]
\label{zpt:thm:noncollapse}
Let $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_6$ be distinct elements of a field of
characteristic different from two. For every pentad $\Sigma$, the five
values $A_P$, $P\in\Sigma$, are not all equal.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Relabel the pentad as in \eqref{zpt:eq:pentad}. Under a common affine change
$\alpha_i\mapsto r\alpha_i+s$, with $r\ne0$,
\[
 A_P\mapsto r^2 A_P+rs\sum_i\alpha_i+3s^2.
\]
Equality of the five values is preserved. Normalize the six roots to
$(0,1,a,b,c,d)$. Their distinctness implies, in particular,
$c,d\notin\{0,1\}$.

Subtracting $A_{P_0}$ from the other four values gives
\begin{align}
 F_1&=-ab+bd-cd+c,&F_2&=-ab+ac-cd+d,\notag\\
 F_3&=-ab+ad+b-cd,&F_4&=-ab+a+bc-cd.\label{zpt:eq:F}
\end{align}
Define four integer polynomials
\begin{align}
 H_1&=-\bigl(a^2b-a^2c+ab-a-bc+b+2cd-2d\bigr),\notag\\
 H_2&=a^2-ab^2+abc+ab-ac+b^2-2bd,\notag\\
 H_3&=(b-1)(ab-ac+a-c),\notag\\
 H_4&=(a^2+1)(b-c).\label{zpt:eq:H}
\end{align}
Direct multiplication in $\ZZ[a,b,c,d]$ gives the identity
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\quad\sum_{i=1}^4 H_iF_i=2c(c-1)d(d-1).\quad}
 \label{zpt:eq:certificate}
\end{equation}
This displayed identity is the complete certificate: it can be checked by
expanding its two sides, without trusting how the cofactors were found.
The package supplies both a SymPy expansion check and an independent
sparse-polynomial checker using only Python integers.

If all five $A$-values were equal, all $F_i$ would vanish. The right-hand
side of \eqref{zpt:eq:certificate} is nonzero under our assumptions, a
contradiction.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[A certificate rather than a numerical test]
The right-hand side of \eqref{zpt:eq:certificate} is a product of differences
that must be nonzero in the normalized configuration. This is an exact
ideal-membership obstruction on the open set of distinct roots. Testing
many root tuples would not prove that obstruction. Conversely, the short
identity is sufficient; no Gr\"obner-basis completeness theorem is needed
in the final proof.
\end{remark}

\begin{corollary}[The simpler matching test is safe]
\label{zpt:cor:A-safe}
If $f$ is irreducible over a characteristic-zero field and
$\roots{\Ares}\ne\varnothing$, then $G$ is solvable.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Suppose instead that $G$ is nonsolvable, and let $c\in K$ be a root of
$\Ares$. Its nonempty fiber
$\mathcal F=\{P\in\tpoM:A_P=c\}$ is $G$-invariant.
Distinct matchings in the fiber cannot share an edge. Indeed, on their
four other vertices, with root values $a,b,c',d$, two different pairings
have product-sum difference such as
\[
 ab+c'd-ac'-bd=(a-d)(b-c'),
\]
which is nonzero for distinct roots.

If $r=|\mathcal F|$, its matchings therefore form a simple $r$-regular
$G$-invariant graph. Necessarily $1\le r\le5$. Lemma \ref{zpt:lem:graphs}
excludes $r=1,2,3,4$, so $r=5$ and $\mathcal F$ is a pentad. Theorem
\ref{zpt:thm:noncollapse} excludes that final possibility.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Reciprocal transport and the zero parameter]
\label{zpt:cor:zero}
For irreducible $f$ as above, a root in $K$ of $\Rtwo(0,Z)$ implies that
$G$ is solvable.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The six reciprocals are distinct, and their Galois permutation action is
identical. For every matching,
\begin{equation}
 C_P(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_6)
 =e_6 A_P(\alpha_1^{-1},\ldots,\alpha_6^{-1}).\label{zpt:eq:reciprocal}
\end{equation}
For example, multiplication of one reciprocal pair product by $e_6$
gives the product of the other four roots, precisely one term of $C_P$.
Since $h_P(0)=-C_P$ and $e_6\in K^\times$, a rational fiber for
$h_P(0)$ gives one for $A_P$ on reciprocal roots. Apply Corollary
\ref{zpt:cor:A-safe} to the reciprocal irreducible sextic.
\end{proof}

The same argument also proves that, for any six distinct \emph{nonzero}
roots, the five $C_P$ in a pentad cannot all coincide, regardless of their
Galois group.

\section{The safe triple test and the decision theorem}
\label{zpt:sec:decision}
We reproduce the necessary triple argument from the predecessor report
\cite{zpt:repo-report}. Its role is complementary to the new matching
certificate; it is not a new claim in this article.

\begin{lemma}[Distinct shifted pair products]
\label{zpt:lem:pair-products}
Assume $G$ is nonsolvable, fix $u\in K$, and set $\beta_i=u-\alpha_i$.
The fifteen products $\beta_i\beta_j$ with $i<j$ are pairwise distinct.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The $\beta_i$ are distinct and nonzero. Equality of products defines a
$G$-invariant equivalence relation on edges. Edge transitivity, supplied
by Lemma \ref{zpt:lem:graphs}, makes all classes have a common size $m$.
Edges in the same class are disjoint: cancellation at a shared vertex
would equate two distinct $\beta$'s. Thus $m\le3$, and $m$ divides fifteen.
The only possibilities are $1$ and $3$.

In the latter case the five classes are perfect matchings. Their five
distinct common products $c$ satisfy
$c^3=\prod_i\beta_i=f(u)$, impossible for a cubic polynomial over a field.
Hence $m=1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Triple safety at every base-field parameter]
\label{zpt:prop:triple}
If $G$ is nonsolvable, then the ten $k_T(u)$ are distinct for every $u\in K$,
and $\Rthree(u,Z)$ has no root in $K$. In particular $\Tres$ has no root
in $K$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
After relabeling and, if necessary, exchanging the blocks of a partition,
two different triple partitions can be written
$abc|def$ and $abd|cef$. Their shifted block-product sums differ by
\[
 (\beta_c-\beta_d)(\beta_a\beta_b-\beta_e\beta_f),
\]
which is nonzero by Lemma \ref{zpt:lem:pair-products}. The shifted sum is
\[
 \prod_{i\in B}(u-\alpha_i)+\prod_{i\notin B}(u-\alpha_i)
 =2u^3-e_1u^2+k_T(u).
\]
Thus all $k_T(u)$ are distinct. A value in $K$ would have a unique
antecedent $T$, which equivariance would make $G$-fixed. Lemma
\ref{zpt:lem:block} gives a contradiction. At $u=0$, $k_T(0)=-b_T$.
\end{proof}

added; the arguments are the same, as the manuscript says above.
\end{writenote}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem \ref{zpt:thm:main}]
In characteristic zero, radical solvability is equivalent to solvability
of the finite Galois group. By Lemma \ref{zpt:lem:block}, a solvable $G$ fixes
a matching or a triple partition. In the first case its $A_P$, $C_P$ and
$h_P(0)$ belong to $K$; in the second case its $b_T$ and $k_T(u)$ belong
to $K$. This proves both positive disjunctions. Conversely, Corollaries
\ref{zpt:cor:A-safe} and \ref{zpt:cor:zero}, and Proposition \ref{zpt:prop:triple},
show that any positive test in those disjunctions forces solvability.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Why the original descriptor must not be evaluated at zero}
For a block $B$, put
$p_B(u)=\prod_{i\in B}(u-\alpha_i)$ and
$D_P(u,v)=\prod_{B\in P}(v-p_B(u))$.
The repository uses
\begin{equation}
 D_P(t,2t^2)=K_2(t)-2t^2h_P(t),\qquad
 K_2(t)=2t^6+2e_2t^4-f(t).\label{zpt:eq:curve}
\end{equation}
For nonzero $t$, this is a common invertible affine transformation of the
matching values. At zero, however, $D_P(0,0)=-e_6$ for every $P$.
The original descriptor test there is useless: it has a base-field root
for every input. Our safe test uses the well-defined polynomial
$\Rtwo(0,Z)=\prod_P(Z+C_P)$ instead. A source-level implementation must
introduce or evaluate that compressed polynomial directly. Deleting an
interface check that rejects zero, while leaving the uncompressed
formula unchanged, would be an error.

\section{Affine-rank rigidity and a linear exceptional polynomial}
\label{zpt:sec:rank}
The next result is independent of the explicit certificate and gives a
second improvement to the repository's collision analysis.

\begin{theorem}[Pentad affine rank]
\label{zpt:thm:rank}
For six distinct elements of any field and any pentad $\Sigma$,
\begin{equation}
 \dim\aff\{(A_P,B_P,C_P):P\in\Sigma\}\ge2.\label{zpt:eq:rank}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Under a common translation of all roots by $s$, differences between
matching coordinates transform as
\begin{equation}
 \Delta A'=\Delta A,\quad
 \Delta B'=\Delta B+2s\Delta A,\quad
 \Delta C'=\Delta C+s\Delta B+s^2\Delta A.\label{zpt:eq:translation}
\end{equation}
Expanding \eqref{zpt:eq:ABC} proves these identities. The transformation is
triangular with diagonal entries one, so it preserves affine rank.
Translate one root to zero and name the other five distinct nonzero
roots $x_1,\ldots,x_5$. Put
$D=\prod_i x_i$ and $E_j=e_j(x_1,\ldots,x_5)$.

The pentad contains exactly one matching $P_i$ pairing zero with $x_i$.
Write $(A_i,B_i,C_i)$ for its coordinates. Then
\begin{equation}
 C_i=\frac D{x_i},\qquad
 B_i=x_iA_i+E_3-x_iE_2+x_i^2E_1-x_i^3.\label{zpt:eq:rank-coords}
\end{equation}
For the first identity, $C_i$ is the product of the four roots not paired
with zero. For the second, the terms of $B_i$ involving the edge
$\{0,x_i\}$ contribute $x_iA_i$; the other two edges contribute the
third elementary symmetric function of the remaining four roots.
Removing $x_i$ from $E_3$ gives the stated expression.

The $C_i$ are distinct. If the five points were contained in an affine
line, there would therefore be $v_0,v_1,w_0,w_1$ in the field such that
\[
 A_i=v_0+\frac{v_1}{x_i},\qquad
 B_i=w_0+\frac{w_1}{x_i}.
\]
Substitute into \eqref{zpt:eq:rank-coords} and multiply by $x_i$. Every $x_i$
would be a root of the monic quartic
\begin{equation}
 X^4-E_1X^3+(E_2-v_0)X^2+(w_0-v_1-E_3)X+w_1.\label{zpt:eq:quartic-obstruction}
\end{equation}
Five distinct roots of a monic quartic are impossible. Rank zero is
already excluded by the distinct $C_i$, so \eqref{zpt:eq:rank} follows.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[At most one common collapse parameter for a pentad]
\label{zpt:cor:one-pentad}
For six distinct roots in a field and a pentad $\Sigma$, the nonzero
polynomials among $h_P-h_Q$, $P,Q\in\Sigma$, have a greatest common
divisor of degree at most one. If the roots are nonzero and the field has
characteristic different from two, that gcd does not vanish at zero.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
A common divisor of degree two would make every difference a scalar
multiple of the same quadratic. Comparing coefficients in
$\Delta h=\Delta A\,t^2+\Delta B\,t-\Delta C$ would put all difference
vectors in a one-dimensional space, contrary to Theorem \ref{zpt:thm:rank}.
The differences are not all zero for the same reason. At zero, simultaneous
vanishing would make all $C_P$ equal, which is excluded by
\eqref{zpt:eq:reciprocal} and Theorem \ref{zpt:thm:noncollapse}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Recovering the nonsolvable fiber geometry}
For an edge $e=\{i,j\}$ set
\begin{equation}
 q_e(t)=t^2+(\alpha_i+\alpha_j)t-\alpha_i\alpha_j.\label{zpt:eq:q}
\end{equation}
If distinct matchings $P,Q$ share $e$, expansion gives
\begin{equation}
 h_P(t)-h_Q(t)=(A_P-A_Q)q_e(t),\quad A_P-A_Q\ne0.\label{zpt:eq:shared}
\end{equation}
Indeed, changing the pairing of the other four roots changes $B$ by
$s_e\Delta A$ and $C$ by $p_e\Delta A$.

Assume $G$ nonsolvable and $t\in K$. Edges with $q_e(t)=0$ form a
$G$-invariant graph of maximum degree one. For fixed $i$, the equation
\[
 (t-\alpha_i)\alpha_j=-t^2-t\alpha_i
\]
has at most one possible partner because $t\ne\alpha_i$. Transitivity
makes the graph either empty or a perfect matching; the latter would make
$G$ solvable. Thus all $q_e(t)$ are nonzero.

A base-field value $c=h_P(t)$ consequently has an edge-disjoint invariant
fiber. The invariant-graph argument makes it a pentad, of size exactly
five. Moreover $G$ preserves at most one pentad: two invariant pentads
would have a common matching fixed by $G$, by Lemma \ref{zpt:lem:pentads}.
Finally, \eqref{zpt:eq:sums} shows that the common value on the pentad is
necessarily
\begin{equation}
 \mu_f(t)=\frac{e_2t^2+3e_3t-e_4}{5}.\label{zpt:eq:mu}
\end{equation}
This reconstructs the predecessor's pentad obstruction without assuming
its other claims.

\eqref{zpt:eq:q} and \eqref{zpt:eq:shared} are \eqref{tpo:eq:qedge} and
\cref{tpo:lem:shared} (Part~I's \cref{sbr:lem:shared}); the paragraphs above
reprove \cref{tpo:lem:disjoint}, \cref{tpo:thm:fiber} and \cref{tpo:cor:mu},
and \eqref{zpt:eq:mu} is \eqref{tpo:eq:mu}.
\end{writenote}

\begin{theorem}[The exceptional polynomial is constant or linear]
\label{zpt:thm:exceptional}
Let $f$ be irreducible with nonsolvable Galois group over $K$. Define
\[
 \mathcal B_f=\{t\in K:\roots{\Rtwo(t,Z)}\ne\varnothing\}.
\]
Then $|\mathcal B_f|\le1$ and $0\notin\mathcal B_f$.
If $G$ preserves no pentad, $\mathcal B_f$ is empty. Otherwise let
$\Sigma$ be its unique invariant pentad and define
\begin{equation}
 J_f(t)=\operatorname{monicgcd}_{P\in\Sigma}
           \bigl(h_P(t)-\mu_f(t)\bigr)\quad\text{in }L[t].\label{zpt:eq:J}
\end{equation}
Then $J_f\in K[t]$, $\deg J_f\le1$, $J_f(0)\ne0$, and
$\mathcal B_f=\roots{J_f}$. A nonconstant $J_f$ is therefore $t-\tau$
for one nonzero $\tau\in K$. At that parameter the unique base-field
resolvent root has multiplicity five.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The preceding fiber argument proves the assertions in the absence of an
invariant pentad and the necessity of collapse on $\Sigma$ otherwise.
The family in \eqref{zpt:eq:J} is permuted by $G$, so its monic gcd is fixed
coefficientwise and belongs to $K[t]$. Its generated ideal is the same as
the ideal generated by all $h_P-h_Q$: taking the average over the pentad
expresses $h_P-\mu_f$ as a $1/5$-weighted sum of differences, and differences
of the centered polynomials give the reverse inclusion. Corollary
\ref{zpt:cor:one-pentad} gives the degree and nonvanishing assertions.

Conversely, a zero $t\in K$ of $J_f$ makes all five values equal to
$\mu_f(t)\in K$. This is a resolvent root; the fiber argument proves its
multiplicity and uniqueness. A linear polynomial over $K$ has its single
root in $K$, and the constant case has none.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Two arbitrary matching parameters]
\label{zpt:cor:two}
For distinct $t_1,t_2\in K$ and any $u\in K$, an irreducible sextic is
solvable by radicals if and only if
\[
 \roots{\Rthree(u,Z)}\ne\varnothing
 \quad\text{or}\quad
 \bigl(\roots{\Rtwo(t_1,Z)}\ne\varnothing
       \ \text{and}\ \roots{\Rtwo(t_2,Z)}\ne\varnothing\bigr).
\]
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
A fixed matching supplies roots at both parameters, and a fixed triple
partition supplies the other branch. A nonsolvable group cannot pass the
triple test or two distinct matching tests, by Theorem
\ref{zpt:thm:exceptional}.
\end{proof}
This resolves the predecessor's ``every choice of two distinct parameters''
version. Theorem \ref{zpt:thm:main} separately resolves its ``one universally
chosen parameter'' version by selecting zero. It does not prove that an
arbitrary prescribed nonzero single parameter is safe.

\Cref{zpt:thm:exceptional} sharpens \cref{tpo:thm:exceptional}
($\deg J_f\le2$, $|\mathcal B_f|\le2$ there), and \cref{zpt:cor:two} and
\cref{zpt:thm:main} sharpen \cref{tpo:thm:main} (three matching tests

\section{A denominator-free Pfaffian triple resolvent}
\label{zpt:sec:pfaffian}
The decision theorem can use $\Tres$, which has a particularly clean
coefficient-only realization. We state the matrix identity without any
irreducibility or invertibility hypothesis.

Let $V$ have ordered basis $v_1,\ldots,v_6$, and equip $\bigwedge^3V$ with
the bilinear pairing defined by
\[
 u\wedge v=\langle u,v\rangle\,
 v_1\wedge\cdots\wedge v_6.
\]
In the lexicographically ordered basis of triples this has a fixed
$20\times20$ matrix $J$. Only complementary triples pair nontrivially;
the nonzero entries are signs. In particular,
\begin{equation}
 J^T=-J,\qquad J^2=-I,\qquad \pf(J)\in\{1,-1\}.\label{zpt:eq:Jmatrix}
\end{equation}
For a $6\times6$ matrix $C$, let
\begin{equation}
 D=\bigwedge^3 C,\qquad D^\dagger=J^{-1}D^TJ,
 \qquad H=D+D^\dagger.\label{zpt:eq:D-H}
\end{equation}
The entries of $D$ are the $3\times3$ minors of $C$; the entries of
$D^\dagger$ have the same polynomial degree, since $J^{-1}=-J$.

\begin{theorem}[Middle-exterior Pfaffian formula]
\label{zpt:thm:pf}
Let $C$ be the companion matrix of an arbitrary monic sextic, with roots
$\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_6$ counted with multiplicity in a splitting ring or
field as appropriate. Then
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\quad
 T_f(Z)=\frac{\pf\bigl(J(ZI-H)\bigr)}{\pf(J)}.
 \quad}\label{zpt:eq:pf}
\end{equation}
The right-hand side is a monic polynomial of degree ten whose coefficients
are integer polynomials in the entries of $C$. The identity is valid for
singular and nonseparable inputs; it involves no division by $e_6$.
Furthermore
\begin{equation}
 \det(ZI-H)=T_f(Z)^2.\label{zpt:eq:pf-square}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The adjoint is an involution, so $H^\dagger=H$. Equivalently $H^TJ=JH$;
therefore $J(ZI-H)$ is skew-symmetric. Its Pfaffian is a polynomial in its
entries. Its leading coefficient is $\pf(J)$, which is a unit sign, proving
monicity and integrality of the coefficients in \eqref{zpt:eq:pf}.

Work first with six independent eigenvalues over characteristic zero.
In an eigenbasis of $C$, $D$ is diagonal with entries
$d_I=\prod_{i\in I}\alpha_i$. The pairing pairs $I$ with its complement,
so $D^\dagger$ has diagonal entries $d_{I^c}$. Hence $H$ has the ten
values $d_I+d_{I^c}$, each twice. These assertions are independent of
rescaling the volume form or changing the basis: the adjoint defined by
the pairing and the endomorphism $H$ transport with that basis. Thus
\eqref{zpt:eq:pf-square} holds on the dense set of diagonalizable matrices
with distinct eigenvalues.

The identity $\pf(A)^2=\det A$ shows that the square of the monic
polynomial in \eqref{zpt:eq:pf} is the same product square. In a
characteristic-zero integral domain the two monic polynomials must be
equal. Both sides are polynomial expressions with integer coefficients
in the universal matrix entries, so equality on this dense set proves a
polynomial identity. It specializes to every matrix, including singular
ones, and after reduction to every commutative coefficient ring. For
nonseparable polynomials, the product interpretation uses repeated roots;
there is no separability step left in the specialized identity.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Why no inverse is needed}
The exterior pairing also gives the universal identity
\begin{equation}
 D^TJD=(\det C)J.\label{zpt:eq:similitude}
\end{equation}
It follows directly by applying $C$ to the wedge of two three-vectors.
When $C$ is invertible,
\begin{equation}
 D^\dagger=(\det C)D^{-1},\qquad
 H=D+(\det C)D^{-1}.\label{zpt:eq:inverse}
\end{equation}
Thus the predecessor's inverse-based construction is recovered in its
nonsingular domain. Formula \eqref{zpt:eq:D-H} extends it polynomially rather
than attempting to cancel a numerical zero denominator. The Pfaffian also
fixes the sign that an unqualified square root of a determinant would leave
ambiguous.

Pfaffian algorithms and their exact or numerical variants are standard
linear algebra; Wimmer gives a systematic algorithmic treatment
\cite{zpt:wimmer}. The contribution here is the complementary-triple
construction and its precise specialization to the sextic coefficient
problem, not Pfaffian theory itself.

\begin{writenote}[Part~II's triple matrix]
``The predecessor's inverse-based construction'' is Part~II's
\cref{tpo:thm:triplematrix}, $\det(ZI_{20}-B_3(u))=\Rthree(u,Z)^2$ with
$B_3(u)=D+FD^{-1}-(2u^3-e_1u^2)I_{20}$, where Part~II's $D$ is
$(uI_6-C)^{\wedge3}$ and $F=f(u)=\det(uI_6-C)$ must be nonzero. With
$uI_6-C$ in place of $C$, \eqref{zpt:eq:inverse} turns Part~II's $D+FD^{-1}$
into the $H$ of \eqref{zpt:eq:D-H}. \Cref{zpt:thm:pf} itself is stated for
the companion matrix, that is for $T_f$, whose roots are $b_T=-k_T(0)$; the
general \cref{zpt:thm:general-pf} applies to any matrix.
\end{writenote}

\subsection{Generalization to odd middle degree}
\begin{theorem}[Complementary block-product resolvents]
\label{zpt:thm:general-pf}
Let $k$ be odd, $n=2k$, and $N=\binom{2k}{k}$. For an $n\times n$ matrix
$C$, form $D=\bigwedge^k C$ and the middle-wedge pairing matrix $J$ on
$\bigwedge^k V$. Set $H=D+J^{-1}D^TJ$. Then
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\pf\bigl(J(ZI-H)\bigr)}{\pf(J)}
 =\prod_{\substack{\{I,I^c\}\\|I|=k}}
       \left(Z-\prod_{i\in I}\alpha_i-\prod_{i\notin I}\alpha_i\right).
 \label{zpt:eq:general-pf}
\end{equation}
This is a monic polynomial of degree $N/2$ and an integral universal
identity, including at singular matrices.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The wedge pairing is alternating because $k$ is odd, and it pairs each
basis subset only with its distinct complement. Hence $N$ is even and
$J$ is unimodular. Repeat the proof of Theorem \ref{zpt:thm:pf}: the adjoint
exchanges complementary eigenvalue products, each sum occurs twice,
and the Pfaffian selects the monic square root as a polynomial identity.
\end{proof}
No radical-solvability characterization in higher degree is being deduced.
The group-theoretic safety argument was specific to six vertices. When
$k$ is even, the middle pairing is symmetric, so this direct Pfaffian
construction does not apply.

\section{A cubic matrix polynomial for the matching resolvent}
\label{zpt:sec:matching-matrix}
Let $C$ now be the companion matrix of \eqref{zpt:eq:f}, with no
irreducibility needed for this section. On $\bigwedge^2K^6$ put
\begin{equation}
 S=C^{[2]},\qquad P=\bigwedge^2C,\label{zpt:eq:SP}
\end{equation}
where the additive compound $C^{[2]}$ acts by
$C^{[2]}(u\wedge v)=Cu\wedge v+u\wedge Cv$.
Both matrices have size fifteen. They commute: this follows immediately
in an eigenbasis for generic $C$, and then universally by polynomial
specialization. Their eigenvalues on an edge $e=\{i,j\}$ are $s_e$ and
$p_e$.

Define commuting matrices $E_{-1}=0$, $E_0=I$, and
\begin{equation}
 E_j=e_jI-SE_{j-1}-PE_{j-2}\qquad(1\le j\le4).\label{zpt:eq:Ej}
\end{equation}
On the edge eigenspace, $E_j$ is the $j$th elementary symmetric function
of the other four roots. This is coefficient comparison in
\[
 \prod_{i=1}^6(1+\alpha_i z)
 =(1+s_ez+p_ez^2)\prod_{i\notin e}(1+\alpha_i z).
\]

\begin{lemma}[Four-root completion cubic]
\label{zpt:lem:completion}
For four roots with elementary symmetric functions $E_1,E_2,E_3,E_4$,
their three pairing product sums are the roots of
\begin{equation}
 U^3-E_2U^2+(E_1E_3-4E_4)U
 -(E_1^2E_4+E_3^2-4E_2E_4).\label{zpt:eq:completion}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The three values are $ab+cd$, $ac+bd$, and $ad+bc$.
Their sum is $E_2$, their pairwise-product sum is $E_1E_3-4E_4$, and their
product is $E_1^2E_4+E_3^2-4E_2E_4$. Multiplication proves the displayed
cubic. The package checks the identity with four independent symbols.
\end{proof}

Use \eqref{zpt:eq:completion} in the commutative algebra generated by $S,P$,
and substitute $U=ZI-P$. Write the resulting cubic matrix polynomial as
$Q(Z)=Z^3I+L_1Z^2+L_2Z+L_3$. Explicitly, with
$V=E_1E_3-4E_4$ and $W=E_1^2E_4+E_3^2-4E_2E_4$,
\begin{align}
 L_1&=-3P-E_2,\notag\\
 L_2&=3P^2+2E_2P+V,\notag\\
 L_3&=-P^3-E_2P^2-VP-W.\label{zpt:eq:Ls}
\end{align}

\begin{theorem}[Fifteen-dimensional cubic determinant]
\label{zpt:thm:cubic-matrix}
For every monic sextic,
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\qquad\det Q(Z)=M_f(Z)^3.\qquad}\label{zpt:eq:cubic-det}
\end{equation}
The identity is universal, including at repeated or zero roots.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
In a generic eigenbasis, the cubic associated to edge $e$ has roots
$p_e$ plus each of the three pairing sums of the complementary four
roots. These are exactly the three values $A_P$ for matchings containing
$e$. Taking the determinant multiplies over all fifteen edges; each
matching is counted once for each of its three edges. This proves
\eqref{zpt:eq:cubic-det} generically. Both sides are integral polynomial
expressions, so the identity specializes without a separability
hypothesis.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Extracting coefficients without a large characteristic polynomial}
Let $W_j$ be the power-sum matrices for the roots of the cubic $Q$ over
the commuting coefficient algebra. Newton identities give
\begin{align}
 W_0&=3I,& W_1&=-L_1,\notag\\
 W_2&=-L_1W_1-2L_2,&
 W_3&=-L_1W_2-L_2W_1-3L_3,\label{zpt:eq:Winitial}\\
 W_j&=-L_1W_{j-1}-L_2W_{j-2}-L_3W_{j-3}\quad(j\ge4).\notag
\end{align}
The matching power sums are
\begin{equation}
 s_j=\sum_{P\in\tpoM}A_P^j=\frac{\tr W_j}{3}\qquad(1\le j\le15).
 \label{zpt:eq:traces}
\end{equation}
Writing $M_f(Z)=Z^{15}+m_1Z^{14}+\cdots+m_{15}$, put $m_0=1$ and use
\begin{equation}
 m_j=-\frac1j\sum_{i=1}^j m_{j-i}s_i.\label{zpt:eq:Newton}
\end{equation}
Every operation is in a fixed-size rational matrix or a fixed finite list.
The divisions are by fixed positive integers and are exact in $\QQ$;
for an integral input the final coefficients are integers by symmetry.
There is no search for an elementary-symmetric coefficient table.

Similarly the triple coefficient power sums are $\tr(H^j)/2$ for
$1\le j\le10$, followed by \eqref{zpt:eq:Newton} with degree ten. This trace
implementation is compared with a separate Pfaffian interpolation routine:

supplied elimination \emph{algorithm} uses rational pivot divisions.
Those two statements should not be confused.

\section{Exact decision, bounds, and reproducibility}
\label{zpt:sec:algorithm}
\subsection{A usable coefficient-only procedure}
For $f\in\QQ[X]$ of degree six, normalize its leading coefficient to one
and check irreducibility. On the irreducible branch, compute $M_f$ using
\eqref{zpt:eq:Winitial}--\eqref{zpt:eq:Newton}. If it has a rational root, accept.
Otherwise compute $T_f$ using the middle-exterior construction and accept
exactly when it has a rational root. Rational roots are found by exact
factorization over $\QQ$ and extraction of linear factors. This procedure
is implemented by \code{decide\_irreducible\_sextic}.

The public function rejects reducible inputs. This is a mathematical
precondition, not an incidental software restriction: transitivity was
used in the safety proofs. For a general sextic, factorization can expose
irreducible factors of degrees at most four, which are solvable, or a
quintic requiring a separate quintic criterion. The repository already
has a reducible branch; this article does not silently replace it.

The actual compressed zero resolvent can also be computed. Define
$f^*(X)=X^6f(1/X)/e_6$, the monic polynomial of the reciprocal roots.
Then
\begin{equation}
 R_{2,f}(0,Z)=(-e_6)^{15}M_{f^*}(-Z/e_6).\label{zpt:eq:zero-implementation}
\end{equation}
The implementation provides this operation and checks the corresponding
two-test decision separately from the simpler $M_f,T_f$ decision.

\subsection{Explicit bounded arithmetic}
Suppose $f$ is monic integral, let $H$ be the maximum absolute value of
its six nonleading coefficients, and put $R=1+H$. The geometric-sum
estimate gives $|\alpha_i|\le R$. Hence
\begin{equation}
 |A_P|\le3R^2,\qquad |b_T|\le2R^3,\qquad |C_P|\le3R^4.
 \label{zpt:eq:bounds}
\end{equation}
The resolvents are monic integral, so their rational roots are integers.
Thus $M_f$ and $T_f$ need only be tested on
\begin{equation}
 [-3R^2,3R^2]\cap\ZZ,\qquad[-2R^3,2R^3]\cap\ZZ.\label{zpt:eq:boxes}
\end{equation}
For $R_{2,f}(0,Z)$ the corresponding box is $[-3R^4,3R^4]\cap\ZZ$.
These are smaller root-height exponents for the simpler invariants than
for general $h_P(t)$, but enumeration of the boxes is not recommended as
the fastest practical algorithm.

\begin{writenote}[Also in Part~II]
The bounds \eqref{zpt:eq:bounds} are among those displayed before
Part~II's \eqref{tpo:eq:bounds}, with the same $H$ and $R$ (Part~I's
\cref{sbr:sec:algorithm} too). \Cref{zpt:prop:bounded} is a second route to
\cref{tpo:prop:primrec}: its boxes \eqref{zpt:eq:boxes} replace Part~II's
$[-66R^4,66R^4]$ and $[-8R^3,8R^3]$, and no matrix inverse is needed.
\end{writenote}

\begin{proposition}[A bounded realization]
\label{zpt:prop:bounded}
On monic irreducible integer sextics, the two-resolvent criterion has a
primitive-recursive realization with explicit bounds and no parameter
minimization. Irreducibility of a monic integer sextic can itself be checked
by a bounded coefficient search.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The fixed-size matrix formulas, finite recurrences, and rational
arithmetic operations have primitive-recursive encodings. The final root
searches are bounded by \eqref{zpt:eq:boxes}. To check irreducibility, a
reducible monic integral sextic has a monic integral factor of degree at
most three. Its coefficient at distance $j$ from the leading term is
bounded by $\binom djR^j$, since its roots are among the six roots of $f$.
Enumerate those finite coefficient boxes and test exact division.
\end{proof}
This is a mathematical realization, not a newly accepted Lean
\code{Primrec} theorem. It is not a new general decidability theorem.
The naive searches in \eqref{zpt:eq:boxes} are polynomial in a coefficient
\emph{magnitude} bound and can be exponential in its bit length. No
bit-complexity or performance superiority over a production Galois package
is claimed. For integral nonmonic input with leading coefficient $a$,
$a^5f(Y/a)$ is monic integral and preserves the splitting field; rational
coefficients can first be cleared of denominators.

\section{A positive decision does not recover a matching}
\label{zpt:sec:example}
Consider
\begin{equation}
 f(X)=X^6+X^3+2.\label{zpt:eq:example}
\end{equation}
The exact coefficient formulas give
\begin{align}
 M_f(Z)&=Z^3(Z^3-54)
       (Z^9-33Z^6+147Z^3+343),\label{zpt:eq:example-M}\\
 T_f(Z)&=(Z+1)(Z^3-6Z+2)^3.\label{zpt:eq:example-T}
\end{align}
Thus the matching invariant has the rational root zero with multiplicity
three. Nevertheless, this irreducible sextic has no Galois-fixed matching.
We give a field argument for that distinction, rather than relying only
on its computed group label.

Let $y_1,y_2$ be the roots of $Y^2+Y+2$, and let $\zeta$ be a primitive
cube root of unity. Put $F=\QQ(\sqrt{-7},\zeta)$, a degree-four biquadratic
field, and choose $u^3=y_1$, $v^3=y_2$. The splitting field is
$L=F(u,v)$, since its roots are $u\zeta^j,v\zeta^j$, $j=0,1,2$.
Conversely those roots contain their cubes, their ratio $\zeta$, and
$y_1-y_2=\sqrt{-7}$, so $F\subset L$.

The cube classes of $y_1,y_2$ in $F^\times/F^{\times3}$ are independent.
Here is a direct valuation check. In $\QQ(\sqrt{-7})$, the polynomial
$Y^2+Y+2$ has two distinct roots modulo two. The two primes above two
therefore separate $y_1$ and $y_2$: their valuation pairs are $(1,0)$
and $(0,1)$, because $y_1y_2=2$ and their sum is $-1$. Adjoining $\zeta$
has ramification index one at these primes: locally its polynomial
$X^2+X+1$ has irreducible separable reduction over $\mathbb F_2$.
The same valuation pairs hold in $F$. If $y_1^a y_2^b$ is a cube, both
$a$ and $b$ must be divisible by three.

For completeness, this independence gives $[L:F]=9$ without invoking a
classification of groups. First $X^3-y_1$ is irreducible by its valuation,
and $F(u)/F$ is cyclic of order three. If $w\in F(u)$ had $w^3=y_2$,
a generator $\sigma$ would satisfy $\sigma(w)/w\in\{1,\zeta,\zeta^2\}$.
The corresponding eigenspaces in the basis $1,u,u^2$ are $F$, $Fu$, and
$Fu^2$. Hence $w=cu^j$, implying $y_2=c^3y_1^j$, contrary to the two
valuation conditions. Thus adjoining $v$ has degree three also.

It follows that $[L:\QQ]=36$, and the subgroup over $F$ is
$C_3\times C_3$. The action on the six roots is transitive: this subgroup
cycles each triple, and an automorphism extending the interchange of
$y_1,y_2$ exchanges the triples. Thus \eqref{zpt:eq:example} is irreducible.
A group fixing a matching is a subgroup of $S_2\wr S_3$, which has order
$48$ and has no subgroup of order nine. Consequently the Galois group of
\eqref{zpt:eq:example} fixes no matching.

Finally the three matchings pairing $u\zeta^i$ with $v\zeta^{i+k}$,
for $k=0,1,2$, all have
\[
 A_P=uv\zeta^k\sum_{i=0}^2\zeta^{2i}=0.
\]
They explain the rational collision in \eqref{zpt:eq:example-M}. The correct
conclusion is \emph{solvability}, supplied here also by the invariant
partition into the two root triples; it is not ``this root of $M_f$
exhibits an invariant matching.'' This example is not a counterexample
to the decision theorem or to the predecessor's nonsolvable-fiber lemma.

The classical matching invariant $e_6A_P$ has the same collision. This
shows why a generic stabilizer argument cannot, by itself, justify a
partition-recovery statement for every specialized sextic. It is not
being used to refute a theorem restricted to a different normal form or
to additional hypotheses.

\section{A practical route into the formal development}
\label{zpt:sec:formalization}
The main Boolean theorem has a smaller formalization boundary than the
full coefficient implementation. A useful order is the following.

\textbf{First: the ring certificate and finite geometry.} Formalize
\eqref{zpt:eq:certificate} over an arbitrary commutative ring, then deduce
Theorem \ref{zpt:thm:noncollapse} in fields with $2\ne0$. The polynomial
identity itself is a direct normalization target. Prove the pentad
incidence statements structurally, or by finite reflection against a
checker whose soundness theorem connects the enumeration to matchings.

\textbf{Second: Galois-fiber safety.} Formalize the regular-graph lemma,
then the pair-product and matching-fiber arguments. No classification of
all transitive sextic subgroups is needed. The reciprocal identity and
nonzero constant-term hypothesis give the safe compressed zero test.
This produces the mathematical decision theorem before matrix code is
involved.

\textbf{Third: coefficient evaluation.} Define the two compounds and the
middle pairing. Prove the universal polynomial identities for independent
indeterminates, then specialize. The Pfaffian theorem can be postponed if
a trace or perfect-square extraction route is easier to formalize; it is
not a logical prerequisite for the two-test criterion. Likewise the
rank theorem is needed for the arbitrary-two-parameter improvement,
not for the selected-zero theorem.

\textbf{Finally: replace the decision path, not the separator API.}
The existing separating-invariant interfaces may still be required for
witness recovery or other clients. A new compressed decision route should
prove its own semantic equivalence and only then be composed with the
repository's monicization and reducible branches. In particular, the
coordinate convention in the existing bivariate code is $(v,u)$;
changing it accidentally while introducing a new evaluator would not be
caught by a proof stated only about abstract roots.

No Lean file is supplied as if it were already checked. The intended new
statements are Theorem \ref{zpt:thm:main}, Theorem \ref{zpt:thm:rank}, Theorem
\ref{zpt:thm:exceptional}, and the two universal coefficient identities.
The source audit and their proofs, not a borrowed formal-status label,
are the deliverable of this article.

Nothing of this section exists in ProveIt; \cref{zpt:sec:formal} says what

\end{writenote}

\section{Further research questions}
\label{zpt:sec:questions}
The first two questions below continue genuinely unresolved parts of the
inspected report. The later questions are proposed targets, not claims
that no one has studied them elsewhere.

\begin{question}[Does a nonzero exception exist?]
\label{zpt:q:nonzero}
Find an irreducible nonsolvable sextic over $\QQ$ and a nonzero rational
$t$ at which $R_{2,f}(t,Z)$ has a rational root, or prove that none exists.
The present work confines any example to a unique invariant pentad and a
single simple common-collapse parameter. An example must carry both an
exact multiplicity-five certificate and a nonsolvable Galois certificate.
\end{question}

\begin{question}[Classify the affine-rank-two locus]
\label{zpt:q:rank-two}
Determine the equations and irreducible components of the locus of six
distinct roots for which a pentad's $(A,B,C)$ vectors have affine rank two.
Which components meet the Galois-specialization locus of Question~\ref{zpt:q:nonzero}?
The quartic obstruction rules out rank one, but does not classify rank
two or prove that its normal vector gives an admissible $h$-parameter.
\end{question}

\begin{question}[Uniform safety at nonzero parameters]
\label{zpt:q:nonzero-safe}
For a prescribed nonzero $t\in\QQ$, is the matching test safe for every
irreducible sextic? What changes over a general characteristic-zero base
field? The chosen universal value zero is now settled; the assertion for
an arbitrary chosen nonzero value is stronger and is not proved here.
\end{question}

\begin{question}[Recover a useful witness from a collided value]
\label{zpt:q:witness}
Given a positive $M_f$ or $T_f$ test, recover an invariant block system or
a small subfield without assuming the displayed rational value has a
single antecedent. The example in Section \ref{zpt:sec:example} rules out a
naive matching-recovery rule. One may need to switch block type or refine
the invariant after the Boolean decision is already known.
\end{question}

\begin{question}[Smaller structured coefficient realizations]
\label{zpt:q:smaller}
Can the $15\times15$ cubic matrix polynomial be replaced by a degree-fifteen
linear representation built directly from the coefficient algebra,
without a search for a universal coefficient table? Can the degree-ten
Pfaffian be evaluated by a smaller structured exact circuit? Constructing
a companion matrix after already knowing the resolvent does not answer
the coefficient-generation problem.
\end{question}

\begin{question}[Certified modular computation]
\label{zpt:q:modular}
Develop modular trace or Pfaffian evaluation, coefficient bounds, Chinese
remainder reconstruction, and small independently checkable certificates.
Compare actual bit complexity and running time with established Galois
implementations. The present rational prototype is a correctness-oriented
baseline, not a claimed optimized solver.
\end{question}

\begin{question}[Other characteristics and even middle degree]
\label{zpt:q:char}
The noncollapse certificate works in characteristic different from two;
the affine-rank theorem works in every characteristic. Determine the
corresponding solvable-group criteria for separable sextics in positive
characteristic, with careful treatment of radical semantics. Separately,
find an appropriate structured counterpart of the middle-exterior
Pfaffian when the middle degree is even and the pairing is symmetric.
\end{question}

\begin{question}[A minimal formal proof boundary]
\label{zpt:q:formal}
Implement the explicit certificate, regular-graph classification, and
reciprocal transport in Lean, then connect the compressed test to the
existing all-roots semantics. Measure the actual trusted dependencies and
proof size before adding the optional rank and Pfaffian layers. This
would turn the paper-level improvement into a repository theorem rather
than merely another archived report.
\end{question}

\begin{writenote}[Relation to Part~II's questions]
Question~\ref{zpt:q:nonzero} is Part~II's Question~\ref{zpt:q:tpo-nonempty},
narrowed to one nonzero parameter; Question~\ref{zpt:q:nonzero-safe} is the
version of Question~\ref{zpt:q:tpo-fewer} left open;
Questions~\ref{zpt:q:witness}, \ref{zpt:q:smaller}, \ref{zpt:q:modular},
\ref{zpt:q:char} and~\ref{zpt:q:formal} continue Part~II's
Questions~30.5, \ref{tpo:q:smaller}, 30.7, 30.10 and~30.8. Part~II's
Questions~30.4 and~30.9 are not addressed. Question~\ref{zpt:q:rank-two} is
new.
\end{writenote}

\section{Conclusion}
The safe parameter is already present in the compressed family, but the
vanishing affine multiplier of the original descriptor curve hides it.
An explicit pentad noncollapse certificate makes the zero test rigorous.
A different argument, based on five roots of an impossible quartic, lowers
the remaining exceptional degree from two to one. Together these results
answer two concrete questions in the inspected ProveIt report while
preserving the distinction between decision, separation, and witness
recovery. The Pfaffian and cubic-matrix formulas supply exact
coefficient-level realizations, and the delivered checks exercise their
singular, repeated-root, and Galois-theoretic edge cases.

The unresolved issue is now narrower: a nonsolvable false positive, if one
exists, must occur at a single nonzero parameter with a multiplicity-five
fiber on the unique invariant pentad. Neither the proof nor the finite
tests establish existence. That precise remaining obstruction is a more
useful research target than an unqualified claim that all specialization
collisions have been eliminated.

\section{Proof dependency and non-claim ledger}
\label{zpt:app:ledger}
The chosen-zero theorem needs the block criterion, invariant graphs,
pentad noncollapse, reciprocal transport, and the inherited triple
argument. It does not need the rank theorem or the Pfaffian construction.
The one-exception theorem additionally needs pentad incidence, the
shared-edge factorization, and affine rank. The coefficient realization
needs only universal algebraic identities, not irreducibility or Galois
group classification. This separation permits independent review and a
staged formalization.

For clarity, none of the following is asserted: a new general algorithmic
decidability theorem; an optimal count of abstract root-finder calls
(one could trivially multiply the two resolvents); an optimal separator
bound; a complete classification of exceptional sextics; a nonzero
exceptional example; automatic recovery of a matching from a rational
matching value; a new proof assistant verification; or a priority claim
for the classical matching resolvent. The theorems actually proved have
explicit hypotheses, and the new computations have separately recorded
finite scopes.
