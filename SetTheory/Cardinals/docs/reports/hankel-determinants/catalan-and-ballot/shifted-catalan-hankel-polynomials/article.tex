\begin{abstract}
For the Catalan numbers $C_n$, consider the size-$N$ determinant
$D_N^{(m)}(a,b)=\det(aC_{i+j+m}+bC_{i+j+m+1})_{0\leq i,j<N}$.
We give a self-contained proof of the denominator-exponent conjecture stated
in Paul Barry's 2020 note on this family.  With $d=\binom m2$, the generating
function is $P_m(t)/(1-(a+2b)t+b^2t^2)^{d+1}$.  The denominator is minimal
whenever $b\ne0$, $a\ne0$, and $a\ne-4b$.  We determine every exceptional
case, prove that $\deg P_m=(m-1)^2$ for $b\ne0$, and establish a reciprocal
symmetry of the numerator.  An explicit positive-integer coefficient formula
also identifies and repairs a one-unit shift in the source's separate
Conjecture~2.  The essential degree reduction is a Vandermonde cancellation
in confluent Christoffel minors.  It yields an exact sum of two exponentials
with polynomial coefficients, a finite full asymptotic expansion, and an
explicit first correction.  Independent integer and rational computations,
including direct determinants and polynomial-identity certificates at fixed
sizes, accompany the proofs.

\emph{Addition of 28 September 2026 (batch 39).} Part~II, from a later
manuscript, replaces $x^m(a+bx)$ by an arbitrary nonzero multiplier
$q(x)=\gamma x^m(x-4)^\ell\prod_i(x-c_i)^{t_i}$.  Each exponential sector
has a polynomial coefficient of exact degree
$\binom m2+\binom{\ell+1}2+\sum_ik_i(t_i-k_i)$ with a nonzero product
leading coefficient, so off resonance the minimal recurrence order is
$\prod_i(t_i+1)\bigl(\binom m2+\binom{\ell+1}2+1+\frac16\sum_it_i(t_i-1)\bigr)$;
a secondary Hankel determinant detects exactly when it drops, and a
certified finite-prefix algorithm handles the resonances.
\end{abstract}

\section{The problem and the contribution}\label{sec:problem}

Let
\begin{equation}\label{eq:catalan}
 C_n=\frac{1}{n+1}\binom{2n}{n}\qquad(n\in\NN)
\end{equation}
be the Catalan numbers.  We use determinant size, rather than the last row
index, throughout:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:D}
 \D{N}{m}(a,b)=\det_{0\leq i,j<N}
       \bigl(aC_{i+j+m}+bC_{i+j+m+1}\bigr),
 \qquad \D{0}{m}=1.
\end{equation}
Here $m,N\in\NN$, and the parameters may be indeterminates or complex
numbers.  Define the ordinary generating function
\begin{equation}\label{eq:F}
 F_m(t;a,b)=\sum_{N\geq0}\D{N}{m}(a,b)t^N.
\end{equation}
All generating-function identities below first make sense as identities of
formal power series.  Assertions about poles concern the associated
rational functions.

Barry's preprint~\cite{Barry2020}, version~1, explicitly conjectures on
page~4 that the exponent of the quadratic generating-function denominator
is $\binom m2+1$.  The same page contains a more complicated coefficient
conjecture.  These are distinct statements: the denominator claim is
correct in the uniform sense proved here, whereas the printed coefficient
formula uses a shift one smaller than its accompanying determinant.
Appendix~\ref{app:barry} makes the translation precise.

There is substantial prior theory.  Dougherty, French, Saderholm, and
Qian~\cite{Dougherty2011} studied recurrences for linear combinations of
Catalan numbers.  Krattenthaler~\cite{Krattenthaler2023} gives a detailed
account of the Christoffel-type determinant identity, confluence, and
constant-coefficient recurrences; his Section~8 also records Elouafi's
prior proof of general Catalan recurrence assertions.  For a multiplier
of degree $r$, that general construction supplies an order at most $2^r$.
Consequently, rationality by itself is \emph{not} the research claim here.
For the special multiplier $x^m(a+bx)$, the objective is the exact,
much smaller order, together with its sharp parameter classification.

The article separates two useful calculations.  Jacobi orthogonality
immediately exposes the individual coefficients.  A confluent determinant,
on the other hand, exposes the dependence on $N$ and explains the precise
recurrence order.  Their independent implementations provide a useful
check against indexing and normalization errors.

\subsection{A notation guide}
We shall repeatedly use
\begin{equation}\label{eq:notation}
 d=\binom m2,\qquad e=d+1,\qquad K=(m-1)^2,\qquad
 q(t)=1-(a+2b)t+b^2t^2.
\end{equation}
The shifted pure Catalan determinant and its leading coefficient are
\begin{align}
 \HH{N}{m}&=\det_{0\leq i,j<N}(C_{i+j+m})
 =\prod_{1\leq j\leq i<m}\frac{2N+i+j}{i+j},\label{eq:Hproduct}\\
 h_m&=\prod_{1\leq j\leq i<m}\frac{2}{i+j}.
 \label{eq:h}
\end{align}
Every empty product and empty determinant equals~$1$.  In particular,
$H_N^{(0)}=H_N^{(1)}=1$ and $h_0=h_1=1$.  We prove
\eqref{eq:Hproduct} rather than assuming it.  As a polynomial in $N$,
$H_N^{(m)}$ has degree $d$ and leading coefficient $h_m$.
The notation $(u)_k=u(u+1)\cdots(u+k-1)$ denotes a rising factorial,
with $(u)_0=1$.

\section{Statements of the main theorems}\label{sec:main}

\begin{theorem}[Explicit coefficients]\label{thm:coeff}
For every $N,m\in\NN$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:coeff}
 \D{N}{m}(a,b)=\sum_{k=0}^N c_{N,m,k}a^k b^{N-k},\qquad
 c_{N,m,k}=\HH{N}{m+1}\binom Nk
       \frac{(N+m+1)_k}{4^k(m+\tfrac12)_k}.
\end{equation}
All the coefficients $c_{N,m,k}$ are positive integers.  Equivalently,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:coeff-factorial}
 c_{N,m,k}=\HH{N}{m+1}\binom Nk
 \frac{(N+m+k)!(2m)!(m+k)!}
 {(N+m)!(2m+2k)!m!}.
\end{equation}
They can be generated without evaluating any determinant:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:coeff-update}
 c_{N,m,0}=\HH{N}{m+1},\qquad
 \frac{c_{N,m,k+1}}{c_{N,m,k}}=
 \frac{(N-k)(N+m+1+k)}{2(k+1)(2m+2k+1)}.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Uniform rational form and sharp denominator]\label{thm:main}
For every $m\in\NN$ there is a polynomial $P_m\in\ZZ[a,b,t]$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mainGF}
 F_m(t;a,b)=\frac{P_m(t;a,b)}{q(t)^{d+1}}.
\end{equation}
It satisfies $P_m(0;a,b)=1$ and $\deg_t P_m\leq K$.
If $b\ne0$, then $\deg_t P_m=K$ and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reciprocity}
 P_m(t;a,b)=s_m(bt)^K P_m\!\left(\frac{1}{b^2t};a,b\right),
 \qquad s_m=(-1)^{(m-1)(m-2)/2}.
\end{equation}
In particular, $[t^K]P_m=s_m b^K$.
If $b\ne0$, $a\ne0$, and $a\ne-4b$, the representation
\eqref{eq:mainGF} is reduced over $\CC[t]$.  The sequence has minimal
constant-coefficient recurrence characteristic polynomial
\begin{equation}\label{eq:charpoly}
 \bigl(X^2-(a+2b)X+b^2\bigr)^{d+1},
\end{equation}
and hence minimal order $2d+2=m(m-1)+2$.
\end{theorem}

Here ``minimal'' refers even to recurrences allowed to start after a
finite initial segment.  Thus minimality is not an artifact of the
empty-determinant convention.  The polynomial numerator in
\eqref{eq:mainGF} remains the \emph{uniform, possibly unreduced} numerator
at exceptional parameters.

\begin{theorem}[Every exceptional denominator]\label{thm:exceptional}
For fixed complex $a,b$, the reduced denominator of $F_m$, normalized to
have constant term $1$, is exactly the following:
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
Parameter condition & Reduced denominator\\
\midrule
$a=b=0$ & $1$\\
$b=0$, $a\ne0$ & $(1-at)^{\binom m2+1}$\\
$a=0$, $b\ne0$ & $(1-bt)^{\binom{m+1}{2}+1}$\\
$a=-4b$, $b\ne0$ & $(1+bt)^{\binom m2+2}$\\
$b\ne0$, $a(a+4b)\ne0$ & $q(t)^{\binom m2+1}$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Finite asymptotic expansion]\label{thm:asymp}
Let $a,b>0$ be fixed and put
\begin{equation}\label{eq:positive-roots}
 r=\frac{a/b+2+\sqrt{(a/b)(a/b+4)}}2>1,\qquad
 \lambda_+=br,\quad \lambda_-=b/r.
\end{equation}
There are explicit degree-$d$ polynomials $B_+(N),B_-(N)$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:two-exp-statement}
 \D{N}{m}(a,b)=B_+(N)\lambda_+^N+B_-(N)\lambda_-^N
 \qquad(N\geq0).
\end{equation}
In particular,
\begin{align}
 \D{N}{m}(a,b)
 &=A_m N^d\lambda_+^N
 \left(1+\frac{d\left(\frac m2-\frac1{r-1}\right)}{N}
       +O(N^{-2})+O(r^{-2N})\right),\label{eq:asymp}\\
 A_m&=h_m\frac{r^{m+1}}{(r-1)^m(r+1)}.\label{eq:A}
\end{align}
The entire algebraic expansion terminates after $N^{-d}$; the second
exponential is also multiplied by a polynomial of degree~$d$.
For $d=0$, there are no algebraic correction terms at all.
\end{theorem}

\section{Moments, orthogonality, and the coefficient formula}\label{sec:coeffproof}

\subsection{Catalan moments and a normalized Jacobi polynomial}
For $m\in\NN$ define a positive measure on $(0,4)$ by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:measure}
 \dd\mu_m(x)=\frac1{2\pi}x^{m-1/2}(4-x)^{1/2}\,\dd x.
\end{equation}
The beta integral, after $x=4t$, gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:moments}
 \int_0^4 x^n\,\dd\mu_m(x)
 =\frac{4^{n+m+1}}{2\pi}B(n+m+\tfrac12,\tfrac32)
 =C_{n+m}.
\end{equation}
Consequently every moment matrix is positive definite, so its determinant
is nonzero and its monic orthogonal polynomials are well-defined.

\begin{lemma}[The monic polynomial]\label{lem:Jacobi}
The degree-$N$ monic orthogonal polynomial for $\mu_m$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Jacobi}
 p_N^{(m)}(x)=(-1)^N
 \frac{4^N(m+\tfrac12)_N}{(N+m+1)_N}
 \hyp{-N}{N+m+1}{m+\tfrac12}{x/4}.
\end{equation}
The hypergeometric series terminates after $N+1$ terms.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Write $\alpha=m-\tfrac12$, $\beta=\tfrac12$, so both are greater than $-1$.
A Rodrigues representation on $0<t<1$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Rodrigues}
 \frac{t^{-\alpha}(1-t)^{-\beta}}{(\alpha+1)_N}
 \frac{\dd^N}{\dd t^N}
       \left(t^{N+\alpha}(1-t)^{N+\beta}\right)
 =\hyp{-N}{N+\alpha+\beta+1}{\alpha+1}{t}.
\end{equation}
Leibniz's rule gives the polynomial on the right, including its
normalization at $t=0$.  Multiplying the left side by
$t^j t^\alpha(1-t)^\beta$ and integrating by parts $N$ times yields
zero for $0\leq j<N$.  Every boundary term vanishes: after at most
$N-1$ derivatives, the endpoint exponents that can occur are at least
$\alpha+1>0$ and $\beta+1>0$.  The final derivative of $t^j$ is zero.

The coefficient of $t^N$ on the right is
$(-1)^N(N+\alpha+\beta+1)_N/(\alpha+1)_N$.
Replacing $t$ by $x/4$ and normalizing this leading coefficient gives
\eqref{eq:Jacobi}.  This also identifies the polynomial with the standard
Jacobi family; see the conventional hypergeometric representation in
DLMF~\cite{DLMF}, Eq.~18.5.7.
\end{proof}

\subsection{A characteristic-polynomial determinant identity}
The following elementary identity will be used twice.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:characteristic}
Let $\nu_j$ be the moments of a positive measure, let
$H_N=\det(\nu_{i+j})_{0\leq i,j<N}$, and let $p_N$ be its monic orthogonal
polynomial.  Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:characteristic}
 \det_{0\leq i,j<N}(\nu_{i+j+1}-z\nu_{i+j})
   =(-1)^N H_Np_N(z).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The monic polynomial is represented by
\[
 p_N(z)=\frac1{H_N}
 \det\begin{pmatrix}
 \nu_0&\nu_1&\cdots&\nu_N\\
 \nu_1&\nu_2&\cdots&\nu_{N+1}\\
 \vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
 \nu_{N-1}&\nu_N&\cdots&\nu_{2N-1}\\
 1&z&\cdots&z^N
 \end{pmatrix}.
\]
Its leading coefficient is one, and integrating it against any power
$z^j$, $j<N$, creates a repeated row.  Thus it is indeed $p_N$.
Starting with the last column and moving left, replace
$\mathrm{col}_j$ by $\mathrm{col}_j-z\,\mathrm{col}_{j-1}$.
The last row becomes $(1,0,\ldots,0)$.  Expansion along it gives
\eqref{eq:characteristic}, with sign $(-1)^N$.
\end{proof}

Applying the lemma to $\mu_m$ and $z=-a/b$ gives, for $b\ne0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:characteristicD}
 \D{N}{m}(a,b)=(-b)^N\HH{N}{m}\,p_N^{(m)}(-a/b).
\end{equation}

\subsection{A proof of the shifted Catalan product}
For $m=0$, \eqref{eq:Jacobi} reduces to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:p0}
 p_n^{(0)}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^n(-1)^{n-j}\binom{n+j}{2j}x^j.
\end{equation}
Its initial values and recurrence are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:p0rec}
 p_0^{(0)}=1,\quad p_1^{(0)}=x-1,\quad
 p_{n+2}^{(0)}=(x-2)p_{n+1}^{(0)}-p_n^{(0)}.
\end{equation}
These follow directly by comparing the binomial coefficients in
\eqref{eq:p0}.  If $\eta_n$ denotes the squared norm of $p_n^{(0)}$, the
coefficient $1$ of $p_{n-1}^{(0)}$ in $xp_n^{(0)}$ implies
$\eta_n=\eta_{n-1}$.  Indeed, the two ways of writing
$\langle xp_n^{(0)},p_{n-1}^{(0)}\rangle$
give $\eta_{n-1}$ and $\eta_n$, respectively.  Since
$\eta_0=C_0=1$, all these norms equal~$1$.  Changing from monomials to
monic orthogonal polynomials is unitriangular, and therefore
$H_N^{(0)}=\prod_{n=0}^{N-1}\eta_n=1$.

Next set $z=0$ in \eqref{eq:characteristic} and use
\eqref{eq:Jacobi}.  We obtain
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Hratio}
 \frac{\HH{N}{m+1}}{\HH{N}{m}}
 =(-1)^Np_N^{(m)}(0)
 =\frac{4^N(m+\tfrac12)_N}{(N+m+1)_N}
 =\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{2N+m+j}{m+j}.
\end{equation}
For completeness, the middle expression equals
\[
 \frac{(2m+2N)!\,m!\,(N+m)!}
      {(m+N)!\,(2m)!\,(2N+m)!}
 =\frac{(2N+2m)!\,m!}{(2m)!\,(2N+m)!},
\]
which is the last product.  Induction on $m$, beginning with
$H_N^{(0)}=1$, proves \eqref{eq:Hproduct}.

\subsection{Completion of the coefficient proof}
Insert \eqref{eq:Jacobi} into \eqref{eq:characteristicD}.
The constant factor is $H_N^{(m+1)}$ by \eqref{eq:Hratio}.
In each summand, $(-N)_k(-1)^k/k!=\binom Nk$.  This gives exactly
\eqref{eq:coeff}.  The formula is a polynomial identity for $b\ne0$,
so it holds for $b=0$ as well.  The duplication identity for a rising
half-integer factorial, or direct cancellation, gives
\eqref{eq:coeff-factorial}.  Taking adjacent coefficient ratios gives
\eqref{eq:coeff-update}.

All factors in \eqref{eq:coeff} are positive rational numbers.  The
left side is a determinant with entries in $\ZZ[a,b]$, so its
coefficients are integers.  This proves positivity and integrality
simultaneously and completes Theorem~\ref{thm:coeff}.
In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:extremalcoeff}
 c_{N,m,0}=\HH{N}{m+1},\qquad c_{N,m,N}=\HH{N}{m}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Real roots and coefficient inequalities}
\begin{corollary}\label{cor:roots}
For $N\geq1$, the polynomial $a\mapsto D_N^{(m)}(a,1)$ has exactly $N$
simple real roots, all in $(-4,0)$.  For $N\geq2$, its normalized
coefficients $u_k=c_{N,m,k}/\binom Nk$ satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ultra}
 u_k^2>u_{k-1}u_{k+1}\qquad(1\leq k<N).
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
A degree-$N$ orthogonal polynomial for a strictly positive density on
$(0,4)$ has $N$ simple roots in that interval.  One elementary proof
is to multiply it by the product of its distinct sign-changing roots
in $(0,4)$.  If there were fewer than $N$ such roots, that product
would have degree below $N$, while the integral of its product with
the orthogonal polynomial would have constant nonzero sign.  This
contradicts orthogonality.  Equation~\eqref{eq:characteristicD} now
negates the roots.

Alternatively, the coefficient inequality follows without any root
theory.  Theorem~\ref{thm:coeff} gives
\[
 \frac{u_{k+1}}{u_k}
 =\frac{N+m+1+k}{4(m+\tfrac12+k)}.
\]
This is strictly decreasing in $k$, because the numerator's constant
term exceeds the denominator's unscaled constant term by $N+1/2$.
The decrease is equivalent to \eqref{eq:ultra}.
\end{proof}

\section{A fixed-size confluent determinant}\label{sec:confluent}

The explicit coefficient formula grows in length with $N$.  To understand
the recurrence order, we need a representation whose determinant size
depends only on $m$.
Define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:R}
 R_n(y)=(-1)^np_n^{(0)}(-y)
       =\sum_{i=0}^n\binom{n+i}{2i}y^i.
\end{equation}
Thus
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Rrec}
 R_0(y)=1,\quad R_1(y)=y+1,\quad
 R_{n+2}(y)=(y+2)R_{n+1}(y)-R_n(y).
\end{equation}
Extend $R_n$ to all integer indices using this recurrence.  Since
$R_{-1}=R_0=1$, it also satisfies the reflection identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Rreflection}
 R_{-n-1}(y)=R_n(y)\qquad(n\in\ZZ).
\end{equation}
The reversed form of the polynomials is convenient because it eliminates
unnecessary signs from Christoffel's identity.

\begin{lemma}[Christoffel determinant in the present normalization]
\label{lem:Christoffel}
For pairwise distinct $y_1,\ldots,y_r$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Christoffel}
 \det_{0\leq i,j<N}\int_0^4 x^{i+j}
        \prod_{\ell=1}^r(x+y_\ell)\,\dd\mu_0(x)
 =\frac{\det\bigl[R_{N+j}(y_i)\bigr]_{\substack{1\leq i\leq r\\0\leq j<r}}}
        {\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq r}(y_j-y_i)}.
\end{equation}
The quotient extends polynomially when some $y_i$ coincide.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Here is a proof that also verifies the normalization.  Write
$\Delta(x_1,\ldots,x_N)=\det[x_i^{j-1}]_{1\leq i,j\leq N}$.
Expanding two determinants and integrating termwise gives the identity
\[
 \frac1{N!}\int\cdots\int
 \det[f_i(x_j)]\det[g_i(x_j)]\prod_j\dd\mu_0(x_j)
 =\det\left[\int f_i(x)g_j(x)\,\dd\mu_0(x)\right].
\]
No analytic interchange difficulty occurs here: the sums are finite and
all polynomials are integrable on a bounded interval.

For external variables $z_1,\ldots,z_r$ we have
\[
 \Delta(x,z)=\Delta(x)\Delta(z)
            \prod_{i=1}^N\prod_{\ell=1}^r(z_\ell-x_i).
\]
In the determinant defining $\Delta(x,z)$, replace monomials by the
monic polynomials $p_j^{(0)}$; the determinant is unchanged.  Expand
along its first $N$ rows and integrate against $\Delta(x)$.  By
orthogonality, every choice of columns vanishes except columns of
degrees $0,\ldots,N-1$.  That surviving integral equals
$N!H_N^{(0)}=N!$.  The remaining minor is
$\det[p_{N+j}^{(0)}(z_i)]$.  Dividing by $\Delta(z)$ yields the identity
for the multiplier $\prod_\ell(z_\ell-x)$.

Finally put $z_\ell=-y_\ell$ and use
$p_{N+j}^{(0)}(-y)=(-1)^{N+j}R_{N+j}(y)$.  The signs from the multiplier,
column factors, and Vandermonde cancel, giving \eqref{eq:Christoffel}.
The numerator is alternating in the $y_i$, hence divisible by their
Vandermonde as a polynomial.  This proves the extension statement.
This is the classical fixed-size identity discussed in
\cite{Krattenthaler2023}; its inclusion here is a normalization proof,
not a novelty claim.
\end{proof}

Take $r=m+1$, let the first $m$ variables tend to zero, and put the last
one equal to $y=a/b\ne0$.  Dividing by the Vandermonde and taking the
limit replaces those first rows by Taylor coefficients.  Since
$[y^i]R_{N+j}(y)=\binom{N+j+i}{2i}$, we obtain:

\begin{proposition}[Confluent representation]\label{prop:confluent}
If $ab\ne0$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:smallD}
 \D{N}{m}(a,b)=b^Ny^{-m}
 \det\begin{pmatrix}
 \binom{N+j+i}{2i}\ \ (0\leq i<m,\ 0\leq j\leq m)\\[2pt]
 R_{N+j}(y)\ \ (0\leq j\leq m)
 \end{pmatrix},\qquad y=a/b.
\end{equation}
The upper block has $m$ rows and the lower block has one row.  For $m=0$
the formula simply reads $D_N^{(0)}=b^NR_N(a/b)$.
\end{proposition}

Define the $m\times m$ minors
\begin{equation}\label{eq:M}
 M_{m,\ell}(N)=
 \det_{\substack{0\leq i<m\\j\in J_\ell}}
       \binom{N+j+i}{2i},\qquad
 J_\ell=\{0,1,\ldots,m\}\setminus\{\ell\},
\end{equation}
where columns are in increasing order and $0\leq\ell\leq m$.
Expansion of \eqref{eq:smallD} along its last row gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:minor-expansion}
 \D{N}{m}(a,b)=b^Ny^{-m}
 \sum_{\ell=0}^{m}(-1)^{m+\ell}M_{m,\ell}(N)R_{N+\ell}(y).
\end{equation}
Although a naive degree bound for $M_{m,\ell}$ is $m(m-1)=2d$, its
actual degree is only $d$.  This is the key point.

\section{The degree cancellation and its first correction}\label{sec:degree}

\begin{lemma}[Sharp minor degree]\label{lem:minor-degree}
For $0\leq\ell\leq m$, $M_{m,\ell}(N)$ is a polynomial of degree
$d=\binom m2$ with leading coefficient
\begin{equation}\label{eq:minor-leading}
 [N^d]M_{m,\ell}(N)=h_m\binom m\ell.
\end{equation}
For $d\geq1$, the next coefficient is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:minor-subleading}
 [N^{d-1}]M_{m,\ell}(N)
 =h_m\binom m\ell
   \left(\frac{d(m+2)}2-\frac{(m-1)\ell}{2}\right).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Let $f_i(x)=\binom{x+i}{2i}$, so $f_i$ has degree $2i$ and leading
coefficient $1/(2i)!$.  The determinant $\det[f_i(x_j)]_{0\leq i,j<m}$
is alternating in its $m$ independent variables.  It is divisible by
$\Delta(x)=\prod_{p<q}(x_q-x_p)$, whose degree is $d$.  The determinant
has total degree at most $\sum_i2i=2d$; its quotient by $\Delta$ therefore
has total degree at most~$d$.  Substituting $x_j=N+j$ for
$j\in J_\ell$ makes $\Delta$ a nonzero constant.  Hence the degree in
$N$ is at most~$d$.

To obtain the leading coefficient, expand $f_i(N+j)$ in Taylor
powers of $j$.  The first possible nonzero determinant uses distinct
Taylor orders $0,1,\ldots,m-1$; these have the least possible sum $d$.
The coefficient of $N^d$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Taylorleading}
 \frac{\det[\binom{2i}{r}]_{0\leq i,r<m}}{\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}(2i)!}
       \Delta(J_\ell)
 =\frac{2^d\Delta(J_\ell)}{\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}(2i)!}.
\end{equation}
To see the second equality, regard $\binom{2i}{r}$ as a polynomial
of degree $r$ in $i$ with leading coefficient $2^r/r!$ and evaluate
its determinant at $i=0,\ldots,m-1$.  The Vandermonde is
$\prod_{r=1}^{m-1}r!$, so these factorials cancel.
Removing $\ell$ from $0,\ldots,m$ also gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:deletedVand}
 \Delta(J_\ell)=\binom m\ell\prod_{r=1}^{m-1}r!.
\end{equation}
Since
\[
 h_m=\frac{2^d\prod_{r=1}^{m-1}r!}{\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}(2i)!}
 \qquad(m\geq1),
\]
we obtain \eqref{eq:minor-leading}.  It is nonzero, proving the degree
assertion.  The case $m=0$ is the empty determinant and is immediate.

For the next coefficient, observe the symmetric factorization
\begin{equation}\label{eq:centered-binomial}
 f_i(x)=\frac1{(2i)!}\prod_{r=1}^{i}
          \left((x+\tfrac12)^2-(r-\tfrac12)^2\right).
\end{equation}
Thus $f_i(x)=(x+\tfrac12)^{2i}/(2i)!$ plus terms of degree at most
$2i-2$.  Replacing one or more rows by those lower-degree terms
reduces the total degree by at least two.  The same alternating
argument then bounds their degree after translation by $d-2$.
The leading two terms are consequently obtained from
\begin{equation}\label{eq:evenVand}
 \det\left[\frac{(N+j+\tfrac12)^{2i}}{(2i)!}\right]_
           {0\leq i<m,\ j\in J_\ell}
 =\frac{\displaystyle\prod_{\substack{p<q\\p,q\in J_\ell}}
        (q-p)(2N+p+q+1)}{\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}(2i)!}.
\end{equation}
The relative coefficient of $N^{d-1}$ is
\[
 \frac12\sum_{\substack{p<q\\p,q\in J_\ell}}(p+q+1)
 =\frac12\left((m-1)\left(\frac{m(m+1)}2-\ell\right)+d\right),
\]
which simplifies to \eqref{eq:minor-subleading}.  If $d=1$, the
putative remainder of degree $d-2=-1$ is identically zero.
\end{proof}

It is useful to collect the minors in a polynomial in an auxiliary
variable $r$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Q}
 Q_m(N;r)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}(-1)^{m+\ell}M_{m,\ell}(N)r^\ell.
\end{equation}
By the binomial theorem,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Q-leading}
 [N^d]Q_m(N;r)=h_m(r-1)^m.
\end{equation}
When $r\ne1$ and $d\geq1$, the relative next coefficient is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Q-subleading}
 \frac{[N^{d-1}]Q_m(N;r)}{[N^d]Q_m(N;r)}
 =\frac{d(m+2)}2-d\frac{r}{r-1}
 =d\left(\frac m2-\frac1{r-1}\right).
\end{equation}
Indeed, applying $r\,\dd/\dd r$ to $(r-1)^m$ sums the terms weighted
by $\ell$ in \eqref{eq:minor-subleading}.  The exact value $d$, rather
than an upper bound $2d$, is what makes the denominator exponent sharp.

\section{Two exponentials and the minimal recurrence}\label{sec:recurrence}

Suppose first that $b\ne0$ and $a(a+4b)\ne0$.  Set $y=a/b$ and choose
one ordering of the two roots
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rroots}
 r_\pm=\frac{y+2\pm\sqrt{y(y+4)}}2,
 \qquad r_+r_-=1.
\end{equation}
They are distinct and nonzero.  Solving the recurrence
\eqref{eq:Rrec} with its two initial values gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Rclosed}
 R_n(y)=\frac{(r_+-1)r_+^n-(r_--1)r_-^n}{r_+-r_-}.
\end{equation}
Write
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lambda}
 \lambda_\pm=br_\pm
    =\frac{a+2b\pm\sqrt{a(a+4b)}}2.
\end{equation}
Substitution in \eqref{eq:minor-expansion} gives the exact identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:exactbranches}
 \D{N}{m}(a,b)=
 \frac{(r_+-1)Q_m(N;r_+)\lambda_+^N
       -(r_--1)Q_m(N;r_-)\lambda_-^N}
      {y^m(r_+-r_-)}.
\end{equation}
In particular, both polynomial coefficients in this two-exponential
representation have degree \emph{exactly} $d$: neither $r_+$ nor $r_-$
can equal $1$, since $y\ne0$.

\begin{lemma}[Minimal annihilator of an exponential polynomial]
\label{lem:minimal}
If $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_s$ are distinct nonzero complex numbers
and the nonzero polynomials $A_i(N)$ have degrees $d_i$, then
$u_N=\sum_i A_i(N)\lambda_i^N$ has minimal constant-coefficient
annihilator
\begin{equation}\label{eq:generalminimal}
 \prod_{i=1}^s(E-\lambda_i)^{d_i+1},\qquad Eu_N=u_{N+1}.
\end{equation}
Its generating function has poles of orders $d_i+1$ at
$t=1/\lambda_i$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
On $A(N)\lambda^N$, the operator $E-\lambda$ is
$\lambda^{N+1}(A(N+1)-A(N))$; it lowers the polynomial degree by
exactly one until the result vanishes.  For $\mu\ne\lambda$,
$E-\mu$ preserves that degree, multiplying the leading coefficient
by $\lambda-\mu$.  The generalized eigenspaces for distinct eigenvalues
are independent: polynomial projectors from the Chinese remainder
identity isolate each one.  Thus every annihilator needs the stated
multiplicity at every eigenvalue.  This argument remains valid after
any finite shift in $N$.

Equivalently, applying $(z\,\dd/\dd z)^k$ to $1/(1-z)$ shows that
$\sum_{N\geq0}N^k z^N$ has a pole of exact order $k+1$ at $z=1$,
with nonzero leading pole coefficient $k!$.  Distinct pole locations
cannot cancel.  This proves the generating-function assertion too.
\end{proof}

Applying the lemma to \eqref{eq:exactbranches} proves the minimal
characteristic polynomial \eqref{eq:charpoly}.  It also proves that
$q^{d+1}F_m$ is a polynomial of degree at most $2d+1=2e-1$ for these
parameters.  Section~\ref{sec:numerator} sharpens its degree to $K$.

\subsection{Why the identity extends to all parameters}
Each $D_N^{(m)}$ is a polynomial in $a,b$ with integer coefficients.
The coefficients of $q^{d+1}F_m$ are therefore such polynomials too.
Every coefficient that vanishes for all $b\ne0$ and $a(a+4b)\ne0$
vanishes identically: a polynomial cannot be nonzero and vanish on
that nonempty open set.  Hence the uniform rational identity,
and its coefficient integrality, extend to every $a,b$, even where
its displayed denominator is not reduced.  This argument is
coefficientwise in a formal series and does not require a convergence
limit in $t$.

\subsection{Proof of the exceptional classification}
When $b=0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bzero}
 \D{N}{m}(a,0)=a^N\HH{N}{m}.
\end{equation}
For $a\ne0$, the polynomial $H_N^{(m)}$ has degree $d$, so
Lemma~\ref{lem:minimal} gives denominator $(1-at)^{d+1}$.
When $a=0$ and $b\ne0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:azero}
 \D{N}{m}(0,b)=b^N\HH{N}{m+1},
\end{equation}
whose polynomial degree is $\binom{m+1}{2}$.

At the remaining collision $a=-4b$, with $b\ne0$, the recurrence gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Rminus4}
 R_n(-4)=(-1)^n(2n+1).
\end{equation}
Inserting this into \eqref{eq:minor-expansion} and tracking the signs
produces
\begin{align}
 \D{N}{m}(-4b,b)&=(-b)^N E_m(N),\label{eq:endpoint}\\
 E_m(N)&=4^{-m}\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}(2N+2\ell+1)M_{m,\ell}(N).
 \label{eq:E}
\end{align}
By \eqref{eq:minor-leading}, its degree is exactly $d+1$, with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:E-leading}
 [N^{d+1}]E_m(N)=4^{-m}\,2h_m\sum_{\ell=0}^m\binom m\ell
              =2^{1-m}h_m\ne0.
\end{equation}
Thus the reduced denominator is $(1+bt)^{d+2}$.
Finally, if $a=b=0$, every nonempty determinant vanishes and $F_m=1$.
Together with the nonexceptional result, this proves
Theorem~\ref{thm:exceptional}.

The two root-collision lines have different pole orders.  This is
not a contradiction: $x=0$ and $x=4$ are inequivalent endpoints of the
weight $x^{m-1/2}(4-x)^{1/2}$.  In particular, blindly substituting
$a=0$ into a generic minimal-order statement would be incorrect.

\section{The numerator degree and reciprocal symmetry}\label{sec:numerator}

A rational function can have a denominator of large degree and a
surprisingly short numerator.  Here the explanation is a string of
zero values in the natural bilateral extension of the determinant.

For the moment retain the nonexceptional hypotheses.  Define $D_N$ for
all $N\in\ZZ$ by the right side of \eqref{eq:smallD}.  The upper rows
are interpreted through their polynomial binomial coefficients; this
agrees with the Taylor coefficients of $R_n$ at zero, including
negative $n$, because
\[
 \binom{-n-1+i}{2i}=\binom{n+i}{2i}.
\]
It also agrees with the unique bilateral extension supplied by
\eqref{eq:exactbranches}.

\begin{lemma}[Reflection and a zero interval]\label{lem:bilateral}
Let $\sigma_m=(-1)^{\binom{m+1}{2}}$.  Then
\begin{align}
 D_{-N-m-1}&=\sigma_m b^{-2N-m-1}D_N\qquad(N\in\ZZ),
       \label{eq:Dreflect}\\
 D_{-1}=D_{-2}=\cdots=D_{-m}&=0.
       \label{eq:Dzeros}
\end{align}
The second statement is empty for $m=0$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The index change $N\mapsto-N-m-1$, followed by
$R_{-n-1}=R_n$, reverses all $m+1$ columns of the determinant.
The sign of that reversal is $\sigma_m$.  Comparing its prefactor
$b^N$ on the two sides gives \eqref{eq:Dreflect}.
If $N=-s$ with $1\leq s\leq m$, columns $j=s-1$ and $j=s$ correspond
to $R_{-1}$ and $R_0$, respectively, which are the same polynomial.
Their values and all needed Taylor coefficients coincide, so the
determinant vanishes.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[The expansion at infinity]\label{lem:infinity}
For a sequence of exponential-polynomial form with nonzero characteristic
roots and its bilateral extension, its proper rational generating
function satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:infinity}
 F(t)=-\sum_{r\geq1}D_{-r}t^{-r}
\end{equation}
as a formal Laurent expansion at infinity.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For $D_N=\lambda^N$ the assertion is simply
$(1-\lambda t)^{-1}=-\sum_{r\geq1}\lambda^{-r}t^{-r}$.
Apply powers of $t\,\dd/\dd t$ to obtain the assertion for
$D_N=N^k\lambda^N$, and then use linearity.
\end{proof}

Combining \eqref{eq:Dzeros}, \eqref{eq:Dreflect}, and $D_0=1$ yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:leadinginfinity}
 F_m(t)=-\sigma_m b^{-m-1}t^{-m-1}+O(t^{-m-2})
 \qquad(t\longrightarrow\infty).
\end{equation}
Since $q^e$ has degree $2e$ and leading coefficient $b^{2e}$, the
numerator has degree
\begin{equation}\label{eq:degreeK}
 2e-m-1=m(m-1)+2-m-1=(m-1)^2=K
\end{equation}
and leading coefficient $-\sigma_m b^{2e-m-1}=s_m b^K$.
The identity $s_m=-\sigma_m$ follows because the difference of the two
exponents $\binom{m+1}{2}$ and $(m-1)(m-2)/2$ is $2m-1$, an odd integer.
This calculation proves the sharp degree assertion, including $m=0$.

The full reflection identity yields more than the leading term.
Putting $1/(b^2t)$ into \eqref{eq:infinity} and reindexing with
\eqref{eq:Dreflect}, we find
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Freflect}
 F_m\!\left(\frac1{b^2t}\right)
 =s_m b^{m+1}t^{m+1}F_m(t).
\end{equation}
But $q(1/(b^2t))=q(t)/(b^2t^2)$.  Substitution into
\eqref{eq:Freflect} gives exactly \eqref{eq:reciprocity}.
After denominators in $b$ are cleared, each of these numerator identities
is polynomial in $a,b$.  Thus they extend to the exceptional parameters;
for $b\ne0$ the leading coefficient remains nonzero.  This completes the
proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}.

\subsection{An explicit finite prescription for the numerator}
No uncomputed infinite series is needed to determine $P_m$.  Expand
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qcoeffs}
 q(t)^e=\sum_{j=0}^{2e}q_jt^j.
\end{equation}
For example, its coefficients have the finite expression
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qexplicit}
 q_j=\sum_{v=\max(0,j-e)}^{\lfloor j/2\rfloor}
 \frac{e!}{(j-2v)!v!(e-j+v)!}
       (-(a+2b))^{j-2v}b^{2v}.
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Pcompute}
 P_m(t)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{K}
 \left(\sum_{j=0}^{\min(\ell,2e)}q_j\D{\ell-j}{m}(a,b)\right)t^\ell,
\end{equation}
where every determinant in the right side is given by
Theorem~\ref{thm:coeff}.  Equations~\eqref{eq:qexplicit} and
\eqref{eq:Pcompute} therefore constitute a finite sum/product formula
for every numerator coefficient.  All coefficients of $q^eF_m$ after
degree $K$ vanish.

The usual recurrence is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:recurrence}
 \sum_{j=0}^{2e}q_j\D{N-j}{m}(a,b)=0\qquad(N\geq2e).
\end{equation}
In fact the truncated convolution already vanishes for $N>K$ when
negative-index terms are taken as zero for the purpose of multiplying
ordinary power series.  Those artificial zeros should not be confused
with the distinct bilateral extension used in the proof above.

\section{Exact exponential expansions and asymptotics}\label{sec:asymptotics}

Equation~\eqref{eq:exactbranches} supplies every coefficient of the two
polynomials in Theorem~\ref{thm:asymp}.  Explicitly,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Bpolys}
 B_+(N)=\frac{(r_+-1)Q_m(N;r_+)}{y^m(r_+-r_-)},\qquad
 B_-(N)=-\frac{(r_--1)Q_m(N;r_-)}{y^m(r_+-r_-)}.
\end{equation}
These formulas are valid for arbitrary nonexceptional complex parameters;
the assumption $a,b>0$ is only needed to select a strictly dominant real
exponential for the asymptotic statement.

For $a,b>0$, write $r=r_+$, so $r_-=r^{-1}$ and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:yparam}
 y=r+r^{-1}-2=\frac{(r-1)^2}{r}.
\end{equation}
Using \eqref{eq:Q-leading}, the coefficient of $N^d$ in $B_+$ is
\[
 \frac{h_m(r-1)^{m+1}}{y^m(r-r^{-1})}
 =h_m\frac{r^{m+1}}{(r-1)^m(r+1)}=A_m.
\]
Its relative next coefficient is exactly \eqref{eq:Q-subleading}.
Also $\lambda_-/\lambda_+=r^{-2}$.  Division of
\eqref{eq:exactbranches} by $A_mN^d\lambda_+^N$ proves
\eqref{eq:asymp}.

One may write the complete answer in the finite form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fullfinite}
 \D{N}{m}=\lambda_+^N\sum_{j=0}^{d}\beta_j^+N^{d-j}
          +\lambda_-^N\sum_{j=0}^{d}\beta_j^-N^{d-j},
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}\label{eq:allbetas}
 \beta_j^+=\frac{r_+-1}{y^m(r_+-r_-)}[N^{d-j}]Q_m(N;r_+),\qquad
 \beta_j^-=-\frac{r_--1}{y^m(r_+-r_-)}[N^{d-j}]Q_m(N;r_-).
\end{equation}
Thus the ``full asymptotic expansion'' is particularly strong here:
it is an exact expression, with no uncomputed remainder once both
finite sectors are retained.  No assertion of asymptotic convergence
or summability is needed.  The leading coefficient ratio of the two
sectors is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:branchratio}
 \frac{\beta_0^-}{\beta_0^+}=(-1)^m r^{-(m+1)}.
\end{equation}

These estimates are for fixed $m,a,b$.  They are not claimed to be
uniform when $a\to0$, when the two roots coalesce, or when $m$ grows
with $N$.  In such regimes the exact finite formula remains valid
away from the collision itself, but a different uniform asymptotic
analysis would be required.  At a collision, use the exact exceptional
formulas from Section~\ref{sec:recurrence} instead.

\section{Worked examples and integer sequences}\label{sec:examples}

\subsection{Small shifts}
The first uniform generating functions are
\begin{align}
 F_0(t)&=\frac{1-bt}{q(t)},&
 F_1(t)&=\frac1{q(t)},\label{eq:low01}\\
 F_2(t)&=\frac{1+bt}{q(t)^2},&
 F_3(t)&=\frac{(1-b^2t^2)(1+(a+6b)t+b^2t^2)}{q(t)^4}.
 \label{eq:low23}
\end{align}
They can be obtained directly from \eqref{eq:Pcompute}.  Notice that
$m=0$ is a genuine boundary case: its numerator has degree $1$, not $0$.
For $m=3$, the numerator is anti-reciprocal; for $m=2$, it is reciprocal.
This is the pattern described uniformly by $s_m$.

For $m=2$, put $A=a+2b$.  The recurrence becomes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:m2rec}
 D_N=2A D_{N-1}-(A^2+2b^2)D_{N-2}
         +2Ab^2D_{N-3}-b^4D_{N-4}\qquad(N\geq4).
\end{equation}
The initial polynomials are
\begin{align*}
 D_0&=1,\\
 D_1&=2a+5b,\\
 D_2&=3a^2+14ab+14b^2,\\
 D_3&=4a^3+27a^2b+54ab^2+30b^3.
\end{align*}
The recurrence is minimal outside the exceptional lines; displaying
only a valid order-four relation would not by itself prove that fact.

\subsection{The specialization \texorpdfstring{$a=b=1$}{a=b=1}}
The first sequences, all beginning with the empty determinant, are
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.2}
\begin{tabular}{@{}r rrrrrrr@{}}
\toprule
$m\backslash N$ & 0 & 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 & 6\\
\midrule
0 & 1 & 2 & 5 & 13 & 34 & 89 & 233\\
1 & 1 & 3 & 8 & 21 & 55 & 144 & 377\\
2 & 1 & 7 & 31 & 115 & 390 & 1254 & 3893\\
3 & 1 & 19 & 170 & 1075 & 5580 & 25529 & 107036\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The first two are $F_{2N+1}$ and $F_{2N+2}$, respectively, as follows
from their common recurrence $u_{N+2}=3u_{N+1}-u_N$ and initial values.
They connect the family to the odd and even Fibonacci subsequences in
OEIS A001519 and A001906~\cite{OEISfib}; Catalan numbers themselves are
A000108~\cite{OEIScat}.  The higher rows and their coefficient triangles
are natural OEIS-style objects, but the archive does not assert that
any particular row is absent from OEIS.

For every fixed shift, the dominant exponential is
\[
 \lambda_+=\frac{3+\sqrt5}{2},
\]
while its polynomial prefactor has degree $\binom m2$.  Thus increasing
the shift changes the power of $N$, not the exponential growth constant.
For $m=2$, the first relative asymptotic correction equals
$1-1/(r-1)=(3-\sqrt5)/2$ with $r=(3+\sqrt5)/2$.

\subsection{Rational characteristic roots: \texorpdfstring{$a=1,b=2$}{a=1,b=2}}
This specialization gives $r_+=2$, $r_-=1/2$ and
$\lambda_+=4$, $\lambda_-=1$.  Therefore the exact formulas have
rational polynomial coefficients and no radicals:
\begin{align}
 \D{N}{0}(1,2)&=\frac{2\cdot4^N+1}{3},\label{eq:example0}\\
 \D{N}{1}(1,2)&=\frac{4\cdot4^N-1}{3},\label{eq:example1}\\
 \D{N}{2}(1,2)&=\frac{8N\,4^N+N+3}{3},\label{eq:example2}\\
 \D{N}{3}(1,2)&=
 \frac{(32N^3+48N^2+16N+96)4^N
       -(2N^3+21N^2+73N+78)}{18}.
 \label{eq:example3}
\end{align}
For example, the $m=2$ sequence starts
$1,12,87,514,2733,13656,\ldots$.  Its dominant polynomial is
$(8/3)N$ with no constant term.  This vanishing agrees with the
first-correction formula $d(m/2-1/(r-1))=1(1-1)=0$; the entire
correction is the smaller exponential sector $(N+3)/3$.
For $m=3$, the relative coefficient of $N^{-1}$ in the dominant sector
is $3/2$, again exactly as predicted.

\subsection{An endpoint example}
For $m=2$, \eqref{eq:E} simplifies to
\[
 E_2(N)=\frac{(N+1)(N+2)}2.
\]
Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:endpointexample}
 \D{N}{2}(-4b,b)=(-b)^N\frac{(N+1)(N+2)}2,
 \qquad F_2(t;-4b,b)=\frac1{(1+bt)^3}.
\end{equation}
This also follows by cancelling $1+bt$ from
$(1+bt)/(1+bt)^4$, the uniform form of $F_2$.
At the other collision, $a=0$, one instead obtains
$F_2(t;0,b)=(1+bt)/(1-bt)^4$.  These explicit cases illustrate why the
reduced denominator needs a separate classification.

\section{Algorithms, exact experiments, and reproducibility}\label{sec:code}

\subsection{Implementation choices}
The file \texttt{code/catalan\_hankel.py} implements the coefficient
formula, pure shifted product, homogeneous polynomial evaluation,
fixed-size determinant, and uniform and reduced denominators.  All
numerical parameters in its public computation interface are integers;
internal fractions use Python's exact \texttt{Fraction}.  The
mathematical theorems have the broader complex-parameter scope stated
above.  No floating-point arithmetic enters the tests.

For a coefficient row, first compute the triangular product
$H_N^{(m+1)}$ and then apply \eqref{eq:coeff-update} successively.
The number of arithmetic operations is $O(m^2+N)$; this is an
\emph{arithmetic-operation count}, not a bit-complexity claim.
Every division in the update is checked for exactness.  Homogeneous
Horner evaluation avoids division by $a$ or $b$, so zero parameters
are handled without special numerical limits.

The independent determinant path uses a fraction-free Bareiss algorithm
on the original matrix of Catalan moments, including pivoting when a
diagonal pivot vanishes.  This matters for signed parameters: an
implementation tested only on positive definite examples can conceal
pivot failures.  The source-indexing formula is implemented separately,
not obtained by calling the corrected coefficient routine.

For many consecutive values at fixed $m$, it is also possible to use
\eqref{eq:recurrence} after producing the first $2e$ values.  For very
large isolated indices, powering the companion matrix or reducing
$X^N$ modulo \eqref{eq:charpoly} provides standard recurrence-based
methods.  These methods are consequences of the theorem; the supplied
reference implementation prioritizes transparent exact verification
rather than optimized large-integer throughput.

\section{What has been resolved, and what has not}\label{sec:status}

The all-shift denominator formula is proved here, with a stronger
minimality assertion and a complete description of the degenerate
parameters.  The coefficient expression is likewise proved for all
$N,m$; comparison with the exact formula printed in the target source
requires the explicit indexing correction in the next appendix.
The reciprocal numerator and finite exponential expansions are proved
from the same structural determinant calculation.

These are substantive mathematical conclusions about the selected
conjectural family.  They are not evidence that the Christoffel identity,
Jacobi representation, Catalan shifted product, or general rationality
are new.  A literature search was performed against the source title,
arXiv identifier, denominator phrase, and related recurrence work.
The cited version still labels the sharp exponent conjectural, and the
retrieved sources did not supply an explicit statement of this full
sharp classification.  That is a bounded search result, not a proof
that no earlier equivalent theorem exists.  Before a priority claim
or submission, the exact formulas should be checked against the wider
orthogonal-polynomial and determinant literature and by a human referee.

Several natural extensions remain separate from the theorem.  One may
seek a direct combinatorial interpretation of the individual positive
integers $c_{N,m,k}$, or a product formula for the minors
$M_{m,\ell}(N)$ beyond their first two coefficients.  Multiple distinct
linear factors in the multiplier lead to more exponential sectors and
new collision patterns.  A joint limit with growing $m$ or a parameter
approaching $a=0$ would require uniform, rather than fixed-parameter,
asymptotics.  None of these extensions is assumed in the present proofs.

For a nonzero polynomial $q$, let $H_N(q)$ be the size-$N$ Hankel
determinant of the Catalan moments multiplied by $q$.  General
constant-coefficient recurrences for this family are classical.  We
determine their sharp multiplicities.  Write
$q(x)=\gamma x^m(x-4)^\ell\prod_i(x-c_i)^{t_i}$, with distinct
$c_i\notin\{0,4\}$, and put $c_i=2+z_i+z_i^{-1}$.
The branch-labelled exponential with rate
$(-1)^{\ell+\sum t_i}\gamma\prod_i z_i^{2k_i-t_i}$ has a polynomial
coefficient whose degree is exactly
$\binom m2+\binom{\ell+1}2+\sum_i k_i(t_i-k_i)$.
A nonvanishing confluent-Vandermonde product gives its leading coefficient.
Consequently, when the rates are distinct, the minimal recurrence order is
$\prod_i(t_i+1)\bigl(\binom m2+\binom{\ell+1}2+1
+\frac16\sum_i t_i(t_i-1)\bigr)$.
We prove an exact secondary-Hankel factorization that detects every loss
of this maximal order, and give a finite, root-free algorithm determining
the minimal recurrence at all rational or algebraic specializations.
For one repeated nonendpoint root the generic order is cubic in the
multiplicity, rather than the classical exponential bound.  The example
$q=(2x-9)^4$ has order $15$, while resonant fourth powers can have order $5$.
The proofs extend the repository's linear-multiplier result to arbitrary
root-multiplicity patterns, including both endpoints, and are accompanied
by exact-arithmetic verification code.

\section{The research question and its provenance}\label{rc:sec:scope}

\subsection{A concrete extension of the ProveIt report}
The starting point is the report \emph{Shifted Catalan Hankel Polynomials:
Exact coefficients, sharp recurrences, and a denominator conjecture},
dated 19 September 2026, in ProveIt, printed above as Part~I
(\S\ref{rc:sec:source} records the pinned snapshot).  For
\[
 D_N^{(m)}(a,b)=\det_{0\le i,j<N}
                  \bigl(aC_{i+j+m}+bC_{i+j+m+1}\bigr),
 \qquad C_n=\frac1{n+1}\binom{2n}{n},
\]
it proves the sharp characteristic polynomial
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:old}
 \bigl(X^2-(a+2b)X+b^2\bigr)^{\binom m2+1}
\end{equation}
when $b\ne0$ and $a(a+4b)\ne0$ (this is \eqref{eq:charpoly} of
Theorem~\ref{thm:main}).  Its concluding discussion
(Section~\ref{sec:status}) explicitly leaves multiple linear factors and
their collision patterns for further work.  That is the extension
addressed here.

The repository was inspected at commit
\begin{center}\small\texttt{fbba58593dc0622aa914972896150d4848f935b5}.\end{center}
The relevant source is under
\begin{quote}\small\raggedright
\nolinkurl{SetTheory/Cardinals/docs/reports/hankel-determinants/}\allowbreak
\nolinkurl{catalan-and-ballot/shifted-catalan-hankel-polynomials/article.tex}.
\end{quote}
The present proof does not assume that an unrefereed repository manuscript
is correct.  We rederive the determinant identity and the local degree
calculus, then recover \eqref{rc:eq:old} as a specialization.

The question is not merely whether another rational generating function
exists.  It is this:
\begin{quote}
Given every root and its multiplicity in a polynomial multiplier, which
exponential terms actually occur, what is the exact polynomial degree
of each term, and precisely when does the minimal recurrence order drop?
\end{quote}
A repeated root is not equivalent to several independently chosen roots.
The associated determinant forces cancellations within a repeated block.
Those cancellations are the main subject of this article.

\subsection{What is classical, and what is being added}
The Christoffel determinant formula and its confluent form are classical;
Krattenthaler's treatment~\cite{Krattenthaler2023} gives several proofs and explicitly
handles coalescing evaluation points.  Its Section~8, Corollary~9, supplies
a constant-coefficient recurrence of order at most $2^D$ for a multiplier
of degree $D$, including Catalan moments.  That account also credits
Elouafi's earlier proof of the Catalan recurrence assertion.  General
rationality is therefore not a new conclusion of this article.

Barry's note~\cite{Barry2020} motivates the shifted linear combination and
its quadratic-denominator exponent.  The earlier work of Cigler and
Krattenthaler~\cite{CiglerKratt2021} gives further context for polynomial
linear combinations of moments.  Our comparison is with these particular
sources and the pinned repository report, not with an exhaustive
classification of the literature.

The additions proved below are an exact degree law for every repeated-root
block, its nonzero leading constant with endpoint factors included, the
resulting sharp recurrence and a factorization detecting its exceptional
locus.  A root-free finite procedure handles resonances without assuming
that a generic formula remains minimal after specialization.  The
underlying determinant techniques are familiar; the full multiplicity
law and its assembled consequences are the proposed research contribution.
An equivalent earlier formulation in the wider orthogonal-polynomial or
symmetric-function literature has not been ruled out.

\section{Definitions and principal results}\label{rc:sec:main}

\subsection{The weighted determinant and its spectral parameters}
For $q(x)=\sum_{r=0}^{D}q_rx^r\in\CC[x]\setminus\{0\}$, define
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:HN}
 H_N(q)=\det_{0\le i,j<N}\left(\sum_{r=0}^{D}q_r C_{i+j+r}\right),
 \qquad N\in\NN,\qquad H_0(q)=1.
\end{equation}
Write its factorization as
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:qfactor}
 q(x)=\gamma x^m(x-4)^\ell\prod_{i=1}^{s}(x-c_i)^{t_i},
\end{equation}
where $\gamma\ne0$, $m,\ell\in\NN$, $t_i\ge1$, and the $c_i$ are pairwise
distinct and avoid $0,4$.  Thus
\[
 T=\sum_{i=1}^{s}t_i,\qquad D=m+\ell+T.
\]
An empty product is $1$.  Constant multipliers are included by taking
$m=\ell=s=0$.  The identically zero multiplier is excluded; its sequence
would simply be $1,0,0,\ldots$.

For each $i$ choose a solution of
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:spectral}
 c_i=2+z_i+z_i^{-1}.
\end{equation}
Then $z_i\in\CC^\times\setminus\{1,-1\}$.  Replacing $z_i$ by $z_i^{-1}$
only relabels an index below.  No final recurrence depends on this choice.
Define
\begin{align}
 \mathcal K&=\prod_{i=1}^{s}\{0,1,\ldots,t_i\},\label{rc:eq:Kset}\\
 \kappa&=\binom m2+\binom{\ell+1}2,\label{rc:eq:kappa}\\
 \lambda_{\bk}&=(-1)^{\ell+T}\gamma
                       \prod_{i=1}^{s}z_i^{2k_i-t_i},\label{rc:eq:lambda}\\
 d_{\bk}&=\kappa+\sum_{i=1}^{s}k_i(t_i-k_i),
 \qquad r_{\bk}=d_{\bk}+1.\label{rc:eq:degree}
\end{align}
When $s=0$, the index set $\mathcal K$ has one element, the empty tuple.

\begin{definition}[Resonance]
The parameters are \emph{nonresonant} if the values
$\lambda_{\bk}$, $\bk\in\mathcal K$, are pairwise distinct.
Otherwise there is a \emph{resonance}.  Equivalently, a resonance is an
identity
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:resonance}
 \prod_i z_i^{2(k_i-j_i)}=1
 \quad\text{for some distinct }\bk,\bj\in\mathcal K.
\end{equation}
This is different from a repeated root of $q$: repeated roots have already
been recorded by the $t_i$.
\end{definition}

\subsection{The exact multiplicity law}
\begin{theorem}[Branch degrees and minimal recurrence]\label{rc:thm:main}
For every multiplier \eqref{rc:eq:qfactor}, there are canonically
branch-labelled polynomials $P_{\bk}\in\CC[N]$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:sectors}
 H_N(q)=\sum_{\bk\in\mathcal K}P_{\bk}(N)\lambda_{\bk}^{N}
 \qquad(N\ge0),
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:exactdegree}
 \deg P_{\bk}=d_{\bk}\quad\text{for every }\bk.
\end{equation}
Their leading coefficients are given by the nonzero product in
Theorem~\ref{rc:thm:leading}.  These individual assertions remain valid at
resonances, when different branch labels can have the same numerical rate.

If the parameters are nonresonant, the minimal characteristic polynomial
of the sequence is
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:minchar}
 \chi_q(X)=\prod_{\bk\in\mathcal K}
                         (X-\lambda_{\bk})^{d_{\bk}+1}.
\end{equation}
Its degree is
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:R}
 \boxed{\quad
 \Rzero=\prod_{i=1}^{s}(t_i+1)
       \left(\kappa+1+\frac16\sum_{i=1}^{s}t_i(t_i-1)\right).
 \quad}
\end{equation}
For arbitrary parameters, \eqref{rc:eq:minchar} is still an annihilating
polynomial of degree $\Rzero$.  The true minimal order equals $\Rzero$
if and only if the parameters are nonresonant.
\end{theorem}

A characteristic polynomial $g(X)=\sum_{j=0}^r g_jX^j$, with $g_r=1$,
means the recurrence
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:recconvention}
 \sum_{j=0}^r g_jH_{N+j}(q)=0\qquad\text{for every }N\ge0.
\end{equation}
Thus the assertions concern the entire sequence, including its initial
values, not only an eventual recurrence.  A polynomial of minimal degree
with this property is unique.

\begin{corollary}[One repeated nonendpoint root]\label{rc:cor:single}
Let $q(x)=\gamma(x-c)^t$, where $c\notin\{0,4\}$ and
$c=2+z+z^{-1}$.  If $z^{2h}\ne1$ for $1\le h\le t$, then
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:singleorder}
 \ord(H(q))=t+1+\binom{t+1}{3}.
\end{equation}
The exponential with rate $(-1)^t\gamma z^{2k-t}$ has polynomial degree
$k(t-k)$.  In particular the first orders, for $t=1,\ldots,8$, are
\[
 2,\ 4,\ 8,\ 15,\ 26,\ 42,\ 64,\ 93.
\]
\end{corollary}

The order in \eqref{rc:eq:singleorder} is asymptotic to $t^3/6$, in contrast
to the universal bound $2^t$.  The first strict improvement occurs at
$t=4$.  This is a uniform family of exact minimality results, rather than
an isolated fitted recurrence.

\begin{corollary}[Simple factors and endpoints]\label{rc:cor:simple}
If every $t_i=1$ and the parameters are nonresonant, the minimal order is
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:simpleorder}
 2^s\left(\binom m2+\binom{\ell+1}2+1\right).
\end{equation}
If $s=0$, no nonresonance hypothesis is needed: $H_N(q)$ is
$((-1)^\ell\gamma)^N$ times a polynomial of degree exactly $\kappa$.
The minimal polynomial is
$(X-(-1)^\ell\gamma)^{\kappa+1}$.
\end{corollary}

The asymmetry between $0$ and $4$ is essential.  A multiplicity $m$ at
$0$ contributes $\binom m2$ degrees, whereas a multiplicity $\ell$ at
$4$ contributes $\binom{\ell+1}2$.  Treating both endpoints as a generic
collision of two equally weighted branches gives the wrong answer.

\subsection{An exact detector for exceptional parameters}
Put $L_{\bk}=\lc(P_{\bk})$, and choose any ordering of $\mathcal K$.
\begin{theorem}[Secondary Hankel factorization]\label{rc:thm:secondary}
With $\Rzero=\sum_{\bk}r_{\bk}$, one has
\begin{align}
 &\det_{0\le u,v<\Rzero}\bigl(H_{u+v}(q)\bigr)\nonumber\\
 &\quad=(-1)^{\sum_{\bk}\binom{r_{\bk}}2}
   \prod_{\bk}
     \left((d_{\bk}!\,L_{\bk})^{r_{\bk}}
                 \lambda_{\bk}^{r_{\bk}(r_{\bk}-1)}\right)
   \prod_{\bk<\bj}
     (\lambda_{\bj}-\lambda_{\bk})^{2r_{\bk}r_{\bj}}.
 \label{rc:eq:secondary}
\end{align}
This identity holds at resonant as well as nonresonant parameters.
All factors outside the final product are nonzero.  Consequently this
secondary determinant vanishes exactly on the resonance locus.
\end{theorem}

The formula is stronger than a statement that a generic matrix has full
rank.  It identifies every source of rank loss and its multiplicity in
spectral coordinates.  Sections~\ref{rc:sec:recurrences} and
\ref{rc:sec:resonances} prove this assertion and make it algorithmic.

\section{Catalan orthogonality and the fixed-size determinant}\label{rc:sec:classical}

This section proves the classical infrastructure with our normalization.
It is included to make the signs, initial index and confluent factorials
fully auditable.  The general identities are not claimed as new.

\subsection{The orthogonal polynomials}
Define monic polynomials by
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:prec}
 p_0(x)=1,\qquad p_1(x)=x-1,\qquad
 p_{n+1}(x)=(x-2)p_n(x)-p_{n-1}(x)\quad(n\ge1).
\end{equation}
The Catalan probability measure is
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:measure}
 \dd\mu(x)=\frac1{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{4-x}{x}}\,
                       \mathbf1_{(0,4)}(x)\,\dd x.
\end{equation}
Substitution $x=4u$ and the beta integral give
\[
 \int x^n\,\dd\mu(x)
 =\frac{2\cdot4^n}{\pi}B\left(n+\frac12,\frac32\right)
 =\frac{(2n)!}{n!(n+1)!}=C_n.
\]
There is also an elementary trigonometric verification of the orthogonality.
For $x=2+2\cos\theta$, $0<\theta<\pi$, the recurrence gives
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:ptrig}
 p_n(x)=\frac{\sin((n+\tfrac12)\theta)}{\sin(\theta/2)},
 \qquad \dd\mu=\frac{1-\cos\theta}{\pi}\,\dd\theta.
\end{equation}
The product-to-sum formula then yields
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:orthogonality}
 \int p_n(x)p_j(x)\,\dd\mu(x)=\delta_{n,j}.
\end{equation}
Indeed, after cancellation of $\sin^2(\theta/2)$ the integral is
$\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi\sin((n+\tfrac12)\theta)
\sin((j+\tfrac12)\theta)\,\dd\theta$.
The change from monomials to monic $p_n$ is triangular with determinant
$1$.  Hence \eqref{rc:eq:orthogonality} also proves
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:purehankel}
 \det_{0\le i,j<N}(C_{i+j})=1.
\end{equation}
\noindent\emph{Relation to Part~I.}  The measure $\mu$ is Part~I's
$\mu_0$ of \eqref{eq:measure}, and $p_n$ is Part~I's $p_n^{(0)}$, with the
same recurrence \eqref{eq:p0rec}.  Part~I proves
\eqref{rc:eq:purehankel}, $H_N^{(0)}=1$, from the constant squared norms
of the $p_n^{(0)}$ (Section~\ref{sec:coeffproof}); the trigonometric
orthogonality above is a second route, kept as the manuscript printed it.

\subsection{Christoffel's determinant, including confluence}
List the distinct roots in \eqref{rc:eq:qfactor} in the order
$0,4,c_1,\ldots,c_s$, omitting endpoints whose multiplicities are zero.
Write this list as $(a_1,\nu_1),\ldots,(a_b,\nu_b)$ and set
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:delta}
 \Delta_q=\prod_{1\le u<v\le b}(a_v-a_u)^{\nu_u\nu_v}.
\end{equation}
It is nonzero.  Let $M_N$ have rows indexed by $(a_u,r)$,
$0\le r<\nu_u$, and columns indexed by $j=0,\ldots,D-1$, with entries
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:MN}
 (M_N)_{(a_u,r),j}=\frac{p_{N+j}^{(r)}(a_u)}{r!}.
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}[Confluent Christoffel identity]\label{rc:lem:Christoffel}
For all $N\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:Christoffel}
 H_N(q)=(-1)^{ND}\gamma^N\frac{\det M_N}{\Delta_q}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
First suppose all roots $a_1,\ldots,a_D$ are distinct.  For an ordered
list $y$, write $V(y)=\prod_{i<j}(y_j-y_i)$.  Expanding two determinants
under the integral and using the product measure proves the elementary
integration identity
\[
 \int\det(f_i(x_j))_{i,j=1}^{N}\det(g_i(x_j))_{i,j=1}^{N}
       \prod_j\dd\mu(x_j)
 =N!\det\left(\int f_i(x)g_j(x)\,\dd\mu(x)\right)_{i,j=1}^{N}.
\]
Taking monomials with the multiplier included gives
\[
 H_N(q)=\frac1{N!}\int V(x_1,\ldots,x_N)^2
                      \prod_{i=1}^N q(x_i)\,\dd\mu(x_i).
\]
Since
\[
 V(x,a)=V(x)V(a)\prod_{i,u}(a_u-x_i),
\]
we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:mixedintegral}
 H_N(q)=\frac{(-1)^{ND}\gamma^N}{N!V(a)}
             \int V(x)V(x,a)\prod_i\dd\mu(x_i).
\end{equation}
Replace the monomials in both Vandermonde determinants by the monic
polynomials $p_j$.  Expand $V(x,a)$ along its first $N$ rows.  On
integration against $V(x)$, orthogonality kills every selected set of
columns except $0,1,\ldots,N-1$.  That surviving integral is $N!$, and
its complementary minor is $(p_{N+j}(a_u))_{u,j}$.  The Laplace sign is
positive because the selected columns are the first $N$.  This proves
\eqref{rc:eq:Christoffel} for distinct roots.

Now let $\nu$ distinct roots tend to a common value $a$.  Taylor expansion
shows that division by their internal Vandermonde replaces their
successive evaluation rows by the rows $p_{N+j}^{(r)}(a)/r!$,
$r=0,\ldots,\nu-1$.  Cross-block differences tend to the product
\eqref{rc:eq:delta}.  Applying this separately to every block gives the
stated formula.  Equivalently, both sides are polynomial identities in
the root variables after the removable Vandermonde factors are canceled.
Thus the limiting argument does not assume that any weighted Hankel
determinant is nonzero.  At $N=0$, both the empty moment determinant and
the normalized confluent Vandermonde equal $1$.
\end{proof}

The important computational consequence is that the determinant on the
right has size $D$, independent of $N$.  The important theoretical
consequence is different: all dependence on $N$ has been localized in
jets of a two-branch orthogonal-polynomial sequence.

\noindent\emph{Relation to Part~I.}  Lemma~\ref{lem:Christoffel} of
Part~I is the case $\gamma=1$, roots $a_u=-y_u$, in the reversed
normalization $R_n(y)=(-1)^np_n(-y)$ of \eqref{eq:R}; its proof follows
the same integration-and-Laplace route for distinct roots.  Part~I then takes the
confluent limit only for the $m$-fold root at $0$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:confluent}).  Lemma~\ref{rc:lem:Christoffel} adds
confluence at every root, both endpoints, and the leading coefficient
$\gamma$ with its sign, so it is printed in full.

\section{Local jet structure}\label{rc:sec:jets}

\subsection{The two branches away from the endpoints}
The characteristic equation of \eqref{rc:eq:prec}, at $x=2+z+z^{-1}$, has
roots $z,z^{-1}$.  Matching the first two values gives
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:branch}
 p_n(2+z+z^{-1})=\frac{z^{n+1}-z^{-n}}{z-1}
       =A_+(z)z^n+A_-(z)z^{-n},
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:AandS}
 A_+(z)=\frac{z}{z-1},\qquad A_-(z)=-\frac1{z-1},\qquad
 s(z)=\frac1{z-z^{-1}}.
\end{equation}
The letter $s(z)$ is a scalar function, not the number $s$ of distinct
nonendpoint roots.  For clarity we often write $s_i=s(z_i)$.
The inverse function theorem applies locally because $z\ne\pm1$, and
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:logderivative}
 \frac{\dd}{\dd x}\log z(x)=s(z),\qquad
 \frac{\dd}{\dd x}=z\,s(z)\frac{\dd}{\dd z}.
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}[Offendpoint jets]\label{rc:lem:offjets}
For $c=2+z+z^{-1}\notin\{0,4\}$ and $r\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:offjets}
 \frac{p_n^{(r)}(c)}{r!}
   =z^n A_{r,+}(n;z)+z^{-n}A_{r,-}(n;z),
\end{equation}
where $A_{r,\pm}(n;z)$ are polynomials of degree exactly $r$ in $n$.
Their leading coefficients are
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:offlc}
 [n^r]A_{r,\pm}(n;z)=\frac{A_\pm(z)(\pm s(z))^r}{r!}.
\end{equation}
They can be constructed recursively from $A_{0,\pm}=A_\pm$ by
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:Arec}
 A_{r+1,\pm}(n;z)=\frac{z s(z)\,\partial_z A_{r,\pm}(n;z)
                   \ \pm\ n s(z)A_{r,\pm}(n;z)}{r+1}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Differentiate \eqref{rc:eq:offjets} once and use \eqref{rc:eq:logderivative}.
The normalization by $(r+1)!$ gives \eqref{rc:eq:Arec}.  The derivative
term does not increase the degree in $n$, while multiplication by
$\pm ns(z)$ increases it by one.  Induction gives \eqref{rc:eq:offlc}.
All its factors are nonzero, so the degree is exact.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The two endpoint jets}
From \eqref{rc:eq:prec},
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:pGF}
 \sum_{n\ge0}p_n(x)u^n=\frac{1+u}{1-(x-2)u+u^2}.
\end{equation}
Differentiation in $x$, normalized by $r!$, gives
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:jetGF}
 \sum_{n\ge0}\frac{p_n^{(r)}(x)}{r!}u^n
       =\frac{(1+u)u^r}{(1-(x-2)u+u^2)^{r+1}}.
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}[Endpoint degrees]\label{rc:lem:endjets}
For all $n,r\ge0$,
\begin{align}
 \frac{p_n^{(r)}(0)}{r!}
   &=(-1)^{n-r}\binom{n+r}{2r},\label{rc:eq:zerojet}\\
 \frac{p_n^{(r)}(4)}{r!}
   &=\frac{2n+1}{2r+1}\binom{n+r}{2r}.\label{rc:eq:fourjet}
\end{align}
After the common factor $(-1)^n$ is removed at $0$, the polynomial
in $n$ has degree $2r$ and leading coefficient $(-1)^r/(2r)!$.
At $4$ the degree is $2r+1$, with leading coefficient $2/(2r+1)!$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
At $x=0$, \eqref{rc:eq:jetGF} becomes $u^r/(1+u)^{2r+1}$, which gives
\eqref{rc:eq:zerojet}.  At $x=4$ it becomes
$(1+u)u^r/(1-u)^{2r+2}$.  The coefficient is
\[
 \binom{n+r+1}{2r+1}+\binom{n+r}{2r+1}
   =\frac{2n+1}{2r+1}\binom{n+r}{2r}.
\]
The assertions on leading coefficients follow immediately.  For $n<r$
the binomial coefficient is zero, as it must be for a derivative whose
order exceeds the polynomial degree.
\end{proof}

The same quadratic recurrence has two different endpoint amplitudes:
$p_n(0)=(-1)^n$ but $p_n(4)=2n+1$.  This single extra degree at $4$ is
what ultimately changes $\binom\ell2$ into $\binom{\ell+1}2$.

\section{The determinant degree calculus}\label{rc:sec:degreecalculus}

\subsection{Polynomial shifts and confluent exponential rows}
The next two identities isolate the cancellation responsible for the
sharp order.  If $f$ is a polynomial of degree $d$ with leading
coefficient $a$, then
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:shiftcoeff}
 [j^h]f(N+j)\text{ has degree at most }d-h\text{ in }N,
 \qquad
 [N^{d-h}j^h]f(N+j)=a\binom dh.
\end{equation}
An expression with $h>d$ is interpreted as zero.

For distinct nonzero numbers $w_1,\ldots,w_b$ and positive integers
$u_1,\ldots,u_b$ summing to $D$, form the matrix
\[
 V_{(a,h),j}=w_a^j j^h,
 \qquad 0\le h<u_a,\quad 0\le j<D.
\]
The convention $0^0=1$ is used only in the polynomial value $j^0$.

\begin{lemma}[Confluent exponential Vandermonde]\label{rc:lem:V}
In the indicated block order,
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:V}
 \det V=
 \prod_{a=1}^{b}\left(w_a^{\binom{u_a}2}
                         \prod_{h=0}^{u_a-1}h!\right)
 \prod_{a<b}(w_b-w_a)^{u_a u_b}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Start with the ordinary Vandermonde determinant and coalesce $u_a$
variables to each $w_a$.  The matrix of ordinary, unnormalized
derivatives of $w^j$ then has determinant
$\prod_a\prod_{h<u_a}h!\prod_{a<b}(w_b-w_a)^{u_a u_b}$.
Within a block replace $\partial_w^h$ by $(w\partial_w)^h$.
This is a triangular change of rows: the coefficient of
$\partial_w^h$ is $w^h$, and all other terms have smaller derivative
order.  The determinant is multiplied by
$\prod_a w_a^{0+1+\cdots+(u_a-1)}$.  Since
$(w\partial_w)^h w^j=j^h w^j$, the resulting matrix is $V$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Three local leading determinants}
For an endpoint block of size $u$, the leading matrices obtained from
\eqref{rc:eq:shiftcoeff} have entries proportional to
$\binom{2r}{h}$ or $\binom{2r+1}{h}$, $0\le r,h<u$.
As a polynomial in its upper argument, $\binom{x}{h}$ has leading
coefficient $1/h!$.  Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:binomV}
 \det_{0\le r,h<u}\binom{2r}{h}
 =\det_{0\le r,h<u}\binom{2r+1}{h}
 =2^{\binom u2}.
\end{equation}
Here the Vandermonde in $0,2,\ldots,2u-2$ or $1,3,\ldots,2u-1$
contributes $2^{\binom u2}\prod_{h<u}h!$, canceling the column
leading denominators.  (The first equality with $u=m$ is the
determinant evaluated in \eqref{eq:Taylorleading} of Part~I, by the same
argument.)

For the nonendpoint calculation let $t=k+h$, and write
$A_+=A_+(z)$, $A_-=A_-(z)$ and $s=s(z)$.  Consider the $t\times t$
matrix with rows $r=0,\ldots,t-1$, followed first by $k$ plus columns
and then by $h$ minus columns:
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:localmatrix}
 B_{r,(+,j)}=\frac{A_+s^r}{r!}\binom rj\quad(0\le j<k),
 \qquad
 B_{r,(-,j)}=\frac{A_-(-s)^r}{r!}\binom rj\quad(0\le j<h).
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}[The mixed-block determinant]\label{rc:lem:mixed}
The determinant in \eqref{rc:eq:localmatrix} equals
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:Bi}
 B_z(k)=
 \frac{A_+^k A_-^h\,
       s^{\binom k2}(-s)^{\binom h2}(-2s)^{kh}}
      {\prod_{r=0}^{t-1}r!},\qquad h=t-k,
\end{equation}
and is nonzero.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Remove the row factors $1/r!$ and the column factors $A_\pm$.
Use
\[
 w^r\binom rj
   =w^j\frac1{j!}\left(\frac{\dd}{\dd w}\right)^j w^r.
\]
The remaining normalized-derivative matrix is the transpose of a
confluent Vandermonde at $w=s$ and $w=-s$.  Its determinant is
$(-2s)^{kh}$.  The column powers supply
$s^{\binom k2}(-s)^{\binom h2}$, proving \eqref{rc:eq:Bi}.
Every factor is nonzero because $z\notin\{0,1,-1\}$.
\end{proof}

This lemma is the point at which a mere degree bound becomes an exact
degree theorem.  It sums all competing assignments of the derivative
rows to the two branches, including their determinant signs.  Ignoring
those signs, or arguing that one assignment has a nonzero leading term,
would not establish noncancellation of the whole sector.

\section{Canonical sectors and the proof of sharpness}\label{rc:sec:sharpness}

\subsection{A definition that still makes sense at resonance}
Set
\[
 f_{0,r}(n)=(-1)^r\binom{n+r}{2r},\qquad
 f_{4,r}(n)=\frac{2n+1}{2r+1}\binom{n+r}{2r}.
\]
Introduce independent marker variables $y_1,\ldots,y_s$.
Let $\widetilde M(N;\by)$ be the $D\times D$ matrix whose column $j$
contains the following rows, in the same root order as before:
\begin{align}
 &(-1)^j f_{0,r}(N+j),&&0\le r<m,\label{rc:eq:marked0}\\
 &f_{4,r}(N+j),&&0\le r<\ell,\label{rc:eq:marked4}\\
 &y_i z_i^j A_{r,+}(N+j;z_i)
       +z_i^{-j}A_{r,-}(N+j;z_i),&&0\le r<t_i.
 \label{rc:eq:markedoff}
\end{align}
Its determinant is a polynomial in $N$ and the markers.  Define
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:canonicalP}
 P_{\bk}(N)=\frac1{\Delta_q}
                   [y_1^{k_1}\cdots y_s^{k_s}]
                      \det\widetilde M(N;\by).
\end{equation}
This definition does not require distinct numerical rates.  It is the
meaning of ``canonically branch-labelled'' in Theorem~\ref{rc:thm:main}.

In the original matrix $M_N$, remove $(-1)^N$ from each of its $m$
zero-endpoint rows and $z_i^{-N}$ from each of the $t_i$ rows at $c_i$.
The remaining matrix is \eqref{rc:eq:marked0}--\eqref{rc:eq:markedoff} with
$y_i=z_i^{2N}$.  Therefore \eqref{rc:eq:Christoffel} gives
\[
 H_N(q)=\left((-1)^{D+m}\gamma\prod_i z_i^{-t_i}\right)^N
                \sum_{\bk}P_{\bk}(N)\prod_i z_i^{2Nk_i}.
\]
Since $D+m$ has the parity of $\ell+T$, this proves
\eqref{rc:eq:sectors}, with the rates in \eqref{rc:eq:lambda}.

\subsection{The leading coefficient in product form}
For each label $\bk$, form an ordered list of spectral blocks
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:Wlist}
 \mathcal W_{\bk}=
 \bigl((-1,m),(1,\ell),
       (z_1,k_1),(z_1^{-1},t_1-k_1),\ldots,
       (z_s,k_s),(z_s^{-1},t_s-k_s)\bigr),
\end{equation}
omitting all blocks of size zero.  Its nodes are pairwise distinct:
an equality between a node from $c_i$ and a node from $c_j$ would imply
$c_i=c_j$, and none is an endpoint node $\pm1$.
For any such ordered list write
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:Vfunction}
 \V\bigl((w_a,u_a)_{a=1}^{b}\bigr)=
 \prod_a\left(w_a^{\binom{u_a}2}\prod_{h=0}^{u_a-1}h!\right)
 \prod_{a<b}(w_b-w_a)^{u_a u_b}.
\end{equation}
Finally define
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:B04}
 B_0(m)=\frac{(-2)^{\binom m2}}{\prod_{r=0}^{m-1}(2r)!},
 \qquad
 B_4(\ell)=\frac{2^{\binom{\ell+1}2}}
                         {\prod_{r=0}^{\ell-1}(2r+1)!}.
\end{equation}
Both equal $1$ at multiplicity zero.

\begin{theorem}[Exact leading coefficient]\label{rc:thm:leading}
For $P_{\bk}$ defined by \eqref{rc:eq:canonicalP},
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:leading}
 \boxed{\quad
 [N^{d_{\bk}}]P_{\bk}(N)=
  \frac{B_0(m)B_4(\ell)\prod_{i=1}^{s}B_{z_i}(k_i)}{\Delta_q}
                    \V(\mathcal W_{\bk})\ne0.
 \quad}
\end{equation}
Here $B_{z_i}(k_i)$ is \eqref{rc:eq:Bi} with $t=t_i$.
Moreover $P_{\bk}$ has no term of degree greater than $d_{\bk}$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Expand each row of $\widetilde M$ in the finite collection of basis
rows
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:basis}
       (w^j j^h)_{j=0}^{D-1}.
\end{equation}
Only the finitely many $h$ up to the largest relevant polynomial degree
are needed.  This factors $\widetilde M=C(N;\by)V$, with $C$ a matrix
of coefficient polynomials and $V$ the matrix of basis rows.
Cauchy--Binet expresses its determinant as a sum over selections of
$D$ basis rows.

A nonzero coefficient minor must select exactly $m$ rows associated
with node $-1$, exactly $\ell$ with node $1$, and exactly $t_i$ rows
from the two nodes $z_i,z_i^{-1}$ for each $i$.  Otherwise a root block
of $C$, whose support is confined to its own nodes, cannot have full
rank.  The coefficient of $\by^{\bk}$ selects precisely $k_i$ rows
at $z_i$ and $t_i-k_i$ rows at $z_i^{-1}$.

For a fixed node with $u$ selected basis rows, their $h$ indices must
be distinct.  Their sum is therefore at least
$0+1+\cdots+(u-1)=\binom u2$, with equality only for the set
$\{0,\ldots,u-1\}$.  By \eqref{rc:eq:shiftcoeff}, selecting such an
index $h$ subtracts $h$ from the maximum possible degree in $N$ of
its coefficient.  Thus the zero-endpoint block contributes at most
\[
 \sum_{r=0}^{m-1}2r-\binom m2=\binom m2,
\]
the four-endpoint block contributes at most
\[
 \sum_{r=0}^{\ell-1}(2r+1)-\binom\ell2=\binom{\ell+1}2,
\]
and the $i$th nonendpoint block contributes at most
\[
 \sum_{r=0}^{t_i-1}r-\binom{k_i}2-\binom{t_i-k_i}2
           =k_i(t_i-k_i).
\]
The sum of these bounds is $d_{\bk}$.

There is exactly one selection of basis rows that can attain this
total degree: at every node take $h=0,\ldots,u-1$.  The associated
basis determinant is \eqref{rc:eq:Vfunction} for the list
$\mathcal W_{\bk}$, by Lemma~\ref{rc:lem:V}.
In its coefficient minor, the root blocks separate.  For the endpoint
$0$, the leading row factors from Lemma~\ref{rc:lem:endjets}, followed by
\eqref{rc:eq:binomV}, give $B_0(m)$.  At $4$ they give $B_4(\ell)$.
At $c_i$ the leading coefficient matrix is exactly
\eqref{rc:eq:localmatrix}, so Lemma~\ref{rc:lem:mixed} gives $B_{z_i}(k_i)$.
Dividing by $\Delta_q$ proves \eqref{rc:eq:leading}.

Every factor in \eqref{rc:eq:leading} is nonzero.  In particular, the
unique maximal-degree basis selection cannot vanish or be canceled by
another selection, because every other selection has strictly smaller
degree.  This proves both exactness and noncancellation.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Why the naive degree is too large]
For a block of multiplicity $t$, multiplying row degrees without taking
the determinant into account suggests $\binom t2$ degrees.  The
$k$ plus-branch rows consume $\binom k2$ degrees, and the $t-k$ minus
rows consume $\binom{t-k}2$.  The surviving degree is their difference,
$k(t-k)$.  The consumption is forced by linear dependence of equal
low-degree basis rows; it is not an accidental cancellation at special
numerical parameters.
\end{remark}

\section{Minimal recurrences, order, and the secondary factorization}
\label{rc:sec:recurrences}

\subsection{Exponential polynomials and minimality}
Let $(\E f)(N)=f(N+1)$ denote forward shift.

\begin{lemma}[Minimal annihilators]\label{rc:lem:annihilator}
Suppose $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_b$ are distinct nonzero complex numbers
and $Q_i$ are nonzero polynomials of degrees $d_i$.  Then
$f(N)=\sum_i Q_i(N)\lambda_i^N$ has minimal characteristic polynomial
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:expmin}
 \prod_{i=1}^{b}(X-\lambda_i)^{d_i+1}.
\end{equation}
Its ordinary generating function has reduced denominator
$\prod_i(1-\lambda_i u)^{d_i+1}$.
\end{lemma}
This is Lemma~\ref{lem:minimal} of Part~I, restated in Part~II's
notation (Part~I writes $E$ for the shift and $t$ for the
generating-function variable).  The manuscript proves it again; its
proof isolates each block by annihilating the others instead of using
Chinese-remainder projectors, and is kept as a second route.
\begin{proof}[Second proof (manuscript~03)]
On a term $Q(N)\lambda^N$, the operator $\E-\lambda$ gives
$\lambda^{N+1}(Q(N+1)-Q(N))$.  It lowers the degree by exactly one
until the polynomial is zero.  In contrast, $\E-\mu$, for
$\mu\ne\lambda$, preserves the degree and multiplies the leading
coefficient by $\lambda-\mu$.

These observations also prove independence of distinct exponential
blocks.  To isolate one block in a purported zero sum, annihilate all
other blocks by their respective powers of $\E-\lambda_i$.
The selected block remains nonzero unless it was zero initially.
If a polynomial $g$ has a zero of multiplicity $e$ at $\lambda_i$,
then its action on that block is nonzero for $e\le d_i$ and zero
for $e>d_i$.  Hence each factor in \eqref{rc:eq:expmin} is necessary
and their product suffices.

For the generating function, apply powers of $u\,\dd/\dd u$ to
$(1-\lambda_i u)^{-1}$.  A degree-$d_i$ polynomial multiplier yields
a pole of order exactly $d_i+1$ at $u=\lambda_i^{-1}$.
Distinct poles cannot cancel.  This gives the stated reduced denominator.
\end{proof}

Applying the lemma to Theorem~\ref{rc:thm:leading} proves
\eqref{rc:eq:minchar} under nonresonance.  Without that hypothesis its
labelled product still annihilates every term.  Summing its exponents
uses
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:ksum}
 \sum_{k=0}^{t}k(t-k)=\frac{t(t+1)(t-1)}6,
\end{equation}
and gives \eqref{rc:eq:R}.  If two or more labels have the same rate,
their sum has degree at most their largest degree, strictly less in
order contribution than the sum of their separate $d+1$ values.
Thus any resonance strictly lowers the order below $\Rzero$.
This completes the proof of Theorem~\ref{rc:thm:main} and its corollaries.

In the nonresonant case the ordinary generating function is therefore
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:GF}
 \sum_{N\ge0}H_N(q)u^N
     =\frac{B_q(u)}{\prod_{\bk}(1-\lambda_{\bk}u)^{d_{\bk}+1}},
 \quad \deg B_q<\Rzero,\quad B_q(0)=1,
\end{equation}
and the displayed fraction is reduced.  A limiting specialization of
this numerator and denominator need not remain reduced.

\subsection{Comparison with the classical exponential bound}
There is a useful elementary consistency check on the sharp formula.
For $0\le k\le t$,
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:localbound}
 1+k(t-k)\le\binom tk.
\end{equation}
To see this, identify a $k$-subset of $\{1,\ldots,t\}$ with a partition
inside a $k\times(t-k)$ rectangle.  Every size from $0$ to $k(t-k)$
is attained: fill complete rows and then at most one partial row.
There are therefore at least $k(t-k)+1$ different partitions.
Also
\[
 1+\binom m2\le2^m,\qquad
 1+\binom{\ell+1}2=1+\ell+\binom\ell2\le2^\ell.
\]
For nonnegative numbers $a_j$, $1+\sum_j a_j\le\prod_j(1+a_j)$.
Combining these inequalities, label by label and then summing, proves
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:universalbound}
 \Rzero\le2^{m+\ell}\prod_i\sum_{k_i=0}^{t_i}\binom{t_i}{k_i}
          =2^D.
\end{equation}
Thus the order bound here is compatible with the classical one, while
retaining the determinant cancellations that the larger bound ignores.
For a single repeated root, summing $1+k(t-k)$ gives
$t+1+\binom{t+1}{3}$.  Strictness for $t\ge4$ already follows from
$\binom t2>1+2(t-2)$ at $k=2$.

\subsection{Proof of the secondary Hankel factorization}
We prove a general identity, then substitute the explicit leading
coefficients of our determinant sequence.

\begin{lemma}[A finite Hankel determinant of exponential polynomials]
\label{rc:lem:secondarygeneral}
Suppose
$f(N)=\sum_{i=1}^{b}Q_i(N)\lambda_i^N$, with
$\deg Q_i=d_i$, leading coefficient $a_i$, and $r_i=d_i+1$.
Put $R=\sum_i r_i$.  The rates may coincide.  Then
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:secondarygeneral}
 \det_{0\le u,v<R}f(u+v)
 =(-1)^{\sum_i\binom{r_i}2}
  \prod_i (d_i!a_i)^{r_i}\lambda_i^{r_i(r_i-1)}
  \prod_{i<j}(\lambda_j-\lambda_i)^{2r_i r_j}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Write $Q_i(N)=\sum_{h=0}^{d_i}a_{i,h}N^h$.
Let $U_{u,(i,h)}=\lambda_i^u u^h$, $0\le h\le d_i$, and let $B$ be
block diagonal, with $i$th block
\[
 (B_i)_{h,j}=\begin{cases}
     \binom{h+j}{h}a_{i,h+j},&h+j\le d_i,\\
     0,&h+j>d_i.
 \end{cases}
\]
The binomial expansion of $Q_i(u+v)$ gives the exact factorization
$(f(u+v))=UBU^{\mathsf T}$.  Each $B_i$ is anti-triangular, so
\[
 \det B_i=(-1)^{\binom{r_i}2}
                  a_i^{r_i}\prod_{h=0}^{d_i}\binom{d_i}{h}.
\]
Lemma~\ref{rc:lem:V} gives $\det U$.  The factorials simplify using
\[
 \left(\prod_{h=0}^{d_i}h!\right)^2
       \prod_{h=0}^{d_i}\binom{d_i}{h}=(d_i!)^{r_i}.
\]
Taking determinants proves \eqref{rc:eq:secondarygeneral} for distinct
rates.  The same identity holds for coincident rates by polynomial
continuation in the $\lambda_i$, or directly because $U$ then has
repeated columns and both sides vanish.
\end{proof}

Theorem~\ref{rc:thm:secondary} follows on taking the canonical labels
$\bk$ as the blocks of Lemma~\ref{rc:lem:secondarygeneral} and inserting
\eqref{rc:eq:leading}.  All its non-resonance factors are nonzero throughout
the admissible root-multiplicity stratum.  In particular, no hidden
amplitude-zero locus can lower the maximal recurrence order.

Suppose, locally, exactly two rates meet, their difference vanishes to
first order along a parameter curve, and no root itself reaches another
root or an endpoint.  Formula~\eqref{rc:eq:secondary} then shows that the
secondary determinant vanishes to exactly $2r_{\bk}r_{\bj}$ orders
along that curve.  This is a quantitative statement about rank loss,
not just a test that some exceptional parameter exists.

\section{Resonance and a root-free exact algorithm}\label{rc:sec:resonances}

\subsection{The exact cancellation rule}
Let $\Lambda$ be the set of distinct numerical values among the rates.
For $\lambda\in\Lambda$ define
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:grouped}
 Q_\lambda(N)=\sum_{\bk:\lambda_{\bk}=\lambda}P_{\bk}(N).
\end{equation}
Some of these sums may be identically zero even though every summand
has a nonzero leading coefficient.

\begin{theorem}[All-parameter minimality rule]\label{rc:thm:resonant}
At every admissible parameter choice, the minimal characteristic
polynomial is
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:resminimal}
 \chi_q(X)=\prod_{\substack{\lambda\in\Lambda\\Q_\lambda\ne0}}
                    (X-\lambda)^{1+\deg Q_\lambda}.
\end{equation}
Before computing any cancellations, a valid smaller annihilator than
the labelled product is
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:groupbound}
 \Psi_q(X)=\prod_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
       (X-\lambda)^{1+\max\{d_{\bk}:\lambda_{\bk}=\lambda\}}.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Group \eqref{rc:eq:sectors} by numerical rate, discard zero polynomials,
and apply Lemma~\ref{rc:lem:annihilator}.  The degree of a sum is at most
the maximum degree of its summands, proving \eqref{rc:eq:groupbound}.
\end{proof}

Equation~\eqref{rc:eq:resminimal} is exact, but it is not a short classification
of every possible resonant cancellation.  Such a classification would
require a more detailed description of the polynomials $Q_\lambda$.
The next theorem instead gives a terminating algebraic procedure for
each exact input, without constructing any spectral roots.

\subsection{Why a finite prefix can be a proof}
\begin{theorem}[Certified finite-prefix recovery]\label{rc:thm:finite}
Let $h=(h_N)_{N\ge0}$ be a nonzero sequence over a characteristic-zero
field, known to satisfy a monic constant-coefficient recurrence of
order at most $R$ from $N=0$ onward.  Its minimal recurrence can be
recovered from $h_0,\ldots,h_{2R-1}$ as follows.
For $r=1,\ldots,R$, solve
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:finitesystem}
 h_{n+r}+\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}g_j h_{n+j}=0,
                  \qquad 0\le n<R.
\end{equation}
The first soluble system gives the unique minimal monic characteristic
polynomial $X^r+\sum_{j<r}g_jX^j$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $A(\E)h=0$ be a known-to-exist monic annihilator of order $R$,
padding a lower-order one by a power of $X$ if necessary.  For any
candidate $g$ the residual sequence $u=g(\E)h$ also satisfies
$A(\E)u=0$, because polynomials in shift commute.  If
\eqref{rc:eq:finitesystem} holds, its first $R$ values are zero.  The
monic recurrence then forces every subsequent value to be zero by
induction.  Thus every soluble system gives a genuine all-index
annihilator, not merely a fitted prefix.

The actual minimal annihilator gives a solution for its order, so the
first successful order is minimal.  Two different monic annihilators
of that degree would differ by a nonzero annihilator of smaller degree,
which is impossible.  The largest index used by the algorithm is
$R-1+R=2R-1$.
\end{proof}

The prior order bound is indispensable.  Without it, an arbitrarily long
matching prefix does not certify a recurrence for an arbitrary sequence.
For \eqref{rc:eq:HN}, Theorem~\ref{rc:thm:main} supplies exactly the bound needed.

\subsection{No factorization into individual roots is required}
Suppose $q\in\QQ[x]\setminus\{0\}$.  Strip its powers of $x$ and $x-4$,
and perform square-free decomposition on the remainder:
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:squarefree}
 q(x)=\gamma x^m(x-4)^\ell\prod_{t\ge1} f_t(x)^t,
\end{equation}
where the nonconstant $f_t$ are monic, square-free and pairwise coprime.
Let $b_t=\deg f_t$.  Ordinary polynomial gcds with the derivative produce
this decomposition; irreducible factorization and numerical root finding
are unnecessary.  The bound becomes
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:rootfreebound}
 R=\prod_{t\ge1}(t+1)^{b_t}
       \left(\kappa+1+\frac16\sum_{t\ge1}b_t t(t-1)\right).
\end{equation}
Compute the first $2R$ determinants directly from their rational moment
matrices and apply Theorem~\ref{rc:thm:finite}.  This determines the true
minimal polynomial even when the roots are complex, multiplicatively
dependent, or not conveniently expressible by radicals.

All recovered coefficients lie in $\QQ$.  More generally, if the input
coefficients lie in an exactly computable algebraic number field, the
same linear systems work over that field.  Solvability over its algebraic
closure is equivalent to solvability over the field itself, by Gaussian
elimination; uniqueness of the minimal monic polynomial gives descent.
For arbitrary unspecified complex numbers, ``algorithm'' presupposes
an exact representation with equality and field operations available.
We do not assert an effective equality test for arbitrary complex oracles.

The transparent root-free implementation uses $O(R^4)$ field operations
for generating the determinants and searching all orders with naive
Gaussian elimination, apart from polynomial preprocessing and moment
generation.  This is an arithmetic-operation estimate, not a
bit-complexity claim.  The included fixed-size Christoffel path is much
faster when the roots are already given exactly.

\subsection{Algebraic rank-drop loci}
The finite-prefix proof also turns exceptional orders into explicit
polynomial conditions.  Fix a degree $D\ge0$ and consider nonzero
polynomial multipliers of exactly that degree.  The uniform all-index
bound $S=2^D$ follows from \eqref{rc:eq:universalbound}.
For $1\le r<S$, form
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:rankmatrix}
 \mathcal H_{S,r}(q)=\bigl(H_{n+j}(q)\bigr)_{
                        0\le n<S,\ 0\le j\le r}.
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:rankcriterion}
 \ord(H(q))\le r\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
                 \rank\mathcal H_{S,r}(q)\le r.
\end{equation}
For the forward direction, the recurrence gives a column dependence.
For the reverse direction, take a nonzero dependence, normalize its
highest nonzero coefficient, and apply the residual argument of
Theorem~\ref{rc:thm:finite}.  Its degree is at most $r$.

Every entry $H_n(q)$ is a polynomial in the coefficients of $q$.
Consequently the order-at-most-$r$ locus is defined, within the degree-$D$
coefficient space, by the vanishing of all $(r+1)$-minors of
\eqref{rc:eq:rankmatrix}.  It is relatively Zariski closed.  On a fixed
root-multiplicity stratum, $S$ may be replaced by the sharper $\Rzero$.
Theorem~\ref{rc:thm:secondary} is an explicit factorization of the maximal-rank
test on that stratum; lower-rank tests can involve further cancellation
conditions after rates have already collided.

 P_2(N)&=-\frac{1728N^4+13824N^3+43344N^2+62784N+35648}{729},
                    \label{rc:eq:examplepolys}\\
 P_3(N)&=-\frac{6144N^3+21504N^2+23552N+10240}{243},\nonumber\\
 P_4(N)&=\frac{65536}{729}.\nonumber
\end{align}
These polynomials may be obtained by \eqref{rc:eq:canonicalP}, or by exact
interpolation after the theorem has established their degrees and
nonvanishing.  The first determinant values are
\[
 1,\quad3401,\quad2480745,\quad1026904425,\quad
 327789036905,\quad92503701028201.
\]
No polynomial of degree $14$ can annihilate this sequence: all five
rates are distinct and every displayed top coefficient is nonzero.
This nonexistence assertion is proved for the entire sequence, not
inferred from the displayed six values.

\subsection{Resonance can cancel an entire branch}
For $q(x)=(x-2)^2$, choose $z=i$.  The three canonical sectors are
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:squarecentral}
 P_0(N)=\frac{i}{4},\qquad P_1(N)=N+1,\qquad
 P_2(N)=-\frac{i}{4},
\end{equation}
with rates $-1,1,-1$.  The two nonzero constant sectors cancel exactly,
so
\[
 H_N((x-2)^2)=N+1,\qquad \chi_q(X)=(X-1)^2.
\]
For completeness, the leading-coefficient formula gives the two constants
and the coefficient $1$ of $N$ in $P_1$.  The condition $H_0=1$ then
fixes its constant term.  This already proves \eqref{rc:eq:squarecentral}.
Taking the maximum degree separately for each collided rate, without
checking cancellation, would retain an unnecessary $X+1$ factor.

A similar phenomenon need not involve repeated roots of $q$.  Take
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:distinctresonance}
 q(x)=(x-\tfrac92)(x+\tfrac12),\qquad z_1=2,\quad z_2=-2.
\end{equation}
The four canonical constants, in order $(0,0),(0,1),(1,0),(1,1)$,
are $-1/15,-1/3,1/3,16/15$; their rates are
$-1/4,-1,-1,-4$.  Hence
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:distinctresresult}
 H_N(q)=\frac{16}{15}(-4)^N-\frac1{15}(-\tfrac14)^N,
 \qquad \chi_q(X)=X^2+\frac{17}{4}X+1.
\end{equation}
The multiplicity profile permits generic order $4$, the grouped
annihilator has order $3$, and exact cancellation lowers the true order
to $2$.  Thus root multiplicities alone cannot determine the order on
the resonance locus.

\subsection{A nine-value proof of a resonant fourth-power formula}
For $q=(x-2)^4$ and $z=i$, the degree law and grouped annihilator give
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:centreann}
       (\E-1)^5(\E+1)^4H=0.
\end{equation}
The exact closed formula is
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:centreproduct}
 H_N((x-2)^4)=\frac{(N+1)(N+2)^2(N+3)}{12}.
\end{equation}
Here is a finite proof using the already established all-index bound,
rather than an extrapolation from data.  The right side is a polynomial
of degree $4$, so it satisfies \eqref{rc:eq:centreann}.  The first nine
values of both sides are
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}rrrrrrrrrr@{}}
\toprule
$N$ &0&1&2&3&4&5&6&7&8\\
\midrule
$H_N$&1&6&20&50&105&196&336&540&825\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
These determinant values follow from the $4\times4$ formula
\eqref{rc:eq:Christoffel}.  They can be reproduced using only integers:
write $u_{n,r}=p_n^{(r)}(2)/r!$, start with
\[
 (u_{0,r})_{r=0}^{3}=(1,0,0,0),\qquad
 (u_{1,r})_{r=0}^{3}=(1,1,0,0),
\]
and apply
\[
 u_{n+1,r}=u_{n,r-1}-u_{n-1,r},\qquad u_{n,-1}=0.
\]
Then $H_N=\det(u_{N+j,r})_{0\le r,j<4}$.
The difference between the two sides of \eqref{rc:eq:centreproduct}
satisfies a monic recurrence of order $9$ and has its first nine
values zero.  Induction proves that it is identically zero.
Since the product polynomial has degree $4$ and nonzero leading
coefficient $1/12$, its minimal polynomial is $(X-1)^5$.
The generic order $15$ has dropped to $5$.

\subsection{An irreducible rational input}
No spectral representation is needed to run the exact algorithm.
For $q=x^2+1$, the square-free profile is $b_1=2$, so $R=4$.
The root-free calculation gives
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:irreducible}
 \chi_q(X)=X^4-5X^3+4X^2-5X+1.
\end{equation}
For $q=(x^2+1)^2$ the profile is $b_2=2$, and the order is $15$.
The archive records its full characteristic polynomial and exact input
coefficients.  These examples illustrate the intended use of the
algorithm: its input is a rational coefficient list, not approximate
complex roots or guessed multiplicative relations among them.

\section{Exact asymptotics and stability of the leading sector}\label{rc:sec:asymptotics}

The expansion \eqref{rc:eq:sectors} is finite and exact.  Consequently it
contains more information than a leading asymptotic equivalence.
Suppose a label $\bk_*$ has strictly larger rate modulus than every
other label.  Put $\lambda_* =\lambda_{\bk_*}$,
$d_*=d_{\bk_*}$, and let $M=\max_{\bk}d_{\bk}$.
Choose $\rho<|\lambda_*|$ no smaller than the other rate moduli.
Then
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:asymptotic}
 H_N(q)=\lambda_*^N P_{\bk_*}(N)+O(\rho^N N^M).
\end{equation}
When there are no other labels the error term is zero.  In particular,
\[
 H_N(q)\sim L_{\bk_*}N^{d_*}\lambda_*^N.
\]
Every algebraic correction in inverse powers of $N$ can be read from the
finite polynomial $P_{\bk_*}$; no infinite asymptotic series is needed
for fixed parameters.

\begin{corollary}[Repeated roots outside the interval]\label{rc:cor:outside}
Suppose all nonendpoint roots satisfy $c_i>4$, and choose $z_i>1$.
Then the unique largest-modulus sector is $k_i=t_i$ for every $i$.
Thus
\begin{equation}\label{rc:eq:outside}
 H_N(q)\sim L_{(t_1,\ldots,t_s)}N^{\kappa}
       \left((-1)^{\ell+T}\gamma\prod_i z_i^{t_i}\right)^N.
\end{equation}
No multiplicative-independence assumption is needed for this conclusion.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Reducing any $k_i$ multiplies the modulus by a factor $z_i^{-2}<1$.
Hence the maximal sector is unique even if some of the smaller sectors
resonate.  At $k_i=t_i$ all $k_i(t_i-k_i)$ vanish, leaving degree
$\kappa$.  Its leading coefficient is nonzero by
Theorem~\ref{rc:thm:leading}.
\end{proof}

This separates two effects that are easy to conflate.  Repeating a root
outside the support changes the exponential growth rate and can greatly
increase the recurrence order.  It does \emph{not} increase the power of
$N$ in the leading asymptotic term; that power comes entirely from the
endpoint multiplicities.  The repeated offendpoint roots instead create
higher polynomial degrees in intermediate, subdominant sectors.
For \eqref{rc:eq:qexample}, for instance,
\[
 H_N((2x-9)^4)\sim\frac{65536}{729}\,256^N,
\]
even though its $16^N$ sector has degree $4$ and its recurrence has
order $15$.

The estimate is for fixed parameters.  As roots approach $0$, $4$ or
each other, the spectral gaps and the constants need not be uniform.
A double-scaling limit cannot be justified by simply using
\eqref{rc:eq:asymptotic} with moving parameters.  That is one of the
separate research directions listed below.

\subsection{A feasible formalization route}
No Lean source has been compiled or checked for this article.  The
repository location does not confer formal status on the present proofs.
A formalization should separate the argument into small algebraic layers.

First formalize normalized jets of \eqref{rc:eq:prec} over a characteristic-zero
field and their generating functions.  Next prove the confluent
Vandermonde identity, including the Euler-derivative row change, and
formalize the polynomial-shift coefficient bound \eqref{rc:eq:shiftcoeff}.
The decisive reusable lemma is the maximal-degree Cauchy--Binet argument:
it tracks one distinct list of derivative orders per spectral node and
identifies the unique smallest such list.

The orthogonality and integral proof of Christoffel may be formalized
analytically, but an algebraic alternative is attractive.  One may define
the moment functional by $L(x^n)=C_n$, prove its Gram identities for
$p_n$ directly from generating functions, and prove Christoffel by
determinant multilinearity.  This would avoid importing measure theory
into the core recurrence theorem.

Finally, the shift-operator minimality lemma, the $UBU^{\mathsf T}$
secondary factorization and the finite-prefix recovery theorem are
pure finite linear algebra over a field.  They should be reusable
outside Catalan determinants.  An audit must distinguish imported
classical facts from newly checked lemmas and must not replace a missing
confluence proof with an admitted theorem hidden inside a computation.

\section{Further research questions and proposed next steps}\label{rc:sec:research}

The questions below are precise continuations of this article.  They
are not all asserted to be unsolved in the published literature; a
broader literature check is part of each proposed project.

\subsection{Closed classification of cyclotomic resonances}
For a single repeated root, classify the minimal recurrence as an
explicit function of $t$ and the order of $z^2$ when $z$ is a root of
unity other than $\pm1$.  The degree law reduces the task to finitely
many grouped polynomials, but the cancellations can be substantial.
The examples at $z=i$ show that counting distinct rates is not enough.
A first target is a uniform closed form for $H_N((x-2)^t)$ for every
$t$, together with a direct explanation of the parity effects, rather
than a separate finite certificate for each $t$.

\subsection{Pairwise resonance and cancellation order}
Suppose exactly two labelled rates agree and all other rates are
separate.  Determine when their leading coefficients cancel, and how
many further coefficients can cancel.  Theorem~\ref{rc:thm:leading} makes
the first test explicit, while \eqref{rc:eq:Arec} determines all later
tests.  The aim is a structural criterion in the root parameters, not
just the output of Gaussian elimination.  The distinct-root example
\eqref{rc:eq:distinctresonance} is a small initial case.

\subsection{Sector polynomials and symmetric functions}
The degree $k(t-k)$ is the area of a $k\times(t-k)$ rectangle, and the
proof uses a mixed confluent Vandermonde.  Seek a Schur-function or
factorial-Schur description of the entire $P_{\bk}$, not only its
leading coefficient.  Useful outputs would be exact factorizations,
coefficient sign rules for real roots outside $[0,4]$, and formulas
that remain stable as two parameters approach one another.  Such a
description could also identify an earlier equivalent formulation of
the multiplicity law.

\subsection{Uniform endpoint and root-collision limits}
A concrete endpoint scaling is
\[
 c_N=2+2\cosh(\tau/N),\qquad q_N(x)=(x-c_N)^t.
\]
Determine the limit, locally uniformly in $\tau$, of
\[
 \frac{H_N(q_N)}{(-1)^{tN}N^{t(t+1)/2}},
\]
including its value at $\tau=0$.  The analogous left-endpoint scaling
$c_N=2-2\cosh(\tau/N)$ should use the exponent $t(t-1)/2$.
For $t=1$ the branch formula already reduces these limits to elementary
hyperbolic functions.  The general problem asks for a uniform confluent
determinant limit and error bounds.  A second version lets two distinct
nonendpoint roots approach at a rate of order $N^{-1}$.

\subsection{Other eventually constant Jacobi recurrences}
Krattenthaler's recurrence theorem covers many moment families beyond
Catalan numbers~\cite{Krattenthaler2023}.  Extend the exact degree analysis to a
three-term recurrence whose coefficients are eventually constant.
Away from branch points, the main new issue is a zero of one branch
amplitude caused by the finite initial perturbation.  Classify how its
vanishing order changes the jet degrees and the noncancellation matrix.
This would distinguish universal collision behavior from behavior tied
to the Catalan boundary condition $p_1=x-1$.

\subsection{Rational multipliers and finite poles}
Replace $q(x)$ by a rational function whose poles avoid the support.
The determinant identities underlying Uvarov transformations provide a
starting point~\cite{Krattenthaler2021Uvarov}.  The target is an exact analogue of the
sector-degree formula when evaluation and Cauchy-transform blocks occur
together.  One must first specify which normalizing factors are removed
and which recurrences hold from the initial index rather than only
eventually.

\subsection{Coefficient-space equations and arithmetic reductions}
Eliminate the spectral variables from \eqref{rc:eq:secondary} to obtain
small defining equations for maximal-order failure directly in the
coefficients of $q$.  The finite minors in \eqref{rc:eq:rankmatrix} are
correct but can be very large.  Seek sharper degrees, irreducible
components and efficient membership tests.  For monic integral
multipliers, a related problem is to classify primes at which the
minimal recurrence drops after reduction.  The factorials in the
leading constants show why characteristic-zero noncancellation cannot
simply be reused unchanged in small characteristic.

\subsection{Fast certified algorithms and formal verification}
Combine the proved order bound with fast minimal-recurrence algorithms,
but reduce the cost of obtaining the initial determinants as well.
A useful target is an output-sensitive exact algorithm with bit-complexity
bounds in $D$, the coefficient heights and the final recurrence order.
Separately, formalize the maximal-degree determinant lemma and the
finite-prefix certification theorem first: these are the shortest
reusable path from the present article to a machine-checked result.

\section{Conclusion}\label{rc:sec:conclusion}
The selected repository extension has a uniform answer at the level of
exponential sectors.  A root of multiplicity $t_i$ away from $0,4$
contributes the exact degree $k_i(t_i-k_i)$ to its $k_i$th branch sector.
The two endpoints contribute different triangular numbers.  The leading
coefficient is a nonzero product, so distinct sectors cannot disappear
at nonresonant parameters.  This proves the sharp order formula
\eqref{rc:eq:R}, with cubic rather than exponential complexity for a single
repeated root.

The secondary-Hankel factorization identifies the maximal-order
exceptional locus exactly.  Resonances may cause further cancellations,
but they do not obstruct an exact answer for a specified rational or
algebraic multiplier: a proved order bound turns finitely many determinant
values into a complete minimal-recurrence certificate.  The remaining
research questions concern structural descriptions of those cancellations,
uniform collision limits, broader moment families and formalization,
not gaps in the stated multiplicity proof.

\section{Normalization and sign audit}\label{rc:app:audit}

There are four easy ways to obtain an incorrect statement in this problem.
This section (Appendix~A of the manuscript) records the conventions that
prevent them.

\paragraph{Size versus last index.}
Throughout, $N$ is the determinant size.  In a convention that indexes
rows by $0\le i,j\le n$, one must substitute $N=n+1$.  The initial
term $H_0=1$ is part of the recurrence sequence used here.

\paragraph{The Christoffel sign.}
The product in the multiplier is $\prod(x-a_u)$, whereas the mixed
Vandermonde supplies $\prod(a_u-x)$.  This contributes $(-1)^{ND}$.
The $m$ zero-endpoint rows add $(-1)^{mN}$ when their factors are
removed.  The remaining sign is therefore $(-1)^{(\ell+T)N}$,
exactly the sign in \eqref{rc:eq:lambda}.

\paragraph{Normalized versus unnormalized jets.}
The rows of $M_N$ are $p_{N+j}^{(r)}(a)/r!$.  Thus $\Delta_q$ contains
only cross-root differences.  Extra products of $r!$ in its denominator
would double-count confluence.  The factorials appearing later in
$B_0,B_4,B_z$ and $\V$ have separate origins and must not be removed
by changing the jet convention halfway through the proof.

\paragraph{Two different kinds of collision.}
A collision of roots of $q$ changes the factorization profile and must
be treated by normalized jets.  A collision of rates $\lambda_{\bk}$
can occur even when all roots of $q$ are distinct, and must be treated
by adding their polynomial coefficients.  These are logically different
operations.  The first creates the degree law; the second can reduce
the actual recurrence order.

As a final check, $H_N(uq)=u^N H_N(q)$.  The determinant on the left
of \eqref{rc:eq:secondary} therefore scales by $u^{\Rzero(\Rzero-1)}$.
On its right, the rate powers and rate differences contribute
\[
 \sum_{\bk}r_{\bk}(r_{\bk}-1)
    +2\sum_{\bk<\bj}r_{\bk}r_{\bj}=\Rzero(\Rzero-1),
\]
while the sector leading coefficients do not depend on the overall
scale $\gamma$.  The scaling agrees exactly.

\section{The precise correction to the printed conjecture}\label{app:barry}

This appendix distinguishes two kinds of index.  The source uses a
size-$(n+1)$ determinant.  We write $N=n+1$.  To avoid silently
identifying incompatible shift conventions, write $s$ for the source's
symbol in its displayed coefficient formula, and reserve $m$ for
our actual Catalan shift.

For $s\geq2$ and $0\leq k\leq n+1$, the coefficient expression printed
in Conjecture~2 of~\cite{Barry2020} can be written as
\begin{align}
 T_{n,k,s}
 &=\frac{C_s}{2s-2}
   \binom{s+k-2}{s-2}\binom{n+k+2s-2}{2k+2s-3}\nonumber\\[-1pt]
 &\quad\times
  \prod_{j=0}^{\lfloor(s-1)/2\rfloor-1}
       \frac{\binom{2n+2s-2j-1}{2s-4j-5}}
            {\binom{2s-2j-1}{2s-4j-5}}
  \prod_{j=0}^{s-3}\frac{2s-j-2}{n+k+2s-j-2}.
 \label{eq:BarryT}
\end{align}
Only the natural, nonambiguous range $s\geq2$ is needed here.  Empty
products have value~$1$.

\begin{proposition}[Correct shift]\label{prop:Barry}
For $N\geq1$, $s\geq2$, and $0\leq k\leq N$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Barrycorrect}
 T_{N-1,k,s}=c_{N,s-1,k}.
\end{equation}
Consequently, the polynomial using $T_{N-1,k,s}$ is
$D_N^{(s-1)}(a,b)$, not $D_N^{(s)}(a,b)$.  Equivalently, to use that
printed expression for an actual shift $m\geq1$, replace its shift
symbol by $s=m+1$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Taking a ratio of successive terms in \eqref{eq:BarryT} and cancelling
factorials gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Tkratio}
 \frac{T_{n,k+1,s}}{T_{n,k,s}}
 =\frac{(n+1-k)(n+k+s+1)}{2(k+1)(2k+2s-1)}.
\end{equation}
This agrees with \eqref{eq:coeff-update} after $N=n+1$ and $m=s-1$.
It remains to match the initial coefficient.
Putting $n=N-1$, $k=0$, and simplifying the factorials gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:TB0}
 T_{N-1,0,s}=C_s\frac{(N+s-1)!}{N!s!}
 \prod_{j=0}^{p-1}
 \frac{\binom{2N+2s-2j-3}{2s-4j-5}}
      {\binom{2s-2j-1}{2s-4j-5}},
 \qquad p=\left\lfloor\frac{s-1}{2}\right\rfloor.
\end{equation}
At $N=1$ this is $C_s=H_1^{(s)}$.  Its ratio at successive $N$ is
\begin{align}
 \frac{T_{N,0,s}}{T_{N-1,0,s}}
 &=\frac{N+s}{N+1}
   \prod_{j=0}^{p-1}
   \frac{(2N+2s-2j-1)(2N+2s-2j-2)}
        {(2N+2j+4)(2N+2j+3)}\nonumber\\
 &=\frac{(2N+2s)!(2N+1)!}
         {(2N+s)!(2N+s+1)!}.
 \label{eq:TNr}
\end{align}
In the product, the numerator factors form a consecutive interval of
integers, as do the denominator factors; the last equality holds for
either parity of $s$.  The shifted-product formula gives the identical
ratio
\[
 \frac{H_{N+1}^{(s)}}{H_N^{(s)}}
 =\prod_{i=1}^{s-1}
     \frac{(2N+2i+1)(2N+2i+2)}{(2N+i+1)(2N+i+2)}
 =\frac{(2N+2s)!(2N+1)!}{(2N+s)!(2N+s+1)!}.
\]
Induction in $N$ therefore proves $T_{N-1,0,s}=H_N^{(s)}$.
The ratio \eqref{eq:Tkratio} then proves \eqref{eq:Barrycorrect}
for every $k$.
\end{proof}

The discrepancy is visible in the smallest nontrivial instance.
With $N=1$ and $s=2$, the printed expression is
\[
 T_{0,0,2}b+T_{0,1,2}a=2b+a,
\]
whereas the determinant with the stated shift $s=2$ is
\[
 D_1^{(2)}(a,b)=aC_2+bC_3=2a+5b.
\]
At $a=b=1$, these are $3$ and $7$.  The expression $a+2b$ is exactly
$D_1^{(1)}(a,b)$, so the correction is systematic rather than an isolated
numerical exception.  The original PDF page was inspected as well as
its HTML rendering; this is not an artifact of an HTML conversion.
The denominator-exponent statement is separate and uses the actual
shift $m$ correctly.
