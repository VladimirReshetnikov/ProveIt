\begin{abstract}
We study the shifted Hankel determinants of Cigler's polynomial extension
$c_n(t)$ of the middle binomial coefficients. An even--odd decomposition
factors every normalized determinant into two principal minors of powers of
simple tridiagonal matrices. This gives a proof of polynomiality, strict
coefficient positivity, reciprocity, and the exact degree. An elementary
alternant evaluation of the two factors proves the stable-coefficient
assertions of Conjecture 16. More precisely, if
$a=\lfloor k/2\rfloor$, $b=\lfloor(k-1)/2\rfloor$, and
$\Ht_k(n;t)=(-1)^{k\binom n2}t^{-\binom n2}
\det(c_{k+i+j}(t))_{0\le i,j<n}$, then for $k\ge2$,
\[
\Ht_k(n;t)=\frac{1}{(1-t)^a(1-t^2)^{ab}}
-\binom{n+a}{a-1}t^{n+1}+O(t^{n+2}).
\]
Thus the conjectured stable range is optimal, and its first defect has a
closed formula. We also obtain fixed-size determinant evaluations, an
explicit nonminimal recurrence, and convergence on compact subsets of the
open unit disk. Exact integer and polynomial verification programs accompany
the article. A stronger rectangular-Schur identity suggested by computation
is separated from the proved results and is not used in any proof.
[Added 29 September 2026, batch 56: Part~II proves this identity and the
exact minimal recurrence.]
\end{abstract}

Here the family is $c_n(t)$ from equation (6), and the target is

\subsection{Definitions and conventions}
A binomial coefficient $\binom rj$ is zero unless $0\le j\le r$.
Set $c_0(t)=1$, and for $r\ge1$ define
\begin{equation}
 c_r(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{r}
 \binom{\lfloor(r-1)/2\rfloor}{\lfloor(j-1)/2\rfloor}
 \binom{\lfloor r/2\rfloor}{\lfloor j/2\rfloor}t^{j-1}.
 \label{eq:source-moments}
\end{equation}
This is Cigler's equation (6), with its zero $j=0$ term omitted so that no
negative power of $t$ is displayed. For example,
\begin{align*}
 c_0&=1,&c_1&=1,&c_2&=1+t,\\
 c_3&=1+t+t^2,&
 c_4&=1+2t+2t^2+t^3,&
 c_5&=1+2t+4t^2+2t^3+t^4.
\end{align*}
For $k,n\ge0$, write
\begin{equation}
 D_k(n;t)=\det(c_{k+i+j}(t))_{0\le i,j<n},\qquad D_k(0;t)=1.
 \label{eq:D}
\end{equation}
For $k\ge1$ initially define, in $\Q(t)$,
\begin{equation}
 \Ht_k(n;t)=(-1)^{k\binom n2}\frac{D_k(n;t)}{t^{\binom n2}}.
 \label{eq:normalization}
\end{equation}
Polynomiality, including a well-defined value at $t=0$, will be proved.
It is important not to substitute $t=0$ into the quotient before this step.

Let
\begin{equation}
 a=\left\lfloor\frac k2\right\rfloor,\qquad
 b=\left\lfloor\frac{k-1}{2}\right\rfloor,\qquad
 \F_k(t)=\frac{1}{(1-t)^a(1-t^2)^{ab}}.
 \label{eq:F}
\end{equation}
An expression $O(t^r)$ in a formal-series identity means an element of
$t^r\Q[[t]]$; it has no analytic meaning unless one is explicitly stated.

\begin{theorem}[Conjecture 16, with a sharp first defect]
\label{thm:main}
For every $k\ge1$ and $n\ge0$, $\Ht_k(n;t)$ is a polynomial in $\Z[t]$ of
exact degree $(k-1)n$. Its constant and leading coefficients are both $1$,
every coefficient between them is strictly positive, and
\begin{equation}
 \Ht_k(n;t)=t^{(k-1)n}\Ht_k(n;t^{-1}).
 \label{eq:pal}
\end{equation}
For $k=1$, $\Ht_1(n;t)=1$. For $k\ge2$,
\begin{equation}
 \Ht_k(n;t)=\F_k(t)
 -\binom{n+a}{a-1}t^{n+1}+O(t^{n+2}).
 \label{eq:sharp}
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\begin{equation}
 [t^j]\Ht_k(n;t)=[t^j]\F_k(t)\quad(0\le j\le n),
 \label{eq:stable}
\end{equation}
and $n+1$ is the first degree of disagreement for every $k\ge2$.
\end{theorem}

For $k=2a$, equation~\eqref{eq:stable} has denominator
$(1-t)^a(1-t^2)^{a^2-a}$; for $k=2a+1$ it has denominator
$(1-t)^a(1-t^2)^{a^2}$. These are precisely the two stable-coefficient
assertions in the source. The first-defect formula \eqref{eq:sharp} is a
refinement, not part of the original statement.

\subsection{Proof architecture}
The proof has three independent ingredients. First, the relation
$c_{2r+2}=(1+t)c_{2r+1}$ turns a Hankel matrix into a two-by-two block matrix
whose determinant factors by row operations. Second, the resulting factors
are principal minors of powers of coefficientwise totally nonnegative
tridiagonal matrices. This establishes positivity and degree without any
alternant or Schur identity. Finally, small alternant determinants give the
first omitted terms of the two factors and hence the exact stable range.
All determinant identities needed below are proved in the article.

\section{Two auxiliary moment sequences}
\label{sec:moments}

Put $B=1+t^2$ and $s=1+t$, so that $s^2=B+2t$.
For a Laurent polynomial in $z$, $\CT_z$ denotes its constant coefficient.
Define
\begin{equation}
 Q(z)=B+t(z+z^{-1})=(1+tz)(1+t/z),
 \quad
 u_r=\CT_z(1+z)Q(z)^r,
 \quad
 v_r=\CT_z(1-z^2)Q(z)^r.
 \label{eq:uv-ct}
\end{equation}
Both $u_r$ and $v_r$ belong to $\Z[t]$.

\begin{lemma}[The pairing relation]
\label{lem:pair}
For every $r\ge0$,
\begin{equation}
 c_{2r+1}=u_r,\qquad c_{2r+2}=s u_r,\qquad
 s^2u_r-u_{r+1}=t v_r.
 \label{eq:pair}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Expanding $Q(z)^r=(1+tz)^r(1+t/z)^r$ gives
\begin{equation}
 u_r=\sum_{j=0}^r\binom rj^2t^{2j}
 +\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\binom rj\binom r{j+1}t^{2j+1}.
 \label{eq:u-binomial}
\end{equation}
Separating the even and odd indices of
\eqref{eq:source-moments} gives $c_{2r+1}=u_r$.
The coefficients of $t^{2j}$ and $t^{2j+1}$ in $(1+t)u_r$ are respectively
\[
 \binom rj\biggl(\binom rj+\binom r{j-1}\biggr),\qquad
 \binom rj\biggl(\binom rj+\binom r{j+1}\biggr).
\]
Pascal's identity identifies these with the coefficients of $c_{2r+2}$.
For the last assertion, observe that
\[
 (s^2-Q(z))(1+z)=t(1+z-z^2-z^{-1}).
\]
Since $Q(z)=Q(z^{-1})$, the constant terms of $zQ(z)^r$ and
$z^{-1}Q(z)^r$ agree. The unwanted two terms cancel, leaving $t v_r$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Tridiagonal realizations}
Let $e_0,e_1,\ldots$ be the standard basis, let $S e_j=e_{j+1}$ be the
unilateral shift, and let $E_{00}=e_0e_0^{\mathsf T}$. For $\eps=0,1$ put
\begin{equation}
 M_\eps=B I+t(S+S^{\mathsf T})+\eps t E_{00}.
 \label{eq:M}
\end{equation}
Thus every off-diagonal entry adjacent to the diagonal is $t$, every
diagonal entry is $1+t^2$, and for $M_1$ the first diagonal entry has the
additional summand $t$. These are infinite banded matrices: every entry
of a fixed power and every minor used below is a finite calculation.

\begin{lemma}[Moment realization]
\label{lem:realization}
For $r\ge0$,
\begin{equation}
 u_r=(M_1^r)_{00},\qquad v_r=(M_0^r)_{00}.
 \label{eq:moment-M}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For the first identity use Laurent polynomials
$f_h=z^h+z^{-h-1}$, $h\ge0$. Multiplication by $z+z^{-1}$ sends
$f_0$ to $f_0+f_1$ and sends $f_h$, $h>0$, to $f_{h-1}+f_{h+1}$.
Also $\CT f_h$ is $1$ for $h=0$ and $0$ otherwise. Multiplication by
$Q(z)$ in this basis therefore has matrix $M_1$, and
$\CT Q(z)^r f_0=(M_1^r)_{00}$. Replacing $z$ by $z^{-1}$ gives the
constant term defining $u_r$.

For the second identity use $g_h=z^h-z^{-h-2}$. Multiplication by
$z+z^{-1}$ sends $g_0$ to $g_1$ and, for $h>0$, sends $g_h$ to
$g_{h-1}+g_{h+1}$. Again $\CT g_h=\delta_{h0}$. This gives $M_0$ and
$\CT Q(z)^r(1-z^{-2})=v_r$.
\end{proof}

\section{Hankel determinants as principal minors}
\label{sec:gram}

For a matrix $A$, $A[m]$ means its principal submatrix on indices
$0,\ldots,m-1$, and $\det A[0]=1$. Define
\begin{equation}
 \U_a(m;t)=\det(M_1^a[m]),\qquad
 \V_a(m;t)=\det(M_0^a[m])\qquad(a,m\ge0).
 \label{eq:UV}
\end{equation}
Here $M_\eps^a[m]$ means $(M_\eps^a)[m]$, \emph{not}
$(M_\eps[m])^a$. Paths may leave the first $m$ indices and return before
the $a$ multiplications are complete.

\begin{lemma}[The auxiliary Gram identity]
\label{lem:gram}
For $a,m\ge0$,
\begin{align}
 \det(u_{a+i+j})_{0\le i,j<m}&=t^{m(m-1)}\U_a(m;t),\label{eq:gram-u}\\
 \det(v_{a+i+j})_{0\le i,j<m}&=t^{m(m-1)}\V_a(m;t).\label{eq:gram-v}
\end{align}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
We prove both statements together. Write $M=M_\eps$ and define a moment
functional $L(X^r)=e_0^{\mathsf T}M^re_0$. Let
\[
 p_0=1,\quad p_1=X-B-\eps t,\quad
 p_{j+1}=(X-B)p_j-t^2p_{j-1}\quad(j\ge1).
\]
Induction gives $p_j(M)e_0=t^j e_j$. Consequently,
\begin{equation}
 L(p_i p_j)=t^{i+j}\delta_{ij},\qquad
 L(X^a p_i p_j)=t^{i+j}(M^a)_{ij}.
 \label{eq:orthogonality}
\end{equation}
The change from $1,X,\ldots,X^{m-1}$ to the monic polynomials
$p_0,\ldots,p_{m-1}$ is unit triangular. Changing both bases in the Hankel
Gram matrix therefore preserves its determinant. Taking the factors $t^i$
and $t^j$ out of its rows and columns gives
$t^{2\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}i}=t^{m(m-1)}$, as required.
This argument is an identity over $\Z[t]$ and does not require $t\ne0$.
\end{proof}

The normalized auxiliary quantities are thus polynomials before we use any
rational evaluation of them. In particular $\U_0(m)=\V_0(m)=1$ and
$\U_a(0)=\V_a(0)=1$.

\section{The parity factorization}
\label{sec:factor}

\begin{theorem}[Four parity cases]
\label{thm:factor}
For $a,m\ge0$,
\begin{align}
 \Ht_{2a+1}(2m;t)&=\U_a(m;t)\V_a(m;t),\label{eq:factor-oo}\\
 \Ht_{2a+1}(2m+1;t)&=\U_a(m+1;t)\V_a(m;t).\label{eq:factor-oe}
\end{align}
For $a\ge1$ and $m\ge0$,
\begin{align}
 \Ht_{2a}(2m;t)&=\U_a(m;t)\V_{a-1}(m;t),\label{eq:factor-eo}\\
 \Ht_{2a}(2m+1;t)&=(1+t)\U_{a-1}(m+1;t)\V_a(m;t).\label{eq:factor-ee}
\end{align}
In particular the normalization in \eqref{eq:normalization} is polynomial.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Reorder both rows and columns of the Hankel matrix by putting the even
indices first. Applying the same permutation on both sides contributes no
sign. Write $U_a(r)$ for the unnormalized matrix
$(u_{a+i+j})_{0\le i,j<r}$ and $V_a(r)$ for the analogous matrix of $v$'s.

For an odd shift $2a+1$, the four blocks, with the appropriate rectangular
sizes, are
\[
 \begin{pmatrix}
 (u_{a+i+j})& (s u_{a+i+j})\\
 (s u_{a+i+j})& (u_{a+i+j+1})
 \end{pmatrix}.
\]
Subtract $s$ times the corresponding even row from each odd row. The lower
left block becomes zero and the lower right block becomes
$(-t v_{a+i+j})$, by Lemma~\ref{lem:pair}. The upper left block has size
$m$ when $n=2m$ and size $m+1$ when $n=2m+1$. Therefore
\begin{align*}
 D_{2a+1}(2m)&=(-t)^m\det U_a(m)\det V_a(m),\\
 D_{2a+1}(2m+1)&=(-t)^m\det U_a(m+1)\det V_a(m).
\end{align*}
Using Lemma~\ref{lem:gram}, the total powers of $t$ are respectively
$2m^2-m=\binom{2m}{2}$ and $2m^2+m=\binom{2m+1}{2}$.
Also $(-1)^m=(-1)^{\binom n2}$ for both $n=2m$ and $n=2m+1$.
This proves the two odd-shift formulas.

For shift $2a$ and size $2m$, the reordered matrix is
\[
 \begin{pmatrix}sU_{a-1}(m)&U_a(m)\\ U_a(m)&sU_a(m)\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Work temporarily in $\Q(t)$ and subtract $s^{-1}$ times each bottom row
from the matching top row. The upper right block becomes zero, and the
upper left block is $(t/s)V_{a-1}(m)$. Hence
\[
 D_{2a}(2m)=t^m\det V_{a-1}(m)\det U_a(m).
\]
For size $2m+1$, the even block has $m+1$ rows. Subtract $s^{-1}$ times
even row $i+1$ from odd row $i$, for $0\le i<m$. The lower left block
vanishes; the lower right block becomes $(t/s)V_a(m)$, while the upper
left block is $sU_{a-1}(m+1)$. Thus
\[
 D_{2a}(2m+1)=s t^m\det U_{a-1}(m+1)\det V_a(m).
\]
The Gram identities give exactly the same two normalization exponents as
before. These are polynomial identities after denominators are cleared,
so the temporary use of $1/s$ causes no exception at $t=-1$.
\end{proof}

\section{Strict positivity, degree, and reciprocity}
\label{sec:positive}

For polynomials $P,Q$, write $P\coeffge Q$ if $P-Q$ has nonnegative
integer coefficients. A matrix is \emph{coefficientwise totally nonnegative}
if every finite minor, with row and column indices in increasing order,
is a polynomial with nonnegative coefficients.

\begin{lemma}[Total nonnegativity of the auxiliary matrices]
\label{lem:TP}
Both $M_0$ and $M_1$, and all of their nonnegative integer powers, are
coefficientwise totally nonnegative.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Since $S^{\mathsf T}S=I$ for the unilateral shift,
\begin{equation}
 M_0=(I+tS^{\mathsf T})(I+tS).
 \label{eq:bidiagonal}
\end{equation}
The two bidiagonal factors are totally nonnegative: a nonzero minor has
an order-preserving matching of its selected rows and columns and has a
nonnegative monomial weight. This can also be checked by expanding at the
first selected row; the bidiagonal zero pattern prevents a negatively
signed nonzero matching.

The Cauchy--Binet formula proves total nonnegativity of their product.
Although the matrices are infinite, each of these sums is finite because
the factors are banded. Next $M_1=M_0+tE_{00}$. A minor not selecting both
row $0$ and column $0$ is unchanged. A minor selecting both acquires
$t$ times the minor obtained by deleting its first row and first column.
The cofactor sign is positive because the selected indices are increasing.
Thus adding this corner entry preserves coefficientwise total
nonnegativity. Repeated Cauchy--Binet proves the assertion for powers.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Support and reciprocity of the factors]
\label{prop:factor-positive}
For $a,m\ge0$, $\U_a(m)$ and $\V_a(m)$ are reciprocal polynomials of
exact degree $2am$, with constant and leading coefficients $1$.
For $a,m\ge1$, every coefficient of $\U_a(m)$ is positive, whereas
$\V_a(m)$ has a positive coefficient at every even degree between $0$ and
$2am$ and no odd-degree terms. More quantitatively,
\begin{align}
 \U_a(m;t)&\coeffge(1+t+\cdots+t^{2m})^a,\label{eq:U-lower}\\
 \V_a(m;t)&\coeffge(1+t^2+\cdots+t^{2m})^a.\label{eq:V-lower}
\end{align}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The matrices satisfy $M_\eps(t^{-1})=t^{-2}M_\eps(t)$.
Taking the $a$th power and an $m$-row principal minor gives
\[
 \U_a(m;t)=t^{2am}\U_a(m;t^{-1}),\qquad
 \V_a(m;t)=t^{2am}\V_a(m;t^{-1}).
\]
At $t=0$, the matrices are $I$, so both determinants have constant term
$1$. Their exact degrees and leading coefficients follow.

The tridiagonal determinant recurrence, with $B=1+t^2$, gives
\[
 \det M_1[m]=1+t+\cdots+t^{2m},\qquad
 \det M_0[m]=1+t^2+\cdots+t^{2m}.
\]
For example the second sequence has initial values $1,B$ and recurrence
$P_m=BP_{m-1}-t^2P_{m-2}$; the first has initial values $1,B+t$
and the same recurrence. The displayed finite sums satisfy these recurrences.

Expand the principal minor of $M_\eps^a$ by repeated Cauchy--Binet. All
terms are coefficientwise nonnegative. Selecting the same index set
$\{0,\ldots,m-1\}$ at every intermediate stage supplies the term
$(\det M_\eps[m])^a$, proving the two lower bounds.
Finally $M_0(-t)=J M_0(t)J$, where $J=\diag(1,-1,1,-1,\ldots)$.
Its principal minors of powers are unchanged, so $\V_a(m)$ is even.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of the positivity and reciprocity part of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}]
Apply Theorem~\ref{thm:factor}. The degrees in its four lines are
\[
 4am,\quad 2a(2m+1),\quad 2m(2a-1),\quad (2a-1)(2m+1),
\]
which equal $(k-1)n$ in each case. All factors are reciprocal, including
$1+t$, and their constant terms are $1$.

For strict positivity, the full consecutive support of a nonconstant
$\U$ factor overlaps the translates arising from the even support of a
$\V$ factor. More explicitly, an interval $\{0,\ldots,L\}$ with $L\ge1$,
added to $\{0,2,\ldots,2R\}$, covers every integer from $0$ to $L+2R$.
The only exceptional-looking even-shift case is $a=1$, $n$ odd: there the
factor $1+t$ supplies precisely the needed interval of length one.
The cases $n=0$ and $k=1$ give the constant polynomial $1$ directly.
\end{proof}

\section{A small alternant evaluation}
\label{sec:alternants}

Positivity did not require symmetric-function identities. The stabilization
proof will also be elementary: the relevant formulas are quotients of
explicit alternating determinants. This section supplies their derivation,
including the classical moment determinant identity that is often used for
this purpose; compare the general framework in \cite{CK}.

\subsection{The Christoffel determinant identity}
\begin{lemma}[Moment modification]
\label{lem:christoffel}
Let $L$ be a moment functional over a characteristic-zero field, with monic
orthogonal polynomials $p_j$ of nonzero norms $h_j=L(p_j^2)$.
Put $H_m=\det(L(X^{i+j}))_{0\le i,j<m}$.
For independent parameters $\eta_1,\ldots,\eta_a$,
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\det\left(L\left(X^{i+j}\prod_{r=1}^a(X-\eta_r)\right)\right)_{0\le i,j<m}}{H_m}
 =(-1)^{am}
 \frac{\det(p_{m+j-1}(\eta_i))_{1\le i,j\le a}}
 {\Dlt(\eta)},
 \label{eq:christoffel}
\end{equation}
where $\Dlt(\eta)=\prod_{i<j}(\eta_j-\eta_i)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Form an $(m+a)$-square matrix whose first $m$ rows, indexed by $r$, are
$L(X^r X^j)$ for $0\le j<m+a$, and whose last $a$ rows are
$\eta_i^j$. Change the column polynomials from the monomials to
$p_0,\ldots,p_{m+a-1}$. The change is unit triangular. Orthogonality makes
the upper right block zero, and the determinant of the upper left block is
$\prod_{r=0}^{m-1}h_r=H_m$. Thus this determinant is
$H_m\det(p_{m+j-1}(\eta_i))$.

Alternatively change the column polynomials to
$1,X,\ldots,X^{a-1},P,XP,\ldots,X^{m-1}P$, where
$P=\prod_r(X-\eta_r)$. This is again a monic unit-triangular change.
Move the last $a$ rows to the top, contributing $(-1)^{am}$.
The top left block is a Vandermonde matrix and the top right block is zero.
The bottom right block is the modified moment matrix on the left of
\eqref{eq:christoffel}. Equating the two evaluations proves the identity.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The two explicit numerators}
For $a,m\ge0$ and $\delta\in\{0,1\}$, define
\begin{equation}
 N^{\delta}_{a,m}(t)=
 \det\left(
 \binom{j-1}{i-1}t^{j-i}
 -\binom{2m+2a-j+\delta}{i-1}
 t^{2m+2a-j+\delta-i+1}
 \right)_{1\le i,j\le a}.
 \label{eq:N}
\end{equation}
The first summand is interpreted as zero when $j<i$, without evaluating
its negative power. An empty determinant is $1$.

\begin{theorem}[Fixed-size evaluations]
\label{thm:alternant}
For all $a,m\ge0$,
\begin{align}
 \U_a(m;t)&=
 \frac{N^0_{a,m}(t)}{(1-t)^a(1-t^2)^{\binom a2}},\label{eq:Uclosed}\\
 \V_a(m;t)&=
 \frac{N^1_{a,m}(t)}{(1-t^2)^{\binom{a+1}{2}}}.
 \label{eq:Vclosed}
\end{align}
These identities hold in $\Q(t)$ and determine the polynomials already
constructed in \eqref{eq:UV}; apparent singularities at $t=1$ or $t=-1$
are removable. The determinant orders are $a$, independently of $m$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Work first over $\Q(t)$. Let $C_0(y)=1$, $C_1(y)=y$, and
$C_{j+1}(y)=yC_j(y)-C_{j-1}(y)$; also put $C_{-1}=0$.
The orthogonal polynomials in Lemma~\ref{lem:gram} are
\[
 p_j^\eps(X)=t^j\left[C_j\left(\frac{X-B}{t}\right)
 -\eps C_{j-1}\left(\frac{X-B}{t}\right)\right].
\]
Define monic polynomials $A_j^\eps(y)=C_j(y)+\eps C_{j-1}(y)$ and put
\[
 \eta_i=B-t(x_i+x_i^{-1}).
\]
Then $p_j^\eps(\eta_i)=(-t)^jA_j^\eps(x_i+x_i^{-1})$.
The elementary recurrence gives
\begin{align}
 A_j^0(x+x^{-1})&=\frac{x^{j+1}-x^{-j-1}}{x-x^{-1}},\label{eq:A0}\\
 A_j^1(x+x^{-1})&=\frac{x^{j+1/2}-x^{-j-1/2}}
 {x^{1/2}-x^{-1/2}}=\sum_{r=-j}^{j}x^r.\label{eq:A1}
\end{align}
The half powers in the middle expression cancel; the expression on the
right is a Laurent polynomial.

Since the $A_j^\eps$ are monic in $y$, their first $a$ evaluation columns
have determinant $\Dlt(y)$, where $y_i=x_i+x_i^{-1}$.
Apply Lemma~\ref{lem:christoffel}, cancel the column powers of $-t$ and
the Vandermonde scale, and then specialize every $\eta_i$ to zero.
Choosing every $x_i=t$ gives
\begin{equation}
 \det(M_\eps^a[m])=
 t^{am}\left.
 \frac{\det(A_{m+j-1}^\eps(x_i+x_i^{-1}))}
 {\det(A_{j-1}^\eps(x_i+x_i^{-1}))}
 \right|_{x_1=\cdots=x_a=t}.
 \label{eq:character-step}
\end{equation}
The equal-parameter value is the limit of an alternating quotient, not a
quotient of two separately substituted zero determinants.

Reverse all columns in the numerator and denominator of this quotient and
use \eqref{eq:A0}--\eqref{eq:A1}. For $\delta=1-\eps$, multiplication
of row $i$ by the appropriate power of $x_i$ gives
\begin{equation}
 \left(\prod_{i=1}^a x_i\right)^m
 \frac{\det(A_{m+j-1}^\eps(x_i+x_i^{-1}))}
 {\det(A_{j-1}^\eps(x_i+x_i^{-1}))}
 =
 \frac{\det(x_i^{j-1}-x_i^{2m+2a-j+\delta})}
 {\det(x_i^{j-1}-x_i^{2a-j+\delta})}.
 \label{eq:alternant-ratio}
\end{equation}
At $x_i=t$, the prefactor on the left is exactly $t^{am}$.

The denominator on the right has the factorization
\begin{equation}
 \det(x_i^{j-1}-x_i^{2a-j+\delta})
 =\Dlt(x)\prod_{i=1}^a(1-x_i^{1+\delta})
 \prod_{i<j}(1-x_ix_j).
 \label{eq:den-factor}
\end{equation}
Here is a direct verification. Alternation supplies $\Dlt(x)$.
At $x_i=1$ a row vanishes, and for $\delta=1$ it also vanishes at $x_i=-1$.
At $x_j=x_i^{-1}$ the two rows are proportional, with proportionality
factor $-x_i^{-(2a-1+\delta)}$, so every $1-x_ix_j$ divides the determinant.
These factors have exactly its total degree. Its lowest homogeneous term
is $\Dlt(x)$, which fixes the multiplicative constant as $1$.

Finally, for polynomials $f_j$, Taylor expansion gives the confluent identity
\begin{equation}
 \left.\frac{\det(f_j(x_i))}{\Dlt(x)}\right|_{x_1=\cdots=x_a=t}
 =\det\left(\frac{f_j^{(i-1)}(t)}{(i-1)!}\right)_{1\le i,j\le a}.
 \label{eq:confluent}
\end{equation}
Indeed the lowest alternating term in the Taylor expansion around $t$ uses
the distinct exponents $0,1,\ldots,a-1$.
Apply \eqref{eq:confluent} to the numerator in
\eqref{eq:alternant-ratio}. It gives exactly \eqref{eq:N}.
The remaining factors in \eqref{eq:den-factor}, after equal specialization,
give the denominators in \eqref{eq:Uclosed} and \eqref{eq:Vclosed}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The quotients before equal specialization are familiar rectangular
orthogonal and symplectic character alternants. No character theory,
branching formula, or bounded Schur-sum identity is being imported: the
moment identity, the recurrence, the denominator factorization, and the
confluent limit above give a complete determinant proof.
\end{remark}

\section{Sharp stable coefficients}
\label{sec:stable}

\begin{lemma}[First nonconstant numerator term]
\label{lem:first-term}
For $a\ge1$, $m\ge0$, and $\delta\in\{0,1\}$, put
$r=2m+1+\delta$. Then
\begin{equation}
 N^\delta_{a,m}(t)=1-\binom{a+r-1}{a-1}t^r+O(t^{r+1}).
 \label{eq:N-first}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Before equal specialization, expand
$\det(x_i^{j-1}-x_i^{2m+2a-j+\delta})$ by choosing either the low or the
high monomial in each column. If the high monomial is selected in column
$j$, the total degree increases, relative to $\Dlt(x)$, by
\[
 2m+2a-2j+1+\delta=r+2(a-j).
\]
The all-low choice is $\Dlt(x)$. The unique choice of smallest positive
additional degree is to select only the high monomial in column $a$;
its sign is negative and its additional degree is $r$.
Every other choice has strictly larger additional degree.

After division by $\Dlt(x)$ and equal specialization, the coefficient of
this first correction can be computed directly from
\eqref{eq:confluent}. The column exponents are
$0,1,\ldots,a-2,a-1+r$. The first $a-1$ columns are triangular with
unit diagonal, so the remaining determinant is
$\binom{a-1+r}{a-1}t^r$. This proves the assertion, including $a=1$.
\end{proof}

Define the formal series
\begin{equation}
 U_a^\infty(t)=\frac{1}{(1-t)^a(1-t^2)^{\binom a2}},\qquad
 V_a^\infty(t)=\frac{1}{(1-t^2)^{\binom{a+1}{2}}}.
 \label{eq:limits}
\end{equation}
Their constant terms are $1$. For $a=0$ both are exactly $1$.
Theorem~\ref{thm:alternant} and Lemma~\ref{lem:first-term} imply, for
$a\ge1$,
\begin{align}
 \U_a(m;t)&=U_a^\infty(t)
 -\binom{2m+a}{a-1}t^{2m+1}+O(t^{2m+2}),\label{eq:Udefect}\\
 \V_a(m;t)&=V_a^\infty(t)
 -\binom{2m+a+1}{a-1}t^{2m+2}+O(t^{2m+3}).\label{eq:Vdefect}
\end{align}
Multiplying by a series with constant term $1$ does not change the first
nonzero correction, which is why the same binomial coefficient appears in
these formulas.

\begin{proof}[Completion of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}]
For $n=2m$ the earliest correction in both
\eqref{eq:factor-oo} and \eqref{eq:factor-eo} comes from $\U_a(m)$,
at degree $2m+1=n+1$. Its magnitude is
$\binom{2m+a}{a-1}=\binom{n+a}{a-1}$.
The products of the corresponding limiting series are respectively
\[
 U_a^\infty V_a^\infty
 =\frac1{(1-t)^a(1-t^2)^{a^2}},\qquad
 U_a^\infty V_{a-1}^\infty
 =\frac1{(1-t)^a(1-t^2)^{a(a-1)}}.
\]
For $n=2m+1$ the earliest correction in both
\eqref{eq:factor-oe} and \eqref{eq:factor-ee} comes from $\V_a(m)$,
at degree $2m+2=n+1$, with magnitude
$\binom{2m+a+1}{a-1}=\binom{n+a}{a-1}$.
For the last parity case the limiting product simplifies as
\[
 (1+t)U_{a-1}^\infty V_a^\infty
 =\frac{1+t}{(1-t)^{a-1}(1-t^2)^{a^2-a+1}}
 =\frac1{(1-t)^a(1-t^2)^{a(a-1)}}.
\]
All multiplying factors have constant term $1$, so they do not alter the
first correction coefficient. When $a=1$, any factor with index $a-1=0$
is identically $1$, and the same argument applies. The positive binomial
coefficient proves optimality of the range. The case $k=1$ was already
obtained directly from the factorization.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Coefficient extraction without a Hankel determinant]
For $k\ge2$, $0\le j\le n$, and $a,b$ as in \eqref{eq:F},
\begin{equation}
 [t^j]\Ht_k(n;t)
 =\sum_{\ell=0}^{\lfloor j/2\rfloor}
 W(ab,\ell)W(a,j-2\ell),
 \label{eq:stable-sum}
\end{equation}
where $W(q,r)=\binom{q+r-1}{r}$ for $q\ge1$, and
$W(0,r)=1$ for $r=0$ and $0$ otherwise.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Expand $(1-t)^{-a}$ and $(1-t^2)^{-ab}$ and use
\eqref{eq:stable}. The convention for $W(0,r)$ handles $k=2$ without
an artificial negative upper binomial index.
\end{proof}

\section{Examples and checks on the statement}
\label{sec:examples}

\subsection{The first three shifts}
The factorization immediately gives
\begin{equation}
 \Ht_1(n;t)=1,\qquad
 \Ht_2(n;t)=1+t+\cdots+t^n.
 \label{eq:k12}
\end{equation}
For $k=3$, insert
$\U_1(m)=1+t+\cdots+t^{2m}$ and
$\V_1(m)=1+t^2+\cdots+t^{2m}$ in the two parity cases. Both simplify to
\begin{equation}
 \Ht_3(n;t)=\frac{(1-t^{n+1})(1-t^{n+2})}{(1-t)(1-t^2)}.
 \label{eq:k3}
\end{equation}
These small-shift formulas are already printed in Cigler's paper; they
serve as indexing and sign checks, not as new results.

\subsection{A first-defect example}
For $k=4$, the limiting series is
\[
 \F_4(t)=\frac1{(1-t)^2(1-t^2)^2}
 =1+2t+5t^2+8t^3+14t^4+\cdots.
\]
At $n=2$ the determinant gives
\[
 \Ht_4(2;t)=1+2t+5t^2+4t^3+5t^4+2t^5+t^6.
\]
The first three coefficients agree. At degree $3=n+1$ the deficit is
$8-4=4=\binom{2+2}{2-1}$, exactly as predicted.
At $n=3$,
\[
 \Ht_4(3;t)=1+2t+5t^2+8t^3+9t^4+9t^5+8t^6+5t^7+2t^8+t^9,
\]
and the first deficit is $14-9=5=\binom{3+2}{2-1}$.

These examples also delimit the result. The coefficients $5,4,5$ in
$\Ht_4(2;t)$ have a strict valley. Thus positivity and reciprocity do
\emph{not} imply unimodality here. No unimodality or log-concavity assertion
is included in Theorem~\ref{thm:main}.

\subsection{Signs and exceptional parameters}
For real $t>0$, strict coefficient positivity gives the exact sign
\begin{equation}
 \sgn D_k(n;t)=(-1)^{k\binom n2}.
 \label{eq:sign}
\end{equation}
At $t=0$ the normalized polynomial has value $1$, although the original
Hankel determinant vanishes when $n\ge2$. At $t=1$ the source moments
are the middle binomial coefficients. At $t=-1$ the factor $1+t$ in
\eqref{eq:factor-ee} shows that every positive even-shift, odd-size determinant
vanishes. These statements follow from polynomial identities; no argument
uses an undefined specialization of a rational formula.

\section{Algorithms and further consequences}
\label{sec:consequences}

\subsection{Exact computation at fixed shift}
Theorem~\ref{thm:alternant} evaluates the factors through determinants of
order at most $\lfloor k/2\rfloor$, even when $n$ is very large.
A practical algorithm is to determine the parity of $k,n$, compute the
one or two numerators in \eqref{eq:N}, divide by their proved exact
denominators, and multiply according to Theorem~\ref{thm:factor}.
For $t=\pm1$, use the principal-minor definition or take the polynomial
limit instead of evaluating a zero denominator.

The reduction is in determinant \emph{order}, not in bit complexity.
The entries contain powers whose exponents grow with $n$, and an expanded
answer has degree $(k-1)n$. Once entries are constructed, ordinary dense
elimination uses a cubic number of field operations in the small determinant
order; large integers and polynomial output still carry their usual costs.
For only the first $j\le n$ coefficients, \eqref{eq:stable-sum} avoids
both determinant constructions.

\subsection{A concrete, nonminimal recurrence}
\begin{proposition}[Rationality at each fixed shift]
\label{prop:recurrence}
For $k\ge2$, set
\[
 d_k=\begin{cases}(a-1)^2,&k=2a,\\ a(a-1),&k=2a+1.\end{cases}
\]
The series $\sum_{n\ge0}\Ht_k(n;t)x^n$ is rational over $\Q(t)$,
with a denominator dividing
\begin{equation}
 \prod_{q=0}^{k-1}(1-t^{2q}x^2)^{d_k+1}.
 \label{eq:coarse-den}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, with $E$ denoting forward shift in $n$,
\begin{equation}
 \prod_{q=0}^{k-1}(E^2-t^{2q})^{d_k+1}\Ht_k(n;t)=0.
 \label{eq:coarse-rec}
\end{equation}
The bound is not asserted to be minimal and is not a resolution of all the
more precise denominator and numerator assertions in Conjecture 18.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Expand \eqref{eq:N} column by column. A term selecting $q$ high columns
has the form $P(m;t)t^{2qm}$, where $P$ is a polynomial in $m$ with
coefficients in $\Q(t)$. Its degree in $m$ is at most
$\sum_{i=1}^a(i-1)=\binom a2$, because the binomial in row $i$ has degree
at most $i-1$. The denominators in Theorem~\ref{thm:alternant} do not
depend on $m$. Thus each auxiliary factor is a finite sum of
$m^j(t^{2q})^m$, with $0\le q\le a$ and $j\le\binom a2$.

The products in the two parity subsequences have $0\le q\le k-1$.
Their degree bounds are $2\binom a2=a(a-1)$ in the odd-shift case and
$\binom a2+\binom{a-1}{2}=(a-1)^2$ in the even-shift case.
Replacing $m$ by $m+1$ changes neither bound. A polynomial of degree at
most $d$ times $\lambda^m$ is annihilated by $(E_m-\lambda)^{d+1}$,
as follows by taking successive finite differences. Interleaving the
even and odd subsequences replaces $E_m$ by $E^2$, proving the claim.
\end{proof}

For the unadjusted normalized determinant
$d_k(n;t)=t^{-\binom n2}D_k(n;t)$, the even-$k$ recurrence is unchanged.
For odd $k$, the period-four sign replaces $1-t^{2q}x^2$ in the displayed

\subsection{Actual convergence, not just formal stabilization}
\begin{proposition}[Uniform convergence inside the unit disk]
\label{prop:analytic}
Fix $k\ge2$ and $0<r<1$. There is a constant $C_{k,r}$ such that, for all
$n\ge0$,
\begin{equation}
 \sup_{|t|\le r}|\Ht_k(n;t)-\F_k(t)|
 \le C_{k,r}(n+1)^{\binom a2}r^{n+1}.
 \label{eq:analytic}
\end{equation}
In particular the polynomials converge to $\F_k$ locally uniformly on
$|t|<1$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
In the expansion of $N^\delta_{a,m}-1$, every nonzero term has degree at
least $2m+1+\delta$ in $t$. There are finitely many determinant terms for
fixed $a$, and each coefficient is bounded by a constant times
$(m+1)^{\binom a2}$, by the row-degree estimate just used.
It follows, uniformly on $|t|\le r$, that
\[
 N^\delta_{a,m}(t)-1
 =O_{a,r}\bigl((m+1)^{\binom a2}r^{2m+1+\delta}\bigr).
\]
The two fixed denominators in Theorem~\ref{thm:alternant} are bounded
away from zero on this disk. Hence the factors converge uniformly to
\eqref{eq:limits}, with these bounds, and in particular remain uniformly
bounded as $m$ varies. Subtract the limiting products in the four parity
formulas. The earliest error has exponent $n+1$, exactly as in
Section~\ref{sec:stable}; the largest polynomial exponent is $\binom a2$.
The finitely many small cases can be absorbed in $C_{k,r}$.
\end{proof}

with relative error $O(1/n)$ locally uniformly on $(0,1)$

\section{A stronger identity suggested by the computations}
\label{sec:schur}

The discovery phase suggested a compact formula not needed for any theorem
above. We record it as a separate conjecture so that a promising observation
is not silently promoted to a proof.

For an alphabet $X=(x_1,\ldots,x_k)$, define $h_j(X)$ by
$\sum_{j\ge0}h_j(X)z^j=\prod_{i=1}^k(1-x_i z)^{-1}$, with $h_j=0$ for
$j<0$. The rectangular Schur polynomial is
\[
 s_{(n^a)}(X)=\det(h_{n-i+j}(X))_{1\le i,j\le a}.
\]

\begin{conjecture}[Experimental rectangular-Schur formula]
\label{conj:schur}
With $a=\lfloor k/2\rfloor$ and $b=\lfloor(k-1)/2\rfloor$,
\begin{equation}
 \Ht_k(n;t)=s_{(n^a)}
 (\underbrace{1,\ldots,1}_{a},\ t,\
 \underbrace{t^2,\ldots,t^2}_{b}).
 \label{eq:schur-candidate}
\end{equation}
\end{conjecture}

The conjecture agrees with all 1,248 exact evaluations in the range recorded
in Section~\ref{sec:verify}. For instance, at $k=4,n=2$, its right-hand
side is $h_2^2-h_1h_3$ in the alphabet $(1,1,t,t^2)$, giving the
polynomial displayed in Section~\ref{sec:examples}.

A proof of \eqref{eq:schur-candidate} would give an attractive unified
symmetric-function interpretation. It may be approachable through
factorizations of rectangular Schur polynomials into classical-character
alternants. This article does not assert such a factorization without
proof. The proof of Conjecture 16 instead uses the parity factors,
coefficientwise total nonnegativity, and the fully derived alternant
formulas. The larger conjecture can be deleted from this article without
changing any proved statement.

\subsection{Logical dependencies and possible failure points}
The main theorem depends only on the binomial definition, the Laurent
basis calculation, elementary block row operations, Cauchy--Binet,
monic orthogonal-polynomial recurrences, and alternating determinants.
The proof of the moment-modification identity is included rather than
used as an unexplained import. The exact role of each delicate point is
as follows.

The odd/even row reorder is applied to both rows and columns, so there is
no permutation sign. The only sign remaining is $(-1)^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}$
for odd shifts, which equals the prescribed $(-1)^{\binom n2}$.
The temporary division by $1+t$ in the even-shift factorization is removed
before specialization. The auxiliary normalization is justified by an
identity in $\Z[t]$, not by dividing a determinant whose vanishing has
not been established. Equal-variable alternants are evaluated after
cancelling the Vandermonde, not by a $0/0$ substitution. Finally,
strict positivity is obtained from explicit Cauchy--Binet summands;
nonnegativity alone would not have been sufficient.

investigated separately: indexed material identifies its target as the

successfully fetched, so this disambiguation rests on the indexed author

The article makes no absolute priority claim. It also does not claim to
settle all parts of Cigler's Conjectures 17 and 18, or the separate Schur
formula \eqref{eq:schur-candidate}. What is claimed mathematically is the
full argument for Conjecture 16 and the additional results explicitly

\section{The removable specialization at \texorpdfstring{$t=1$}{t=1}}
\label{app:one}

The factors have convenient product evaluations at $1$:
\begin{align}
 \U_a(m;1)&=
 \prod_{r=1}^a\frac{2m+2r-1}{2r-1}
 \prod_{1\le r<s\le a}\frac{2m+r+s-1}{r+s-1},\label{eq:Uone}\\
 \V_a(m;1)&=
 \prod_{r=1}^a\frac{m+r}{r}
 \prod_{1\le r<s\le a}\frac{2m+r+s}{r+s}.
 \label{eq:Vone}
\end{align}
Empty products have value $1$.
To see these formulas directly from the proved alternants, set
$x_i=e^{z_i}$ before taking the equal-parameter limit in
\eqref{eq:character-step}. For $\V$, the numerator contains
$\sinh((m+r)z_i)$ and the denominator $\sinh(rz_i)$, with $1\le r\le a$.
For $\U$, replace $r$ by $r-\tfrac12$.
The lowest nonzero alternating term of
$\det(\sinh(\rho_j z_i))$ is a common nonzero constant times
\[
 \left(\prod_i z_i\right)\Dlt(z_1^2,\ldots,z_a^2)
 \left(\prod_j\rho_j\right)\Dlt(\rho_1^2,\ldots,\rho_a^2).
\]
This follows by selecting the powers $1,3,\ldots,2a-1$ in the Taylor
series. In the ratio, the differences between the $\rho_j$ cancel,
whereas the sums give the second products in
\eqref{eq:Uone}--\eqref{eq:Vone}. These specialize the new parity formula
to the classical middle-binomial Hankel setting already treated in the
source; the unweighted setting itself is not claimed as new.

\subsection{Definitions}
For $r\ge1$, put
\begin{equation}
 c_r(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{r}
 \binom{\lfloor(r-1)/2\rfloor}{\lfloor(j-1)/2\rfloor}
 \binom{\lfloor r/2\rfloor}{\lfloor j/2\rfloor}t^{j-1},
 \qquad c_0(t)=1.
 \label{rs:eq:moments}
\end{equation}
A binomial coefficient is zero when its lower index is outside the usual
range. These are Cigler's equation~(6), with the zero $j=0$ summand omitted.
For a positive shift $k$ and order $n\ge0$, define
\begin{align}
 D_k(n;t)&=\det(c_{k+i+j}(t))_{0\le i,j<n},\label{rs:eq:D}\\
 \Ht_k(n;t)&=(-1)^{k\binom n2}t^{-\binom n2}D_k(n;t).
 \label{rs:eq:H}
\end{align}
Empty determinants are one. Initially \eqref{rs:eq:H} is read in $\Q(t)$;
polynomiality and specialization at $t=0$ are conclusions.
Throughout, write
\begin{equation}
 a=\lfloor k/2\rfloor,\quad b=\lfloor(k-1)/2\rfloor,\quad
 M=k-1,\quad \XX_k=(\rep1a,t,\rep{t^2}b).
 \label{rs:eq:ab}
\end{equation}
Here $x^{[r]}$ means $r$ copies of $x$, including no copies when $r=0$.
The alphabet $\XX_k$ has exactly $k$ entries.

For an alphabet $X$, define $h_j(X)$ and $e_j(X)$ by
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{j\ge0}h_j(X)w^j=\prod_{x\in X}(1-xw)^{-1},\qquad
 \sum_{j\ge0}e_j(X)w^j=\prod_{x\in X}(1+xw).
 \label{rs:eq:symmetric}
\end{equation}
Subscripts below zero give zero. The Schur polynomial of the rectangle with
$a$ rows of length $n$ has the classical Jacobi--Trudi descriptions
\begin{equation}
 s_{(n^a)}(X)=\det(h_{n-i+j}(X))_{1\le i,j\le a}
 =\det(e_{a-i+j}(X))_{1\le i,j\le n}.
 \label{rs:eq:JT}
\end{equation}
These identities, the tableau interpretation, and the bialternant formula
are standard Schur identities \cite{Fulton,GSM}. Empty rectangles have value
one. They are the only symmetric-function identifications needed for the
main determinant proof.

\subsection{Main statements}
\begin{theorem}[The repository's rectangular-Schur conjecture]
\label{rs:thm:schur}
For all $k\ge1$ and $n\ge0$, there is an identity in $\Z[t]$
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{D_k(n;t)=(-1)^{k\binom n2}t^{\binom n2}
                 s_{(n^a)}(\XX_k).}
 \label{rs:eq:main}
\end{equation}
Consequently $\Ht_k(n;t)=s_{(n^a)}(\XX_k)$, with no exceptional value of $t$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Exact generic recurrence]
\label{rs:thm:recurrence-main}
For $k\ge1$, set
\begin{equation}
 e_{k,q}=1+\left\lfloor\frac{q(k-1-q)}2\right\rfloor,
 \qquad N_k=\sum_{q=0}^{k-1}e_{k,q}.
 \label{rs:eq:exponents}
\end{equation}
Over $\Q(t)$, the reduced denominator of
$\GG_k(z,t)=\sum_{n\ge0}\Ht_k(n;t)z^n$, normalized to have constant term one,
is exactly
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\QQ_k(z,t)=\prod_{q=0}^{k-1}(1-t^qz)^{e_{k,q}}.}
 \label{rs:eq:denominator}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, the monic minimal constant-coefficient recurrence operator is
$\prod_q(E-t^q)^{e_{k,q}}$, where $Ef(n)=f(n+1)$.
There is no cancellation in \eqref{rs:eq:denominator}. The same statement holds
at each real $t\notin\{0,1,-1\}$.
\end{theorem}

The precise numerator and its sign are given in
Theorem~\ref{rs:thm:numerator}; the period-four signed normalization used by
Cigler is treated in Theorem~\ref{rs:thm:signed}. Theorem~\ref{rs:thm:real} completes
the real-parameter classification. The general mechanism that makes the
recurrences minimal is Theorem~\ref{rs:thm:cluster}, whose explicit leading
coefficient also gives the sharp asymptotic equivalent in
Theorem~\ref{rs:thm:asymptotic}.

\subsection{Two elementary Gram identities}
Let $q(z)=q(z^{-1})$ be a symmetric Laurent polynomial, and put
$q_j=[z^j]q(z)$, so $q_{-j}=q_j$. Define
\begin{align}
 \UU_m(q)&=\det(q_{i-j}+q_{i+j+1})_{0\le i,j<m},\label{rs:eq:U}\\
 \VV_m(q)&=\det(q_{i-j}-q_{i+j+2})_{0\le i,j<m}.
 \label{rs:eq:V}
\end{align}
Both empty determinants are one. Set $x=z+z^{-1}$ and $y=z-z^{-1}$.
For $i\ge0$, the Laurent expressions
\begin{equation}
 P_i(x)=\frac{z^{i+1}+z^{-i}}{z+1},\qquad
 R_i(x)=\frac{z^{i+1}-z^{-i-1}}{z-z^{-1}}
 \label{rs:eq:PR}
\end{equation}
are monic polynomials in $x$ of degree $i$. Indeed, $P_0=R_0=1$,
$P_1=x-1$, $R_1=x$, and each sequence satisfies
$F_{i+1}=xF_i-F_{i-1}$.
A direct multiplication gives
\begin{align}
 \tfrac12\CT_z q(z)(x+2)P_i(x)P_j(x)&=q_{i-j}+q_{i+j+1},\label{rs:eq:gramU}\\
 \tfrac12\CT_z q(z)(4-x^2)R_i(x)R_j(x)&=q_{i-j}-q_{i+j+2}.
 \label{rs:eq:gramV}
\end{align}
For example, $(x+2)=(z+1)^2/z$, and the product on the first line
has four monomials before the constant term is taken. Symmetry of $q$
merges them in pairs. For the second line use $4-x^2=-y^2$.
Thus $\UU_m$ and $\VV_m$ are ordinary polynomial Gram determinants for
the two displayed constant-term weights. Monicity means that replacing
$P_i$ or $R_i$ by $x^i$ does not change either determinant.

\subsection{The moments}
Put
\begin{equation}
 Q_t(z)=1+t^2+t(z+z^{-1})=(1+tz)(1+t/z),\qquad s=1+t,
 \label{rs:eq:Q}
\end{equation}
and define
\begin{equation}
 u_r=\CT_z(1+z)Q_t(z)^r,\qquad
 v_r=\CT_z(1-z^2)Q_t(z)^r.
 \label{rs:eq:uv}
\end{equation}
Symmetry allows the alternative weights $(x+2)/2$ and $(4-x^2)/2$,
respectively. Expansion of the two binomials in $Q_t^r$ yields
\begin{equation}
 u_r=\sum_{j=0}^{r}\binom rj^2t^{2j}
       +\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\binom rj\binom r{j+1}t^{2j+1}.
 \label{rs:eq:u-expansion}
\end{equation}
Separating parity in \eqref{rs:eq:moments} and applying Pascal's identity gives
\begin{equation}
 c_{2r+1}=u_r,\qquad c_{2r+2}=s u_r.
 \label{rs:eq:pair}
\end{equation}
Moreover,
$(s^2-Q_t)(1+z)=t(1+z-z^2-z^{-1})$.
The constant terms of $zQ_t^r$ and $z^{-1}Q_t^r$ agree, so
\begin{equation}
 s^2u_r-u_{r+1}=t v_r.
 \label{rs:eq:christoffel-pair}
\end{equation}
For short, write
\begin{equation}
 U_h(m)=\UU_m(Q_t^h),\qquad V_h(m)=\VV_m(Q_t^h)
 \quad(h,m\ge0).
 \label{rs:eq:UVh}
\end{equation}
Since $Q_t=1+t^2+tx$, its $i$th power has leading coefficient $t^i$
as a polynomial in $x$. Applying the monic basis changes in
\eqref{rs:eq:gramU}--\eqref{rs:eq:gramV} therefore gives
\begin{align}
 \det(u_{h+i+j})_{0\le i,j<m}&=t^{m(m-1)}U_h(m),\label{rs:eq:Gram-u}\\
 \det(v_{h+i+j})_{0\le i,j<m}&=t^{m(m-1)}V_h(m).
 \label{rs:eq:Gram-v}
\end{align}
The exponent is twice $\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}i$. These are polynomial identities,
not assertions obtained by substituting zero into an undefined quotient.

\subsection{Block elimination}
\begin{proposition}[Parity formulas]
\label{rs:prop:parity}
For $h,m\ge0$,
\begin{align}
 \Ht_{2h+1}(2m;t)&=U_h(m)V_h(m),\label{rs:eq:parity1}\\
 \Ht_{2h+1}(2m+1;t)&=U_h(m+1)V_h(m).
 \label{rs:eq:parity2}
\end{align}
For $h\ge1$ and $m\ge0$,
\begin{align}
 \Ht_{2h}(2m;t)&=U_h(m)V_{h-1}(m),\label{rs:eq:parity3}\\
 \Ht_{2h}(2m+1;t)&=(1+t)U_{h-1}(m+1)V_h(m).
 \label{rs:eq:parity4}
\end{align}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Reorder both rows and columns of the Hankel matrix with the even indices
first. The permutation signs cancel. For an odd shift $2h+1$, the four
blocks have entries $u_{h+i+j}$, $s u_{h+i+j}$, $s u_{h+i+j}$, and
$u_{h+i+j+1}$. Subtract $s$ times even row $i$ from odd row $i$.
The lower-left block becomes zero; the lower-right block becomes
$-t(v_{h+i+j})$. Its size is $m$ for both orders $2m$ and $2m+1$;
the upper-left block has size $m$ or $m+1$. Equations
\eqref{rs:eq:Gram-u}--\eqref{rs:eq:Gram-v} give the normalization powers
$2m^2-m=\binom{2m}{2}$ and $2m^2+m=\binom{2m+1}{2}$.
The remaining sign $(-1)^m$ equals $(-1)^{\binom n2}$.

For an even shift $2h$ and even order, abbreviate the unnormalized moment
matrices by $U'_r=(u_{r+i+j})_{0\le i,j<m}$. The reordered matrix is
\[
 \begin{pmatrix}sU'_{h-1}&U'_h\\ U'_h&sU'_h\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Over $\Q(t)$, subtract $s^{-1}$ times each lower row from the corresponding
upper row. The upper-right block vanishes and the upper-left becomes
$(t/s)(v_{h-1+i+j})$. The determinant is consequently
$t^m\det(v_{h-1+i+j})\det(u_{h+i+j})$.

For odd order $2m+1$, subtract $s^{-1}$ times even row $i+1$ from odd
row $i$, for $0\le i<m$. The lower-left block vanishes; the lower-right
is $(t/s)(v_{h+i+j})$, and the upper-left is
$s(u_{h-1+i+j})$ of size $m+1$. The remaining scalar factor is $s t^m$.
The same two normalization powers give \eqref{rs:eq:parity3} and
\eqref{rs:eq:parity4}. All final expressions are polynomials, so the temporary
use of $s^{-1}$ introduces no exception at $t=-1$.
\end{proof}

\section{The missing Toeplitz factorization}
\label{rs:sec:toeplitz}
For a Laurent polynomial $f$, let
\begin{equation}
 T_n(f)=\det([z^{j-i}]f(z))_{0\le i,j<n}.
 \label{rs:eq:T}
\end{equation}
The following two formulas are the bridge needed by the repository's
experimental conjecture. We prove them by elementary finite-dimensional
linear algebra, rather than requiring a classical-group character
factorization as a black box.

\begin{theorem}[Two factorizations with a boundary parameter]
\label{rs:thm:Toeplitz}
For every symmetric Laurent polynomial $q$ and every $m\ge0$,
\begin{align}
 T_{2m}((1+z)(1+u/z)q)&=\UU_m(Q_uq)\VV_m(q),\label{rs:eq:Toeplitz-even}\\
 T_{2m+1}((1+z)(1+u/z)q)&=(1+u)\UU_{m+1}(q)\VV_m(Q_uq),
 \label{rs:eq:Toeplitz-odd}
\end{align}
where $Q_u=1+u^2+u(z+z^{-1})$. These are polynomial identities in $u$
and the coefficients of $q$. In particular,
\begin{equation}
 T_n((1+z)q)=\UU_{\lceil n/2\rceil}(q)\VV_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}(q).
 \label{rs:eq:Toeplitz-zero}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
It is enough first to work over a characteristic-zero rational-function
field, with $u\ne0,1,-1$ and independent coefficients for $q$.
For Laurent polynomials define
\[
 \langle f,g\rangle_q=\CT_z q(z)f(z)g(z^{-1}).
\]
This form is symmetric. In consecutive monomial bases for a space $V$, the
pairing of the left vectors $(1+z)z^i$ with the right vectors
$(1+uz)z^j$ is the Toeplitz matrix of $(1+z)(1+u/z)q$.
Translating all exponents by the same integer leaves its entries unchanged.
We choose centered exponent ranges to expose the symmetry.

\paragraph{Even order.}
For $n=2m$, take
\[
 V=\operatorname{span}\{z^{-m},\ldots,z^{m-1}\},\qquad
 W=\operatorname{span}\{z^{-m},\ldots,z^m\}.
\]
Every element of $W$ has a unique decomposition
$p(x)+y r(x)$ with $\deg p\le m$ and $\deg r\le m-1$.
The left space $(1+z)V$ is exactly the subspace of $W$ vanishing at $z=-1$.
Thus it has basis
\[
 (x+2)x^i\ (0\le i<m),\qquad yx^i\ (0\le i<m).
\]
Put
\[
 z_0=-u^{-1},\qquad x_0=-(u+u^{-1}),\qquad y_0=u-u^{-1}.
\]
The right space $(1+uz)V$ is the kernel of evaluation at $z_0$ and has basis
\[
 (x-x_0)x^j\ (0\le j<m),\qquad
 yx^j-y_0x_0^j\ (0\le j<m).
\]
Linear independence follows separately from the symmetric and antisymmetric
parts; their total dimensions are $2m$. Symmetric Laurent polynomials are
orthogonal to antisymmetric ones for $\langle\ ,\ \rangle_q$.
The lower-left block is therefore zero. The two diagonal blocks are the
moment matrices for the weights
\[
 (x+2)(x-x_0)q\quad\hbox{and}\quad(4-x^2)q.
\]
Because $u(x-x_0)=Q_u$, their determinants are, up to factors depending
only on $u$ and $m$, $\UU_m(Q_uq)$ and $\VV_m(q)$ by
\eqref{rs:eq:gramU}--\eqref{rs:eq:gramV}.

Every basis change just made is independent of $q$. Consequently there is
a nonzero rational function $C_{2m}(u)$, independent of $q$, such that the
left side of \eqref{rs:eq:Toeplitz-even} equals
$C_{2m}(u)\UU_m(Q_uq)\VV_m(q)$. We determine this scalar below.
This observation avoids hiding any powers of two, powers of $u$, or basis
orientation signs.

\paragraph{Odd order.}
For $n=2m+1$, take
\[
 V=\operatorname{span}\{z^{-m},\ldots,z^m\},\qquad
 W=\operatorname{span}\{z^{-m},\ldots,z^{m+1}\}.
\]
The involution $Jf(z)=zf(z^{-1})$ is an isometry of the pairing.
Its two eigenspaces are orthogonal, and every element of $W$ is uniquely
\[
 (1+z)p(x)+(z-1)r(x),\qquad \deg p,\deg r\le m.
\]
A basis of the left space is
\[
 (1+z)x^i\ (0\le i\le m),\qquad
 (z-1)(x+2)x^i\ (0\le i<m).
\]
This follows by writing the symmetric and antisymmetric parts of $V$ and
using $(1+z)y=(z-1)(x+2)$.
Set $\gamma=(u-1)/(u+1)$. A basis of the right evaluation kernel is
\[
 (1+z)x^j+\gamma(z-1)x_0^j\ (0\le j\le m),\qquad
 (z-1)(x-x_0)x^j\ (0\le j<m).
\]
Indeed $(1+z_0)+\gamma(z_0-1)=0$, and dimensions and independence again
follow from the two eigenspaces. The upper-right block is zero. The diagonal
blocks now have weights
\[
 (x+2)q\quad\hbox{and}\quad(4-x^2)(x-x_0)q,
\]
because $(z-1)(z^{-1}-1)=2-x$. Their determinants are proportional to
$\UU_{m+1}(q)\VV_m(Q_uq)$, with a scalar $C_{2m+1}(u)$ independent of $q$.

\paragraph{Normalization and specialization.}
For $q=1$, both $\UU_m(1)$ and $\VV_m(1)$ equal one. Tridiagonal
continuant recurrences give
\begin{align*}
 T_n((1+z)(1+u/z))&=1+u+\cdots+u^n,\\
 \UU_m(Q_u)&=1+u+\cdots+u^{2m},\\
 \VV_m(Q_u)&=1+u^2+\cdots+u^{2m}.
\end{align*}
For example, all three recurrences follow by expanding the last row; the
first has diagonal $1+u$ and off-diagonal product $u$, while the other two
have diagonal $1+u^2$, off-diagonal product $u^2$, and, for $\UU$, an extra
$u$ in the first diagonal position. Their initial values fix the displayed
sums. They imply $C_{2m}=1$ and $C_{2m+1}=1+u$.
Both sides of the asserted identities are polynomials. Equality on the
generic parameter field proves them identically, including $u=0,1,-1$ and
singular choices of $q$. Substitution $u=0$ gives
\eqref{rs:eq:Toeplitz-zero}.
\end{proof}

\section{Proof of the Schur identity and coefficient consequences}
\label{rs:sec:schurproof}
\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{rs:thm:schur}]
Write $E_k(w)=\prod_{x\in\XX_k}(1+xw)$ and, temporarily over $\Q(t)$, put
\begin{equation}
 f_k(z)=t^a z^{-a}E_k(z/t).
 \label{rs:eq:fk}
\end{equation}
Its coefficient at $z^{j-i}$ is $t^{i-j}e_{a+j-i}(\XX_k)$.
The row factors $t^i$ and column factors $t^{-j}$ cancel in the determinant,
so dual Jacobi--Trudi gives
\begin{equation}
 T_n(f_k)=s_{(n^a)}(\XX_k).
 \label{rs:eq:Tschur}
\end{equation}
The point of the chosen alphabet is the following exact factorization:
\begin{equation}
 f_k(z)=
 \begin{cases}
 (1+z)Q_t(z)^a,&k=2a+1,\\
 (1+z)(1+t/z)Q_t(z)^{a-1},&k=2a.
 \end{cases}
 \label{rs:eq:symbol}
\end{equation}
For instance, each paired factor contributes
$t z^{-1}(1+z/t)(1+tz)=Q_t(z)$.
For odd $k$, \eqref{rs:eq:Toeplitz-zero} yields exactly
\eqref{rs:eq:parity1}--\eqref{rs:eq:parity2}. For even $k$, use
\eqref{rs:eq:Toeplitz-even}--\eqref{rs:eq:Toeplitz-odd} with $u=t$ and
$q=Q_t^{a-1}$; these yield \eqref{rs:eq:parity3}--\eqref{rs:eq:parity4}.
Thus $T_n(f_k)=\Ht_k(n;t)$ in all four cases.
The resulting equality \eqref{rs:eq:main} is between polynomials in $\Z[t]$,
so it holds there and at every specialization. The case $k=1$ gives the
empty-height Schur polynomial one.
\end{proof}

\subsection{A tableau model and reciprocal coefficients}
The identity says that $\Ht_k(n;t)$ counts semistandard tableaux of shape
$(n^a)$, with letters in an ordered alphabet of $k$ symbols whose weights are
$0$ for the first $a$ letters, $1$ for the next, and $2$ for the last $b$.
The exponent of $t$ is the sum of those weights. Thus the normalization in
\eqref{rs:eq:H}, including its sign, turns the original Hankel determinant into
a genuine nonnegative generating polynomial.

\begin{corollary}[Previously known shape properties, recovered]
\label{rs:cor:shape}
The polynomial $\Ht_k(n;t)$ has exact degree $(k-1)n$, constant and leading
coefficients one, positive coefficients at every intermediate degree, and
\begin{equation}
 \Ht_k(n;t^{-1})=t^{-(k-1)n}\Ht_k(n;t).
 \label{rs:eq:Hreciprocity}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The all-zero-weight tableau is unique: every column must contain the first
$a$ letters in order. Thus the constant term is one.
For odd $k$, the inverse alphabet is a permutation of $t^{-2}\XX_k$.
Homogeneity of Schur degree $an$ proves \eqref{rs:eq:Hreciprocity}.
For even $k=2a$, use the rectangular complement identity
\begin{equation}
 s_{(n^{K-A})}(X)=(\prod X)^n s_{(n^A)}(X^{-1})
 \quad(|X|=K).
 \label{rs:eq:complement}
\end{equation}
It follows immediately by reversing the exponent columns in the
bialternant, or by complementing columns of a rectangular tableau.
Here $A=K-A=a$ and $\prod\XX_k=t^{k-1}$. This proves reciprocity and the
assertion about the top degree and coefficient.

For full support, regard a column as an increasing $a$-subset of
$\{1,\ldots,k\}$. There is a saturated componentwise chain from the first
$a$ letters to the last $a$ letters, increasing a single letter by one at
each step. Each step changes the column weight by zero or one, so the chain
contains columns of all weights $0,1,\ldots,k-1$. For a desired total
weight between $0$ and $(k-1)n$, use some initial zero-weight columns, at
most one intermediate-weight column from this chain, and some final
maximum-weight columns. The columns are componentwise increasing, hence
form a semistandard tableau. The empty cases are immediate.
\end{proof}

These shape properties are not claimed as new relative to Part~I (Theorem~\ref{thm:main}).
The Schur identity gives a unified interpretation and an alternative proof.
Ordinary unimodality is false: already
\[
 \Ht_4(2;t)=1+2t+5t^2+4t^3+5t^4+2t^5+t^6.
\]
A different, weaker coefficient inequality does follow for odd shifts.

\begin{proposition}[Parity-wise unimodality for odd shifts]
\label{rs:prop:parityunimodal}
If $k=2a+1$ and $\Ht_k(n;t)=\sum_j c_jt^j$, then
$c_j=c_{2an-j}$ and $c_j\le c_{j+2}$ whenever $0\le j\le an-1$.
Thus the coefficients in each fixed parity class are unimodal.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The eigenvalue alphabet $(\rep{t^{-1}}a,1,\rep ta)$ is the character
alphabet of the $\SL_2(\C)$ module $(\C^2)^{\oplus a}\oplus\C$.
The classical Schur-module character theorem \cite{Fulton} identifies
$t^{-an}\Ht_{2a+1}(n;t)$ with the character of the Schur functor of shape
$(n^a)$ on that module. Finite-dimensional $\SL_2$ modules in characteristic
zero are sums of irreducibles, whose characters are
$t^r+t^{r-2}+\cdots+t^{-r}$. Each such string is symmetric and has
nondecreasing coefficients in steps of two toward the center. Their sum has
the same properties. The representation-theoretic facts are imported
classical results; they are not needed elsewhere in this article.
\end{proof}

\section{A confluent spectral theorem for rectangles}
\label{rs:sec:cluster}
The next theorem separates recurrence existence from recurrence minimality.
A determinant expansion easily produces exponential terms, but cancellation
can remove their highest powers of $n$. We identify a unique extremal
confluent minor and evaluate its leading coefficient, which prevents that
cancellation.

\subsection{Statement with an explicit leading coefficient}
Let $\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_\ell$ be distinct nonzero elements of a field of
characteristic zero. Give them positive multiplicities $m_1,\ldots,m_\ell$,
put $K=\sum_i m_i$, choose $1\le A<K$, and put $C=K-A$.
For each allocation
\begin{equation}
 0\le r_i\le m_i,\qquad \sum_i r_i=A,
 \label{rs:eq:allocation}
\end{equation}
define
\begin{equation}
 s_i=m_i-r_i,\qquad
 \lambda_{\boldsymbol r}=\prod_i\xi_i^{r_i},\qquad
 d_{\boldsymbol r}=\sum_i r_i s_i,
 \label{rs:eq:lambdad}
\end{equation}
and use the factorial ratio
\begin{equation}
 B(m,r)=\frac{\prod_{h=0}^{r-1}h!}
              {\prod_{h=m-r}^{m-1}h!}.
 \label{rs:eq:Bfactor}
\end{equation}
An empty product is one, so $B(m,0)=B(m,m)=1$.

\begin{theorem}[Cluster allocation and exact polynomial degree]
\label{rs:thm:cluster}
There are polynomials $P_{\boldsymbol r}(n)$ such that, for every $n\ge0$,
\begin{equation}
 s_{(n^A)}(\rep{\xi_1}{m_1},\ldots,\rep{\xi_\ell}{m_\ell})
 =\sum_{\boldsymbol r}P_{\boldsymbol r}(n)\lambda_{\boldsymbol r}^{\,n}.
 \label{rs:eq:cluster-expansion}
\end{equation}
The canonical polynomials obtained by the confluent Laplace expansion have
exact degree $d_{\boldsymbol r}$. Their leading coefficients are
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\displaystyle
 L_{\boldsymbol r}=
 (-1)^{\sum_{i<j}r_i s_j}
 \frac{\prod_i B(m_i,r_i)\xi_i^{C r_i-r_i s_i}}
 {\prod_{i<j}(\xi_j-\xi_i)^{s_i r_j+r_i s_j}}.}
 \label{rs:eq:LC}
\end{equation}
In particular every $L_{\boldsymbol r}$ is nonzero. If the products
$\lambda_{\boldsymbol r}$ are pairwise distinct, the reduced generating
function denominator is exactly
\begin{equation}
 \prod_{\boldsymbol r}(1-\lambda_{\boldsymbol r}z)^{d_{\boldsymbol r}+1},
 \label{rs:eq:cluster-denominator}
\end{equation}
and the corresponding characteristic polynomial is the minimal recurrence.
\end{theorem}

Distinctness of the nodes is not the same as distinctness of the products.
The first hypothesis suffices for the degree and leading-coefficient
statements for the canonical summands. The second is essential to the
last assertion: summands with a common product must otherwise be combined.

\subsection{The confluent bialternant}
For distinct variables $X=(x_1,\ldots,x_K)$, the rectangular bialternant is
\begin{equation}
 s_{(n^A)}(X)=
 \frac{\det(x_i^{e_j})_{1\le i,j\le K}}
      {\prod_{i<j}(x_j-x_i)},\qquad
 (e_j)=(0,1,\ldots,C-1,n+C,\ldots,n+K-1).
 \label{rs:eq:bialternant}
\end{equation}
To coalesce $m_i$ variables at $\xi_i$, replace their rows by the normalized
derivative rows of orders $0,1,\ldots,m_i-1$. Taylor expansion and multilinearity
show that the within-cluster Vandermonde factor cancels between numerator
and denominator. Thus the numerator matrix has rows $(i,h)$ and entries
\begin{equation}
 \binom{e_j}{h}\xi_i^{e_j-h},\qquad 0\le h<m_i,
 \label{rs:eq:confluent-entries}
\end{equation}
and its denominator is
\begin{equation}
 \Delta_{\boldsymbol m}(\boldsymbol\xi)
   =\prod_{i<j}(\xi_j-\xi_i)^{m_i m_j}.
 \label{rs:eq:confluent-den}
\end{equation}
Rows are ordered by increasing cluster label, then increasing derivative
order. This convention fixes all signs. This limiting identity is a standard
repeated-variable bialternant; \cite{GSM} supplies general treatments.
The Taylor argument just described explains the factorial normalization
used here.

Expand the numerator along its last $A$ columns. Suppose these columns
select $r_i$ rows from cluster $i$, with derivative-order set $R_i$.
The remaining $C$ columns contain no $n$. From every selected cluster row
in the last block one can extract $\xi_i^n$, leaving a polynomial in $n$.
Grouping by $(r_1,\ldots,r_\ell)$ proves the existence of
\eqref{rs:eq:cluster-expansion}. The main issue is its exact degree.

\subsection{Degree loss forced by confluent rows}
Write $D^h f=f^{(h)}/h!$ and $g_j(x)=x^{C+j}$ for $0\le j<A$.
Normalized Leibniz expansion gives
\begin{equation}
 D^h(x^n g_j(x))\big|_{x=\xi_i}
 =\xi_i^n\sum_{p=0}^{h}
       \binom{n}{h-p}\xi_i^{p-h}D^p g_j(\xi_i).
 \label{rs:eq:Leibniz}
\end{equation}
For a fixed cluster, the $r_i$ selected rows are linear combinations of the
rows $(D^p g_j(\xi_i))_j$. In a nonzero determinant contribution, the selected
$p$'s in that cluster must be distinct. Cauchy--Binet makes this statement
literal: the coefficient matrix is block diagonal by cluster, so each
nonzero minor selects exactly $r_i$ derivative rows from cluster $i$.
The sum of their orders is at least $\binom{r_i}{2}$. Hence the degree of
the high-column determinant, after removing $\lambda_{\boldsymbol r}^n$,
is at most
\begin{equation}
 \sum_i\left(\sum_{h\in R_i}h-\binom{r_i}{2}\right).
 \label{rs:eq:degree-bound}
\end{equation}
Among all $r_i$-subsets of $\{0,\ldots,m_i-1\}$, the sum of orders is
uniquely maximized by
\begin{equation}
 R_i=\{s_i,s_i+1,\ldots,m_i-1\}.
 \label{rs:eq:toprows}
\end{equation}
For that set the contribution to \eqref{rs:eq:degree-bound} is $r_i s_i$.
Every other row selection has strictly lower total degree. This proves that
only one Laplace row selection can contribute to $n^{d_{\boldsymbol r}}$.
It remains to show that its coefficient is nonzero and to calculate it.

\subsection{Evaluation of the unique leading term}
For the choice \eqref{rs:eq:toprows}, the lowest possible derivative orders
in \eqref{rs:eq:Leibniz} are $p=0,\ldots,r_i-1$. Since
$\binom n{h-p}$ has leading coefficient $1/(h-p)!$, the local leading
coefficient determinant is
\begin{equation}
 \det\left(\frac{1}{(s_i+h-p)!}\right)_{0\le h,p<r_i}
 =B(m_i,r_i),
 \label{rs:eq:factorial-det}
\end{equation}
where a negative factorial is interpreted as giving entry zero.
To verify this identity, multiply row $h$ by $(s_i+h)!$. The entry in
column $p$ becomes the monic falling-factorial polynomial
$(s_i+h)_{\underline p}$ of degree $p$. Its determinant is the Vandermonde
of $s_i,s_i+1,\ldots,s_i+r_i-1$, namely
$\prod_{h=0}^{r_i-1}h!$. Division by the row factors proves
\eqref{rs:eq:factorial-det}.
The powers of $\xi_i$ in \eqref{rs:eq:Leibniz} supply
$\xi_i^{-r_i s_i}$.

The low-column minor uses derivative orders $0,\ldots,s_i-1$ in each cluster,
so its determinant is
$\prod_{i<j}(\xi_j-\xi_i)^{s_i s_j}$.
The high-column derivative matrix with orders $0,\ldots,r_i-1$ is the
confluent matrix of $g_j(x)=x^C x^j$. Multiplication by $x^C$ induces, within
each cluster, a lower-triangular derivative change with diagonal $\xi_i^C$.
Its determinant is therefore
\[
 \left(\prod_i\xi_i^{C r_i}\right)
 \prod_{i<j}(\xi_j-\xi_i)^{r_i r_j}.
\]
In the full Laplace expansion the selected high rows follow the low rows
within each cluster. Moving all low rows before all high rows crosses
$r_i s_j$ pairs for each $i<j$, so the sign is
$(-1)^{\sum_{i<j}r_i s_j}$. Finally, divide by
\eqref{rs:eq:confluent-den}. The cross exponent becomes
$m_i m_j-s_i s_j-r_i r_j=s_i r_j+r_i s_j$, giving exactly
\eqref{rs:eq:LC}. None of its factors vanishes. This proves the exact degree.

\subsection{Why the recurrence is minimal}
For a nonzero polynomial $P$ of degree $d$ and nonzero $\lambda$,
\[
 (E-\lambda)(P(n)\lambda^n)
 =\lambda^{n+1}(P(n+1)-P(n)).
\]
Thus $(E-\lambda)^{d+1}$ kills this sequence, whereas the $d$th power does
not. If $\mu\ne\lambda$, applying $E-\mu$ preserves its polynomial degree
and multiplies its leading coefficient by $\lambda-\mu$.
These observations prove independence of the spaces
$\{P(n)\lambda^n\}$ for distinct $\lambda$: apply operators killing all
but one of a putative relation.
Equivalently, $\sum_{n\ge0}P(n)(\lambda z)^n$ has a pole of exact order
$d+1$ at $z=\lambda^{-1}$; its top pole coefficient is a nonzero multiple
of $d!\LC(P)$. Distinct poles cannot cancel one another. Applying this to
\eqref{rs:eq:cluster-expansion} proves \eqref{rs:eq:cluster-denominator} and
finishes Theorem~\ref{rs:thm:cluster}.

\section{Exact recurrences for Cigler's family}
\label{rs:sec:recurrence}
\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{rs:thm:recurrence-main}]
For $k\ge2$, specialize Theorem~\ref{rs:thm:cluster} to cluster nodes
$(1,t,t^2)$ and multiplicities $(a,1,b)$, omitting a zero-multiplicity cluster.
The height is $A=a$. An allocation satisfies $r_1+r_2+r_3=a$ and
$r_2\in\{0,1\}$. Its product is $t^q$, where $q=r_2+2r_3$.
For each feasible $q$ the allocation is unique:
\begin{equation}
 r_3=\lfloor q/2\rfloor,\qquad r_2=q-2\lfloor q/2\rfloor,
 \qquad r_1=a-\lceil q/2\rceil.
 \label{rs:eq:qallocation}
\end{equation}
The feasible values are exactly $0\le q\le k-1$. Uniqueness is crucial:
the algebraic relation between the three nodes creates no collision between
different allocations because the middle cluster has multiplicity one.

The exact degree is
\begin{equation}
 d_q=r_1(a-r_1)+r_3(b-r_3)
     =\left\lfloor\frac{q(k-1-q)}2\right\rfloor.
 \label{rs:eq:dq}
\end{equation}
For example, if $q=2h$ the first expression is $h(a+b-2h)$.
If $q=2h+1$, it is $(a-h-1)(h+1)+h(b-h)$; substituting $b=a$ or $a-1$
gives the displayed floor in either parity of $k$.
Thus
\begin{equation}
 \Ht_k(n;t)=\sum_{q=0}^{k-1}P_{k,q}(n;t)t^{qn},\qquad
 \deg_n P_{k,q}=e_{k,q}-1,
 \label{rs:eq:spectralH}
\end{equation}
with nonzero leading coefficients supplied by \eqref{rs:eq:LC}.
Over $\Q(t)$ the products $t^q$ are distinct; Theorem~\ref{rs:thm:cluster}
proves minimality. At a real parameter outside $\{0,1,-1\}$, the nodes
$1,t,t^2$ and the products $t^q$ remain distinct and nonzero, and all the
same leading coefficients remain nonzero. The case $k=1$ is simply the
constant sequence one.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Exact recurrence orders]
\label{rs:cor:orders}
For $a\ge1$,
\begin{align}
 N_{2a}&=2a+\frac{a(a-1)(2a-1)}3,\label{rs:eq:N-even}\\
 N_{2a+1}&=2a+1+\frac{2a(a^2-1)}3.
 \label{rs:eq:N-odd}
\end{align}
Also $N_1=1$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Sum \eqref{rs:eq:exponents}. If $k=2a$, the product $q(2a-1-q)$ is always
even, so there is no rounding. If $k=2a+1$, subtract $1/2$ for each odd
$q$ from $q(2a-q)/2$; there are $a$ such values. The usual sums of
integers and squares give the formulas.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Exact computation without a large Hankel matrix}
For fixed $k$, Jacobi--Trudi computes $\Ht_k(n;t)$ as an $a\times a$
determinant, not an $n\times n$ determinant. Its entries are generated by
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{j\ge0}h_j(\XX_k)w^j
 =\frac1{(1-w)^a(1-tw)(1-t^2w)^b}.
 \label{rs:eq:h-generating}
\end{equation}
For many successive orders, one can instead compute an initial segment and
use the exact order-$N_k$ recurrence. For an isolated very large order,
recurrence exponentiation gives an alternative whose field-operation count
is logarithmic in $n$ for fixed $k$. None of these statements removes output
size or integer bit complexity: an expanded polynomial still has degree
$(k-1)n$, and integer specializations may have linearly growing bit length.
The polynomial $P_{k,q}(n;t)$ can be obtained directly from the finite
confluent Laplace expansion, without guessing it by interpolation.

\section{Generating numerators, negative indices, and a corrected sign}
\label{rs:sec:numerator}
Set
\begin{equation}
 \delta_k=a(k-a)=\lfloor k^2/4\rfloor,\qquad
 C_k=\binom k2,\qquad R_k=N_k-k.
 \label{rs:eq:deltaR}
\end{equation}
Here $\delta_k$ is not a Hankel determinant. It is the degree of the dimension
polynomial that appears at $t=1$.

\subsection{Negative-width continuation}
The exponential-polynomial expression \eqref{rs:eq:spectralH} defines
$\Ht_k(n;t)$ for every integer $n$, over $\Q(t)$. This is an algebraic
continuation in the width index, not a combinatorial interpretation of a
negative rectangle.

\begin{lemma}[A zero gap and negative-index reciprocity]
\label{rs:lem:negative}
For $k\ge2$,
\begin{align}
 \Ht_k(-j;t)&=0&& (1\le j\le k-1),\label{rs:eq:negativezero}\\
 \Ht_k(-k-r;t)&=(-1)^{\delta_k}t^{-C_k-(k-1)r}\Ht_k(r;t)
 &&(r\ge0).
 \label{rs:eq:negativeH}
\end{align}
For $k=1$, the second identity holds and the zero range is empty.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Use the bialternant \eqref{rs:eq:bialternant}, initially for distinct nonzero
variables. Its exponent columns are $0,\ldots,K-A-1$ and
$n+K-A,\ldots,n+K-1$. If $n=-j$ with $1\le j\le K-1$, the two intervals
overlap, so two columns coincide. If $n=-K-r$, the second interval is
$-A-r,\ldots,-r-1$. Move that block before the first block, with sign
$(-1)^{A(K-A)}$, then multiply each variable row by $x_i^{A+r}$.
The resulting bialternant is that of $s_{(r^{K-A})}(X)$. Therefore the
continued value is
\[
 (-1)^{A(K-A)}(\prod X)^{-A-r}s_{(r^{K-A})}(X)
 =(-1)^{A(K-A)}(\prod X)^{-A}s_{(r^A)}(X^{-1}),
\]
where the last step is \eqref{rs:eq:complement}. The identities survive
confluence. On $\XX_k$, one has
$(\prod\XX_k)^a=t^{C_k}$, and \eqref{rs:eq:Hreciprocity} gives the remaining
factor. Since both the bialternant and the spectral expansion agree for all
$n\ge0$, their polynomial-exponential extensions agree identically.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The exact numerator theorem}
\begin{theorem}[Degree and corrected reciprocity]
\label{rs:thm:numerator}
The reduced generating function has the form
\begin{equation}
 \GG_k(z,t)=\frac{\AN_k(z,t)}{\QQ_k(z,t)},\qquad
 \AN_k\in\Z[t,z],\quad \AN_k(0,t)=1.
 \label{rs:eq:Gfraction}
\end{equation}
Its numerator has exact $z$-degree $R_k=N_k-k$, with leading coefficient
\begin{equation}
 [z^{R_k}]\AN_k=(-1)^{N_k+\delta_k+1}t^{(k-1)R_k/2},
 \label{rs:eq:numleading}
\end{equation}
and satisfies
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\displaystyle
 t^{(k-1)R_k/2}z^{R_k}
 \AN_k\!\left(\frac1{t^{k-1}z},t\right)
 =(-1)^{N_k+\delta_k+1}\AN_k(z,t).}
 \label{rs:eq:numreciprocity}
\end{equation}
The sign is $(-1)^{a+1}$ when $k=2a$ and is $+1$ when $k$ is odd.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Every exponential-polynomial sequence $F(n)$ has the following expansion
of its ordinary rational generating function at infinity:
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{n\ge0}F(n)z^n=-\sum_{j\ge1}F(-j)z^{-j}.
 \label{rs:eq:infinity}
\end{equation}
This is a statement about expansions of one rational function at two
places, not an equality between two convergent series on a common annulus.
For $F(n)=\lambda^n$ it is the two expansions of $(1-\lambda z)^{-1}$.
Applying $(z\,d/dz)^h$ proves it for $n^h\lambda^n$, hence in general.

Lemma~\ref{rs:lem:negative} shows that the first nonzero term at infinity is
$-\Ht_k(-k;t)z^{-k}=(-1)^{\delta_k+1}t^{-C_k}z^{-k}$.
The denominator has degree $N_k$, so the numerator has degree $N_k-k$.
Its leading coefficient is obtained by multiplying that term by the leading
coefficient $(-1)^{N_k}t^{\sum_q q e_{k,q}}$ of $\QQ_k$.
Symmetry $e_{k,q}=e_{k,k-1-q}$ gives
$\sum_q q e_{k,q}=(k-1)N_k/2$, proving \eqref{rs:eq:numleading}.
The recurrence makes the numerator a finite convolution of polynomials in
$\Z[t]$, so it lies in $\Z[t,z]$.

Substitute $1/(t^{k-1}z)$ in the expansion at infinity and use
\eqref{rs:eq:negativeH}. Since $k(k-1)=2C_k$, this gives
\begin{equation}
 \GG_k\!\left(\frac1{t^{k-1}z},t\right)
 =(-1)^{\delta_k+1}t^{C_k}z^k\GG_k(z,t).
 \label{rs:eq:Greciprocity}
\end{equation}
On the other hand,
\[
 \QQ_k\!\left(\frac1{t^{k-1}z},t\right)
 =(-1)^{N_k}t^{-(k-1)N_k/2}z^{-N_k}\QQ_k(z,t).
\]
Combining these two formulas proves \eqref{rs:eq:numreciprocity}.
For even $k=2a$, $N_k$ is even and $\delta_k=a^2$.
For odd $k$, $N_k$ is odd and $\delta_k=a(a+1)$ is even.
These parity facts give the stated signs. They also show explicitly that
all displayed half-exponents of $t$ are integers.
\end{proof}

\subsection{What this says about the printed Conjecture 18}
For even shift $k=2a$, Cigler's $d_{2a}(n,t)$ is exactly our
$\Ht_{2a}(n;t)$. The denominator exponent in his equation~(84) is
\[
 aj-\frac{(j-1)(j+2)}2
 =1+\frac{j(2a-1-j)}2=e_{2a,j}.
\]
Thus the present theorem proves that proposed denominator and proves it is
minimal. Its numerator degree is
\[
 R_{2a}=\frac{a(a-1)(2a-1)}3.
\]
However, the positive-sign reciprocity printed in equation~(85) is false
when $a$ is even. The corrected sign is $(-1)^{a+1}$.
This is not a subtle large-index discrepancy: the same source prints
\begin{equation}
 \GG_4(z,t)=\frac{1-t^3z^2}
 {(1-z)(1-tz)^2(1-t^2z)^2(1-t^3z)}.
 \label{rs:eq:G4}
\end{equation}
For its numerator,
$t^3z^2\AN_4(1/(t^3z),t)=t^3z^2-1=-\AN_4(z,t)$.
This directly contradicts the positive sign in the printed equation.
We therefore prove a corrected formulation, not the literal whole of

\subsection{The period-four signed normalization}
The source uses $d_k(n,t)=t^{-\binom n2}D_k(n;t)$. For odd $k$ this is
$\sigma_n\Ht_k(n;t)$, where
\begin{equation}
 \sigma_n=(-1)^{\binom n2}
 =\frac{1-i}{2}i^n+\frac{1+i}{2}(-i)^n.
 \label{rs:eq:sigma}
\end{equation}
The two normalizations must not be interchanged in a generating function.

\begin{theorem}[Exact signed denominator and numerator]
\label{rs:thm:signed}
Let $k=2a+1$ and write
$\widehat\GG_k(z,t)=\sum_{n\ge0}d_k(n,t)z^n$.
Its reduced denominator over $\Q(t)$ is exactly
\begin{equation}
 \widehat\QQ_k(z,t)=\prod_{q=0}^{k-1}(1+t^{2q}z^2)^{e_{k,q}}.
 \label{rs:eq:hatden}
\end{equation}
The numerator $\widehat\AN_k$ lies in $\Z[t,z]$, has constant term one
and exact degree $\widehat R_k=2N_k-k$, and satisfies
\begin{equation}
 t^{(k-1)\widehat R_k/2}z^{\widehat R_k}
 \widehat\AN_k\!\left(\frac1{t^{k-1}z},t\right)
 =(-1)^a\widehat\AN_k(z,t).
 \label{rs:eq:hatreciprocity}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Equation~\eqref{rs:eq:sigma} changes each spectral root $t^q$ into the two
roots $it^q$ and $-it^q$, with nonzero coefficients. They are all distinct
over $\Q(t,i)$, so the minimality argument doubles the recurrence order
and gives \eqref{rs:eq:hatden}. The product has rational coefficients, and
minimality over the extension implies minimality over $\Q(t)$.
For negative indices, $\sigma_{-k-r}=(-1)^{a+1}\sigma_r$.
Since $\delta_k$ is even, the proof of
\eqref{rs:eq:Greciprocity} becomes
\[
 \widehat\GG_k\!\left(\frac1{t^{k-1}z},t\right)
 =(-1)^a t^{C_k}z^k\widehat\GG_k(z,t).
\]
The gap of $k-1$ zero negative indices gives degree $2N_k-k$ as before.
The quadratic denominator transforms without a sign, with factor
$t^{-(k-1)N_k}z^{-2N_k}$. This proves
\eqref{rs:eq:hatreciprocity} and the remaining assertions.
\end{proof}

As a useful normalization check, the first nontrivial odd shift gives
\begin{equation}
 \widehat\GG_3(z,t)=
 \frac{1+(1+t+t^2)z-t(1+t+t^2)z^2-t^3z^3}
 {(1+z^2)(1+t^2z^2)(1+t^4z^2)}.
 \label{rs:eq:Ghat3}
\end{equation}
It has $d_3(1,t)=c_3(t)=1+t+t^2$, as the moment definition requires.
The cubic term and the sign in \eqref{rs:eq:Ghat3} cannot be omitted. This
provides a second direct check on the printed small-shift generating
functions, independent of the general numerator proof.

\section{Sharp convergence and all real specializations}
\label{rs:sec:asymptotic}

\subsection{The leading nonconstant spectral term}
\begin{theorem}[Sharp fixed-parameter convergence equivalent]
\label{rs:thm:asymptotic}
Fix $k\ge2$ and $0<t<1$. Then, as $n\to\infty$,
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\displaystyle
 \frac1{(1-t)^a(1-t^2)^{ab}}-\Ht_k(n;t)
 \sim
 \frac{n^{a-1}t^{n+1}}
 {(a-1)!(1-t)^{b+1}(1-t^2)^{(a-1)b}}.}
 \label{rs:eq:sharp-asymptotic}
\end{equation}
The equivalent is uniform for $t$ in each compact subinterval of $(0,1)$;
more precisely, its relative error is $O(1/n)$ on such a subinterval.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
At $q=0$, the allocation is $(a,0,0)$ and $d_0=0$.
Formula~\eqref{rs:eq:LC} consequently gives the whole constant spectral term,
not merely a leading approximation:
\begin{equation}
 P_{k,0}(n;t)=F_k(t):=\frac1{(1-t)^a(1-t^2)^{ab}}.
 \label{rs:eq:Fexact}
\end{equation}
At $q=1$, the allocation is $(a-1,1,0)$, of degree $a-1$.
Here $B(a,a-1)=1/(a-1)!$. Substitution into \eqref{rs:eq:LC}, followed by
cancellation of the factors $t^2-t=t(t-1)$, yields
\begin{equation}
 [n^{a-1}]P_{k,1}(n;t)
 =-\frac{t}{(a-1)!(1-t)^{b+1}(1-t^2)^{(a-1)b}}.
 \label{rs:eq:L1}
\end{equation}
All remaining terms in \eqref{rs:eq:spectralH} contain $t^{qn}$ with $q\ge2$.
For fixed $k$, their polynomial degrees are bounded and their rational
coefficients are bounded on any compact subinterval of $(0,1)$.
They are exponentially smaller than $n^{a-1}t^n$ there.
The lower coefficients of $P_{k,1}$ give relative error $O(1/n)$;
when $a=1$ there are no such coefficients. Equations
\eqref{rs:eq:Fexact}--\eqref{rs:eq:L1} prove the assertion.
\end{proof}

The repository already identifies $F_k$ as the limiting series and proves
the formal first defect
\begin{equation}
 \Ht_k(n;t)=F_k(t)-\binom{n+a}{a-1}t^{n+1}+O(t^{n+2})
 \quad\hbox{in }\Q[[t]]
 \label{rs:eq:old-defect}
\end{equation}
for fixed $n$ (Part~I, Theorem~\ref{thm:main}). The limit in
\eqref{rs:eq:sharp-asymptotic} is a different statement: $t$ is fixed and
$n$ grows, so all powers beyond $t^{n+1}$ can affect the prefactor.
The rational factor in \eqref{rs:eq:sharp-asymptotic} accounts for those
contributions. In particular, a formal first-defect formula alone would
not justify this asymptotic equivalent. For example,
\[
 F_4(1/2)-\Ht_4(n;1/2)\sim\frac83 n\,2^{-n}.
\]
No uniform assertion near $t=1$ is made; the pole collision there requires
a different scaling analysis.

\subsection{The exceptional real parameters}
\begin{theorem}[Complete real-parameter denominator classification]
\label{rs:thm:real}
For $\sum_{n\ge0}\Ht_k(n;t)z^n$, the reduced denominator at every real
parameter is as follows, always normalized to constant term one:
\begin{equation}
\begin{array}{c|l}
\text{parameter} & \text{reduced denominator}\\ \hline
 t\notin\{0,1,-1\} & \displaystyle\prod_{q=0}^{k-1}(1-t^qz)^{e_{k,q}}\\[5pt]
 t=0 & 1-z\\[2pt]
 t=1 & (1-z)^{\delta_k+1}\\[2pt]
 t=-1,\ k=2a & (1-z^2)^{a(a-1)+1}\\[2pt]
 t=-1,\ k=2a+1 & (1-z)^{a^2+1}(1+z)^{a^2}.
\end{array}
\label{rs:eq:real-table}
\end{equation}
All exponents are exact, not just upper bounds. In particular the last
line includes $k=1$ with $a=0$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The generic real case was proved in Theorem~\ref{rs:thm:recurrence-main}.
At zero, the alphabet contains $a$ ones and zeros. Only the tableau using
all $a$ one-valued letters in each column contributes, so $\Ht_k(n;0)=1$.

At one, every alphabet entry is one. The confluent bialternant reduces to
the elementary dimension product
\begin{equation}
 \Ht_k(n;1)=\prod_{i=1}^{a}\prod_{j=a+1}^{k}\frac{n+j-i}{j-i}.
 \label{rs:eq:dimension}
\end{equation}
One can derive this directly: the determinant of the normalized derivative
matrix at one is the Vandermonde of its exponent columns divided by
$\prod_{h=0}^{k-1}h!$. The within-block factors cancel, leaving precisely
the cross-block ratios in \eqref{rs:eq:dimension}. The product is a nonzero
polynomial in $n$ of exact degree $\delta_k$. Its generating denominator is
therefore $(1-z)^{\delta_k+1}$.

At minus one, the alphabet is $1$ repeated $k-1$ times together with a
single $-1$. For $k\ge2$, apply Theorem~\ref{rs:thm:cluster} with these two
nodes. There are only two allocations: the $-1$ cluster contributes zero
or one selected row. The spectral roots are $1$ and $-1$, and the polynomial
degrees are respectively
\[
 d_+=a(k-1-a)=ab,\qquad d_-=(a-1)(k-a)=(a-1)(b+1).
\]
Their leading coefficients are nonzero by \eqref{rs:eq:LC}. If $k=2a$, both
degrees are $a(a-1)$; if $k=2a+1$, they are $a^2$ and $a^2-1$.
This proves the two last lines. For $k=1$ the sequence is one directly.
\end{proof}

The determinant values at $t=1$ and the Catalan-related simplifications at
$t=-1$ are related to the source's specializations (\cite{Cigler}; Part~I, Section~\ref{sec:examples} and Appendix~\ref{app:one}).
The conclusion here is an exact reduced-recurrence classification at those
specializations, including the proof that the surviving poles really occur.
It would be incorrect to substitute $t=1$ into the generic product and call
the resulting multiplicity $N_k$ minimal: the actual multiplicity is
$\delta_k+1$.

There is a useful independent exact test of \eqref{rs:eq:LC}. For distinct
spectral roots $\lambda_j$ with degrees $d_j$, the leading coefficient of
$P_j$ can be extracted directly from the sequence $F$ by
\begin{equation}
 \LC(P_j)=
 \frac{\left[\displaystyle\prod_{\ell\ne j}(E-\lambda_\ell)^{d_\ell+1}
 (E-\lambda_j)^{d_j}F\right](0)}
 {\lambda_j^{d_j}d_j!\displaystyle\prod_{\ell\ne j}
 (\lambda_j-\lambda_\ell)^{d_\ell+1}}.
 \label{rs:eq:projection-check}
\end{equation}
The verifier constructs the left-side sequence from Jacobi--Trudi, applies
the operator using exact arithmetic, and compares the result with the
factorial product \eqref{rs:eq:LC}. This checks the signs, the powers of the
cluster nodes, and the cross-difference exponents independently of an
implementation of the confluent determinant itself.

Finite checks are not a proof of an infinite family. In particular they
cannot establish the all-index minimality theorem or historical novelty.
Those depend on the mathematical arguments in Sections~\ref{rs:sec:toeplitz}
and~\ref{rs:sec:cluster}. No theorem prover was run for this article.

\subsection{Dependency and trust boundaries}
The main identity depends on the binomial definition, constant terms,
finite-dimensional basis changes, block elimination, and dual Jacobi--Trudi.
The exact recurrence additionally uses the bialternant, its Taylor confluence,
Cauchy--Binet, and finite differences. The numerator theorem uses only those
results and an algebraic expansion at infinity. The exceptional-parameter
and asymptotic theorems depend on the same explicit leading coefficients.
Only Proposition~\ref{rs:prop:parityunimodal} imports Schur-module characters
and complete reducibility for $\SL_2$.

Several points are especially important for an independent audit. The
Toeplitz pairing is bilinear, not a positive Hermitian inner product; no
positivity assumption is used. The odd-order pairing uses the involution
$Jf=zf(z^{-1})$, not the ordinary inversion involution. The normalizing
constants in the factorization are explicitly fixed at $q=1$.
In the confluence calculation, normalized derivatives remove hidden
factorials, and only the highest derivative-order row sets contribute to
the maximal degree. Minimality is asserted only after spectral products
have been checked for distinctness. Finally, generating functions for
$\Ht_k$ and $d_k$ are kept separate throughout.

\subsection{A finite-algebra formalization plan}
A Lean implementation can be divided into three layers. First formalize
symmetric Laurent coefficient sequences and the two Gram matrices, using
finite support; prove the pairing identities and the parameterized
Toeplitz factorization. A formal proof can compute basis-change determinants
directly or prove their independence from the symbol and use the
normalization specialization as above. All exceptional parameters should
be handled by polynomial identity rather than separate division arguments.

The second layer identifies the Toeplitz matrix with the elementary
Jacobi--Trudi matrix. Depending on available symmetric-function support,
one can initially define the rectangular Schur object by this determinant
and separately prove its equality with the complete-homogeneous determinant.
This already yields the Hankel identity without a representation-theoretic
library.

The third layer formalizes normalized derivative matrices, the factorial
Vandermonde determinant \eqref{rs:eq:factorial-det}, and degree bounds for
Laplace selections. The unique-maximizer argument is finite combinatorics;
it is the most important piece to keep inside the trusted proof boundary.
The final recurrence follows from explicit shift-operator lemmas. The
Python code may generate examples and candidate identities but is not a
trusted replacement for any of these steps. No uncompiled Lean skeleton is
included as if it were a completed formalization.

\section{Further research questions and topics}
\label{rs:sec:further}
The following questions are proposed extensions of the results in this
article. Except where the original Cigler conjecture is named explicitly,
they are not claims about the status of a separately published conjecture.

\subsection{Full coefficient structure in Cigler's Conjecture 17}
The spectral polynomials $P_{k,q}(n;t)$ have exact degrees in $n$, but the
present paper does not identify all of their coefficient signs and
factorizations after a common denominator is cleared. The numerator
polynomials in the source's Conjecture~17 organize related information in a
more refined way. A useful next target is to express those polynomials as
flagged tableaux or minors with manifest sign, and to determine exactly
which of the printed degree and reciprocity claims survive normalization.
The confluent Laplace expansion gives a finite formula from which to start;
it is not itself a proof of every Conjecture~17 assertion.

\subsection{Complex roots of unity and spectral cancellation}
At a complex root of unity, the roots $t^q$ can coincide even when
$1,t,t^2$ are distinct. The exact reduced multiplicity is the degree of a
sum of canonical $P_{k,q}$'s in one residue class, plus one, unless that sum
vanishes. Determine a closed formula for these multiplicities as a function
of $k$, the root order, and the residue class. The real points $0,1,-1$ are
settled by Theorem~\ref{rs:thm:real}; simply substituting into the generic
product does not solve the complex problem. Schur root-of-unity identities
and cyclic-sieving methods may give a structural approach, but cancellation
must be checked, not assumed absent.

\subsection{Coefficient shape for even shifts}
Odd shifts have an $\SL_2$ interpretation that proves parity-wise
unimodality. Does the analogous step-two inequality hold for every even
shift and every order? If not, what is the first structural obstruction, and
what weaker inequalities characterize these tableau weights? The ordinary
unimodality assertion is already false, so one should not promote a few
unimodal examples into a general conjecture. A satisfactory result would
explain the difference between the equal outer multiplicities of odd shifts
and the unequal outer multiplicities of even shifts.

\subsection{A two-parameter Hankel deformation}
The Schur alphabet $(\rep1a,u,\rep vb)$ makes sense for independent $u,v$.
The present moment construction lies on $v=u^2$. Find a natural two-parameter
moment family whose normalized shifted Hankel determinants realize the
full alphabet, or prove that a specified class of finite-period Jacobi
recurrences cannot do so. The parameterized Toeplitz factorization offers a
starting point, but the compatibility of all shifts within one moment
sequence is a genuine additional condition.

\subsection{A uniform crossover as the poles merge}
Theorem~\ref{rs:thm:asymptotic} applies for fixed $t<1$, while at $t=1$ the
answer grows like $n^{\delta_k}$. In the scaling $t=e^{-s/n}$, study
\[
 n^{-\delta_k}\Ht_k(n;e^{-s/n})
\]
for fixed $k$ and $s$ in bounded complex sets. Determine the limiting
function, uniform error bounds, and an all-orders expansion. The finite
confluent bialternant should allow a systematic derivation, but the separate
spectral coefficients become singular and their cancellations must be
organized before taking the limit. This is a natural meeting point of the
repository's determinant and asymptotic-analysis programs.

\subsection{Broader noncollision criteria and certified algorithms}
The middle multiplicity one in $(a,1,b)$ makes
$q=r_2+2r_3$ determine the whole allocation. For alphabets with more clusters,
classify linear relations among their exponents that still yield a unique
allocation, or yield a sum whose leading coefficient can be proved nonzero.
Such a criterion would turn Theorem~\ref{rs:thm:cluster} into a reusable exact
minimal-recurrence algorithm for specialized rectangular Schur families.
A formalized implementation should output both the recurrence and a
certificate for every pole multiplicity, rather than an annihilator alone.

\section{Conclusion}
The experimental Schur formula in the inspected ProveIt report admits a
finite algebraic proof. Its decisive step is the equality of the original
Hankel parity factors with the factors of a particular Toeplitz determinant.
The resulting Schur structure supports a sharper conclusion than rationality:
all generic recurrence roots and multiplicities are exact, with explicitly
nonzero confluent leading coefficients. Negative-width continuation then
fixes the numerator degree and reciprocity sign, including a necessary
correction to the original printed conjecture. The same calculation gives
a sharp convergence equivalent and all reduced denominators at real
parameters. The remaining questions concern finer coefficient structure,
complex spectral collisions, and uniform pole-merger asymptotics, rather
than an unproved bridge in the central determinant identity.

\section{Small-shift formulas and independent checks}
\label{rs:app:examples}
The first three sign-adjusted sequences are
\begin{align*}
 \Ht_1(n;t)&=1,\\
 \Ht_2(n;t)&=1+t+\cdots+t^n,\\
 \Ht_3(n;t)&=h_n(1,t,t^2)
 =\frac{(1-t^{n+1})(1-t^{n+2})}{(1-t)(1-t^2)}.
\end{align*}
The displayed quotient is interpreted by its polynomial continuation at
$t=1,-1$. Their ordinary generating functions are respectively
\[
 \frac1{1-z},\qquad
 \frac1{(1-z)(1-tz)},\qquad
 \frac1{(1-z)(1-tz)(1-t^2z)}.
\]
For shift four, \eqref{rs:eq:G4} has numerator degree two, in agreement with
$N_4-4=2$. At $t=-1$ it becomes
\[
 \sum_{n\ge0}\Ht_4(n;-1)z^n=\frac{1+z^2}{(1-z^2)^3},
\]
so $\Ht_4(2m;-1)=(m+1)^2$ and $\Ht_4(2m+1;-1)=0$.
At $t=1$, the dimension product gives
\[
 \Ht_4(n;1)=\frac{(n+1)(n+2)^2(n+3)}{12}.
\]
These two specializations make visible the difference between a generic
recurrence, a pole-merger specialization, and a parity-supported sequence.
