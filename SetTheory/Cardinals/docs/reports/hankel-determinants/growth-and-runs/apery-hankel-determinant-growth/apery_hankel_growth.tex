\begin{abstract}
For the Ap\'ery numbers $A_k=\sum_{j=0}^k\binom{k}{j}^2\binom{k+j}{j}^2$, let
$D_n=\det(A_{i+j})_{0\leq i,j\leq n}$. The OEIS entry A228143 records a conjecture,
added by Vaclav Kotesovec on 13 September 2026, that
$D_n^{1/n^2}\to(1+\sqrt2)^4/4$. Starting from Edgar's known positive moment-density
theorem, we prove this limit and the stronger estimate
$\log D_n=n(n+1)\log((1+\sqrt2)^4/4)+O(n)$.
The proof uses a polynomial lower bound for the density, monic orthogonal
polynomials, and elementary Legendre--Chebyshev estimates. We also obtain an
explicit growth law for $\det(A_{m(i+j)+r_n})$ when $m$ is fixed and $r_n/n$
converges, including nonzero linear shifts. A further consequence is that the
formal ordinary generating series of $D_n$ is not D-finite. The package includes
exact determinants through $n=200$, independent arithmetic checks, and runnable
Python and C++/GMP code. The density representation is credited, not reproved;
the subsequent determinant arguments are given in full.

Part~II, added on 1 October 2026 (batch 73O1), answers the question Part~I
leaves open: the normalized norms $h_n/\Lambda^{2n}=D_n/(D_{n-1}\Lambda^{2n})$
converge, to $K=(\pi C/2)\exp\langle\log\varphi\rangle$, an arcsine average
of Edgar's density ($K\approx0.633427435172$, a numerical evaluation). Hence
$D_n/D_{n-1}\sim K\Lambda^{2n}$ and
$\log D_n=n(n+1)\log\Lambda+(n+1)\log K+o(n)$. The proof lifts the density to
the unit circle and uses a proved Szeg\H{o} minimum principle; it recovers
Part~I's main theorem only as a corollary. Part~II also gives interleaved
monotone Hankel envelopes $E_m\downarrow K$ computable in
$\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$, sharp constants for fixed polynomial modifications and
fixed strides and shifts, a two-term inverse-index law, and a conditional
transfer theorem whose hypothesis is not proved for the Ap\'ery density. The
full multiplicative prefactor of $D_n$ remains open.
\end{abstract}

\section{The selected problem and the stronger result}
The Ap\'ery numbers, OEIS A005259, are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:apery}
 A_k=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}^{\!2}\binom{k+j}{j}^{\!2},
 \qquad A_0=1,\quad A_1=5,\quad A_2=73.
\end{equation}
Define the leading Hankel determinants
\begin{equation}\label{eq:det}
 D_n=\det\bigl(A_{i+j}\bigr)_{0\leq i,j\leq n}.
\end{equation}
Thus the matrix has order $n+1$, not $n$. The first values are
$1,48,161856,39002646528,\ldots$; they agree with OEIS A228143
\cite{oeis-apery,oeis-hankel}.

The target is the asymptotic formula in the \texttt{\%F} field of revision
\texttt{\#40} of A228143, dated 13 September 2026. It is attributed to Vaclav
Kotesovec and asserts
\begin{equation}\label{eq:conjecture}
 \lim_{n\to\infty}D_n^{1/n^2}=\frac{(1+\sqrt2)^4}{4}.
\end{equation}
It is distinct from the divisibility and root-integrality assertions also
recorded in that entry. The source status here refers to the version accessed
on 18 September 2026 \cite{oeis-hankel}.

Use the constants
\begin{equation}\label{eq:constants}
 C=17+12\sqrt2=(1+\sqrt2)^4,\qquad
 c=C^{-1}=17-12\sqrt2,\qquad \Lambda=\frac C4.
\end{equation}
The small positive number $c$ is an interior singular point of the moment
density; it is \emph{not} the left endpoint of its support.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
There exists $\kappa\in(0,1]$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sandwich}
 \kappa^n\Lambda^{n(n+1)}\leq D_n\leq
 4^n\Lambda^{n(n+1)}\qquad(n\geq0).
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\begin{align}
 \log D_n&=n(n+1)\log\Lambda+O(n),\label{eq:logmain}\\
 D_n^{1/n^2}&=\Lambda\exp\bigl(O(1/n)\bigr),\label{eq:rootmain}\\
 \left(\frac{D_n}{D_{n-1}}\right)^{1/(2n)}&\longrightarrow\Lambda
 \qquad(n\to\infty).\label{eq:ratiomain}
\end{align}
In particular, \eqref{eq:conjecture} is true.
\end{theorem}

The proof will bound each monic orthogonal-polynomial norm separately.
This is stronger than trying to estimate one large determinant after replacing
its entries by asymptotic approximations. The latter operation is generally
unsafe; Section~\ref{sec:pitfall} gives an exact counterexample to that method.

\section{The known moment density and a global lower bound}
\label{sec:density}
\sourceinput\quad Edgar's Theorem~1, Proposition~4, Notation~6,
Propositions~25--26, and Corollary~27 \cite{edgar} give an integrable density
$\varphi$ on $[0,C]$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:moments}
 A_k=\int_0^C x^k\varphi(x)\dd x,\qquad \int_0^C\varphi(x)\dd x=1.
\end{equation}
It is positive and real analytic on $(0,c)$ and $(c,C)$, with
\begin{align}
 \varphi(x)&=-\frac6{\pi^2}\log x+o(1) &&(x\downarrow0),\label{eq:left}\\
 \varphi(x)&=\frac{(C-x)^{1/2}}{2^{5/4}C\pi^2}
       +O\bigl((C-x)^{3/2}\bigr) &&(x\uparrow C).\label{eq:right}
\end{align}
At $c$, on each side, it is a nonzero combination of convergent Frobenius
solutions with leading orders $0,\tfrac12,1$. Here our $C,c$ correspond to
Edgar's $c,c_0$.

In particular, for positive distance $t$ from $c$ on either side, the first
nonzero term gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:interior}
 \varphi(c\pm t)=a_\pm t^{\alpha_\pm}(1+o(1)),\qquad
 a_\pm>0,\quad\alpha_\pm\in\{0,\tfrac12,1\}.
\end{equation}
Indeed, the three basis solutions start with distinct powers. A nonzero
solution has a first nonzero coefficient, and positivity fixes the sign of
its leading term. No value of $\varphi(c)$ needs to be assigned, and equality
of its one-sided limits is not required below.

\begin{lemma}[Polynomial minorant]\label{lem:minorant}
Let $Q(x)=(x-c)(x-C)$. There is $\eta>0$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:minorant}
 \varphi(x)\geq\eta Q(x)^2
 \quad\text{for almost every }x\in[0,C].
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Consider $R(x)=\varphi(x)/Q(x)^2$ away from $c,C$.
On each of $(0,c)$ and $(c,C)$ this is continuous and positive.
Since $cC=1$, $Q(0)=1$, and \eqref{eq:left} implies $R(x)\to+\infty$
as $x\downarrow0$. Equation~\eqref{eq:right} gives
$R(x)\asymp(C-x)^{-3/2}\to+\infty$ as $x\uparrow C$.
At $c$, \eqref{eq:interior} gives
$R(c\pm t)\asymp t^{\alpha_\pm-2}\to+\infty$.
Choose small neighborhoods of these three points on which $R$ is bounded
below by a positive constant. On the remaining compact pieces, its positive
continuous minimum is also positive. The least of these bounds is an
admissible $\eta$.
\end{proof}

The interior singularity is the delicate point in this argument. Positivity
alone would not justify a strictly positive uniform lower bound for the
density. Squaring a polynomial that vanishes at $c$ and $C$ avoids that
incorrect inference. The constant $\eta$ is established to exist; this report
does not claim a certified numerical value for it.

\section{Elementary norm estimates and the proof}
\subsection{A moment determinant is a product of minimum norms}
For a nonzero real polynomial $p$, positivity of $\varphi$ on an interval gives
$\int p(x)^2\varphi(x)\dd x>0$. Thus its polynomial inner product is positive
definite. Let $p_k$ be its monic orthogonal polynomial of degree $k$, and put
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hk}
 h_k=\int_0^C p_k(x)^2\varphi(x)\dd x
 =\min_{\substack{p\in\R[x]\text{ monic}\\\deg p=k}}
      \int_0^C p(x)^2\varphi(x)\dd x.
\end{equation}
The minimum follows by writing $p=p_k+q$, $\deg q<k$, and using orthogonality.
The change of basis from $1,x,\ldots,x^n$ to $p_0,\ldots,p_n$ is unitriangular.
Its determinant is one, so taking Gram determinants gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:product}
 D_n=\prod_{k=0}^n h_k,\qquad h_0=1.
\end{equation}
These are the usual monic-norm identities; compare DLMF \S18.2
\cite{dlmf}. The argument above also proves $D_n>0$ for every $n$.

\subsection{The Lebesgue lower comparison}
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:legendre}
For $L>0$ and $k\geq0$, the minimum squared norm of a monic polynomial of
degree $k$ for Lebesgue measure on $[0,L]$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:legendre}
 \ell_k(0;L)=\frac{L^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)\binom{2k}{k}^2}
 \geq L\left(\frac L4\right)^{2k}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
On $[0,1]$, the polynomial
\[
 R_k(t)=\frac1{k!}\frac{\mathrm d^k}{\mathrm dt^k}
       \bigl[t^k(t-1)^k\bigr]
\]
is orthogonal to all lower-degree polynomials by $k$ integrations by parts.
Its leading coefficient is $\binom{2k}{k}$. Another integration by parts gives
\[
 \int_0^1 R_k(t)t^k\dd t
 =\int_0^1 t^k(1-t)^k\dd t
 =\frac{(k!)^2}{(2k+1)!}.
\]
Orthogonality therefore implies $\int_0^1R_k(t)^2\dd t=1/(2k+1)$.
Dividing by its leading coefficient and scaling $x=Lt$ proves the equality
in \eqref{eq:legendre} and the minimizing property.

For the inequality, set $u_k=(2k+1)\binom{2k}{k}^2/16^k$. Direct cancellation
gives $u_0=1$ and
\[
 \frac{u_{k+1}}{u_k}=1-\frac1{4(k+1)^2}<1.
\]
Thus $(2k+1)\binom{2k}{k}^2\leq16^k$, as needed.
\end{proof}

If $p$ is monic of degree $k$, then $pQ$ is monic of degree $k+2$.
Lemmas~\ref{lem:minorant} and \ref{lem:legendre} give
\[
 \int_0^C p(x)^2\varphi(x)\dd x
 \geq\eta\int_0^C(p(x)Q(x))^2\dd x
 \geq\eta\ell_{k+2}(0;C)
 \geq\eta C\Lambda^{2k+4}.
\]
Consequently, with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kappa}
 \kappa=\eta C\Lambda^4>0,
 \qquad h_k\geq\kappa\Lambda^{2k}\quad(k\geq0).
\end{equation}
The case $k=0$, together with $h_0=1$, shows $\kappa\leq1$.

\subsection{The Chebyshev upper comparison}
The Chebyshev polynomial $T_k$ satisfies $T_k(\cos\theta)=\cos(k\theta)$.
The recurrence $T_{k+1}(t)=2tT_k(t)-T_{k-1}(t)$ shows that its leading
coefficient is $2^{k-1}$ for $k\geq1$. Hence
\[
 q_k(x)=2\Lambda^k T_k\left(\frac{2x}{C}-1\right)
\]
is monic of degree $k$ and has absolute value at most $2\Lambda^k$ on $[0,C]$.
Since the measure has total mass one, the minimum in \eqref{eq:hk} satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uppernorm}
 h_k\leq\int_0^Cq_k(x)^2\varphi(x)\dd x\leq4\Lambda^{2k}
 \qquad(k\geq1).
\end{equation}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}]
Multiply \eqref{eq:kappa} and \eqref{eq:uppernorm} over $k=1,\ldots,n$ and
use \eqref{eq:product} with $h_0=1$. Since $2\sum_{k=1}^n k=n(n+1)$, this
is \eqref{eq:sandwich}. Taking logarithms gives \eqref{eq:logmain}; dividing
by $n^2$ gives \eqref{eq:rootmain}. Finally $D_n/D_{n-1}=h_n$, and the
individual bounds imply
\[
 \Lambda\kappa^{1/(2n)}\leq h_n^{1/(2n)}\leq\Lambda4^{1/(2n)},
\]
which proves \eqref{eq:ratiomain}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The proof does not assert that $h_n/\Lambda^{2n}$ converges. It proves this
sequence is bounded above and bounded away from zero. Nor does
\eqref{eq:logmain} give a full multiplicative asymptotic equivalent for $D_n$.
[Added 1 October 2026, batch~73O1: Part~II proves that $h_n/\Lambda^{2n}$
converges, to the constant $K\approx0.6334$ of \eqref{szego:eq:arcsine}
(Theorem~\ref{szego:thm:main}), so $D_n/D_{n-1}\sim K\Lambda^{2n}$ and
$\log D_n=n(n+1)\log\Lambda+(n+1)\log K+o(n)$. A full multiplicative
equivalent for $D_n$ is still not established: Part~II determines the
prefactor only conditionally (Theorem~\ref{szego:thm:transfer}).]
\end{remark}

\section{An explicit law for linearly shifted determinants}
\label{sec:shifts}
The same comparison idea gives more than fixed shifts. For integers $m\geq1$
and $r\geq0$, define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:shiftdef}
 H_n^{(m,r)}=\det\bigl(A_{m(i+j)+r}\bigr)_{0\leq i,j\leq n}.
\end{equation}
Here $m$ is the stride in the moment indices. The shift $r$ is held constant
throughout each matrix, but may vary from one matrix size to another.

Define, for $\sigma\geq0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Psi}
 \Psi(\sigma)=\frac12\left[
 (\sigma+2)^2\log(\sigma+2)-2(\sigma+1)^2\log(\sigma+1)
 +\sigma^2\log\sigma\right],
\end{equation}
with $0^2\log0:=0$.

\begin{theorem}[Linear-shift growth law]\label{thm:shift}
Fix a positive integer $m$. If $r_n$ are nonnegative integers and
$r_n/n\to\rho\in[0,\infty)$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:shiftlaw}
 \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log H_n^{(m,r_n)}}{n^2}
 =(m+\rho)\log C-\Psi(\rho/m).
\end{equation}
Equivalently,
\[
 \lim_{n\to\infty}\bigl(H_n^{(m,r_n)}\bigr)^{1/n^2}
 =C^{m+\rho}\exp\bigl(-\Psi(\rho/m)\bigr).
\]
In particular, every sublinear shift $r_n=o(n)$ has limit $C^m/4$.
\end{theorem}

For example,
\begin{align}
 \lim_{n\to\infty}\det(A_{2i+2j})_{0\leq i,j\leq n}^{1/n^2}
   &=\frac{C^2}{4},\label{eq:stride2}\\
 \lim_{n\to\infty}\det(A_{i+j+n})_{0\leq i,j\leq n}^{1/n^2}
   &=\frac{16C^2}{81\sqrt3}.\label{eq:linear1}
\end{align}
The constant $C^m/4$ for a stride $m$ is not $(C/4)^m$. The proof below also
provides a finite-size comparison with a completely explicit determinant.

\subsection{The power-weight model}
For $s>-1$ and $L>0$, put
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Jdef}
 \J_n(s;L)=\det\left(\frac{L^{i+j+s+1}}{i+j+s+1}\right)_{0\leq i,j\leq n}.
\end{equation}
This is the moment determinant of $y^s\dd y$ on $[0,L]$.
Its monic minimum norms are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:jacobinorm}
 \ell_k(s;L)=\frac{L^{2k+s+1}}
 {(2k+s+1)\binom{2k+s}{k}^2},\qquad
 \binom{2k+s}{k}=\prod_{j=1}^k\frac{k+s+j}{j}.
\end{equation}
To verify \eqref{eq:jacobinorm}, replace the Rodrigues polynomial in
Lemma~\ref{lem:legendre} by
\[
 R_{k,s}(t)=\frac{t^{-s}}{k!}\frac{\mathrm d^k}{\mathrm dt^k}
        \bigl[t^{k+s}(t-1)^k\bigr].
\]
It is a polynomial of degree $k$, with leading coefficient
$\binom{2k+s}{k}$. Integration by parts proves orthogonality for weight $t^s$.
The boundary terms vanish because $s>-1$. Its pairing with $t^k$ is the beta
integral $\int_0^1t^{k+s}(1-t)^k\dd t$; dividing by the leading coefficient
and scaling the interval gives \eqref{eq:jacobinorm}.

We will use both its norm product and its Cauchy determinant form:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Jexact}
 \J_n(s;L)=\prod_{k=0}^n\ell_k(s;L)
 =L^{(n+1)(n+s+1)}
   \frac{\prod_{j=0}^n(j!)^2}
        {\prod_{i=0}^n\prod_{j=0}^n(s+1+i+j)}.
\end{equation}
For completeness, the second equality follows by factoring powers of $L$
and applying the Cauchy identity
\[
 \det\left(\frac1{x_i+y_j}\right)
 =\frac{\prod_{i<j}(x_j-x_i)(y_j-y_i)}{\prod_{i,j}(x_i+y_j)}
\]
with $x_i=i$, $y_j=s+1+j$. One elementary proof of that identity clears the
common denominator: the resulting polynomial is alternating in the $x_i$
and in the $y_j$, so is divisible by both Vandermonde products. Total degrees
agree. The remaining constant is one, by induction on the residue at
$x_n=-y_n$, starting with the $1\times1$ case.

\subsection{Uniform comparison with the power-weight model}
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:comparison}
For fixed $m\geq1$ and $T<\infty$, uniformly for integers $0\leq r\leq Tn$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:modelcomparison}
 \log H_n^{(m,r)}=\log\J_n(r/m;C^m)+O_{m,T}(n)
 \qquad(n\geq1).
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Set $L=C^m$, $a=c^m$, $s=r/m$, and change variables $y=x^m$ in
\eqref{eq:moments}. The moments in \eqref{eq:shiftdef} are those of
$y^s w_m(y)\dd y$, where
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pushforward}
 w_m(y)=\frac1m y^{1/m-1}\varphi(y^{1/m}),\qquad0<y<L.
\end{equation}
The map is smooth with nonzero derivative near $a$ and $L$. Thus the
one-sided finite orders at $a$ and the square-root order at $L$ are unchanged.
At zero,
\[
 w_m(y)\sim\frac6{m^2\pi^2}y^{1/m-1}\log(1/y)\longrightarrow+\infty.
\]
The argument of Lemma~\ref{lem:minorant} therefore supplies $\eta_m>0$ with
$w_m(y)\geq\eta_m[(y-a)(y-L)]^2$ almost everywhere.

Set $\beta=-1+1/(2m)\in(-1,0)$. Near zero,
$w_m(y)/y^\beta\to0$, and this ratio is bounded on the remaining intervals,
including their one-sided limits at $a$ and $L$. Hence a constant $K_m>0$
also exists such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:twoenvelopes}
 \eta_m[(y-a)(y-L)]^2\leq w_m(y)\leq K_m y^\beta.
\end{equation}
Neither constant depends on $r$ or $n$.

Let $h_k(s)$ denote the monic minimum norm for $y^s w_m(y)\dd y$.
Multiplication by the monic quadratic on the left of
\eqref{eq:twoenvelopes}, and a minimizing trial polynomial for the upper
power weight, give
\[
 \eta_m\ell_{k+2}(s;L)\leq h_k(s)\leq K_m\ell_k(s+\beta;L).
\]
The parameter $s+\beta$ is greater than $-1$. Multiplying over $k=0,\ldots,n$
yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:modelbounds}
 \eta_m^{n+1}\J_n(s;L)
 \frac{\ell_{n+1}(s;L)\ell_{n+2}(s;L)}{\ell_0(s;L)\ell_1(s;L)}
 \leq H_n^{(m,r)}\leq K_m^{n+1}\J_n(s+\beta;L).
\end{equation}

We check that both corrections to $\log\J_n(s;L)$ are $O(n)$.
Write $b=-\beta\in(0,1)$. Formula~\eqref{eq:Jexact} gives
\begin{align*}
 \log\frac{\J_n(s+\beta;L)}{\J_n(s;L)}
 &=(n+1)\beta\log L
  -\sum_{i,j=0}^n\log\left(1-\frac b{s+1+i+j}\right).
\end{align*}
For $t\geq1$,
$0\leq-\log(1-b/t)\leq b/((1-b)t)$. Counting pairs with fixed $i+j$ gives
\[
 \sum_{i,j=0}^n\frac1{s+1+i+j}
 \leq\sum_{i,j=0}^n\frac1{1+i+j}\leq2n+1.
\]
Thus the logarithmic upper correction is $O_m(n)$, uniformly in $s\geq0$.

For the lower correction, $s\leq(T/m)n$. At $k=0,1,n+1,n+2$, formula
\eqref{eq:jacobinorm} shows $\abs{\log\ell_k(s;L)}=O_{m,T}(n)$.
For $k=0,1$ this is immediate. For the other two indices use
\[
 1\leq\binom{2k+s}{k}
 \leq\frac{(2k+s)^k}{k!}
 \leq\left(\frac{e(2k+s)}k\right)^k,
\]
whose logarithm is $O_{m,T}(n)$ since $k\geq n+1$ and $s=O(n)$.
Here $k!\geq(k/e)^k$ follows by integrating $\log x$ on $[1,k]$.
The remaining factors in \eqref{eq:jacobinorm} have logarithms $O(n)$ as well.
Applying these estimates to \eqref{eq:modelbounds} proves
\eqref{eq:modelcomparison}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Evaluating the limiting constant}
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:modelasym}
If $s_n\geq0$ and $s_n/n\to\sigma\geq0$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:modelasym}
 \frac1{n^2}\log\J_n(s_n;L)
 \longrightarrow(1+\sigma)\log L-\Psi(\sigma).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Taking logarithms in \eqref{eq:Jexact}, the factorial part satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:factorialasym}
 2\sum_{j=0}^n\log(j!)=n^2\log n-\frac32n^2+O(n\log(n+1)).
\end{equation}
For example, interchange the sums to obtain
$2\sum_{k=1}^n(n+1-k)\log k$ and compare the sums of $\log k$ and $k\log k$
with their integrals.
For the denominator, Riemann sums give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:denomasym}
 \frac1{n^2}\sum_{i,j=0}^n\log(s_n+1+i+j)-\log n
 \longrightarrow\int_0^1\!\int_0^1\log(\sigma+x+y)\dd x\dd y.
\end{equation}
When $\sigma>0$ the integrand is continuous. When $\sigma=0$, split off
$i+j\leq\delta n$. The normalized absolute contribution there is bounded by
$O(\delta^2(1+\abs{\log\delta}))+o(1)$: count the $i+j=k$ pairs and use
$\log(n/(k+1))$ as an upper bound for the absolute logarithm for large $n$.
Outside this corner, convergence is uniform. Letting $\delta\downarrow0$
justifies \eqref{eq:denomasym} also at $\sigma=0$.

Two integrations, with $t^2\log t$ assigned its continuous value at zero,
yield
\[
 \int_0^1\!\int_0^1\log(\sigma+x+y)\dd x\dd y
 =\Psi(\sigma)-\frac32.
\]
The two $\tfrac32$ terms cancel between this expression and
\eqref{eq:factorialasym}. The power of $L$ in \eqref{eq:Jexact} contributes
$(1+\sigma)\log L$, proving the claim.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:shift}]
The convergence $r_n/n\to\rho$ implies $r_n\leq Tn$ eventually for some
finite $T$. Apply Proposition~\ref{prop:comparison}, then
Lemma~\ref{lem:modelasym} with $s_n=r_n/m$, $L=C^m$, and
$\sigma=\rho/m$. The error $O(n)$ disappears after division by $n^2$.
Finally $\Psi(0)=\log4$.
\end{proof}

For fixed $m,r$, \eqref{eq:modelcomparison} and the norm formula also give
$\log H_n^{(m,r)}=n(n+1)\log(C^m/4)+O_{m,r}(n)$.
To see this without a further asymptotic theorem, changing the power parameter
from $0$ to a fixed $s=r/m$ changes $\log\J_n$ by $O_s(n)$, using
$\log(1+s/t)\leq s/t$ and the same reciprocal sum estimate as above.
Lemma~\ref{lem:legendre} and the Chebyshev upper comparison for Lebesgue
measure then bound $\log\J_n(0;L)-n(n+1)\log(L/4)$ by $O_L(n)$.
The stride $m$ is fixed throughout these statements; no uniform assertion for
$m\to\infty$ is made.

\section{A generating-function consequence}
\label{sec:dfinite}
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:nonrecursive}
The sequence $(D_n)$ is not P-recursive over $\mathbb C$. Its formal ordinary
generating series $F(z)=\sum_{n\geq0}D_nz^n$ is not D-finite.
Moreover, for every fixed real $s$, the series
$\sum_{n\geq0}D_n z^n/(n!)^s$ has radius of convergence zero.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Suppose that, for all sufficiently large integers $n$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:prec}
 \sum_{j=0}^q P_j(n)D_{n+j}=0,
 \qquad P_j\in\mathbb C[n],\quad P_q\not\equiv0.
\end{equation}
The case $q=0$ contradicts $D_n>0$. For $q\geq1$, the leading coefficient
polynomial has no zeros at sufficiently large integers. Ratios of the other
coefficient polynomials to it have at most polynomial growth. Therefore
some $K>0$, integer $d\geq0$, and $N$ satisfy
\[
 \abs{D_{n+q}}\leq K n^d\max_{0\leq j<q}\abs{D_{n+j}}\qquad(n\geq N).
\]
Taking running maxima and increasing $K$ if necessary gives
$\abs{D_n}\leq K_0K_1^n(n!)^d$. Thus
$\log D_n=O(n\log n)$, contradicting \eqref{eq:logmain}, since $\Lambda>1$.
This proves the first assertion.

A polynomial-coefficient linear differential equation for $F$ would, by
coefficient comparison, give a nontrivial polynomial-coefficient recurrence
for its coefficients for all sufficiently large indices. Hence $F$ cannot
be D-finite. This argument is about formal power series and does not assume
that $F$ converges.

Finally,
\[
 \frac1n\log\frac{D_n}{(n!)^s}
 =n\log\Lambda+O(\log(n+1))\longrightarrow+\infty.
\]
The root test gives radius zero for every fixed $s$.
\end{proof}

Divergence alone would \emph{not} have proved non-D-finiteness: a formal
D-finite series can have radius zero. The polynomial-recurrence growth bound
is the essential extra argument.

\section{Why entrywise asymptotics do not determine this limit}
\label{sec:pitfall}
Consider, for $0\leq a<C$, the elementary moments
\[
 m_k^{(a)}=\int_a^Cx^k\dd x=\frac{C^{k+1}-a^{k+1}}{k+1}.
\]
For each fixed $0<a<C$,
\[
 \frac{m_k^{(a)}}{m_k^{(0)}}=1-(a/C)^{k+1}.
\]
Thus these two moment sequences agree to every algebraic relative asymptotic
order in $1/k$, with an exponentially small relative difference.
Nevertheless their determinant limits differ.
Translation by $a$ is a unitriangular change of the monomial basis, so it does
not change a Gram determinant. Applying Lemma~\ref{lem:legendre} on the
translated interval of length $C-a$ gives the exact formula
\begin{equation}\label{eq:counterexample}
 \det(m_{i+j}^{(a)})_{0\leq i,j\leq n}
 =\frac{(C-a)^{(n+1)^2}}
 {\prod_{k=0}^n(2k+1)\binom{2k}{k}^2}.
\end{equation}
Its $n^2$-root limit is $(C-a)/4$, rather than $C/4$.
Indeed, the same lower and upper monic-norm comparisons used above prove
this limit directly.

This is a counterexample to a tempting inference about Hankel determinants,
not to Kotesovec's conjecture. It explains why a single-entry asymptotic
formula cannot safely be substituted into a growing determinant. In the
Ap\'ery case, incorrectly discarding the short interval $(0,c)$ would likewise
suggest the wrong constant
\[
 \frac{C-c}{4}=6\sqrt2=8.485281374\ldots,
 \qquad\text{instead of}\qquad
 \frac C4=8.492640687\ldots.
\]
The full positive support and the global comparison in
Lemma~\ref{lem:minorant} retain this small but consequential interval.

\section{The sharp asymptotic theorem}
Set
\begin{equation}\label{szego:eq:constants}
 C=17+12\sqrt2,\qquad c=C^{-1},\qquad
 \Lam=C/4,\qquad L=\log\Lam.
\end{equation}
Let $\varphi$ be the positive moment density in Edgar's theorem, so
$A_k=\int_0^C x^k\varphi(x)\dd x$.
Write
\begin{equation}\label{szego:eq:arcsine}
 \mean{f}_C=\frac1\pi\int_0^C\frac{f(x)}{\sqrt{x(C-x)}}\dd x,
 \qquad K=\frac{\pi C}{2}\exp\mean{\log\varphi}_C.
\end{equation}
The bracket is an average with respect to a probability measure, not an
average with respect to $\varphi(x)\dd x$.

\begin{theorem}[Sharp Ap\'ery ratio and logarithmic growth]\label{szego:thm:main}
The integral in \eqref{szego:eq:arcsine} is absolutely convergent, and $0<K\leq2$.
As $n\to\infty$,
\begin{align}
 \frac{D_n}{D_{n-1}}&=K\Lam^{2n}(1+o(1)),\label{szego:eq:sharpnorm}\\
 \log D_n&=n(n+1)L+(n+1)\log K+o(n),\label{szego:eq:loggrowth}\\
 \frac{D_{n+1}}{(1+1/\sqrt2)^{8n+4}D_n}&\longrightarrow\Lam K,\label{szego:eq:oeisratio}\\
 \frac{D_{n+1}D_{n-1}}{D_n^2}&\longrightarrow\Lam^2.\label{szego:eq:curvature}
\end{align}
\end{theorem}
The proof is completed in Sections~\ref{szego:sec:density}--\ref{szego:sec:envelopes}.
No conjecture about the shape or monotonicity of the plotted ratios is used.

\begin{remark}[A crucial distinction]
Equation~\eqref{szego:eq:loggrowth} says
\[
 D_n=\Lam^{n(n+1)}K^{n+1}\exp(o(n)).
\]
It does \emph{not} say $D_n\sim\Lam^{n(n+1)}K^{n+1}$.
Powers of $n$, powers of $\log n$, and a further multiplicative constant
are all compatible with the theorem. A full all-orders expansion for
$D_n$ is not asserted here.
\end{remark}

\begin{corollary}[Fixed windows and quadratic normalization]\label{szego:cor:windows}
For each fixed integer $k\geq0$,
\begin{equation}\label{szego:eq:window}
 \frac{D_{n+k}}{D_n}\sim K^k\Lam^{2kn+k(k+1)}.
\end{equation}
For fixed nonnegative integers $r,s$,
\begin{equation}\label{szego:eq:cross}
 \frac{D_{n+r+s}D_n}{D_{n+r}D_{n+s}}\longrightarrow\Lam^{2rs}.
\end{equation}
The series
\begin{equation}\label{szego:eq:qnormal}
 \mathcal B(z)=\sum_{n\geq0}\frac{D_n}{\Lam^{n(n+1)}}z^n
\end{equation}
has radius of convergence $1/K$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Multiply the $k$ consecutive instances of \eqref{szego:eq:sharpnorm} to obtain
\eqref{szego:eq:window}; the sum of their exponents is
$2\sum_{j=1}^k(n+j)=2kn+k(k+1)$.
Equation~\eqref{szego:eq:cross} follows by cancellation.
For $b_n=D_n/\Lam^{n(n+1)}$, \eqref{szego:eq:sharpnorm} gives
$b_n/b_{n-1}\to K$, hence $b_n^{1/n}\to K$ and the root test proves
\eqref{szego:eq:qnormal}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The Szeg\H{o} route recovers Theorem~\ref{thm:main} of Part~I]\label{szego:rem:partI}
Theorem~\ref{szego:thm:main} implies Part~I's Theorem~\ref{thm:main} except
for its explicit sandwich: \eqref{szego:eq:loggrowth} gives
\eqref{eq:logmain} and hence \eqref{eq:rootmain} and the A228143 limit
\eqref{eq:conjecture}, and \eqref{szego:eq:sharpnorm} gives
\eqref{eq:ratiomain}. This is a second route to a result that Part~I proved
first, and it is printed as a corollary, not as a new result. The two routes
differ. Part~I compares the density with the polynomial minorant of
Lemma~\ref{lem:minorant} and with Legendre and Chebyshev norms, which yields
the two-sided estimate \eqref{eq:sandwich} for every $n$, with an
existential $\kappa$; Part~II passes to the circle and uses the Szeg\H{o}
minimum principle (Theorem~\ref{szego:thm:minimum}), which yields the
constant $K$ but no lower bound at finite $n$, and upper bounds only
through the envelopes of Theorem~\ref{szego:thm:envelope}. The two are
consistent: \eqref{eq:kappa} gives $h_n/\Lambda^{2n}\geq\kappa$ for every $n$,
so every admissible $\kappa$ of Part~I satisfies $\kappa\leq K\approx0.6334$,
and \eqref{eq:uppernorm} is compatible with $K\leq2$.
\end{remark}

\section{The moment density satisfies the Szeg\H{o} condition}
\label{szego:sec:density}
\textbf{Known analytic input.}
The input is the one stated in Part~I's Section~\ref{sec:density}, from
\cite[Theorem 1, Proposition 4, Propositions 25--26, Corollary 27]{edgar}:
$\varphi$ is integrable and positive on $(0,c)\cup(c,C)$, represents the
moments $A_k$ by \eqref{eq:moments}, has the endpoint laws \eqref{eq:left} at
$0$ and \eqref{eq:right} at $C$, and at the interior singularity $c$ its
one-sided local expansions are nonzero combinations of convergent Frobenius
solutions beginning with powers $0,1/2,1$. (The manuscript restated these
displays; they are not repeated here.)
These facts are used as input; their Heun-function positivity proof is not
repeated.

\begin{lemma}\label{szego:lem:szegocond}
One has
\begin{equation}\label{szego:eq:szegocond}
 \int_0^C\frac{\abs{\log\varphi(x)}}{\sqrt{x(C-x)}}\dd x<\infty.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Near zero, \eqref{eq:left} gives
$\log\varphi(x)=\log\log(1/x)+O(1)$. This is integrable against $x^{-1/2}\dd x$,
as is seen with $x=t^2$.
Near $C$, \eqref{eq:right} gives
$\log\varphi(x)=\tfrac12\log(C-x)+O(1)$, which is integrable against
$(C-x)^{-1/2}\dd x$.

On either side of $c$, select the first nonzero coefficient among the three
Frobenius branches. Their leading powers are distinct, so cancellation
cannot remove that first power. Positivity gives
$\varphi(c\pm t)=a_\pm t^{\beta_\pm}(1+o(1))$ for some $a_\pm>0$ and
$\beta_\pm\in\{0,1/2,1\}$ (this is \eqref{eq:interior} of Part~I, where the
exponents are called $\alpha_\pm$). Thus the absolute logarithm is bounded by
a constant times $1+\abs{\log t}$ there.
Away from these three points the density is positive and analytic, so its
logarithm is bounded on compact subintervals. Combining the bounds proves
\eqref{szego:eq:szegocond}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Hankel determinants as products of minimum norms}
For a nonzero nonnegative polynomial $P$ on $[0,C]$, let
\begin{align}
 D_n[P]&=\det\!\left(\int_0^C x^{i+j}P(x)\varphi(x)\dd x\right)_{i,j=0}^n,
 &D_{-1}[P]&=1,\label{szego:eq:weightedD}\\
 h_n[P]&=\min_{\substack{p\in\R[x]\text{ monic}\\\deg p=n}}
            \int_0^C p(x)^2P(x)\varphi(x)\dd x.\label{szego:eq:minnorm}
\end{align}
Write $h_n=h_n[1]$. Positivity on an interval makes the polynomial inner
product positive definite. Its monic orthogonal polynomial $p_n[P]$ realizes
the minimum: every other monic polynomial is $p_n[P]+q$, with $\deg q<n$,
and orthogonality separates the squared norms.
A unitriangular change of basis in the Gram determinant therefore gives
\begin{equation}\label{szego:eq:gramproduct}
 D_n[P]=\prod_{j=0}^n h_j[P],\qquad
 h_n[P]=\frac{D_n[P]}{D_{n-1}[P]}.
\end{equation}
In particular, every determinant used below is positive.
For $P=1$, $h_n[1]$ is Part~I's $h_n$ of \eqref{eq:hk} and
\eqref{szego:eq:gramproduct} is Part~I's \eqref{eq:product}; the weighted
version is the same argument.

\section{A proof of the circle minimum principle}
\label{szego:sec:circle}
The method is classical Szeg\H{o} theory; see \cite{simon} for its broader
setting. The precise minimum principle needed here has a short proof.
It is included to make the normalization and hypotheses explicit.

Let $W>0$ almost everywhere on the unit circle, with
$\int W(\theta)\dd\theta/(2\pi)<\infty$ and
$\int\abs{\log W(\theta)}\dd\theta<\infty$. Put
\begin{equation}\label{szego:eq:prediction}
 e_m=\min_{q(0)=1,\,\deg q\leq m}
       \int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\abs{q(e^{i\theta})}^2W(\theta)\frac{\dd\theta}{2\pi},
 \qquad G=\exp\!\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\log W(\theta)\frac{\dd\theta}{2\pi}.
\end{equation}
Reversing coefficients shows that $e_m$ is also the minimum squared norm
of a monic polynomial of degree $m$.

\begin{theorem}[Szeg\H{o} minimum principle]\label{szego:thm:minimum}
The numbers $e_m$ are nonincreasing and converge to $G>0$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The admissible spaces in \eqref{szego:eq:prediction} increase with $m$, proving
monotonicity. For a polynomial $q$ with $q(0)=1$, Jensen's formula gives
$\int\log\abs{q(e^{i\theta})}\dd\theta/(2\pi)\geq0$.
The arithmetic--geometric mean inequality applied to $|q|^2W$ then gives
$\norm{q}_W^2\geq G$. Hence $e_m\geq G$.

For the converse, fix $\varepsilon>0$ and let $W_\varepsilon=W+\varepsilon$,
$G_\varepsilon=\exp\int\log W_\varepsilon\dd\theta/(2\pi)$.
Define the zero-free analytic function in the unit disk
\[
 D_\varepsilon(z)=\exp\!\left\{\frac1{4\pi}
 \int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{e^{i\theta}+z}{e^{i\theta}-z}
                      \log W_\varepsilon(\theta)\dd\theta\right\}.
\]
The real part of the kernel is the Poisson kernel. Consequently
$D_\varepsilon(0)=\sqrt{G_\varepsilon}$,
$|D_\varepsilon(e^{i\theta})|^2=W_\varepsilon(\theta)$ almost everywhere,
and $|D_\varepsilon(z)|\geq\sqrt\varepsilon$.
The standard radial boundary assertion here follows from the Poisson
boundary theorem and the Hardy-space outer-function construction.
Equivalently, it can be obtained by first truncating $\log W_\varepsilon$
and passing to its Poisson integrals.

Thus
$f(z)=D_\varepsilon(0)/D_\varepsilon(z)$ is bounded analytic, $f(0)=1$,
and its boundary values satisfy
\[
 |f(e^{i\theta})|^2=G_\varepsilon/W_\varepsilon(\theta).
\]
For fixed $r<1$, the Taylor polynomials of $f(rz)$ converge uniformly on
$|z|\leq1$ and have constant term one. They are admissible competitors.
As $r\uparrow1$, boundedness of $f$ and integrability of $W$ permit dominated
convergence, giving
\[
 \inf_m e_m\leq\int|f|^2W\frac{\dd\theta}{2\pi}
 =G_\varepsilon\int\frac W{W+\varepsilon}\frac{\dd\theta}{2\pi}
 \leq G_\varepsilon.
\]
Finally $\log(W+\varepsilon)\to\log W$ in integral as $\varepsilon\downarrow0$.
For $0<\varepsilon\leq1$, the nonnegative difference is dominated by
$\log(W+1)-\log W$, an integrable function. Hence
$G_\varepsilon\to G$ and the upper bound follows.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Reflection coefficients and an exact product}
Let $\Phi_m(z)$ be the monic orthogonal polynomial for $W$ and write
$\Phi_m^*(z)=z^m\overline{\Phi_m(1/\bar z)}$.
Assume from now on that $W$ is even, so all coefficients of $\Phi_m$ are real.
Define $\alpha_m=-\Phi_{m+1}(0)$.

\begin{lemma}\label{szego:lem:recurrence}
For $m\geq0$,
\begin{equation}\label{szego:eq:opuc}
 \Phi_{m+1}=z\Phi_m-\alpha_m\Phi_m^*,\qquad
 e_{m+1}=(1-\alpha_m^2)e_m,\qquad |\alpha_m|<1.
\end{equation}
If $\int W\dd\theta/(2\pi)=1$, then
\begin{equation}\label{szego:eq:product}
 e_m=\prod_{j=0}^{m-1}(1-\alpha_j^2),\quad
 G=\prod_{j=0}^{\infty}(1-\alpha_j^2),\quad
 \sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\alpha_j^2<\infty.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Both $\Phi_{m+1}$ and $z\Phi_m$ are orthogonal to
$z,z^2,\ldots,z^m$. Their difference has degree at most $m$ and therefore
is a scalar multiple of $\Phi_m^*$, the one-dimensional orthogonal
complement of those $m$ monomials in that space.
The constant term gives the scalar $-\alpha_m$.
The identity $z\Phi_m=\Phi_{m+1}+\alpha_m\Phi_m^*$ is an orthogonal sum;
its norms give $e_m=e_{m+1}+\alpha_m^2e_m$.
All norms are positive, proving $|\alpha_m|<1$.
Iterate and use Theorem~\ref{szego:thm:minimum}.
Since $-\log(1-t)\geq t$ for $0\leq t<1$, positivity of the limiting
product implies the final assertion.
\end{proof}

\section{Exact circle-to-Hankel identities}
\label{szego:sec:identities}
Lift the measure $\varphi(x)\dd x$ to the circle by
\begin{equation}\label{szego:eq:lift}
 x=\frac C2(1+\cos\theta)=\Lam(z+2+z^{-1}),\quad z=e^{i\theta},\qquad
 W(\theta)=\frac{\pi C}{2}\varphi(x)|\sin\theta|.
\end{equation}
This is an even probability density relative to $\dd\theta/(2\pi)$.
Indeed, its pushforward under $\theta\mapsto x$ is $\varphi(x)\dd x$.
Lemma~\ref{szego:lem:szegocond}, together with the integrability of $\log|\sin\theta|$,
verifies the hypotheses of Theorem~\ref{szego:thm:minimum}.
Since the mean of $\log|\sin\theta|$ is $-\log2$,
\begin{equation}\label{szego:eq:K2G}
 G=\frac{\pi C}{4}\exp\mean{\log\varphi}_C=K/2.
\end{equation}
Jensen's inequality and $\int W=1$ also give $G\leq1$.

\begin{lemma}[Reciprocal and anti-reciprocal minimum norms]\label{szego:lem:reciprocal}
For $m\geq1$, put $a_0=\Phi_m(0)=-\alpha_{m-1}$. The monic polynomials
\[
 S_m=\frac{\Phi_m+\Phi_m^*}{1+a_0},\qquad
 T_m=\frac{\Phi_m-\Phi_m^*}{1-a_0}
\]
minimize the norm among real monic reciprocal and anti-reciprocal
polynomials of degree $m$, respectively. Their squared norms are
\begin{equation}\label{szego:eq:reciprocalnorms}
 \norm{S_m}^2=\frac{2e_m}{1-\alpha_{m-1}},\qquad
 \norm{T_m}^2=\frac{2e_m}{1+\alpha_{m-1}}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The denominators are nonzero by Lemma~\ref{szego:lem:recurrence}.
A variation within either monic constrained space has zero leading and
constant coefficients and is therefore a polynomial of degree at most $m-1$.
It is orthogonal to $\Phi_m$. Reciprocity or anti-reciprocity implies it
is also orthogonal to $\Phi_m^*$: on the circle, use
$\Phi_m^*=z^m\overline{\Phi_m}$ and $R^*=\pm R$.
Thus $S_m$ and $T_m$ are orthogonal to their respective variation spaces
and are the unique minimizers.

Moreover, $\norm{\Phi_m^*}^2=e_m$ and
$\langle\Phi_m,\Phi_m^*\rangle=a_0e_m$, since the leading coefficient of
$\Phi_m^*$ is $a_0$. Expanding the squared norms of the sum and difference
proves \eqref{szego:eq:reciprocalnorms}.
\end{proof}

Define, with the weighted norms from \eqref{szego:eq:minnorm},
\begin{align}
 U_n&=\frac{h_n[1]}{\Lam^{2n}},
 &V_n&=\frac{h_{n-1}[x(C-x)]}{\Lam^{2n}} &&(n\geq1),\label{szego:eq:UV}\\
 X_n&=\frac{h_n[x]}{\Lam^{2n+1}},
 &Y_n&=\frac{h_n[C-x]}{\Lam^{2n+1}} &&(n\geq0).\label{szego:eq:XY}
\end{align}

\begin{proposition}[Four exact norm identities]\label{szego:prop:four}
One has
\begin{align}
 U_n&=\frac{2e_{2n}}{1-\alpha_{2n-1}},
 &V_n&=\frac{2e_{2n}}{1+\alpha_{2n-1}},\label{szego:eq:evennorms}\\
 X_n&=\frac{2e_{2n+1}}{1-\alpha_{2n}},
 &Y_n&=\frac{2e_{2n+1}}{1+\alpha_{2n}}.\label{szego:eq:oddnorms}
\end{align}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Every monic reciprocal polynomial of degree $2n$ has the form
$z^np(x)/\Lam^n$, where $p$ is monic of degree $n$.
Every monic anti-reciprocal polynomial of degree $2n$ has the form
$z^{n-1}(z^2-1)q(x)/\Lam^{n-1}$, with $q$ monic of degree $n-1$.
These statements follow by expressing symmetric Laurent polynomials as
polynomials in $z+z^{-1}$.
Their minimum norms are $U_n$ and $V_n$, because
\[
 |z-z^{-1}|^2=4\sin^2\theta=\frac{x(C-x)}{\Lam^2}.
\]
For degree $2n+1$, the corresponding forms are
$z^n(z+1)p(x)/\Lam^n$ and $z^n(z-1)p(x)/\Lam^n$.
Use
$|z+1|^2=x/\Lam$ and $|z-1|^2=(C-x)/\Lam$ to obtain $X_n,Y_n$.
Now apply Lemma~\ref{szego:lem:reciprocal}.
\end{proof}

\section{Monotone envelopes and the proof of the main theorem}
\label{szego:sec:envelopes}
\begin{theorem}[Interleaved Hankel upper envelopes]\label{szego:thm:envelope}
Set $E_0=2$ and
\begin{equation}\label{szego:eq:envelopes}
 E_{2n}=\frac{2U_nV_n}{U_n+V_n}\quad(n\geq1),\qquad
 E_{2n+1}=\frac{2X_nY_n}{X_n+Y_n}\quad(n\geq0).
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}\label{szego:eq:envelopemain}
 E_m=2e_m,\qquad E_0\geq E_1\geq E_2\geq\cdots\downarrow K.
\end{equation}
In particular, every $E_m$ is a finite expression in moments that is an upper
bound for the integral constant $K$.
The reflection coefficients themselves are given exactly by
\begin{equation}\label{szego:eq:alphahankel}
 \alpha_{2n-1}=\frac{U_n-V_n}{U_n+V_n},\qquad
 \alpha_{2n}=\frac{X_n-Y_n}{X_n+Y_n}.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Substitute \eqref{szego:eq:evennorms} and \eqref{szego:eq:oddnorms} into the harmonic
means in \eqref{szego:eq:envelopes}. The result is $2e_m$.
Theorem~\ref{szego:thm:minimum} and \eqref{szego:eq:K2G} give the limit and monotonicity.
Subtracting and adding the paired identities gives \eqref{szego:eq:alphahankel}.
\end{proof}

All entries involved are explicit linear combinations of Ap\'ery numbers:
for example, the $x(C-x)$-modified moment of degree $k$ is
$CA_{k+1}-A_{k+2}$. Thus the theorem uses only the field $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$
for its finite expressions. In direct determinant notation its even part is
\begin{equation}\label{szego:eq:directenvelope}
 E_{2n}=\frac{2D_n[1]D_{n-1}[x(C-x)]}
 {\Lam^{2n}\bigl(D_n[1]D_{n-2}[x(C-x)]+D_{n-1}[1]D_{n-1}[x(C-x)]\bigr)}.
\end{equation}
Our convention $D_{-1}[P]=1$ includes $n=1$.
The first simple certificate is
\begin{equation}\label{szego:eq:E1}
 K\leq E_1=2\left(1-\left(\frac{10}{C}-1\right)^2\right)
 =81120\sqrt2-114720.
\end{equation}
The radical expression is positive; subtracting its rounded decimal terms
in low precision is not a reliable way to evaluate it.

\begin{corollary}[Exact product and separation of fluctuations]\label{szego:cor:separation}
The constant and normalized norms satisfy
\begin{align}
 K&=2\prod_{j=0}^{\infty}(1-\alpha_j^2),\label{szego:eq:Kproduct}\\
 \log\frac{E_m}{K}&=\sum_{j=m}^{\infty}-\log(1-\alpha_j^2),\label{szego:eq:tail}\\
 \frac{U_n}{K}&=\frac1{1-\alpha_{2n-1}}
  \exp\!\left\{\sum_{j=2n}^{\infty}-\log(1-\alpha_j^2)\right\}.\label{szego:eq:exacttransfer}
\end{align}
Furthermore,
\begin{equation}\label{szego:eq:squares}
 \sum_{n\geq1}\left(\frac{U_n}{E_{2n}}-1\right)^2<\infty.
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The first two identities follow from \eqref{szego:eq:product} and $K=2G$.
The third is \eqref{szego:eq:evennorms}. Since $\alpha_j\to0$,
$U_n/E_{2n}-1=\alpha_{2n-1}/(1-\alpha_{2n-1})$ has a square-summable tail;
finitely many initial terms cause no problem.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{szego:thm:main}]
Lemma~\ref{szego:lem:szegocond} proves the integral assertion.
By \eqref{szego:eq:evennorms}, $e_{2n}\to K/2$ and $\alpha_{2n-1}\to0$, so
$U_n\to K$. Equations~\eqref{szego:eq:UV} and \eqref{szego:eq:gramproduct} yield
\eqref{szego:eq:sharpnorm}.
Summing
$\log h_j=2jL+\log K+o(1)$ gives \eqref{szego:eq:loggrowth}; the sum of a sequence
converging to zero is $o(n)$.
The identity $(1+1/\sqrt2)^4=\Lam$ proves \eqref{szego:eq:oeisratio}, with the
extra factor $\Lam$ caused by the indexing of the plotted ratio.
Finally $D_{n+1}D_{n-1}/D_n^2=h_{n+1}/h_n\to\Lam^2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[What an upper envelope does not give]
Monotonicity supplies an exact upper bound for $K$, but not a certified
error estimate from one finite moment table. Equation~\eqref{szego:eq:tail}
identifies the missing tail. The numerical values of the envelopes below
are evaluated in high precision, not by directed interval rounding.
\end{remark}

\section{All fixed nonnegative polynomial modifications}
\label{szego:sec:polynomials}
Let $P(x)=p_d\prod_{j=1}^d(x-\zeta_j)$ be a nonzero real polynomial that is
nonnegative on $[0,C]$. Its zeros on this interval are permitted.
Set
\begin{equation}\label{szego:eq:GP}
 \mathcal G_C(P)=\exp\mean{\log P}_C.
\end{equation}
Zeros are understood in the integrable logarithmic sense.

\begin{theorem}[Polynomial perturbation constants]\label{szego:thm:polynomial}
For each fixed such $P$,
\begin{align}
 h_n[P]&\sim K\mathcal G_C(P)\Lam^{2n},\label{szego:eq:polyh}\\
 \log D_n[P]&=n(n+1)L+(n+1)\log\bigl(K\mathcal G_C(P)\bigr)+o(n),\label{szego:eq:polyD}\\
 \frac{h_n[P]}{h_n[1]}&\longrightarrow\mathcal G_C(P),\qquad
 \left(\frac{D_n[P]}{D_n[1]}\right)^{1/(n+1)}
 \longrightarrow\mathcal G_C(P).\label{szego:eq:polyratio}
\end{align}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The density $P\varphi$ is integrable and positive almost everywhere.
The logarithm of each polynomial factor has an integrable singularity
against the arcsine measure, including at the endpoints. Consequently it
satisfies \eqref{szego:eq:szegocond} with $\log(P\varphi)=\log P+\log\varphi$.
Repeat the circle minimum argument. A probability normalization is
unnecessary for the norm limit: it only changed $e_0$ in the product
formula. The new constant is exactly $K\mathcal G_C(P)$.
The product identity \eqref{szego:eq:gramproduct} and cancellation prove the rest.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The constant is explicit from the roots}
Define the exterior conformal parameter
\begin{equation}\label{szego:eq:conformal}
 \Psi_C(z)=\frac{z-C/2+\sqrt{z(z-C)}}{2\Lam},\qquad z\notin[0,C],
\end{equation}
where the square root is chosen to be $z-C/2+O(1/z)$ at infinity.
Then $|\Psi_C(z)|>1$. On the interval, use either boundary value, of modulus
one, when only its modulus occurs.

\begin{proposition}[Root formula]\label{szego:prop:rootformula}
For the polynomial above,
\begin{equation}\label{szego:eq:rootformula}
 \mathcal G_C(P)=|p_d|\Lam^d\prod_{j=1}^d|\Psi_C(\zeta_j)|.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $w=\Psi_C(\zeta)$, so $\zeta=\Lam(w+2+w^{-1})$ and $|w|\geq1$.
For $|z|=1$,
\[
 \Lam(z+2+z^{-1})-\zeta=\Lam z^{-1}(z-w)(z-w^{-1}).
\]
The circle average of $\log|z-a|$ is $\log\max(1,|a|)$ by Jensen's formula
(or its elementary factorization proof). Hence
$\mean{\log|x-\zeta|}_C=\log\Lam+\log|w|$.
This remains true for $|w|=1$, since the boundary logarithmic singularities
are integrable. Add over the roots and the leading coefficient.
\end{proof}

A particularly useful consequence is the following location-independence:
if all roots of $P$ lie in $[0,C]$, then
\begin{equation}\label{szego:eq:invisible}
 \mathcal G_C(P)=|p_d|\Lam^d.
\end{equation}
Thus a monic square $P(x)=\prod_{j=1}^r(x-t_j)^2$, with fixed
$t_j\in[0,C]$, changes the exact linear term in $\log D_n$ by
$2r(n+1)\log\Lam$, independently of the root locations.
This does not say that the lower-order terms are independent of those locations.

For fixed integers $r,s\geq0$,
\begin{equation}\label{szego:eq:endpointP}
 P(x)=x^r(C-x)^s\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 \mathcal G_C(P)=\Lam^{r+s}.
\end{equation}
For an exterior real point $t>C$,
$P(x)=t-x$ gives $\mathcal G_C(P)=\Lam\Psi_C(t)$.
These are finite algebraic expressions when the roots are algebraic.
All assertions are for a fixed polynomial. No uniformity for growing degree
or moving roots is implied.

\section{Fixed strides and shifts}
\label{szego:sec:strides}
For fixed integers $m\geq1$ and $r\geq0$, consider
\begin{equation}\label{szego:eq:strideD}
 H_n^{(m,r)}=\det(A_{m(i+j)+r})_{0\leq i,j\leq n}.
\end{equation}
This is Part~I's \eqref{eq:shiftdef} with a shift that does not depend on
$n$. Part~I already controls these determinants at the leading scale
(Theorem~\ref{thm:shift}, and for fixed $r$ the remark after its proof:
$\log H_n^{(m,r)}=n(n+1)\log(C^m/4)+O_{m,r}(n)$). Their norm constants can
also be identified.
Put $B_m=C^m$, $\Lam_m=B_m/4$, and, with $w_m$ the pushed-forward density
of Part~I's \eqref{eq:pushforward},
\begin{equation}\label{szego:eq:pushdensity}
 w_m(y)=\frac1m y^{1/m-1}\varphi(y^{1/m}),\qquad
 \mathcal K_m=\frac{\pi B_m}{2}\exp\mean{\log w_m}_{B_m}.
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Sharp fixed-stride constants]\label{szego:thm:strides}
For every fixed $m,r$ as above,
\begin{align}
 \frac{H_n^{(m,r)}}{H_{n-1}^{(m,r)}}
 &\sim \mathcal K_m\Lam_m^{r/m}\Lam_m^{2n},\label{szego:eq:strideh}\\
 \log H_n^{(m,r)}
 &=n(n+1)\log\Lam_m
 +(n+1)\left(\log \mathcal K_m+\frac r m\log\Lam_m\right)+o(n).
 \label{szego:eq:stridelog}
\end{align}
In particular, dividing the consecutive norm ratio for shift $r$ by the
one for shift zero has limit $\Lam_m^{r/m}$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The substitution $y=x^m$ gives
\[
 A_{mk+r}=\int_0^{B_m}y^k y^{r/m}w_m(y)\dd y.
\]
Near zero,
$\log w_m(y)=(1/m-1)\log y+\log\log(1/y)+O(1)$.
At $B_m$ it has the same square-root endpoint order as before, and at
$c^m$ the same finite-order Frobenius behavior after an analytic change
of coordinate. The proof of Lemma~\ref{szego:lem:szegocond} therefore applies.
The further factor $y^{r/m}$ preserves logarithmic integrability.
The circle argument yields a norm constant multiplied by
$\exp((r/m)\mean{\log y}_{B_m})=\Lam_m^{r/m}$.
Summing logarithms proves \eqref{szego:eq:stridelog}.
\end{proof}

This theorem deliberately keeps $m$ and $r$ fixed. It does not replace
Part~I's separate varying-shift Theorem~\ref{thm:shift} when $r$ is
proportional to $n$. An exact linear correction in that varying regime
remains a further problem.

\begin{remark}[Relation to Part~I]\label{szego:rem:strides}
The quadratic term $n(n+1)\log\Lambda_m=n(n+1)\log(C^m/4)$ in
\eqref{szego:eq:stridelog} is Part~I's Theorem~\ref{thm:shift} at $\rho=0$
(limit $C^m/4$, which is not $(C/4)^m$), in the sharper form of the remark
after its proof. What Theorem~\ref{szego:thm:strides} adds is the linear
coefficient $\log\mathcal K_m+(r/m)\log\Lambda_m$. For $m=1$, $r=0$ it is
Theorem~\ref{szego:thm:main}: $w_1=\varphi$ and $\mathcal K_1=K$.
\end{remark}

\section{Inverse growth and the integer-index issue}
\label{szego:sec:inverse}
Set
\begin{equation}\label{szego:eq:B}
 B=L+\log K.
\end{equation}
For sufficiently large $n$, $D_{n+1}>D_n$ by \eqref{szego:eq:sharpnorm}.
Let $F(x)$ be the piecewise-linear interpolation of $\log D_n$ on that
increasing tail, and let $X(y)$ be its inverse.

\begin{theorem}[Two-term inverse-index law]\label{szego:thm:inverse}
As $y\to\infty$,
\begin{equation}\label{szego:eq:inverse}
 X(y)=\sqrt{\frac yL}-\frac{L+\log K}{2L}+o(1).
\end{equation}
For a large threshold $T$, the first index on the increasing tail with
$D_n\geq T$ is exactly $\lceil X(\log T)\rceil$.
Equivalently, it has the form
\begin{equation}\label{szego:eq:integerinverse}
 \left\lceil\sqrt{\frac{\log T}{L}}-\frac{L+\log K}{2L}
              +\varepsilon(T)\right\rceil,
 \qquad \varepsilon(T)\to0.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Equation~\eqref{szego:eq:loggrowth} gives
$\log D_n=Ln^2+Bn+o(n)$.
Linear interpolation changes $Lx^2$ by at most $L/4$, and interpolating
an $o(n)$ remainder still gives $o(x)$. Thus
$F(x)=Lx^2+Bx+o(x)$.
First $X(y)\sim\sqrt{y/L}$. Completing the square gives
\[
 y=L\left(X(y)+\frac B{2L}\right)^2+o(X(y)),
\]
which implies \eqref{szego:eq:inverse} after division by a quantity of order
$\sqrt y$. The ceiling statement follows directly from monotonicity and
the definition of the interpolation.
\end{proof}

Numerically,
\[
 L\approx2.139199986958,\qquad B\approx1.682590155276,
 \qquad -\frac B{2L}\approx-0.393275562251.
\]
Thus the constant missing from the $O(n)$ estimate \eqref{eq:logmain} of Part~I
produces an explicit constant shift in the inverse.
One must not remove the ceiling in \eqref{szego:eq:integerinverse} and claim an
$o(1)$ approximation to an integer-valued staircase. Nor may one drop the
small term inside the ceiling near an integer without a separate error bound.

\section{A conditional transfer theorem for finer transseries}
\label{szego:sec:transfer}
The exact formula \eqref{szego:eq:exacttransfer} separates the remaining analytic
problem from subsequent asymptotic algebra. Once the reflection coefficients
are understood, the determinant expansion follows by tail summation.
The next theorem is an unconditional implication, but its hypothesis is
\emph{not proved here for the Ap\'ery density}.

\begin{theorem}[Two slow scales of reflection coefficients]\label{szego:thm:transfer}
For a lifted probability density as above, suppose that some real
$a,b,d,e$ satisfy, as $j\to\infty$,
\begin{equation}\label{szego:eq:alphaansatz}
 \alpha_j=\frac{a+b(-1)^j}{j}
       +\frac{d+e(-1)^j}{j\log j}
       +O\!\left(\frac1{j\log^2 j}\right).
\end{equation}
Define
\begin{equation}\label{szego:eq:chi_eta}
 \chi=\frac{a^2+b^2+a-b}{2},\qquad
 \xi=ad+be+\frac{d-e}{2}.
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}\label{szego:eq:Uexpansion}
 \log\frac{U_n}{K}
 =\frac\chi n+\frac\xi{n\log n}
  +O\!\left(\frac1{n\log^2n}\right),
\end{equation}
and there exists a finite constant $\mathcal A>0$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{szego:eq:Dconditional}
 D_n=\mathcal A\,K^{n+1}\Lam^{n(n+1)}n^\chi(\log n)^\xi
       \left(1+O\!\left(\frac1{\log n}\right)\right).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Square \eqref{szego:eq:alphaansatz}. The nonalternating terms are
\[
 \frac{a^2+b^2}{j^2}+\frac{2(ad+be)}{j^2\log j}
      +O\!\left(\frac1{j^2\log^2j}\right).
\]
The remaining displayed terms alternate and have tail $O(m^{-2})$ from
$j=m$ onward, by summation by parts. The elementary integral estimates are
\begin{align*}
 \sum_{j=m}^\infty j^{-2}&=m^{-1}+O(m^{-2}),\\
 \sum_{j=m}^\infty\frac1{j^2\log j}
 &=\frac1{m\log m}+O\!\left(\frac1{m\log^2m}\right).
\end{align*}
Also $-\log(1-\alpha_j^2)=\alpha_j^2+O(j^{-4})$.
Therefore
\[
 \sum_{j=m}^\infty-\log(1-\alpha_j^2)
 =\frac{a^2+b^2}{m}+\frac{2(ad+be)}{m\log m}
  +O\!\left(\frac1{m\log^2m}\right).
\]
In \eqref{szego:eq:exacttransfer}, set $m=2n$ and add
$-\log(1-\alpha_{2n-1})$.
Replacing $2n-1$ by $2n$ costs $O(n^{-2})$ in the principal terms, and
replacing $\log(2n)$ by $\log n$ costs $O(1/(n\log^2n))$.
The resulting coefficients are exactly \eqref{szego:eq:chi_eta}.

Now sum \eqref{szego:eq:Uexpansion}. The differences between the partial sums
of $1/n$ and $\log n$, and of $1/(n\log n)$ and $\log\log n$, converge to
constants. The remainder series converges absolutely, with tail
$O(1/\log n)$. Exponentiation and \eqref{szego:eq:gramproduct} prove
\eqref{szego:eq:Dconditional}; finite initial terms are absorbed into $\mathcal A$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{A next inverse term, including the lattice correction}
There is a subtle but useful distinction between inverting a smooth
asymptotic model and inverting the polygonal interpolation of a sequence.
Let $\tau(u)=\{u\}(1-\{u\})$, a continuous, Lipschitz, period-one function.

\begin{proposition}[Conditional inverse with lattice sector]\label{szego:prop:lattice}
Suppose
\begin{equation}\label{szego:eq:loginversehyp}
 \log D_n=Ln^2+Bn+\chi\log n+\xi\log\log n+C_0+o(1),
 \qquad L>0.
\end{equation}
With the same polygonal inverse $X$ as before, put $s=\sqrt{y/L}$.
Then
\begin{equation}\label{szego:eq:latticeinverse}
 \begin{split}
 X(y)={}&s-\frac B{2L}\\
 &+\frac{B^2/(4L)-\chi\log s-\xi\log\log s-C_0
           -L\tau(s-B/(2L))}{2Ls}
 +o(s^{-1}).
 \end{split}
\end{equation}
For the inverse of the smooth expression in \eqref{szego:eq:loginversehyp},
the same formula holds with the $\tau$ term omitted.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For $x=n+t$, $0\leq t\leq1$, linear interpolation of $n^2$ is exactly
$x^2+t(1-t)$. Interpolation of the logarithmic terms differs from their
smooth values by $o(1)$. Thus
\[
 F(x)=Lx^2+Bx+\chi\log x+\xi\log\log x+C_0+L\tau(x)+o(1).
\]
Substitute $x=s-B/(2L)+v/s$.
The quadratic and linear terms become
$y-B^2/(4L)+2Lv+o(1)$ when $v=O(\log s)$.
All logarithms may be evaluated at $s$ to this accuracy.
Since $\tau$ is Lipschitz and periodic, it may be evaluated at
$s-B/(2L)$, even across integer boundaries, with error $o(1)$.
Equating the residual to zero gives the displayed value of $v$.
A residual $o(1)$ in $F$ changes its inverse by $o(s^{-1})$, since the
local slopes are $2Ls+O(1)$.
\end{proof}

Theorem~\ref{szego:thm:transfer} supplies \eqref{szego:eq:loginversehyp} with
$B=L+\log K$ and $C_0=\log\mathcal A+\log K$, whenever its
reflection-coefficient hypothesis is verified.
This isolates a reusable asymptotic-inversion step and prevents a
spurious constant term caused by ignoring interpolation.

\section{Further research questions and a concrete route forward}
\label{szego:sec:questions}
The proved statements identify a precise next analytic task rather than
assuming a generic inverse-power expansion. They re-scope the continuation
proposed at the end of Part~I (Section~8): the limit of $h_n/\Lambda^{2n}$ and
the linear term of $\log D_n$ are now determined
(Theorem~\ref{szego:thm:main}); the prefactor, the oscillation and the
varying-shift linear term are not.

\subsection{Quantitative reflection-coefficient asymptotics}
Determine the first terms of $\alpha_j$ for the lifted Ap\'ery density.
Its two endpoint behaviors and its interior singularity must all be retained.
Can one prove an expansion of the form \eqref{szego:eq:alphaansatz}, with explicit
$a,b,d,e$ and a uniform remainder? This would immediately unlock
Theorem~\ref{szego:thm:transfer}, including a polynomial/logarithmic prefactor for
$D_n$ and the next inverse terms.
A sharper remainder would propagate through the exact tail formula
\eqref{szego:eq:exacttransfer} to higher orders.

\subsection{The oscillatory sector and its frequency}
Let
\[
 \theta_*=\arccos(2c/C-1)=\pi-2\arcsin(1/C).
\]
The interior singularity maps to circle points $e^{\pm i\theta_*}$.
Determine whether the leading oscillatory correction is governed by
$e^{\pm2in\theta_*}$, and calculate its amplitude, phase, and decay exponent.
The corresponding candidate period in $n$ is
$\pi/(2\arcsin(1/C))\approx53.3531$.
The geometry suggests this frequency, but this report does \emph{not}
prove that it is the leading oscillatory term.
The visible oscillations make this a more informative target than an
unqualified fit to powers of $1/n$.

\subsection{A full Hankel prefactor and logarithmic endpoint effects}
Does there exist $\mathcal A>0$ and a rigorously determined exponent $\chi$
with
$D_n\sim\mathcal A K^{n+1}\Lam^{n(n+1)}n^\chi$,
or is an additional power of $\log n$ required?
The logarithmic singularity of $\varphi$ at zero means that one must verify,
not merely assume, the hypotheses of a smooth-background
Fisher--Hartwig theorem. The general determinant asymptotics in
\cite{dik} provide a relevant comparison framework, but are not invoked
here to claim an unproved prefactor.

\subsection{Certified constants and effective upper-envelope tails}
Construct rigorous interval enclosures for $K$ by combining density
quadrature with explicit endpoint and interior remainder bounds.
Alternatively, obtain an effective bound on
$\sum_{j\geq m}-\log(1-\alpha_j^2)$.
The exact finite envelopes already supply algebraic upper bounds; a controlled
tail would turn them into two-sided numerical certificates.
Closed-form simplifications of $K$ in terms of special values are also worth
investigating, but no such simplification is asserted.

\subsection{Growing shifts and moving polynomial zeros}
For $r_n/n\to\rho>0$, Part~I (Theorem~\ref{thm:shift}) determines the leading quadratic
term for $H_n^{(m,r_n)}$. Identify the next linear term as a function of
$\rho$, including regimes where a moving equilibrium-support endpoint
approaches the density's interior singularity.
Separately, extend Theorem~\ref{szego:thm:polynomial} uniformly to moving roots
or growing polynomial degree. The fixed-polynomial result alone does not
justify those limits.

\subsection{Formalization and reuse for other moment sequences}
The exact determinant/Gram identities and the reciprocal-polynomial
reductions form a finite-algebraic formalization target. The next layer is
the circle minimum principle and the transfer of the Szeg\H{o} condition.
Once these are formalized, the Ap\'ery application has a clean dependency
on an independently verified moment-density theorem.
For other OEIS moment sequences, the same pipeline needs three inputs:
a precise positive density, its interval support, and logarithmic
integrability against the arcsine measure. The envelope construction then
uses moments alone.

\section{Reproducing the density evaluation}
\label{szego:app:density}
This appendix specifies the numerical continuation sheet; none of the
main proofs depends on numerical continuation.
Let $F(z)={}_2F_1(1/3,2/3;1;z)$ and
\[
 q(x)=x^3+30x^2-24x+1,\qquad d(x)=x^2-34x+1.
\]
For $0<x<c$, put
\[
 \lambda_\pm(x)=\frac{q(x)\pm(x^2-7x+1)\sqrt{d(x)}}{2(x+1)^3}.
\]
Edgar's hypergeometric representation gives
\begin{equation}\label{szego:eq:numericdensityleft}
 \varphi(x)=\frac{4\sqrt3}{\pi(x+1)}F(\lambda_-(x))F(\lambda_+(x)).
\end{equation}
For $c<x<C$, set $s=1/x$, $d_*=i\sqrt{-d(s)}$, and
\[
 M(s)=\frac{3-3s-d_*}{2(s+1)^2},\qquad
 z(s)=\frac{q(s)-(s^2-7s+1)d_*}{2(s+1)^3}.
\]
The continuation from the upper boundary of the Stieltjes transform is
implemented as
\[
 \widetilde F(s)=
 \begin{cases}
 F(z(s)),&s<(7+3\sqrt5)/2,\\
 F(z(s))+i\sqrt3 F(1-z(s)),&s>(7+3\sqrt5)/2.
 \end{cases}
\]
Then the evaluated density is
$-\pi^{-1}\operatorname{Im}(M(s)\widetilde F(s)^2/x)$.
The additional term accounts for crossing the hypergeometric cut; simply
using the principal branch everywhere gives an incorrect density.
These formulas specialize the representation in \cite[Proposition 23]{edgar}.

Near the integration endpoints the implementation uses
Part~I's \eqref{eq:left} and \eqref{eq:right} at angular distances below $10^{-8}$.
This avoids subtracting nearly equal algebraic quantities or evaluating a
zero-balanced hypergeometric function at a rounded argument equal to one.
The cutoff and its unbounded numerical-error status are recorded explicitly
in the source and output.
