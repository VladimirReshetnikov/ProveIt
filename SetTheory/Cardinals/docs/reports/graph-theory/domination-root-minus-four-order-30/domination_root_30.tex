\begin{abstract}
We construct a connected simple graph on 30 vertices and 31 edges whose
 domination polynomial has the simple integer root \(-4\). This answers
 negatively the explicit question whether 33 is the minimum possible order.
 The graph is planar, has treewidth two, and has domination number ten.
 The root certificate reduces to the rational identity
\[
21-\frac{5}{13}+\frac{256}{13}
 \left(-\frac12-\frac{35}{64}\right)=0.
\]
We prove the reduction, give the complete edge list and polynomial, and
 provide three mutually consistent exact polynomial certificates.
 A supported-edge construction gives connected planar examples at every
 order at least 32, and examples with arbitrarily high multiplicity at
 \(-4\). The accompanying dependency-free Python programs reproduce the
 discovery and verify the certificates. No claim is made that 30 is the
 true minimum order.

\emph{[Added 30 September 2026, batch~67.]} Part~II, from a second
 manuscript, studies the alternating evaluation \(\D(G,-1)\). With
 \(\beta(G)\) the cycle rank it proves the sharp bound
 \(1\le|\D(G,-1)|\le3^{\beta(G)}\), a simultaneous divisibility budget for
 integer roots and their multiplicities, and the exact sets of values of
 \(|\D(G,-1)|\): \(\{1,3,7,9\}\) for \(\beta\le2\), with a conventional
 proof, and a ten-element set for \(\beta\le3\), computer-assisted. So no
 graph of cycle rank at most two has the root \(-6\). It also shows that
 the zero-\(C\) four-cycle search of Part~I forces \(|\D(G,-1)|=3\), so that
 search can produce no integer root below \(-2\) other than a simple
 \(-4\). Part~II constructs no root \(-6\) or \(-8\).
\end{abstract}

There is a connected planar graph \(\Gthirty\) with

\section{The question selected and the scope of the answer}

For a finite simple graph \(G=(V,E)\), a set \(S\subseteq V\) is
\emph{dominating} when every vertex outside \(S\) has a neighbor in \(S\).
The domination polynomial is the generating polynomial
\begin{equation}\label{eq:def}
 \D(G,x)=\sum_{\substack{S\subseteq V\\S\text{ dominating}}}x^{|S|}
        =\sum_{k=0}^{|V|}d(G,k)x^k.
\end{equation}
A domination root is a zero of this polynomial. The coefficients count
sets; all evaluations and cancellations in this note are exact.

Alikhani and Griswold \cite{AG} gave a 33-vertex graph with domination
root \(-4\), already disproving the earlier conjecture that the only
integer domination roots are \(0\) and \(-2\). Their Section~4, question~2,
asks whether 33 is minimal. We attack that follow-up question, not the
already-refuted original conjecture. The present construction shows that
33 is \emph{not} minimal. It also reduces the cyclomatic number from four
in their example to two.

The result is an explicit finite certificate with a conventional proof.
It is not a claim of a globally smallest graph, a classification of
integer domination roots, or a resolution of whether \(-6\) occurs.
Literature searches through the date of this note did not locate the
same improvement, but cannot establish publication priority. Independent
human review remains appropriate before making a novelty claim in a
submission.

\begin{writenote}[Part~II and the root \(-6\)]
Whether \(-6\) occurs is still open, but Part~II narrows it.
Corollary~\ref{cb:cor:no6} shows that no graph of cycle rank at most two
has the root \(-6\), and Corollary~\ref{cb:cor:triroots} that a root
\(-6\) of multiplicity at least two needs cycle rank at least four. The
eight-vertex graph \(X_5\) of Section~\ref{cb:sec:sharp3} shows that the
divisibility obstruction alone cannot exclude \(-6\) at cycle rank three.
Neither part constructs a graph with root \(-6\) or \(-8\).
\end{writenote}

An equivalent structural description starts with the cycle
\(0-1-2-3-0\). Add a common neighbor \(4\) of vertices \(1,2\), and give
\(4\) five leaves. Attach to vertex \(1\) a two-vertex rooted path.
Attach to vertex \(2\) the rooted tree on vertices \(12,\ldots,24\).
Attach to vertex \(3\) the rooted tree on vertices \(25,\ldots,29\).
Only the root of each attached tree meets the cycle, by one added edge.
Vertex \(0\) has no attached tree.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:invariants}
The graph \(\Gthirty\) is connected and planar, has 30 vertices, 31 edges,
15 leaves, maximum degree seven, cyclomatic number two, treewidth two,
and domination number ten.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The construction gives connectivity and the counts directly. Removing
all pendant trees leaves the cycle together with the vertex \(4\) and
its two edges to \(1,2\). This core is planar; attaching trees preserves
planarity. For a connected graph the cyclomatic number is \(|E|-|V|+1\),
which here is \(31-30+1=2\).

For completeness, a tree decomposition of the core has the three bags
\[
 \{1,2,4\},\qquad \{0,1,2\},\qquad \{0,2,3\},
\]
joined in the displayed order. Every core edge lies in a bag, and the
bags containing any fixed vertex form a connected subtree. Attach one
bag \(\{u,v\}\) for each edge in a pendant tree, extending from the bag
containing its parent vertex. The largest bag has size three, so the
width is at most two. The graph contains a cycle and is not a forest,
so its treewidth is not at most one.

To establish the domination number without the polynomial calculation,
consider the nine pairwise disjoint closed neighborhoods of leaves
\[
 \{4,5\},\{10,11\},\{12,13\},\{15,16\},\{17,18\},
 \{19,20\},\{21,22\},\{25,26\},\{28,29\}.
\]
Every dominating set must meet each one. It must also meet
\(N[0]=\{0,1,3\}\), disjoint from all nine. Thus its size is at least ten.
The set
\[
 \{0,4,10,12,15,17,19,21,25,28\}
\]
dominates every vertex, proving the matching upper bound.
\end{proof}

The cyclomatic number has an additional interpretation. Oboudi's
Theorem~7 \cite{Oboudi} excludes integer roots other than \(0,-2\) for
trees and unicyclic graphs. Combining that published result with the
construction below gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mincycle}
\min\{|E(G)|-|V(G)|+1:\ G\text{ connected},\ \D(G,-4)=0\}=2.
\end{equation}
The upper bound in \eqref{eq:mincycle} is proved here; its lower bound
uses the cited theorem. This is an exact minimum of \emph{cycle rank},
not an assertion that 30 is a minimum of \emph{order}.

\begin{writenote}[Cycle rank in Part~II]
Part~II writes \(\beta(G)=|E(G)|-|V(G)|+c(G)\), with \(c(G)\) the number of
components; for connected graphs it is the cyclomatic number used here.
Part~II does not re-prove the lower bound in \eqref{eq:mincycle}: its
obstruction at \(-1\) does not exclude a root \(-4\) at cycle rank one
(\(3\in\mathcal A_1\) in Theorem~\ref{cb:thm:bicyclic}), so that bound
still rests on Oboudi's theorem. Part~II's
Theorem~\ref{cb:thm:roots} adds that a root \(-4\) of multiplicity \(r\)
needs cycle rank at least \(r\).
\end{writenote}

\section{Rooted-tree states: the local calculation}\label{sec:states}

The three-state recurrence below is elementary. We include the details
so that the root certificate does not rely on any black-box graph
polynomial implementation.

\begin{definition}
For a tree \(T\) with distinguished root \(v\), let \(A_T,B_T,C_T\in\Z[x]\)
be the weighted sums of subsets of \(V(T)\) with these respective
properties:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),leftmargin=7mm,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item every vertex is dominated and \(v\) is selected;
\item every vertex is dominated and \(v\) is not selected;
\item every vertex except \(v\) is dominated, and \(v\) is not dominated.
\end{enumerate}
The weight of a subset \(S\) is \(x^{|S|}\). In state \(C_T\), the root
is necessarily unselected. Set \(P_T=A_T+B_T=\D(T,x)\).
\end{definition}

Write \(\Rtree(T_1,\ldots,T_s)\) for the rooted tree obtained by adjoining
one new root and joining it to the roots of disjoint copies of
\(T_1,\ldots,T_s\). In particular, \(L=\Rtree()\) is one vertex.

\begin{lemma}[Rooted recurrence]\label{lem:recurrence}
For \(T=\Rtree(T_1,\ldots,T_s)\),
\begin{align}
 A_T&=x\prod_{j=1}^s(A_{T_j}+B_{T_j}+C_{T_j}),\label{eq:A}\\
 B_T&=\prod_{j=1}^s(A_{T_j}+B_{T_j})-\prod_{j=1}^sB_{T_j},\label{eq:B}\\
 C_T&=\prod_{j=1}^sB_{T_j}.\label{eq:C}
\end{align}
Empty products are one, so \((A_L,B_L,C_L)=(x,0,1)\).
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
If the new root is selected, it contributes \(x\), and each child root
may be in any of the three states: a formerly undominated child root
is now dominated by its selected parent. This proves \eqref{eq:A}.
If the new root is unselected, each child subtree must internally
dominate its own root, allowing only \(A\) and \(B\). Among these
choices, precisely those in which every child is in state \(B\) leave
the new root undominated. Subtracting that class gives \eqref{eq:B},
and retaining it gives \eqref{eq:C}. All decompositions preserve the
number of selected vertices, proving the polynomial identities.
\end{proof}

If a rooted tree has a leaf immediately adjacent to its root, then
\(C_T=0\) as a polynomial: such a leaf contributes the factor \(B_L=0\)
to \eqref{eq:C}. Combinatorially, an undominated root would force its
private leaf to be both unselected and undominated.

For our graph define
\begin{align*}
 E&=\Rtree(L), &J&=\Rtree(E), &S_3&=\Rtree(L,L,L),\\
 W&=\Rtree(E,E,E,S_3), &T&=\Rtree(L,W), &K&=\Rtree(L,J),\\
 S_5&=\Rtree(L,L,L,L,L).&&&&
\end{align*}
The four external gadgets are \(E,T,K,S_5\), of orders \(2,13,5,6\).
Their roots are respectively the vertices \(10,12,25,4\). The first
three attach to one cycle vertex; the last attaches to both \(1,2\).
Each of the four has \(C=0\) identically.

\section{A four-cycle identity and the root proof}\label{sec:identity}

We now prove the identity that turns the table into a root certificate.
Although the search used weighted states at one value of \(x\), all four
gadgets in the final graph satisfy the stronger polynomial condition
\(C=0\).

\begin{proposition}[One hub and one undecorated corner]\label{prop:cycle}
Start with \(C_4\) on \(0,1,2,3\). Attach zero-\(C\) rooted gadgets
\(F_1,F_2,F_3\) to vertices \(1,2,3\), respectively, and attach a
zero-\(C\) rooted gadget \(H\) by joining its root to both \(1,2\).
No gadget is attached to \(0\). Write
\[
 P_i=A_i+B_i,\quad P_H=A_H+B_H,\quad
 a=\frac{B_1}{P_1},\quad b=\frac{B_2}{P_2},\quad
 c=\frac{B_3}{P_3},\quad r=\frac{B_H}{P_H},\quad
 \mathcal P=P_1P_2P_3P_H.
\]
As an identity of rational functions (and after clearing denominators,
of polynomials), the resulting graph \(G\) satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cycle}
 \D(G,x)=x\mathcal P\bigl[x^3+4x^2+6x+3-c-r(a+b)\bigr].
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
First sum over configurations that dominate all gadget vertices but
impose no domination requirement on the four cycle vertices. A root
cannot be in state \(C\), so its total internal weight is \(P\),
independently of which adjacent cycle vertices are selected. The
unrestricted total is consequently \(\mathcal P(1+x)^4\).

Apply inclusion--exclusion to the events that specified cycle vertices
are undominated. For \(U\subseteq\{0,1,2,3\}\), this forces every cycle
vertex in \(N_{C_4}[U]\) to be unselected. The remaining cycle vertices
contribute \((1+x)^{4-|N_{C_4}[U]|}\). Every attached root meeting a
vertex of \(U\) must also be unselected, replacing its factor \(P\) by
\(B\). In particular, the shared root contributes \(r\) once whenever
\(U\cap\{1,2\}\ne\varnothing\), not twice. With
\(u_0=1,u_1=a,u_2=b,u_3=c\), this gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:IE}
 \frac{\D(G,x)}{\mathcal P}=
 \sum_{U\subseteq\{0,1,2,3\}}
 (-1)^{|U|}(1+x)^{4-|N_{C_4}[U]|}
 \prod_{i\in U}u_i\,
 r^{\mathbf1_{U\cap\{1,2\}\ne\varnothing}}.
\end{equation}

Put \(t=1+x\). The empty-set contribution is \(t^4\), and singleton
contributions sum to \(-t[1+c+r(a+b)]\). Any two distinct cycle
vertices have closed-neighborhood union equal to the entire cycle.
The contributions by cardinality two, three and four are therefore
\begin{align*}
 &c+r(a+b+ab+ac+bc),\\
 &-r(ab+ac+bc+abc),\\
 &rabc,
\end{align*}
whose sum is \(c+r(a+b)\). Thus \eqref{eq:IE} becomes
\[
 t^4-t+(1-t)[c+r(a+b)]
 =x[x^3+4x^2+6x+3-c-r(a+b)],
\]
proving the assertion. Clearing denominators also proves the identity
at values where a displayed ratio is undefined.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[The counterexample]\label{thm:root}
The graph specified by \eqref{eq:edges} satisfies \(\D(\Gthirty,-4)=0\).
Consequently, the answer to the 33-vertex minimality question is no.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Apply Proposition~\ref{prop:cycle} with
\(F_1=E,F_2=T,F_3=K,H=S_5\). At \(x=-4\),
\[
 (P_1,P_2,P_3,P_H)=(8,-524\,288,-208,-52),
 \qquad \mathcal P=-45\,365\,592\,064\ne0.
\]
Table~\ref{tab:states} gives
\[
 a=-\frac12,\qquad b=-\frac{35}{64},\qquad
 c=-\frac5{13},\qquad r=\frac{256}{13}.
\]
At \(-4\), the bracket in \eqref{eq:cycle} is
\(-21-c-r(a+b)\). But
\[
 a+b=-\frac{67}{64},\qquad r(a+b)=-\frac{268}{13},
 \qquad 21+c=\frac{268}{13}.
\]
The bracket is exactly zero. Equivalently,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:oneline}
 \frac{\D(\Gthirty,-4)}{4\mathcal P}
 =21-\frac5{13}+\frac{256}{13}
   \left(-\frac12-\frac{35}{64}\right)=0.
\end{equation}
This is an equality in \(\Q\), not a numerical approximation.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The proof uses only the explicit graph and the elementary state and
inclusion--exclusion identities proved above. It does not assume the
correctness of a numerical factorization, any search program, or any
particular normalization of the transfer matrices in earlier work.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Certificate A: expansion of the four-cycle identity}

Proposition~\ref{prop:cycle} has the denominator-free form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:denfree}
\begin{aligned}
\D(G,x)=x\bigl[&
 (x^3+4x^2+6x+3)P_1P_2P_3P_H\\
 &-B_HP_3(B_1P_2+B_2P_1)-B_3P_1P_2P_H\bigr].
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The following compact gadget polynomials, together with
\eqref{eq:denfree}, are one symbolic certificate for every coefficient.
Let
\[
 p=x(x+2),\qquad s=x(1+x)^3+x^3.
\]
Then
\begin{align}
 P_1&=p,& B_1&=x,\notag\\
 P_H&=x(1+x)^5+x^5,&B_H&=x^5,\notag\\
 P_3&=x^2(x^3+5x^2+8x+3),&B_3&=x^2(x^2+3x+1),\label{eq:gadgetpolys}\\
 P_2&=x(x+1)(x+2)p^3s-x^7,&
 B_2&=x(x+1)p^3s-x^7.\notag
\end{align}
These follow directly from Lemma~\ref{lem:recurrence}. Expanding
\eqref{eq:denfree} with \eqref{eq:gadgetpolys} gives
Table~\ref{tab:coeff} and \eqref{eq:factor}.

\subsection{Certificate B: stripping trees and enumerating the 2-core}

Remove vertices of current degree zero or one successively. At a
removed vertex, compute its three rooted polynomial states and absorb
them into its surviving neighbor. The remaining 2-core here has five
vertices: \(0,1,2,3,4\).

At each remaining core vertex \(v\), let
\[
 T_v=\prod_j(A_j+B_j+C_j),\qquad
 U_v=\prod_j(A_j+B_j),\qquad V_v=\prod_j B_j,
\]
where the product runs over its absorbed child trees. For a selected
core subset \(S\), the local contribution of \(v\) is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:coreDP}
 \begin{cases}
 xT_v,&v\in S,\\
 U_v,&v\notin S\text{ and }N_{\rm core}(v)\cap S\ne\varnothing,\\
 U_v-V_v,&v\notin S\text{ and }N_{\rm core}(v)\cap S=\varnothing.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Multiplying local factors and summing over all \(2^5=32\) subsets gives
the full polynomial. Formula~\eqref{eq:coreDP} is exactly the distinction
between root selected, root dominated by a core neighbor, and root
needing domination from a child. Thus it counts each dominating set
once. This calculation does not use the special four-cycle identity.

\subsection{Certificate C: an independent enumeration over non-leaves}

The following identity avoids rooted states and 2-core stripping
altogether.

\begin{proposition}[Leaf-subset certificate]\label{prop:leaf}
Let \(G\) be a finite graph with no component isomorphic to \(K_2\).
Let \(L\) be its set of degree-one vertices and \(C=V\setminus L\).
For \(v\in C\), set \(t_v=|N(v)\cap L|\). Call \(S\subseteq C\)
admissible when every \(v\in C\) with \(t_v=0\) is dominated by \(S\)
in \(G[C]\). Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:leaf}
 \D(G,x)=\sum_{S\text{ admissible}}
 x^{\,|S|+\sum_{v\in C\setminus S}t_v}
 (1+x)^{\sum_{v\in S}t_v}.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Fix the selected non-leaves \(S\). If a non-leaf \(v\) is selected,
its adjacent leaves may each be selected or not, independently, giving
\((1+x)^{t_v}\). If \(v\) is not selected, every one of those leaves
must be selected, contributing \(x^{t_v}\). When \(t_v>0\), these
forced leaves also dominate \(v\). When \(t_v=0\), the only way to
dominate an unselected \(v\) is through the selected non-leaves; this
is precisely admissibility. The exclusion of isolated-edge components
ensures that every leaf has a non-leaf neighbor. These disjoint cases
exhaust all dominating sets and prove \eqref{eq:leaf}.
\end{proof}

For \(\Gthirty\), \(|L|=|C|=15\), so the independent computation tests
\(2^{15}=32768\) non-leaf subsets. Exactly 23065 are admissible.
Grouping the summands in \eqref{eq:leaf} by their two exponents gives
118 terms \(m_{a,b}x^a(1+x)^b\). The file
\texttt{data/leaf\_certificate.csv} records all triples \((a,b,m_{a,b})\).
Binomial expansion reproduces Table~\ref{tab:coeff}, and direct
substitution in that 118-term sum gives zero at \(-4\).

\section{Connected families and arbitrary multiplicity}\label{sec:families}

There is a simple way to preserve the root without leaving connected
graphs. The underlying supported-edge factorization is elementary and
related to the pendant-star products in Oboudi \cite{Oboudi}; it is not
claimed as a new general principle here.

Call a vertex a \emph{support vertex} when it has a neighbor of degree
one, called a private leaf.

\begin{lemma}[Edges between support vertices]\label{lem:support}
Suppose distinct vertices \(u,v\) each have a private leaf outside
\(\{u,v\}\), and these leaves remain private after the edge \(uv\) is
added or deleted. Toggling \(uv\) does not change the
family of dominating vertex subsets, hence does not change the
domination polynomial.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
A dominating set must select either a private leaf of \(u\) or \(u\)
itself, because it must dominate that leaf. Either choice already
dominates \(u\), independently of the edge \(uv\). The same holds for
\(v\). Every other vertex has exactly the same neighbors before and
after the change. Thus a vertex subset dominates one graph exactly
when it dominates the other.
\end{proof}

For disjoint graphs, independently choosing dominating sets proves
\begin{equation}\label{eq:product}
 \D(G\mathbin{\dot\cup}H,x)=\D(G,x)\D(H,x).
\end{equation}
Combining this with the lemma connects factors without changing the
product.

\begin{theorem}[A bounded-width connected family]\label{thm:family}
For each integer \(t\ge0\), there is a connected planar graph \(G_t\)
with \(30+2t\) vertices, cyclomatic number two, treewidth two, maximum
degree seven, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:family}
 \D(G_t,x)=[x(x+2)]^t\D(\Gthirty,x).
\end{equation}
In particular, \(-4\) is a simple root of every \(\D(G_t,x)\).
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For \(t=0\), use \(\Gthirty\). For \(t\ge1\), take \(\Gthirty\) and
\(t\) disjoint edges \(a_i b_i\). Retain each
\(b_i\) as a private leaf. Join vertex \(10\) to \(a_1\), and join
\(a_i\) to \(a_{i+1}\) for \(1\le i<t\). Vertex \(10\) retains its
leaf \(11\). Each added connecting edge joins support vertices and
therefore changes no dominating sets. Since
\(\D(K_2,x)=2x+x^2=x(x+2)\), equations
\eqref{eq:product} and \eqref{eq:family} follow.

The new part is a pendant tree, so planarity, treewidth two, and cycle
rank two are preserved. There are \(2t\) new vertices and \(2t\) new
edges. Vertex \(10\) acquires at most one new incident edge; every
\(a_i\) has degree at most three. The old degree-seven vertex \(4\)
remains, so the maximum degree is still seven. Finally
\((-4)(-4+2)=8\ne0\), so multiplication by \([x(x+2)]^t\) does not
alter the multiplicity at \(-4\).
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[All sufficiently large orders]\label{cor:orders}
There is a connected planar graph of treewidth two and cycle rank two
with domination root \(-4\) at order 30 and at every order \(n\ge32\).
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Besides attaching an isolated edge as above, we may attach a disjoint
star \(K_{1,2}\) by its center to a support vertex. Lemma~\ref{lem:support}
gives an additional factor
\[
 \D(K_{1,2},x)=x(1+x)^2+x^2=x(x^2+3x+1).
\]
At \(-4\) this factor is \(-20\ne0\); it adds three vertices and a
pendant tree. Every integer \(n-30\ge2\) is a nonnegative integer
combination of two and three. Using one three-vertex attachment for
odd \(n-30\), and otherwise only two-vertex attachments, proves the
claim. This construction asserts nothing about order 31.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Unbounded root multiplicity in connected graphs]\label{thm:mult}
For every integer \(k\ge1\), there is a connected planar graph \(H_k\)
of order \(30k\), treewidth two, and maximum degree seven in which
\(-4\) has multiplicity exactly \(k\).
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Take \(k\) disjoint copies of \(\Gthirty\), and connect their copies
of vertex \(10\) in a path. Every connecting edge joins support
vertices retaining private leaves. Thus
\[
 \D(H_k,x)=\D(\Gthirty,x)^k.
\]
The root has multiplicity exactly \(k\) by \eqref{eq:simple}. The
connecting edges are bridges, so they preserve planarity and do not
increase treewidth beyond two. Internal copies of vertex \(10\) have
degree four after the operation; the maximum degree is still seven.
\end{proof}

These results strengthen the bare infinite-family question in
\cite[Section~4, question~3]{AG}: disconnected examples would follow
immediately from disjoint union, whereas the constructions here remain
connected and structurally sparse.

\begin{writenote}[Cycle cost of multiplicity]
The graph \(H_k\) of Theorem~\ref{thm:mult} has cycle rank \(2k\) (each
copy of \(\Gthirty\) brings two independent cycles and the connecting
edges are bridges). Part~II's Theorem~\ref{cb:thm:roots} shows that
multiplicity \(k\) at \(-4\) needs cycle rank at least \(k\), and its
Question~\ref{cb:q:mult} asks for the true minimum; multiplicity two at
cycle rank two would need \(|\D(G,-1)|=9\). Part~I's \(H_k\) is unrelated
to Part~II's eight-vertex graph \(X_5\).
\end{writenote}

\section{How the search was made finite and reproducible}\label{sec:search}

The successful search was not a uniform enumeration of 30-vertex
graphs. It used the sparse core to turn a combinatorial search into
rational arithmetic on a catalogue of small rooted trees.

\subsection{The catalogue and the exact target equation}

A rooted tree is stored as the canonically ordered tuple of the shapes
of its children. The empty tuple is a leaf. Recursively enumerate
multisets of previously generated trees whose total order is \(n-1\),
then place a new root above them. This generates each unlabeled rooted
tree of order \(n\) exactly once: deleting the root yields a uniquely
determined multiset of rooted child components.

For each tree compute \((A(-4),B(-4),C(-4))\) by
Lemma~\ref{lem:recurrence}. Retain its rational ratio
\[
 q=\frac{B(-4)}{A(-4)+B(-4)}
 \quad\text{when}\quad C(-4)=0,\quad A(-4)+B(-4)\ne0.
\]
The condition \(C(-4)=0\) is sufficient for a weighted search at the
single point \(-4\); it need not hold identically for every candidate.
All final gadgets happen to satisfy \(C(x)=0\) identically, which
makes the polynomial proof particularly clean.

At a cycle site, disjoint branches give a product of their ratios.
Store a least-order realization for every attainable product, including
the empty forest with ratio one and order zero. Discarding larger
realizations of the same ratio is valid for this search: they have
identical normalized effects at \(-4\), and cannot improve its order
objective. The catalogue contains all such minimum-cost products within
the prescribed forest-size budget.

For a shared star hub with \(m\ge1\) leaves,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hubratio}
 r_m=\frac{(-4)^m}{-4(-3)^m+(-4)^m}
     =\frac{4^{m-1}}{4^{m-1}-3^m}.
\end{equation}
The denominator is nonzero, since a power of four cannot equal a
positive power of three. Leaving vertex \(0\) undecorated reduces the
root search, by Proposition~\ref{prop:cycle}, to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:searchtarget}
 c=-21-r_m(a+b).
\end{equation}
Thus enumerate two stored ratios \(a,b\), compute the required \(c\),
and look it up exactly. No tolerance or root approximation occurs.
If the three forests have sizes \(n_a,n_b,n_c\), the total order is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cost}
 n=5+m+n_a+n_b+n_c.
\end{equation}
The hit has \((m,n_a,n_b,n_c)=(5,2,13,5)\), giving \(n=30\).

\subsection{The supplied run and its limitations}

The portable reconstruction program uses rooted trees and site forests
of order at most 14 and hub sizes \(1\le m\le15\). It starts with the
strict target \(n<33\), tries the five-leaf hub first, and then tests
the other permitted hub sizes. It retains improving hits and checks
the graph of every hit with the separate 2-core polynomial routine.
A representative completed run gives:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lr}
\toprule
Quantity & Recorded value\\
\midrule
Distinct single-tree ratios & 4\,198\\
Distinct forest ratios, including the empty forest & 6\,118\\
Exact rational candidate lookups & 2\,547\,880\\
Best order returned & 30\\
Time in the execution environment & 6.154 seconds\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The time is an observation on this environment, not a complexity bound
or a cross-machine promise. The deterministic counts and the hit are
stored in \texttt{results/search.json}. The number of rooted trees at
each order from 1 through 14 is also recorded there.

In this bounded search, no smaller realization was returned. That is
\emph{not} an exhaustive lower bound for all graphs, all bicyclic
graphs, or even all rooted attachments to this core: an individual
site forest was limited to 14 vertices. Searching other cores,
allowing a decorated fourth corner, or allowing larger individual
forests can escape the tested class. The abstract minimum remains open
in this note.

\begin{writenote}[What escapes the tested class]
For the \emph{order} of a root \(-4\) the last sentence stands. For any
\emph{other} integer root it does not: by Part~II's
Theorem~\ref{cb:thm:searchobstruction}, every graph built on this core
from rooted trees with \(C=0\) at the target, with any number of trees
of any size at each site, a hub carrying at least one pendant leaf, and
a decorated fourth corner allowed, has \(|\D(G,-1)|=3\). Such a graph has
no integer root below \(-2\) except a simple \(-4\). A search for another
root has to leave this architecture altogether, for example by changing
the core or by admitting trees with \(C\ne0\) at the target.
\end{writenote}

\subsection{Verification and computational trust}

The complete coefficient list was recomputed by all three routes in
Section~\ref{sec:polynomial}: the symbolic four-cycle certificate,
the 2-core state algorithm, and the independent leaf-subset identity.
The latter two are genuinely different enumerations, although all are
implemented in the same Python runtime. They are not independent
proof-assistant formalizations.

The two general graph algorithms were also compared against direct
enumeration of all vertex subsets for \emph{every labeled simple graph
on zero through five vertices}: 1100 graphs in total. Further checks
used 120 deterministically seeded graphs on six through nine vertices.
Five instances of the connected extension in Theorem~\ref{thm:family}
were checked as polynomial identities. All tests passed.

The verification run recorded about 0.23 seconds. The important
properties are the exact outputs, not the timing. All polynomial
arithmetic is integer arithmetic, and all search ratios use Python's
\texttt{fractions.Fraction}. No symbolic algebra package, network
service, cached pickle, numerical optimizer, or proprietary system is
required to run the supplied code.

\section{Conclusions and remaining targets}\label{sec:conclusion}

The attack gives a concrete answer to the selected question: a graph
with domination root \(-4\) need not have 33 vertices. The explicit
order-30 example also demonstrates that cycle rank two and treewidth
two already suffice. The cancellation is additive in three rooted
ratios and a single shared-star ratio; it does not require a large
or highly connected core.

The demonstrated improvement is an \emph{upper bound}: the minimum
order is at most 30. A serious lower-bound project should canonicalize
possible small cores and carry exact rooted-state catalogues with a
global vertex budget, rather than infer minimality from an unsuccessful
restricted search. An especially natural next target is whether some
graph on at most 29 vertices has root \(-4\).

For other integer targets \(z\), the same core identity yields
\[
 c+r(a+b)=z^3+4z^2+6z+3,
\]
with the rooted states evaluated at \(z\). This provides a concrete
search equation, but this note establishes no graph with root \(-6\)
or \(-8\). Nor does it make a claim that all negative even integers
occur. The value of the present result is the fully specified,
independently checked counterexample and its complete elementary proof.

\begin{writenote}[The search equation for other targets is answered negatively]
Part~II settles what this search equation can give. Take an integer
\(z=-k\) with \(k\ge3\), site trees with \(C(z)=0\) (any number at every
corner, the fourth corner included) and a star hub with at least one
leaf, as in \eqref{eq:hubratio}. Theorem~\ref{cb:thm:searchobstruction}
shows that every such graph has \(|\D(G,-1)|=3\). Since \(k-1\) must
divide \(\D(G,-1)\) at a root \(-k\) (Lemma~\ref{cb:lem:divisibility}),
the equation has a solution only at \(z=-4\), and then the root is
simple. The argument that the proof applies to the site trees applies
equally to a hub tree with \(C(z)=0\), as Proposition~\ref{prop:cycle}
allows: such a hub, too, is never selected and is already dominated at
\(x=-1\), so it can be deleted. (This extension is the present write's;
the manuscript states only the star hub.) So the search equation yields no
root \(-6\) or \(-8\). Whether such roots exist is a different question,
and it stays open. Part~II excludes \(-6\) for every graph of cycle rank
at most two (Corollary~\ref{cb:cor:no6}) and lists, in
Section~\ref{cb:sec:questions}, the cases at which a search can still
aim.
\end{writenote}

\section{Definitions and the elementary arithmetic obstruction}\label{cb:sec:definitions}

All graphs in the conclusions are finite, undirected, and simple. The
multigraphs used later are only compressed descriptions of simple graphs.
For a graph $G=(V,E)$, write $n=|V|$, $m=|E|$, and $c(G)$ for the number of
connected components. The empty graph has $c(G)=0$. Define
\[
 \beta(G)=m-n+c(G).
\]
Equivalently, $\beta(G)$ is the number of edges outside any spanning forest.
It is additive over components and unchanged by attaching a tree at one
vertex or by subdividing an edge.

A set $S\subseteq V$ is dominating if every vertex is in $S$ or has a
neighbor in $S$. With $N[S]$ denoting its closed neighborhood, the domination
polynomial is
\begin{equation}\label{cb:eq:domdef}
 D(G,x)=\sum_{S\subseteq V:\,N[S]=V}x^{|S|}.
\end{equation}
We put $D(\varnothing,x)=1$. Domination polynomials multiply over connected
components. For a nonempty graph the polynomial is monic, has integer
coefficients, has no positive real root, and has zero as a root. These
standard facts also follow immediately from the definition \cite{Intro}.

\begin{lemma}[Oddness]\label{cb:lem:odd}
For every finite simple graph, $D(G,-1)$ is an odd, nonzero integer.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Consider the ordered pairs $(A,B)$ of vertex sets such that
$B\cap N[A]=\varnothing$. For fixed $A$ the number of choices for $B$ is
$2^{n-|N[A]|}$. Modulo two, therefore, the number of pairs equals the
number of dominating sets.

Because adjacency is symmetric, interchanging $A$ and $B$ is an involution
on these pairs. A fixed point has $A=B$ and $A\cap N[A]=\varnothing$;
since $A\subseteq N[A]$, this forces $A=B=\varnothing$. There is exactly
one fixed point, so the number of dominating sets is odd. Finally,
$D(G,-1)\equiv D(G,1)\pmod2$.
\end{proof}

The oddness statement is classical; it is attributed to Brouwer in the
literature on $D(G,-1)$ \cite{AlikhaniMinusOne}. The proof above is included
to keep the later deductions independent of an external parity theorem.

\begin{lemma}[Evaluation divisibility]\label{cb:lem:divisibility}
Let $k\ge2$ be an integer. If $(x+k)^r$ divides $D(G,x)$, then
\[
 (k-1)^r\mid D(G,-1).
\]
More generally, for distinct integers $k_1,\ldots,k_s\ge2$, with corresponding
root multiplicities $r_1,\ldots,r_s$, the product
$\prod_j(k_j-1)^{r_j}$ divides $D(G,-1)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Division by a monic linear polynomial with integer root preserves integral
coefficients. Consequently
$D(G,x)=\prod_j(x+k_j)^{r_j}Q(x)$ for $Q\in\Z[x]$. Evaluate at $x=-1$.
\end{proof}

In particular a nonzero rational domination root must be a negative even
integer: the rational-root theorem gives integrality, positivity is
impossible, $-1$ is excluded by Lemma~\ref{cb:lem:odd}, and an odd $k\ge3$
would make the even integer $k-1$ divide the odd integer $D(G,-1)$.
The factors corresponding to roots $0$ and $-2$ are invisible in absolute
value at $x=-1$; this is an essential limitation of the method.

\section{A finite signed-state lemma for constrained forests}\label{cb:sec:forest}

\subsection{The constrained counting problem}
Let $T$ be a forest. At each vertex impose any combination of the following
conditions: the vertex is forced selected, forced absent, or unrestricted;
and its domination requirement is either retained or waived. A waived
requirement models domination already supplied from outside the forest.
The alternating count is
\begin{equation}\label{cb:eq:constrained}
 Z(T)=\sum_{S\text{ satisfying all conditions}}(-1)^{|S|}.
\end{equation}
Conflicting selection constraints give $Z(T)=0$.

Root each tree component. For a rooted subtree, use three states $(A,B,C)$.
State $A$ counts configurations with the root selected. State $B$ counts
configurations with the root absent and its requirement satisfied. State
$C$ counts configurations with the root absent and its requirement not yet
satisfied, with satisfaction still possible from the parent. All other
requirements in the subtree must already be satisfied. The weights are
$(-1)^{|S|}$. A waived root requirement is always satisfied, so its $C$ state
is zero.

Before imposing the root's own constraints, child states $(A_i,B_i,C_i)$
give
\begin{equation}\label{cb:eq:forestrec}
 A=-\prod_i(A_i+B_i+C_i),\qquad
 B=\prod_i(A_i+B_i)-\prod_iB_i,\qquad
 C=\prod_iB_i.
\end{equation}
For a selected root, each child may be in any state. For an absent root,
every child must have its requirement satisfied internally, and at least
one child must be selected to satisfy an ordinary root requirement. This
explains the subtraction in $B$.

Waiving the root requirement replaces $(A,B,C)$ by $(A,B+C,0)$.
Forcing selection replaces it by $(A,0,0)$; forcing absence replaces it by
$(0,B,C)$. A complete component contributes $A+B$ at its root.

\begin{theorem}[Constrained-forest cancellation]\label{cb:thm:forest}
For every finite forest and every set of conditions just described,
\[
 Z(T)\in\{-1,0,1\}.
\]
More precisely, every rooted signature lies in the following thirteen-element
set:
\begin{equation}\label{cb:eq:states}
 \calS=\{0\}\ \cup\ \{\pm e_1,\pm e_2,\pm e_3\}
 \ \cup\ \{\pm(e_1-e_2),\pm(e_1-e_3),\pm(e_2-e_3)\}.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For a signature $(a,b,c)$ introduce transformed coordinates
\[
 \Phi(a,b,c)=(a+b+c,a+b,b).
\]
The image of $\calS$ is $\calP\cup(-\calP)$, where
\[
 \calP=\{000,001,010,011,100,110,111\}.
\]
Here a three-digit string denotes a vector. Notice that $101$ is the only
binary vector omitted. The set $\calP$ is closed under coordinatewise
multiplication: if a product has first and third coordinates one, each
factor has first and third coordinates one; each such factor must be $111$,
so the product cannot be $101$. The signed set is therefore closed under
arbitrary coordinatewise products, including the empty product $111$.

Let $(p,q,r)=\prod_i\Phi(A_i,B_i,C_i)$. The recurrence produces
$(-p,q-r,r)$. For the seven unsigned possibilities, these are respectively
\[
\begin{array}{c|rrrrrrr}
(p,q,r)&000&001&010&011&100&110&111\\\hline
(-p,q-r,r)&0&(0,-1,1)&e_2&e_3&-e_1&-e_1+e_2&-e_1+e_3.
\end{array}
\]
They all lie in $\calS$, and the same is true after a global sign change.
The three root-constraint operations also preserve $\calS$, as follows
directly from the displayed list. This proves the signature assertion by
induction, without any restriction on the number of children.

For each signature in $\calS$, $A+B\in\{-1,0,1\}$. Multiplication over
the forest's components proves the result, including the empty forest.
\end{proof}

The waived-requirement and pinning clauses are important. A bound proved
only for ordinary trees would not justify the feedback-edge argument below:
removing an edge changes both the allowed selections and which vertices
are already dominated.

\section{A sharp cycle-rank bound}

\begin{theorem}[Sharp alternating-sum budget]\label{cb:thm:budget}
Every finite simple graph satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{cb:eq:mainbound}
 1\le |D(G,-1)|\le3^{\beta(G)}.
\end{equation}
For every $b\ge1$ equality at the upper end is attained by a connected planar
graph of maximum degree at most three with cycle rank $b$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Proof of the bound]
Choose a spanning forest $T$ and orient each of the $b=\beta(G)$ remaining
edges arbitrarily, solely to name its endpoints $u,v$. Partition the
possible selections at this edge into three disjoint cases:
\begin{equation}\label{cb:eq:edgecases}
\begin{array}{c|l|l}
\text{case}&\text{selection constraints}&\text{requirement waived after removing }uv\\\hline
0&u\text{ selected},\ v\text{ unrestricted}&v\\
1&u\text{ absent},\ v\text{ selected}&u\\
2&u,v\text{ both absent}&\text{none}.
\end{array}
\end{equation}
In case 0 the selected vertex $u$ already dominates itself and supplies
external domination to $v$; thus it is unnecessary to split further
according to whether $v$ is selected. The other two cases are equally direct.

A choice of cases for all feedback edges yields a constrained-forest
problem on $T$. Contradictory pins contribute zero. Otherwise the forest
requirements, with the stated waivers, are exactly equivalent to domination
in the original graph for that case. Every dominating set belongs to one
and only one case vector. Therefore
\[
 D(G,-1)=\sum_{\eta\in\{0,1,2\}^{b}}Z_\eta(T).
\]
Every summand belongs to $\{-1,0,1\}$ by Theorem~\ref{cb:thm:forest}, proving
the upper bound. Lemma~\ref{cb:lem:odd} proves the lower bound.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[A product-preserving connection]\label{cb:lem:join}
Let $G,H$ be disjoint nonempty graphs with distinguished vertices $u,v$.
Add new vertices $z,t$ and edges $uz,zv,zt$. For the resulting connected
join, denoted $J(G,H)$, one has
\begin{equation}\label{cb:eq:join}
 D(J(G,H),-1)=-D(G,-1)D(H,-1),\qquad
 \beta(J(G,H))=\beta(G)+\beta(H)
\end{equation}
when $G,H$ are connected.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
If $z$ is selected, the leaf $t$ may be selected or absent. Its two weights
sum to zero, and its choice affects no other vertex; all such configurations
cancel. If $z$ is absent, $t$ must be selected and contributes $-1$.
Vertex $z$ is then already dominated, while it provides no domination to
$u$ or $v$. The remaining choices are independent dominating sets of
$G$ and $H$. The cycle-rank identity follows by counting the two added
vertices and three added edges.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of sharpness in Theorem~\ref{cb:thm:budget}]
A four-cycle has $D(C_4,x)=x^4+4x^3+6x^2$ and $D(C_4,-1)=3$.
Take $b$ disjoint four-cycles and join consecutive cycles by the operation
of Lemma~\ref{cb:lem:join}. On an internal four-cycle use distinct vertices
for its left and right connections. The result is a planar chain of
cycles with bridges and pendant leaves; all degrees are at most three.
It has $6b-2$ vertices and $7b-3$ edges, hence cycle rank $b$, and
\[
 D(G^{\mathrm{ch}}_b,-1)=(-1)^{b-1}3^b.
\]
For $b=0$, a single vertex supplies the bound $|D(-1)|=1$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{An exact parameterized algorithm}
The proof computes $D(G,-1)$ using at most $3^\beta$ constrained-forest
passes. Each pass has linear many local state operations, with states in a
fixed thirteen-element set. Thus the algorithm uses
$O(3^\beta(n+m))$ state operations and integer accumulations, and linear
working memory apart from the counters. The output and counters require
$O(\beta+1)$ bits. This is an arithmetic-operation bound, not a claim that
arbitrarily large integers have unit bit cost.

The delivered implementation records how many case vectors contribute
$-1,0,1$. These three counts certify the resulting evaluation once the
forest recursion and the partition have been checked. The algorithm's
interest here is not a claim of the first fixed-parameter graph-polynomial
algorithm, but the particularly strong cancellation at $-1$ and its
arithmetic consequences.

\section{Integer-root and multiplicity budgets}

\begin{theorem}[Simultaneous integer-root budget]\label{cb:thm:roots}
Suppose the distinct integer roots $-k_1,\ldots,-k_s$, with $k_j\ge2$,
have multiplicities $r_j$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{cb:eq:rootbudget}
 \prod_{j=1}^s(k_j-1)^{r_j}\ \mid\ D(G,-1),\qquad
 \prod_{j=1}^s(k_j-1)^{r_j}\le3^{\beta(G)}.
\end{equation}
In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{cb:eq:rootcor}
 k\le3^{\beta(G)}+1,
 \qquad r\log_3(k-1)\le\beta(G)
\end{equation}
for every root $-k$ of multiplicity $r$ with $k\ge2$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Combine Lemma~\ref{cb:lem:divisibility} with Theorem~\ref{cb:thm:budget} and use
$D(G,-1)\ne0$. Taking logarithms gives the second formulation.
\end{proof}

For example, a root $-4$ of multiplicity $r$ requires $\beta\ge r$.
A graph having both roots $-4$ and $-8$ requires
$3\cdot7\le3^\beta$, hence at least three independent cycles.
A root $-6$ of multiplicity two requires $25\le3^\beta$, so the universal
bound gives $\beta\ge3$; the exact spectrum below improves this to
$\beta\ge4$ because no permitted three-cycle value is divisible by $25$.

\begin{remark}[What is sharp]
The $3^\beta$ evaluation bound is sharp. This does \emph{not} imply that
$-(3^\beta+1)$ is a domination root of an extremal graph. Nor does it imply
that the cycle lower bound for every prescribed root multiplicity is
attained. Divisibility of an evaluation is necessary, never sufficient.
\end{remark}

The case $k=2$ has $\log_3(k-1)=0$, so there is no restriction on the
multiplicity of $-2$ from this invariant. This agrees with ordinary
forests: the disjoint union of $r$ edges has domination polynomial
$x^r(x+2)^r$ and cycle rank zero.

\section{Pruning tree decorations without changing the invariant}\label{cb:sec:pruning}

The constrained-forest theorem has a much stronger specialization when
there are no constraints.

\begin{lemma}[Ordinary tree signatures]\label{cb:lem:ordinary}
For a rooted tree at $x=-1$ its three-state signature is exactly one of
\begin{equation}\label{cb:eq:ordinary}
 \pm(-1,0,1),\qquad \pm(0,1,0).
\end{equation}
In particular, $D(T,-1)=\pm1$ for every tree.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
A leaf has signature $(-1,0,1)$. Up to a global sign, the transformed
coordinates of the two displayed types are $(0,-1,0)$ and $(1,1,1)$.
If at least one child is of the first type, the product coordinates have
first and third entries zero and second entry $\pm1$, producing
$(0,\pm1,0)$. Otherwise they equal $\pm(1,1,1)$, producing
$\pm(-1,0,1)$. Induction proves the claim.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Tree-decoration deletion]\label{cb:thm:pruning}
Let $H$ be a graph and attach a rooted tree $T_v$ at each $v\in V(H)$ by
identifying its root with $v$, with the trees otherwise disjoint.
Let the resulting graph be $G$. Write the signature of $T_v$ as either
$\epsilon_v(-1,0,1)$ or $\epsilon_v(0,1,0)$, where $\epsilon_v\in\{-1,1\}$,
and let $U$ be the vertices of the first type. Then
\begin{equation}\label{cb:eq:prune}
 D(G,-1)=\left(\prod_{v\in V(H)}\epsilon_v\right)D(H[U],-1).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
An ordinary vertex of $H$, before interacting with its neighbors in $H$,
has signature $(-1,0,1)$. The first type is exactly this signature times
$\epsilon_v$. The second type has root absent, already dominated, and
never selected. It therefore neither needs domination from nor supplies
domination to its neighbors; it can be deleted, leaving the multiplicative
factor $\epsilon_v$. Applying this observation at every vertex proves the
identity.
\end{proof}

For a graph with nonempty 2-core, all vertices outside that core form
rooted tree decorations of its vertices, together with separate tree
components. Apply Theorem~\ref{cb:thm:pruning}, and then repeat if vertex
deletions create new tree decorations. The process terminates, and proves
that $|D(G,-1)|$ equals the absolute evaluation of a graph whose nonempty
components have minimum degree at least two; separate forest components
only supply signs. At every step cycle rank does not increase. Indeed,
the cycle space of a subgraph embeds in that of the original graph.

The reduced graph need not stay connected, even when $G$ is connected.
This is why products of cycle values enter the exact spectra. The example
in the preceding figure reduces to two disjoint four-cycles.

\begin{writenote}[Relation to Part~I's Certificate~B]
Part~I's Certificate~B (Section~\ref{sec:polynomial}, equation
\eqref{eq:coreDP}) also strips pendant trees down to the 2-core, but it
works for every \(x\): each stripped tree is absorbed into its core vertex
through the three products \(T_v,U_v,V_v\), and the core is then
enumerated. Theorem~\ref{cb:thm:pruning} is the specialization to
\(x=-1\). There the absorbed data take only the four values
\eqref{cb:eq:ordinary}, so a vertex of the second type can be deleted
outright and the evaluation reduces to a smaller graph. At \(x=-1\) the
two compute the same value by different routes; the deletion is what
Part~II adds. Neither source states this comparison; it is the present
write's.
\end{writenote}

\begin{corollary}[Sign reversal without adding a cycle]\label{cb:cor:sign}
Attaching a pendant path with two new vertices at any existing vertex
multiplies $D(G,-1)$ by $-1$ and leaves cycle rank unchanged.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The rooted three-vertex path, rooted at an end, has signature
$(1,0,-1)=-(-1,0,1)$; apply Theorem~\ref{cb:thm:pruning}.
\end{proof}

\section{A period-four transfer identity for all subdivisions}

\subsection{A three-state expansion}
Use states $0,1,2$ at each graph vertex: respectively an unselected
unmarked vertex, a selected vertex, and an unselected vertex marked by
inclusion--exclusion. Put
\begin{equation}\label{cb:eq:matrixbase}
 w=(1,-1,-1),\quad W=\diag(1,-1,-1),\quad
 M_1=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1\\1&1&0\\1&0&1\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}\label{cb:eq:spin}
 D(G,-1)=\sum_{\sigma:V\to\{0,1,2\}}
 \prod_{v\in V}w_{\sigma(v)}
 \prod_{uv\in E}(M_1)_{\sigma(u),\sigma(v)}.
\end{equation}
To verify this identity, first fix the selected set $S$. A state-2 mark is
allowed exactly at an unselected vertex with no selected neighbor.
Summing over the possible marks multiplies $(-1)^{|S|}$ by $(1-1)^h$,
where $h$ is the number of undominated vertices. The contribution is
therefore $(-1)^{|S|}$ for a dominating set and zero otherwise.

For a path of $\ell$ edges, sum the internal states but do not include the
endpoint weights. The resulting matrix is
\begin{equation}\label{cb:eq:transfer}
 M_\ell=M_1(WM_1)^{\ell-1},\qquad \ell\ge1.
\end{equation}
Direct multiplication gives
\begin{equation}\label{cb:eq:fourmatrices}
\begin{split}
 M_2&=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0&0\\0&0&1\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
 M_3=\begin{pmatrix}-1&-1&-1\\-1&0&-1\\-1&-1&0\end{pmatrix},\\
 M_4&=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&-1&0\\0&0&-1\end{pmatrix}=W,
 \qquad (WM_1)^4=I.
\end{split}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Four-subdivision invariance]\label{cb:thm:period}
Replacing any edge of a simple graph by a path with five edges leaves
$D(G,-1)$ unchanged. More generally, the contribution of an internally
disjoint path depends on its length only modulo four.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Equations~\eqref{cb:eq:transfer}--\eqref{cb:eq:fourmatrices} give
$M_{\ell+4}=M_\ell$. Replace the corresponding factor in the three-state
expansion; all endpoint interactions with the rest of the graph are retained.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Compressed kernels}
A connected graph of minimum degree at least two is either a cycle or,
after suppressing its degree-two vertices, a connected multigraph $K$
of minimum degree at least three. Multiple edges and loops are allowed
in $K$. A loop represents a cycle returning to its branch vertex; in the
simple expansion it must have length at least three. Each kernel edge
$e=uv$ is assigned a path length $\ell_e$.

If $b=\beta(K)\ge2$, the kernel has
\begin{equation}\label{cb:eq:kernelsize}
 |V(K)|\le2b-2,\qquad |E(K)|=|V(K)|+b-1\le3b-3.
\end{equation}
Indeed, the degree sum is at least three times the vertex count, whereas
$2|E|=2|V|+2b-2$.

The exact evaluation of an expansion is
\begin{equation}\label{cb:eq:kernel}
 \mathcal Z_K(\boldsymbol\ell)=
 \sum_{\sigma:V(K)\to\{0,1,2\}}
 (-1)^{|\{v:\sigma(v)\ne0\}|}
 \prod_{e=uv\in E(K)}(M_{\ell_e})_{\sigma(u),\sigma(v)}.
\end{equation}
For a loop use the corresponding diagonal matrix entry. Equation~\eqref{cb:eq:kernel}
is valid because each branch-vertex weight is counted once and internal
path vertices are disjoint.

Every assignment of residues in $\{1,2,3,4\}$ is realizable by a simple
expansion. Replace a loop of length one or two by length five or six.
For parallel edges with residue one, keep at most one direct edge and
use length five for the others. Distinct paths receive disjoint internal
vertices. These changes preserve all residues and the exact evaluation.
Thus a residue enumeration gives the exact image, not merely an upper bound
containing unrealizable cases.

Cycles themselves satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{cb:eq:cyclevalue}
 D(C_q,-1)=\tr((WM_1)^q)=
 \begin{cases}3,&q\equiv0\pmod4,\\-1,&q\not\equiv0\pmod4,\end{cases}
 \qquad q\ge3.
\end{equation}

\section{The exact two-cycle spectrum: a conventional proof}\label{cb:sec:bicyclic}

Define the cumulative absolute spectrum
\[
 \mathcal A_b=\{|D(G,-1)|:\ G\text{ finite simple},\ \beta(G)\le b\}.
\]

\begin{theorem}[Bicyclic spectrum]\label{cb:thm:bicyclic}
One has
\begin{equation}\label{cb:eq:bicyclic}
 \mathcal A_0=\{1\},\qquad \mathcal A_1=\{1,3\},\qquad
 \mathcal A_2=\{1,3,7,9\}.
\end{equation}
Every value in these sets is realized by a connected graph within the
stated cycle budget, and either sign can be realized.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Forests contribute absolute value one. By pruning, a graph with cycle rank
at most one reduces to cycles and forests, with at most one cycle. This
gives the first two assertions by \eqref{cb:eq:cyclevalue}.

For a connected minimum-degree-two component of cycle rank two, the
kernel bound gives at most two branch vertices. The possible kernels are
exactly a figure-eight, a dumbbell, and a theta graph: respectively two
loops at one vertex; one loop at each endpoint of a connecting edge; and
three parallel edges. We compute their exact ranges next.

For a figure-eight of cycle lengths $a,b$, write $d_a=\diag(M_a)$ as a
column vector. Then its evaluation is $d_a^TWd_b$. The distinct possible
vectors $d_a$ are
\[
 t=(1,1,1)^T,\qquad s=(-1,0,0)^T,\qquad r=(1,-1,-1)^T.
\]
Their pairings give only $-1,1,3$.

For a dumbbell with cycle lengths $a,b$ and connecting-path length $\ell$,
its evaluation is $(Wd_a)^TM_\ell(Wd_b)$. The possible vectors $Wd_a$
are $r,s,t$. In this order, the complete pairing matrices for
$\ell\equiv1,2,3,0\pmod4$ are
\begin{equation}\label{cb:eq:dumbbelltable}
\begin{gathered}
 \begin{pmatrix}-1&1&-1\\1&1&-3\\-1&-3&7\end{pmatrix},\quad
 \begin{pmatrix}1&1&-3\\1&-1&1\\-3&1&1\end{pmatrix},\\[2mm]
 \begin{pmatrix}1&-1&1\\-1&-1&3\\1&3&-7\end{pmatrix},\quad
 \begin{pmatrix}-1&-1&3\\-1&1&-1\\3&-1&-1\end{pmatrix}.
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
Their entries have absolute values $1,3,7$.

For a theta graph with path lengths $a,b,c$, write
\[
 M_j=\begin{pmatrix}p_j&q_j&q_j\\q_j&r_j&s_j\\q_j&s_j&r_j\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Its evaluation is
\begin{equation}\label{cb:eq:theta}
 p_ap_bp_c-4q_aq_bq_c+2r_ar_br_c+2s_as_bs_c.
\end{equation}
The quadruples $(p_j,q_j,r_j,s_j)$ for residues $j=1,2,3,4$ are
\[
 (1,1,1,0),\quad(-1,0,0,1),\quad(-1,-1,0,-1),\quad(1,0,-1,0).
\]
The $q$ product is nonzero only when all residues lie in $\{1,3\}$;
the $r$ product only for $\{1,4\}$; and the $s$ product only for
$\{2,3\}$. Two products can be nonzero together only when all residues
are one or all are three. In these two cases the values are $-1$ and $1$.
If only the $q$ product is nonzero, the value is $\pm3$; if only the $r$
or $s$ product is nonzero, the value is in $\{-3,-1,1,3\}$; and if none
is nonzero, the value is $\pm1$. Thus theta graphs add no other absolute
value.

Finally, a disconnected reduced graph of total cycle rank two may have
two cycle components, adding the product $3\cdot3=9$ and no other value.
Tree pruning proves that this exhausts arbitrary graphs, not just bare
cores. Values $1$ and $3$ are supplied by a vertex and $C_4$. Two copies
of $C_4$ joined by a direct edge have value $7$, by the first matrix in
\eqref{cb:eq:dumbbelltable}; their leaf-buffered join has value $-9$.
Corollary~\ref{cb:cor:sign} supplies the opposite signs without adding cycles.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[A two-cycle root exclusion]\label{cb:cor:no6}
If $\beta(G)\le2$, every nonzero rational domination root lies in
\[
 \{-2,-4,-8,-10\}.
\]
In particular $D(G,-6)\ne0$ for every such graph.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
A root $-k$ requires $k-1$ to divide one of $1,3,7,9$. Their positive
divisors are exactly $1,3,7,9$.
\end{proof}

The candidate set is not an attainment statement: this proof does not
construct a root $-8$ or $-10$. It does show that searching for a root
$-6$ within any family of cycle rank two is futile, even with arbitrary
numbers of leaves, arbitrary tree shapes, and arbitrarily long subdivisions.
The obstruction does not depend on the special zero-$C$ condition discussed
later.

\section{The exact three-cycle spectrum and its finite certificate}\label{cb:sec:tricyclic}

\begin{theorem}[Tricyclic spectrum; exact finite certificate]\label{cb:thm:tricyclic}
For graphs of cycle rank at most three,
\begin{equation}\label{cb:eq:tricyclic}
 \mathcal A_3=\{1,3,5,7,9,11,15,17,21,27\}.
\end{equation}
Each value occurs in a connected graph, and either sign occurs without
increasing its cycle rank.
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Exhaustion of kernels}
A minimum-degree-three connected multigraph with cycle rank three has
$1\le v\le4$ vertices and $e=v+2$ edges. The following finite enumeration
is exhaustive. For each $v$, list multisets of $e$ unordered endpoint
pairs from $\{0,\ldots,v-1\}$, allowing repeated pairs and loops. Keep
exactly the connected ones in which every degree, with loops counted twice,
is at least three. Identify two multisets only if a vertex permutation
maps one to the other. This yields respectively $1,4,5,5$ kernels on
$1,2,3,4$ vertices, hence fifteen in total.

For each kernel evaluate \eqref{cb:eq:kernel} for every residue assignment.
The number of evaluations is
\[
 1\cdot4^3+4\cdot4^4+5\cdot4^5+5\cdot4^6=26\,688.
\]
No hypothesis about bounded graph order is involved: a path may be
arbitrarily long and still has one of the four transfer matrices.

\subsection{Proof from the certificate}
\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{cb:thm:tricyclic}]
Apply tree pruning. A remaining component is a cycle, a two-cycle kernel
expansion, or a three-cycle kernel expansion. Table~\ref{cb:tab:kernels}
gives the exact absolute image of the last type by the enumeration above
and formula~\eqref{cb:eq:kernel}. The union of its rows is
\[
 \{1,3,5,7,9,11,15,17\}.
\]
Components of cycle ranks two and one may contribute a product. The
only additional value is $7\cdot3=21$; three cycle components additionally
supply $3^3=27$. The value $9$ was already present. These are all partitions
of a total cycle budget at most three, so no other absolute evaluation is
possible.

Every residue assignment in the table is realizable by a simple expansion,
as proved after \eqref{cb:eq:kernel}. Explicit witnesses for every value are
listed in Table~\ref{cb:tab:witnesses} and the accompanying JSON data. Products
are made connected by Lemma~\ref{cb:lem:join}, which preserves the additive
cycle budget. Sign reversal follows from Corollary~\ref{cb:cor:sign}.
\end{proof}

The finite part of this proof is the exact arithmetic table, not a
statistical inference. The program independently regenerates the kernels
rather than reading an asserted catalogue. It tests every vertex permutation
on at most four vertices, so no graph-isomorphism package is trusted.
The proof still has the usual limitations of a computer-assisted argument:
its conventional reductions and executable certificate require review, and
no proof assistant has checked their correspondence.

\begin{corollary}[Three-cycle rational-root restrictions]\label{cb:cor:triroots}
If $\beta(G)\le3$, every nonzero rational domination root is among
\[
 -2,-4,-6,-8,-10,-12,-16,-18,-22,-28.
\]
Thus roots $-14,-20,-24,-26$ each require at least four independent cycles.
A root $-6$ of multiplicity at least two also requires at least four.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
List the positive divisors of the integers in \eqref{cb:eq:tricyclic}, then
add one and negate. None of those integers is divisible by $25$, which
proves the multiplicity assertion.
\end{proof}

\section{Exact cycle complexity of small alternating evaluations}

The same classification resolves a different extremal question outright.
For a positive odd integer $a$, define
\[
 \mu(a)=\min\{\beta(G):G\text{ connected and }|D(G,-1)|=a\}.

Existence of connected realizations for every odd evaluation was established
by Alikhani \cite{AlikhaniMinusOne}; here only the explicit witnesses below
are needed. Theorem~\ref{cb:thm:budget} gives
$\mu(a)\ge\lceil\log_3 a\rceil$.

\[
 \mu(5)=3,\qquad \mu(7)=\mu(9)=2,\qquad

\]
For $a=13,19,23,25$, one has $\mu(a)\ge4$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Compare the cumulative spectra $\mathcal A_0,\ldots,\mathcal A_3$ and use
the connected witnesses. For powers of three, the universal lower bound
and the sharpness construction match for every $b$.
\end{proof}

\section{Why Part~I's four-cycle search cannot find another root}\label{cb:sec:obstruction}

\subsection{The architecture and its zero-\texorpdfstring{$C$}{C} condition}
Part~I's successful search (Section~\ref{sec:search}) starts with a four-cycle. It adds a
vertex $h$ adjacent to two consecutive cycle vertices and gives $h$ at
least one pendant leaf. It then attaches rooted trees by a single edge
from their roots to specified cycle vertices. At the proposed root
$x=-k$, the admitted trees satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{cb:eq:zeroC}
 C_T(-k)=0,
\end{equation}
where $C_T(x)$ is the generating polynomial for selections that dominate
all vertices of $T$ except its root, which is absent and undominated.
The search may combine several such trees into forests at each site.
Its original four-cycle has one undecorated corner, but the theorem below
does not require that restriction.

\begin{theorem}[Zero-$C$ four-cycle obstruction]\label{cb:thm:searchobstruction}
Let $k\ge3$ be an integer and construct $G$ by the architecture just
described, with any finite number of attached rooted trees satisfying
\eqref{cb:eq:zeroC}. Then
\[
 |D(G,-1)|=3.
\]
If $D(G,-k)=0$, necessarily $k=4$, and that root is simple.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The polynomial $C_T$ has integer coefficients, so
\[
 C_T(-1)\equiv C_T(-k)=0\pmod{k-1}.
\]
By Lemma~\ref{cb:lem:ordinary}, $C_T(-1)\in\{-1,0,1\}$. Since $k-1\ge2$,
we must have $C_T(-1)=0$, so the signature of $T$ is
$\epsilon(0,1,0)$. Such an attached tree has root absent and already
dominated; it contributes only its sign, and neither requires nor supplies
domination across the attachment edge. Removing all these trees thus
changes $D(-1)$ by a sign only.

At the hub $h$, the presence of one or more leaves gives rooted-star
signature $(0,(-1)^q,0)$, where $q\ge1$ is the number of leaves. Therefore
$h$ is absent and already dominated after cancellation; delete it, again
retaining only a sign. What remains is precisely $C_4$, with evaluation
three. Thus $|D(G,-1)|=3$.

A root $-k$ now requires $k-1\mid3$. Because $k\ge3$, the only possibility
is $k=4$. A multiple root $-4$ would require $9\mid D(G,-1)$, which is
impossible. Hence the root is simple.
\end{proof}

The zero-$C$ qualification is part of the theorem. It is not legitimate
to conclude that every graph obtained by arbitrary decorations of the
same core has $|D(-1)|=3$. Without that qualification the broader
bicyclic theorem still excludes $-6$, but it allows the necessary
congruences for $-8$ and $-10$.

\subsection{The 30-vertex example revisited}\label{cb:sec:g30}
For the exact edge list \eqref{eq:edges} of Part~I, the feedback-edge algorithm gives
\[
 D(G_{30},-1)=-3.
\]
Its nine case vectors contribute three copies of $-1$ and six zeros.
Consequently the already established root $-4$ is automatically simple:
there is no need to recompute the derivative or its large residual factor
for this particular conclusion. The existence of the root itself remains
Part~I's polynomial certificate (Section~\ref{sec:polynomial}), not a
consequence of the value $-3$.

A prospective search for $-6$ must therefore leave cycle rank two altogether.
A search for $-8$ can in principle retain two cycles, but it must escape
the zero-$C$ four-cycle architecture or otherwise change the invariant
from absolute value three to seven. This is a precise distinction between
an obstruction to a \emph{graph class} and an obstruction to a
\emph{parameterized search design}.

\subsection{Trust boundaries}
The sharp bound, arithmetic deductions, and two-cycle classification have
full proofs in Part~II. The three-cycle theorem additionally depends
on the finite table. The bundled computation is exact and reproducible,
but has not been translated into a proof assistant. No unrefereed theorem
elsewhere in ProveIt is needed for these new structural proofs. Part~I's
root-$-4$ construction is used only as a motivation and an
application to a specific known example.

The literature review confirms the relevant problem statements and standard
background, but is not exhaustive. Some publisher material is accessible
only as an abstract. We therefore make no claim that every lemma or the
$3^\beta$ inequality is absent from all prior literature. The primary
research contribution presented for review is the complete proof package,
the exact low-cycle classification, the simultaneous root-budget
consequences, and their application to the pinned search architecture.

\section{Further research questions and concrete next steps}\label{cb:sec:questions}

\subsection{The first admissible cycle rank for a root \texorpdfstring{$-6$}{minus six}}
\begin{question}\label{cb:q:minus6}
Does there exist a connected graph $G$ with $\beta(G)=3$ and $D(G,-6)=0$?
\end{question}
The obstruction at $-1$ allows this: kernel 7 already has a simple
expansion with evaluation five. A focused search should begin with the
kernel rows admitting values divisible by five, namely rows 3, 5, 7,
10, 12, 13, and 14 of Table~\ref{cb:tab:kernels}. Values $\pm5$ and $\pm15$
are the only three-cycle evaluations compatible with the root. This
restriction is necessary for the reduced graph after pruning, not a
license to drop arbitrary decorations from an evaluation at $-6$.

A useful approach is to work with exact rooted signatures at $-6$ while
simultaneously maintaining their four-state signatures at $-1$. The latter
provide a cheap rejection certificate; the former retain the root equation.
The eight-vertex $X_5$ is a starting core, not a solution.

\subsection{The remaining bicyclic root candidates}
\begin{question}\label{cb:q:bicyclic}
Can $-8$ or $-10$ be a domination root of a graph with cycle rank two?
\end{question}
Such a graph must have $D(-1)=\pm7$ or $\pm9$, respectively. For $-8$,
the nontrivial reduced component must come from the dumbbell range. For
$-10$, the terminal reduction must supply two factors of absolute value
three, hence two cycle components; no connected bare bicyclic core has
absolute value nine. This specifies the type of cancellation that a
successful tree decoration must preserve.

\subsection{Multiplicity as an independent structural problem}
\begin{question}\label{cb:q:mult}
What is the minimum cycle rank for a root $-4$ of multiplicity $r$?
In particular, can multiplicity two occur at cycle rank two?
\end{question}
The bound is at least $r$, and the case $r=1$ has minimum two by
Part~I's example and the published unicyclic exclusion
(equation~\eqref{eq:mincycle}). For $r=2$ and
cycle rank two, the evaluation must be $\pm9$; for $r=3$ and cycle rank
three it must be $\pm27$. These necessary conditions rule out the
zero-$C$ architecture completely, since it forces simplicity. Part~I's
arbitrary-multiplicity constructions (Theorem~\ref{thm:mult}) can be compared with
these lower bounds rather than being mistaken for optimal cycle budgets.

\subsection{Higher spectra and an extremal evaluation function}
\begin{question}\label{cb:q:spectra}
Determine $\mathcal A_4$, and characterize the function $\mu(a)$ for odd
positive integers $a$.
\end{question}
The same method is finite for each fixed budget: kernels have at most
$2b-2$ vertices and $3b-3$ edges, and every edge has four residue states.
Kernel automorphisms and repeated transfer tensors can reduce enumeration.
A certified calculation for $b=4$ would settle whether the next missing
odd values $13,19,23,25$ have cycle complexity four and would sharpen
root exclusions beyond $-14$.

The identity $\mu(3^b)=b$ gives one exact infinite subsequence. The value
$\mu(5)=3>\lceil\log_3 5\rceil$ shows that the logarithmic lower bound
is not always tight. Is the excess over that bound uniformly bounded,
or can arithmetic gaps force arbitrarily large excess?

\subsection{Equality and stability in the cycle budget}
\begin{question}\label{cb:q:equality}
Which connected graphs attain $|D(G,-1)|=3^{\beta(G)}$? What structural
restrictions follow from being close to this maximum?
\end{question}
For a chosen spanning forest, equality means that all $3^\beta$ case
vectors are nonzero and have the same sign. This is an exact certificate
criterion, but not yet an intrinsic graph classification. A more useful
answer would characterize the reduced cores and permitted decorations.
The subcubic chain construction supplies extremizers at every budget;
it need not exhaust them.

\subsection{A practical formalization route}
A Lean development can separate three layers. First formalize dominating
sets, the three-way feedback-edge partition, and the thirteen-state
forest invariant. Second formalize the small integer matrices and the
period-four identity, so that path suppression has a precise semantic
lemma. Third reflect the bounded kernel enumeration and its integer sums
into certified finite computations. This would turn the finite table into
a kernel-checked theorem instead of merely porting the Python code.

The first target should be the universal budget and the purely mathematical
bicyclic exclusion; neither needs a large computed certificate. The exact
tricyclic table is a natural subsequent reflection exercise. This is a
proposed development plan, not a statement that any such formalization
has already been executed.

No Lean or Rocq development in ProveIt defines dominating sets or the
domination polynomial, so neither part of this report has a formal
counterpart. Placement of this report in the repository confers no formal
status on any of its statements.
\end{writenote}

\section{Conclusion of Part~II}
An unsuccessful numerical search became informative only after identifying
what its cancellations preserve. At $x=-1$, arbitrary constrained forests
collapse to a thirteen-element signed state set. Every feedback edge then
costs at most a factor of three, yielding a sharp cycle budget and a
simultaneous restriction on integer roots and their multiplicities.

Tree pruning and a period-four transfer identity make the invariant fully
classifiable at low cycle rank. They exclude $-6$ from all graphs with at
most two independent cycles, exclude $-14$ from all graphs with at most
three, and determine several exact minima for alternating evaluations.
They also explain why Part~I's zero-$C$ four-cycle architecture
is intrinsically a simple-root-$-4$ mechanism, not a general route to new
integer roots. These conclusions leave well-defined construction problems
at the first cycle ranks where the obstruction permits them.

\section{Compact independent formula for the kernel table}\label{cb:app:kernel}

The following pseudocode is sufficient to reproduce every row once the
kernel's endpoint list is given. It uses only the four matrices printed
in \eqref{cb:eq:matrixbase} and \eqref{cb:eq:fourmatrices}.
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
def kernel_value(vertices, edges, lengths):
    answer = 0
    for state in product(range(3), repeat=vertices):
        term = (-1) ** sum(s != 0 for s in state)
        for (u, v), length in zip(edges, lengths):
            term *= M[1 + (length - 1) % 4][state[u]][state[v]]
        answer += term
    return answer

values = {
    abs(kernel_value(vertices, edges, lengths))
    for lengths in product(range(1, 5), repeat=len(edges))
}
\end{lstlisting}
A loop $u=v$ requires no exceptional code. The interpretation of the
compressed kernel is a subdivision with a genuine simple cycle, not a
looped graph's domination polynomial.
