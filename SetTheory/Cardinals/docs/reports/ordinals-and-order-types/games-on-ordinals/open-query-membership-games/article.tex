\begin{abstract}
We answer negatively Problem 1.3 of Chiozini, Csern\'ak, and Soukup's
\emph{Gamification of the $T_0$-pseudoweight via cut-and-choose games on
topological spaces}, in the version available as arXiv:2510.05754v3.
The counterexample consists of two convergent sequences, so it lies entirely
within compact metrizable ordinal spaces.

The main further result is an exact formula for every nonempty Hausdorff space
of finite Cantor--Bendixson height. If $X^{(h)}\ne\varnothing$ and
$X^{(h+1)}=\varnothing$, then
\[
 \sm(X)=
 \begin{cases}
  \ceil{\log_2(h+1)},& |X^{(h)}|=1,\\
  \ceil{\log_2(h+2)},& |X^{(h)}|\geq2.
 \end{cases}
\]
The proof identifies finite open-query complexity with the logarithm of the
minimum length of an alternating closed-layer decomposition. A canonical
alternating-closure procedure computes that length and retains the orientation
information lost by the scalar invariant $\sm$.

Consequences include sharp one-move failures of the proposed sum identity at
every positive finite level, exact finite-product formulae, a classification on
countable scattered Hausdorff spaces, and a quadratic-time target algorithm for
finite posets. Independent minimax computations verify the finite normal form
on all naturally labelled posets through six elements and all their targets:
320,866 tests in total.

\emph{Addition of 29 September 2026 (batch 52).} Part~II, from a later
manuscript, lets the Seeker determine one of $q\ge2$ labels instead of a
membership bit. Optimal depth is the ceiling of the binary logarithm of a
minimum number of ordered open-suffix layers; on posets this number is a shortest common supersequence of
chain words. Components with at most $m$ layers need at most $(q-1)m+1$ jointly,
so combining them can cost exactly $\ceil{\log_2q}$ extra questions, sharply,
already for fixed colorings of compact countable metrizable spaces. A space of
last derivative rank $h$ with $s$ colors on its last derivative needs at most
$(q-1)h+s$ layers, sharply; the worst case over $q$-colorings of $t$ convergent
towers of rank $m-1$ is $\ceil{\log_2((q-1)(m-1)+\min(q,t))}$, which at $q=2$
agrees with the formula above for $h=m-1$. Exact optimization is polynomial for two labels and
NP-complete for three, with a polynomial algorithm within
$\ceil{\log_2(q-1)}$ questions of optimal.
\end{abstract}

\section{The selected problem and the status of the answer}

In the set-membership game, questions are restricted to open subsets of a space,
and the aim is to decide membership in a fixed target subset. The invariant
$\sm(X)$ is the worst, over all targets, of the least ordinal number of questions
needed. Chiozini, Csern\'ak, and Soukup ask whether
\begin{equation}\label{eq:question}
 \sm\!\left(\bigsqsum_{i\in I}X_i\right)
 \stackrel{?}{=}\sup_{i\in I}\sm(X_i).
\end{equation}
This is their Problem 1.3; their Theorem 3.4 gives the upper bound consisting of
the right-hand side followed by one additional move \cite{CCS}.

The answer to \eqref{eq:question} is \emph{no}. The proof in the next section is
short and does not depend on any later theorem. In particular, the counterexample
is not an artifact of non-Hausdorff topology, uncountable spaces, or an ambiguity
about transfinite play. It uses a two-move game on a countable compact metrizable
space. Repeating the same component countably many times also gives a
counterexample when the index set is required to be infinite.

The more substantial development is the classification in
\cref{thm:height}. Its mechanism explains exactly why the counterexample works.
Different components may require opposite interpretations of the same
open-set answer. A scalar question count forgets that orientation, while a pair
of alternating-closure lengths preserves it.

\section{A compact ordinal counterexample}\label{sec:small}

Let $S=[0,\omega]$ have its order topology. Thus every natural number is isolated,
and the neighborhoods of $\omega$ contain a final segment of $\N$.
We use zero questions for a constant target: a subset equal to the whole space
or to the empty set.

\begin{lemma}[The one-question criterion]\label{lem:one}
For a subset $A$ of any topological space $X$, membership in $A$ can be decided
with at most one open-set question if and only if $A$ is open or closed.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
An open target is itself a legal question. A closed target can be tested by
asking its open complement. Conversely, suppose the first question is $U$.
The only two possible final states are $U$ and $X\setminus U$. Both must be
monochromatic for membership in $A$. If they have the same color, $A$ is
constant. Otherwise $A=U$ or $A=X\setminus U$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:small}
For $S=[0,\omega]$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:small}
 \sm(S)=1,\qquad \sm(S\sqcup S)=2.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Every subset of $S$ missing $\omega$ is open, since it consists entirely of
isolated points. Every subset containing $\omega$ is closed, since its complement
consists of isolated points. Hence every target takes at most one question.
There are nonconstant targets, so $\sm(S)=1$.

Write $S_0,S_1$ for two disjoint copies, with limit points $p_0,p_1$, and put
\[
 A=\{p_0\}\ \sqcup\ (S_1\setminus\{p_1\}).
\]
This set is not open: it contains $p_0$ but no isolated point of $S_0$.
It is not closed: it contains all isolated points of $S_1$, but omits their
limit $p_1$. By \cref{lem:one}, one question cannot suffice.

Two questions suffice for \emph{every} target $B$. First ask the clopen set
$S_0$, thereby determining which copy contains the remaining candidates.
Inside that copy, $B$ is open or closed, so one more open question decides
membership. An open set in a component is open in the disjoint union.
\end{proof}

The fact that spaces with at most one accumulation point have one-question
membership complexity is also noted in \cite[Section 7]{CCS}; the failure of
\eqref{eq:question} comes from putting two such components together.

\begin{corollary}
The proposed identity fails even when every component and the whole sum are
compact, countable, metrizable, zero-dimensional, and homeomorphic to ordinal
spaces. Specifically,
\[
 S\sqcup S\cong[0,\omega\cdot2],
 \qquad \sm([0,\omega\cdot2])=2.
\]
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The two clopen intervals $[0,\omega]$ and $(\omega,\omega\cdot2]$ partition
$[0,\omega\cdot2]$. Each has order type $\omega+1$. The stated topological
properties can also be seen directly from two disjoint convergent sequences.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[An infinite index set]
For every cardinal $\kappa\geq2$,
\[
 \sm\!\left(\bigsqsum_{i<\kappa}S\right)=2.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The lower bound follows by restricting to two components. For the upper bound,
fix a target $A$, and split the component indices into two classes so that $A_i$
is open on the first class and closed on the second; when both apply, choose
either class. One question asks the union of the components in the first class.
A second question asks the open set whose trace is $A_i$ on the first class and
$S_i\setminus A_i$ on the second. The two answers determine the color.
\end{proof}

\section{The game, subspaces, and ordinal conventions}

All ordinal intervals in this report carry their order topology. The notation
$[0,\gamma]$ denotes a space whose underlying ordered set has order type
$\gamma+1$. A topological sum $X\sqcup Y$ is not being confused with an ordinal
sum; when they are homeomorphic, this is stated separately.

\begin{definition}[Open-query membership game]
Fix $A\subseteq X$ and an ordinal $\beta$. At stage $\xi<\beta$, the Seeker
chooses an open set $U_\xi$. The Hider answers $e_\xi\in\{0,1\}$. Set
\[
 W_\eta=\bigcap_{\xi<\eta}
 \begin{cases}
  U_\xi,&e_\xi=1,\\
  X\setminus U_\xi,&e_\xi=0.
 \end{cases}
\]
The Seeker wins if $W_\beta\subseteq A$ or $W_\beta\cap A=\varnothing$.
Write $\sm(A,X)$ for the least winning length and
$\sm(X)=\sup_{A\subseteq X}\sm(A,X)$, as in \cite{CCS}.
\end{definition}

For ordinal-valued invariants we work with $T_0$ spaces, where a winning
length exists: asking all open sets separates points. A statement of the form
$\sm(A,X)\leq d$ can also be read on an arbitrary topological space as the
existence of a winning depth-$d$ strategy, whether or not a least ordinal
winning length exists. The finite normal form is valid in this broader sense.

An empty final state is winning. The Hider need not commit to a point in advance.
For a finite decision tree, however, the game formulation and correctness on
every fixed point are equivalent: a bad leaf contains two points of different
colors, while an empty leaf is harmless. This equivalence will be used carefully
in \cref{sec:normal}.

\begin{lemma}[Restriction to a subspace]\label{lem:subspace}
If $Z\subseteq X$ and $A\subseteq X$, then
\[
 \sm(A\cap Z,Z)\leq\sm(A,X),\qquad \sm(Z)\leq\sm(X).
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Restrict every open question in a strategy on $X$ to $Z$. It is open in the
subspace, and every resulting state is the old state intersected with $Z$.
For the second inequality, extend an arbitrary target of $Z$ to a target of $X$.
\end{proof}

Finite question counts are ordinary nonnegative integers. We use
$\ceil{\log_2 L}$ only for positive integers $L$; equivalently, it is the least
integer $d$ for which $L\leq2^d$. This avoids floating-point or ordinal-logarithm
conventions. In particular, $\ceil{\log_2 1}=0$.

\section{Finite decision trees and alternating layers}\label{sec:normal}

\begin{definition}[Alternating closed layers]
For a nonempty space $X$ and $A\subseteq X$, let $C_1=A$ and
$C_0=X\setminus A$. A closed-layer decomposition of length $L\geq1$ and
outer color $c\in\{0,1\}$ is a chain
\begin{equation}\label{eq:layerchain}
 X=F_0\supseteq F_1\supseteq\cdots\supseteq F_L=\varnothing
\end{equation}
of closed sets such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:layercolor}
 F_i\setminus F_{i+1}\subseteq C_{(c+i)\bmod2}
 \quad(0\leq i<L).
\end{equation}
Empty layers are allowed. Let $L(A,X)$ be the least such length, allowing either
outer color, and put $L(A,X)=\infty$ if no finite chain exists.
\end{definition}

These are finite alternating differences of closed sets, equivalently finite
Boolean combinations of open sets. This connects the game to the difference
hierarchy, but the exact decision-depth statement below is proved directly.

\begin{theorem}[Finite normal form]\label{thm:normal}
For every nonempty topological space $X$, target $A$, and integer $d\geq0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normal}
 \sm(A,X)\leq d
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad L(A,X)\leq2^d.
\end{equation}
Consequently, whenever $L(A,X)<\infty$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:logdepth}
 \sm(A,X)=\ceil{\log_2 L(A,X)}.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\subsection{From a query tree to an ordered collection of leaves}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:leaf}
Complete a finite open-query tree to depth $d$, padding terminal branches with
dummy questions. Number its leaves $0,1,\ldots,2^d-1$ in lexicographic order of
answer strings, with $0<1$. If $\ell(x)$ is the leaf reached by the answers of
$x$, then every upper level set
\[
 \{x:\ell(x)\geq j\}
\]
over the leaf order is open.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Proceed by induction on $d$. The case $d=0$ is immediate. Let the root question
be $U$, and put $M=2^{d-1}$. Consider the two subtrees as query trees defined on
all of $X$, regardless of which root answer would actually occur, and write
$\ell_0,\ell_1$ for their leaf functions. If $j\leq M$, then
\[
 \{\ell\geq j\}=U\cup\{\ell_0\geq j\};
\]
for $j=M$, the second set is empty. If $j>M$, then
\[
 \{\ell\geq j\}=U\cap\{\ell_1\geq j-M\}.
\]
The induction hypothesis and closure of the topology under finite unions and
intersections prove the claim.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of the forward implication of \cref{thm:normal}]
A winning depth-$d$ tree has a well-defined output color at each nonempty leaf.
Assign an arbitrary color to each empty leaf. Read the $2^d$ leaf colors from
the largest leaf down to the smallest, and group consecutive equal colors into
runs. There are at most $2^d$ runs, and successive run colors alternate.

After deleting the largest $i$ runs, the set of points still represented is a
lower level set of $\ell$. It is closed by \cref{lem:leaf}. These lower level
sets, starting with $X$ and ending with $\varnothing$, form the required chain.
A run can represent no points, which is why empty layers were allowed.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Balanced search among closed layers}

\begin{proof}[Proof of the reverse implication of \cref{thm:normal}]
Suppose \eqref{eq:layerchain}--\eqref{eq:layercolor} hold. Every point lies in a
unique layer $F_i\setminus F_{i+1}$. The open question $X\setminus F_j$ tests
whether $i<j$. Thus ordinary binary search can identify the layer index among
$L$ possibilities. Split the current interval of indices as evenly as possible
at each question. Its largest remaining size after $d$ questions is at most
$\ceil{L/2^d}$. When $L\leq2^d$, at most one layer remains, and that layer is
monochromatic. The same reasoning applies to arbitrary Hider answers: either
the state is empty or it is contained in a single layer.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Why the logarithm is genuine]
Sequentially peeling $L$ layers would spend roughly $L$ questions. Adaptivity
permits balanced search through nested open thresholds, reducing the count to
$\ceil{\log_2 L}$. Conversely, a depth-$d$ tree has at most $2^d$ ordered leaf
runs. Neither direction assumes that the space is ordered or Hausdorff.
\end{remark}

\begin{writenote}[$q$ labels]
\Cref{fo:thm:normal} in Part~II is the same normal form for a coloring by $q$
labels: the least number of ordered layers with open suffixes replaces
$L(A,X)$, and the depth is again its ceiling logarithm. At $q=2$ it is
\cref{thm:normal}, proved by a second route (\cref{fo:sec:crosswalk}).
\end{writenote}

\section{Canonical alternating closures and orientation}

The least layer length has a concrete canonical description. This is also the
right quantity for understanding disjoint unions.

\begin{definition}[Oriented closure sequence]\label{def:closure}
For $c\in\{0,1\}$ define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:greedy}
 F^c_0=X,\qquad
 F^c_{i+1}=\cl_X\bigl(F^c_i\cap C_{(c+i+1)\bmod2}\bigr).
\end{equation}
Let $N_c(A,X)$ be the first integer $L$ such that $F^c_L=\varnothing$, or
$\infty$ when no such integer exists. The pair $(N_0,N_1)$ is the
\emph{oriented closure profile} of the target.
\end{definition}

Every $F^c_i$ is closed and the sequence is decreasing. At step $i+1$, one keeps
the closure of points whose color would be wrong in the next layer. Therefore
\[
 F^c_i\setminus F^c_{i+1}\subseteq C_{(c+i)\bmod2}.
\]

\begin{theorem}[Canonical minimality]\label{thm:greedy}
For each outer color $c$, $N_c(A,X)$ is the least possible length of a
closed-layer decomposition with that color. In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:profileformula}
 L(A,X)=\min(N_0(A,X),N_1(A,X)).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
If the canonical sequence terminates, its layers are a decomposition of the
required length. Conversely, let $G_0,\ldots,G_L$ be any closed-layer chain
with outer color $c$. We show $F^c_i\subseteq G_i$ by induction.
The assertion is true at $i=0$. If it holds at $i$, then every wrong-colored
point of $F^c_i$ belongs to $G_{i+1}$, because $G_i\setminus G_{i+1}$ has the
prescribed color. Taking closures preserves containment in the closed set
$G_{i+1}$. Hence $F^c_{i+1}\subseteq G_{i+1}$. In particular $F^c_L=\varnothing$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Two elementary profile rules]\label{lem:profiles}
Complementing the target swaps $N_0$ and $N_1$. Also,
\[
 N_c\leq N_{1-c}+1
\]
whenever the latter is finite.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The first assertion follows directly from \eqref{eq:greedy}. For the second,
prepend an empty outer layer of color $c$ to a decomposition whose outer color
is $1-c$; in the chain this means repeating $X$ once at the beginning.
Apply \cref{thm:greedy}.
\end{proof}

Thus finite profile entries differ by at most one, but discarding the distinction
between them can change a question count at a power-of-two threshold.

\begin{proposition}[Exact finite profile of a sum]\label{prop:profile-sum}
Let $X=\bigsqsum_{i\in I}X_i$, $A_i=A\cap X_i$, and let $c\in\{0,1\}$.
For a nonempty family of nonempty components,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:profile-sum}
 N_c(A,X)=\sup_{i\in I}N_c(A_i,X_i),
\end{equation}
where the supremum is taken in $\N\cup\{\infty\}$: an unbounded set of finite
lengths has value $\infty$. Hence, whenever the resulting minimum is finite,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sumexact}
 \sm(A,X)=\ceil{\log_2\!\left(
   \min_{c\in\{0,1\}}\sup_i N_c(A_i,X_i)\right)}.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Closure in a disjoint union is computed componentwise. Inductively,
$F^c_n(A,X)\cap X_i=F^c_n(A_i,X_i)$ for every finite $n$. The global sequence
is empty exactly when every component sequence is empty. Now use
\cref{thm:normal,thm:greedy}.
\end{proof}

The obstruction to \eqref{eq:question} is already visible in the elementary
inequality
\[
 \min_c\sup_i N_{i,c}\ \geq\ \sup_i\min_c N_{i,c}.
\]
The two operations need not commute: each component may have its own preferred
outer color, whereas one global closed-layer chain has only one outer color.

\section{Exact classification at finite Cantor--Bendixson height}
\label{sec:height}

For a closed subspace $F$ of a Hausdorff space, let $F'$ be its set of
nonisolated points. Set $X^{(0)}=X$, $X^{(n+1)}=(X^{(n)})'$; at a limit ordinal,
use the intersection of the previous derivatives. Only finite derivatives are
needed for the principal formula.

\begin{definition}
A nonempty Hausdorff space has \emph{last derivative rank $h$} if
\[
 X^{(h)}\ne\varnothing,\qquad X^{(h+1)}=\varnothing.
\]
Its Cantor--Bendixson height is then $h+1$. The terminal derivative $X^{(h)}$
is discrete; it need not be finite unless the space is compact.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Finite-height classification]\label{thm:height}
Let $X$ be a nonempty Hausdorff space with last derivative rank $h<\omega$.
Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:height}
 \boxed{\quad
 \sm(X)=
 \begin{cases}
  \ceil{\log_2(h+1)},& |X^{(h)}|=1,\\[2pt]
  \ceil{\log_2(h+2)},& |X^{(h)}|\geq2.
 \end{cases}\quad}
\end{equation}
More precisely, the maximum of $L(A,X)$ over all targets is $h+1$ in the first
case and $h+2$ in the second. The maxima are attained.
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Why canonical closures lose one derivative at each step}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:isolation}
If $F=\cl_X B$ and $x$ is isolated in $F$, then $x\in B$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
An open neighborhood $U$ with $U\cap F=\{x\}$ must meet $B$, because
$x\in\cl_X B$. Thus $x\in B$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Derivative descent]\label{lem:descent}
For every target and outer color, the canonical sequence satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:descent}
 F^c_{n+1}\subseteq(F^c_n)'\quad(n\geq1),
 \qquad F^c_n\subseteq X^{(n-1)}\quad(n\geq1).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
By \cref{lem:isolation}, every isolated point of $F^c_n$ has color
$(c+n)\bmod2$. Therefore the opposite-colored points whose closure defines
$F^c_{n+1}$ all lie in $(F^c_n)'$. This derivative is closed in $X$, so their
closure lies there too. Derivation is monotone on closed sets, giving the
second assertion by induction from $F^c_1\subseteq X$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Upper bounds in \cref{thm:height}]
For every target and orientation, \cref{lem:descent} gives
$F^c_{h+2}\subseteq X^{(h+1)}=\varnothing$. Hence $L(A,X)\leq h+2$.

Now assume $X^{(h)}=\{p\}$, and write $d$ for the target color of $p$.
Choose $c$ so that $(c+h)\bmod2=d$. We have $F^c_{h+1}\subseteq\{p\}$.
If this set were nonempty, $p$ would be isolated in it and, by
\cref{lem:isolation}, would have color $(c+h+1)\bmod2=1-d$, a contradiction.
Thus $F^c_{h+1}=\varnothing$ and $L(A,X)\leq h+1$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Alternating rank layers give matching lower bounds}

Write
\[
 D_j=X^{(j)}\setminus X^{(j+1)}\qquad(0\leq j\leq h).
\]
These sets partition $X$. The set $D_j$ is dense in $X^{(j)}$. Here are details
that do not assume compactness: a finite-height space is scattered, because any
nonempty subspace contains a point of least ambient rank, and that point is
isolated in the subspace after restricting an isolating neighborhood at its rank.
Every nonempty open subset of a scattered space contains a point isolated in
that space. Apply this to $X^{(j)}$.

\begin{lemma}[Parity target]\label{lem:parity}
Color every point of $D_j$ by $(b+j)\bmod2$, where $b\in\{0,1\}$, and let
$A_b$ be the color-$1$ set. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:parityprofiles}
 N_b(A_b,X)=h+1,\qquad N_{1-b}(A_b,X)=h+2.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For $h=0$, the target is constant of color $b$, and the two lengths are
$1$ and $2$, as asserted. Assume $h\geq1$.
With outer color $b$, the first closure is $X'$, because the opposite color
contains $D_1$, which is dense in $X'$, and is contained in $X'$.
Repeating the argument shows $F^b_n=X^{(n)}$ for $0\leq n\leq h+1$.
Thus the first empty term has index $h+1$.

With outer color $1-b$, the first closure is $X$, since it includes the dense
rank-zero layer $D_0$. Subsequent closures give
$F^{1-b}_n=X^{(n-1)}$ for $1\leq n\leq h+2$. The first empty term now has
index $h+2$. The same computation covers $h=0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Restriction of profiles]\label{lem:profile-restrict}
For any subspace $Z\subseteq X$ and either color $c$,
\[
 N_c(A\cap Z,Z)\leq N_c(A,X).
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The closure identity
$\cl_Z(B\cap Z)\subseteq Z\cap\cl_X B$ gives, inductively,
$F^c_n(A\cap Z,Z)\subseteq Z\cap F^c_n(A,X)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Lower bounds in \cref{thm:height}]
The parity target of \cref{lem:parity} always has $L=h+1$, proving the required
lower bound when the terminal derivative is a singleton.

Suppose instead that $p\ne q$ lie in $X^{(h)}$. Take disjoint open neighborhoods
$U,V$ of $p,q$. Derivatives commute with restriction to an open subspace:
$U^{(j)}=U\cap X^{(j)}$, and similarly for $V$. In particular both $U$ and $V$
have last derivative rank $h$.

On $U$, choose the parity target with bottom color $0$. On $V$, choose the parity
target with bottom color $1$. Define the target arbitrarily outside $U\cup V$.
By \cref{lem:parity}, orientation $0$ requires $h+2$ layers on $V$, while
orientation $1$ requires $h+2$ layers on $U$. By
\cref{lem:profile-restrict}, both global orientations require at least $h+2$
layers. The upper bound already proved gives equality. Applying
\cref{thm:normal} finishes the proof.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[What information survives]
At finite height, the invariant sees the last derivative rank and whether the
terminal derivative has one point or more than one. It sees neither the exact
larger cardinality of that derivative nor the cardinalities of lower levels.
Compactness and metrizability are not needed for \cref{thm:height}.
\end{remark}

\section{Exact values on ordinal intervals}\label{sec:ordinals}

For $h,m\geq1$ finite, put
\[
 X_{h,m}=[0,\omega^h\cdot m].
\]
These are countable compact ordinal spaces. Standard background on ordinal
spaces and Cantor--Bendixson characteristics is discussed in \cite{AM}; the
specific derivative calculation needed here is elementary.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:ordinal-rank}
For $1\leq j\leq h$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ordinal-derivative}
 X_{h,m}^{(j)}=
 \{\omega^j\eta:0<\eta\leq\omega^{h-j}\cdot m\}.
\end{equation}
In particular,
\[
 X_{h,m}^{(h)}=\{\omega^h,\omega^h\cdot2,\ldots,\omega^h\cdot m\},
 \qquad X_{h,m}^{(h+1)}=\varnothing.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The nonisolated points of an ordinal interval are exactly its nonzero limit
ordinals. Below $\omega^h\cdot m$, these are precisely the positive multiples
of $\omega$. This gives the formula for $j=1$.

Within the ordered set of positive multiples of $\omega^j$, a point with
successor coefficient is isolated, including the first coefficient. A point
with limit coefficient is a limit of smaller such multiples. The nonzero limit
coefficients are the multiples of $\omega$. Therefore the next derivative
consists exactly of the positive multiples of $\omega^{j+1}$ in the indicated
range. This proves the induction. At step $h$ the remaining finite set is
discrete, so the next derivative is empty.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Finite-exponent ordinal formula]\label{cor:ordinal}
For positive finite $h,m$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ordinal-sm}
 \sm([0,\omega^h\cdot m])=
 \begin{cases}
  \ceil{\log_2(h+1)},&m=1,\\
  \ceil{\log_2(h+2)},&m\geq2.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

\toprule

\midrule
0 & 0 & 1 & yes\\
1 & 1 & 2 & yes\\
2 & 2 & 2 & no\\
3 & 2 & 3 & yes\\
4 & 3 & 3 & no\\
5 & 3 & 3 & no\\

8 & 4 & 4 & no\\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The row $h=0$ refers to arbitrary discrete spaces, not the notation
$[0,\omega^0\cdot m]$. A singleton has value $0$; any larger discrete space has
value $1$.

\paragraph{Explicit hard targets.}
On $X_{h,1}$, color a point according to the parity of its Cantor--Bendixson
rank. In Cantor normal form, the rank of a nonzero point is its last nonzero
exponent; the point $0$ has rank $0$. On two copies, reverse all colors in one
copy. The canonical closure lengths are then $h+2$ in both orientations.
This is an explicit witness, not merely an existence argument using the
supremum over targets.

\paragraph{Why two copies form another ordinal space.}
For $h\geq1$, the intervals
\[
 [0,\omega^h],\qquad (\omega^h,\omega^h\cdot2]
\]
are clopen in $[0,\omega^h\cdot2]$ and are each homeomorphic to
$[0,\omega^h]$. The second interval has the same order type because removing
the first point from an infinite indecomposable ordinal $\omega^h$ does not
change that order type. Thus
\[
 X_{h,1}\sqcup X_{h,1}\cong X_{h,2}.
\]

\section{Sharp failures at every finite level, and sums more generally}

\begin{theorem}[Sharpness at every positive finite level]\label{thm:sharp}
For every integer $k\geq1$, let
\[
 K_k=[0,\omega^{\,2^k-1}].
\]
Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sharp}
 \sm(K_k)=k,\qquad
 \sm\!\left(\bigsqsum_{i<\kappa}K_k\right)=k+1
 \quad\text{for every cardinal }\kappa\geq2.
\end{equation}
Thus the one-extra-move upper bound for sums is attained at every positive
finite level, including by sums of two compact metrizable ordinal spaces.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Put $h=2^k-1$. Derivatives of a disjoint union are computed componentwise.
Each component has last rank $h$ and one terminal point; a sum of at least two
has the same last rank and at least two terminal points. By
\cref{thm:height}, the respective values are
\[
 \ceil{\log_2(2^k)}=k,\qquad
 \ceil{\log_2(2^k+1)}=k+1.
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Exact duplication threshold]\label{cor:threshold}
If $X$ is Hausdorff of last finite derivative rank $h$ and
$|X^{(h)}|=1$, then
\[
 \sm(X\sqcup X)>
 \sm(X)
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad h+1\text{ is a power of }2.
\]
In that case the increase is exactly one.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The comparison is between $\ceil{\log_2(h+1)}$ and
$\ceil{\log_2(h+2)}$. Consecutive positive integers cross a ceiling-logarithm
threshold exactly when the smaller is a power of two.
\end{proof}

\begin{writenote}[More than two labels]
With $q$ output labels the one-extra-move bound fails for fixed colorings:
components of depth at most $d$ can need exactly $d+\ceil{\log_2q}$ questions
jointly, for fixed colorings of finite sums of compact countable metrizable
spaces (\cref{fo:thm:sum-depth}); for $q=3$ and $d=1$ already of convergent
sequences (\cref{fo:sec:three-colors}). For the supremum over colorings of
finite sums of the towers $\Tw_m\cong[0,\omega^{m-1}]$ ($m\ge2$) the
increase is still at most one (\cref{fo:thm:tower-invariant} and the remark
after it); in Part~II's notation $K_k\cong\Tw_{2^k}$.
\end{writenote}

\subsection{Bounded finite heights}

Let $\{X_i:i\in I\}$ be any nonempty family of nonempty Hausdorff spaces with
finite last derivative ranks $h_i$. Suppose $H=\sup_i h_i<\omega$. Then $H$
is attained and
\[
 \left(\bigsqsum_iX_i\right)^{(H)}
 =\bigsqsum_{h_i=H}X_i^{(H)}.
\]
If there is a unique component of height $H+1$ and that component has a
singleton terminal derivative, the sum has value $\ceil{\log_2(H+1)}$.
In all other cases its value is $\ceil{\log_2(H+2)}$.
These two formulae can give the same numerical value.

\subsection{Unbounded finite heights}

The next upper bound is useful because the number of components need not be
countable.

\begin{proposition}[Parallel play with finite-time certification]\label{prop:omega-sum}
If every component $X_i$ has finite $\sm(X_i)$, then
\[
 \sm\!\left(\bigsqsum_iX_i\right)\leq\omega.
\]
If the component values are unbounded among the integers, equality holds.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Fix a target $A$ in the sum and, for each $i$, a finite winning strategy for
$A_i=A\cap X_i$. We describe countably many rounds, each using three questions.

In round $n$, first ask the union of the next local questions in every component
whose local strategy has not finished. Use a dummy open question in components
that have finished. The single global answer advances all unfinished local
simulations along the same answer history.

Next ask the union of the whole components whose current local simulation has
finished with output color $0$. Finally ask the analogous union for color $1$.
These are open sets because whole components are open. At either of these
certification questions, a yes answer leaves a monochromatic state, and the
Seeker has won. Empty local states may be assigned either output color.

Suppose all certification answers are no. Any point surviving the final
intersection would lie in a particular component $X_i$. Its local strategy
finishes after finitely many rounds. In that round or an earlier one, a
certification question includes the whole component $X_i$, and a no answer
removes the point. This is a contradiction. Thus the final state is empty.

The three questions per round have total order type $3\cdot\omega=\omega$.
For the lower bound when local values are unbounded, use subspace monotonicity.
\end{proof}

In particular, for any family of finite-height Hausdorff spaces whose last
ranks are unbounded in $\omega$, the sum has membership number exactly $\omega$.
This conclusion is independent of the cardinality of the index set.

\section{Finite products and the information lost by \texorpdfstring{$\sm$}{sm}}

\begin{lemma}[Product derivatives]\label{lem:product}
Let $X,Y$ be Hausdorff spaces of finite last derivative ranks $h,k$.
For finite $n$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:product-derivative}
 (X\times Y)^{(n)}=
 \bigcup_{i+j=n}X^{(i)}\times Y^{(j)}.
\end{equation}
Thus the last rank of the product is $h+k$, and its terminal derivative is
$X^{(h)}\times Y^{(k)}$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For closed subspaces $F\subseteq X$, $G\subseteq Y$, a point of $F\times G$
is isolated precisely when both coordinates are isolated in their respective
subspaces. Therefore
\[
 (F\times G)'=(F'\times G)\cup(F\times G').
\]
Derivation distributes over finite unions of closed sets: if a point is
isolated or absent in every one of finitely many closed sets, intersect finitely
many suitable neighborhoods to witness that it is not an accumulation point
of their union. The reverse inclusion is immediate. Induction proves
\eqref{eq:product-derivative}. At $n=h+k$ only the term with $i=h,j=k$ can be
nonempty, and all terms vanish at the next stage.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Exact product formula]\label{cor:product}
For nonempty finite-height Hausdorff spaces $X_1,\ldots,X_r$, put
$H=\sum_j h_j$. Then
\[
 \sm\!\left(\prod_{j=1}^rX_j\right)=
 \begin{cases}
 \ceil{\log_2(H+1)},&\text{every }X_j^{(h_j)}\text{ is a singleton},\\
 \ceil{\log_2(H+2)},&\text{otherwise}.
 \end{cases}
\]
In particular, for finite $r\geq1$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:power}
 \sm\bigl([0,\omega]^r\bigr)=\ceil{\log_2(r+1)}.
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

Knowing only the values of $\sm$ is not sufficient to compute product values.
For example, let
\[
 P=[0,\omega^2],\qquad Q=[0,\omega^3],\qquad S=[0,\omega].
\]
Then $\sm(P)=\sm(Q)=2$, but
\[
 \sm(P\times S)=2,\qquad \sm(Q\times S)=3.
\]
The finer data consisting of the last rank and singleton-versus-multiple
terminal derivative are compositional for finite products and bounded-height
sums. The logarithmic compression to $\sm$ discards precisely the numerical
information needed in this example.

\section{Countable spaces and the transfinite-height boundary}
\label{sec:countable}

The finite formula is not a transfinite ordinal-logarithm formula. On countable
scattered spaces, all infinite Cantor--Bendixson heights lead to the same game
value, namely $\omega$.

The point-separating number $\ps(X)$ is defined by the same game, but with
winning condition $|W_\beta|\leq1$ instead of monochromaticity for a target
\cite{CCS}.

\begin{lemma}[A countable $T_1$ upper bound]\label{lem:countable}
If $X$ is countable and $T_1$, then $\sm(X)\leq\omega$. If $X$ is also infinite,
then $\ps(X)=\omega$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Enumerate $X=\{x_0,x_1,\ldots\}$. Ask the open complements
$X\setminus\{x_n\}$ successively. A no answer leaves at most the singleton
$\{x_n\}$. If every answer is yes, the final state is empty. This proves both
upper bounds. A finite binary tree has finitely many leaves and cannot separate
all points of an infinite space, proving the point-separation lower bound.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Countable scattered classification]\label{thm:countable}
Let $X$ be a nonempty countable scattered Hausdorff space. If its
Cantor--Bendixson height is finite, \cref{thm:height} applies. Otherwise
\begin{equation}\label{eq:infiniteheight}
 \sm(X)=\omega.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Only the lower bound in the infinite-height case remains. For every finite $h$,
$X^{(h)}$ is nonempty and scattered, hence has an isolated point $p$.
Choose an open neighborhood $U$ in $X$ with $U\cap X^{(h)}=\{p\}$.
Derivatives commute with open restriction, so
\[
 U^{(h)}=\{p\},\qquad U^{(h+1)}=\varnothing.
\]
By \cref{thm:height} and subspace monotonicity,
$\sm(X)\geq\ceil{\log_2(h+1)}$. These integers are unbounded as $h$ varies.
Together with \cref{lem:countable}, this yields \eqref{eq:infiniteheight}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[All countable exponent intervals]\label{cor:allordinals}
Let $0<\alpha<\omega_1$ and $1\leq m<\omega$. Then
\[
 \sm([0,\omega^\alpha\cdot m])=
 \begin{cases}
 \ceil{\log_2(h+1)},&\alpha=h<\omega,\ m=1,\\
 \ceil{\log_2(h+2)},&\alpha=h<\omega,\ m\geq2,\\
 \omega,&\alpha\geq\omega.
 \end{cases}
\]
Every one of these spaces has $\ps=\omega$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The finite-exponent cases were proved in \cref{cor:ordinal}. When
$\alpha\geq\omega$, the interval contains $[0,\omega^h]$ as a clopen subspace
for every positive finite $h$. Their membership numbers are unbounded, while
the whole space is countable and $T_1$. Use \cref{lem:countable}.
\end{proof}

For example,
\[
 \sm([0,\omega^\omega])
 =\sm([0,\omega^{\omega^2}])
 =\sm([0,\omega^{\omega^\omega}])=\omega.
\]
Thus extremely different countable order types can have the same membership
number. This is a mathematical limitation of the invariant, not a limitation
of the ordinal notation.

The countable scattered classification also covers every countable compact
Hausdorff space. For completeness, the fact that such a space is scattered is
proved in \cref{app:compact}, rather than invoked as an unproved classification
of countable compact spaces by ordinals.

\section{A purely order-theoretic form for finite posets}\label{sec:posets}

Equip a finite poset $P$ with the Alexandrov topology of upper sets. This is
$T_0$, and it is Hausdorff only when the order is an antichain. It is important
not to mistake this topology for the order topology on the infinite ordinal
spaces studied above.

Fix a target $A\subseteq P$ and color points by their membership in $A$.
A strictly increasing chain is \emph{alternating} if consecutive colors differ.
Let $a_b$ be the largest number of elements in an alternating chain whose
greatest element has color $b$, with $a_b=0$ if no point has that color.

\begin{theorem}[Alternating-chain formula]\label{thm:posets}
For every nonempty finite poset $P$ and target $A$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:poset-profile}
 N_0(A,P)=1+a_1,\qquad N_1(A,P)=1+a_0,
\end{equation}
and therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:poset-sm}
 \boxed{\ \sm(A,P)=\ceil{\log_2(1+\min(a_0,a_1))}.\ }
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Closure in the upper-set topology is downward closure. We claim that
$F^c_n\ne\varnothing$ exactly when there is an alternating chain of length
$n$ ending in color $1-c$.

More precisely, $F^c_n$ is the downward closure of the least elements of all
such chains. This is immediate for $n=1$. In the next step, intersect with color
$(c+n+1)\bmod2$ and take downward closure. A point of that color below the
least element of an existing length-$n$ alternating chain has the opposite
color from that least element, so the inequality is strict and it can be
prepended to the chain. Conversely, the first point of any length-$(n+1)$
chain is produced this way. The induction follows.

The largest index of a nonempty $F^c_n$ is consequently $a_{1-c}$, giving
\eqref{eq:poset-profile}. Now apply \cref{thm:normal,thm:greedy}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Quadratic-time computation]
Given the comparability relation of an $n$-element poset and a target, its
optimal finite query depth is computable in $O(n^2)$ time after choosing a
linear extension.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Process points in a linear extension. Set
\[
 a(x)=1+\max\{a(y):y<x,\ y\text{ has the opposite color to }x\},
\]
where the maximum of the empty set is $0$. Then
$a_b=\max\{a(x):x\text{ has color }b\}$. Each comparable pair is inspected at
most once. Formula \eqref{eq:poset-sm} completes the computation.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[A finite orientation conflict]
An alternating four-element chain has profile $(4,5)$ or $(5,4)$, depending on
its orientation. Its target takes two questions. Two disjoint chains with
opposite orientations have profile $(5,5)$, so their combined target takes
three questions. The archive contains an explicit optimal query tree for this
eight-point example.
\end{example}

\begin{writenote}[Three labels]
Part~II's six two-point chains $01,02,10,12,20,21$ (\cref{fo:sec:three-colors})
are the three-label analogue: each chain takes one question, their union
three. On posets, the least layer count for $q$ labels is the shortest common
supersequence of the reduced chain words (\cref{fo:thm:chains}); its binary
form \eqref{fo:eq:binary} is \eqref{eq:poset-profile} read from the other end of
each chain, and for three labels the decision problem is NP-complete
(\cref{fo:thm:NP}).
\end{writenote}

\begin{remark}
This order-theoretic formula is not obtained by applying the Hausdorff
finite-height theorem to a non-Hausdorff space. Both results are separate
consequences of the general finite normal form, and their hypotheses are kept
distinct.
\end{remark}

\section{Algorithms, exact checks, and reproducibility}\label{sec:checks}

The proof of \cref{thm:normal} gives a strategy compiler. First construct the two
canonical closure sequences; choose the shorter one; then binary-search its
layer indices. At a node where the possible indices are $a,\ldots,b-1$, choose
$j=\lfloor(a+b)/2\rfloor$ and ask $X\setminus F_j$. A yes answer selects
$a,\ldots,j-1$, and a no answer selects $j,\ldots,b-1$.

For a general infinite space, computing a closure is a mathematical operation,
not necessarily an effective algorithm on a presentation of the space. The
complexity claim in \cref{sec:posets} is specifically for finite posets whose
comparability relation is supplied.

\subsection{Independent verification paths}

The program \texttt{code/verify.py} uses only the Python standard library.
For each finite poset and every target, it compares three independently
implemented calculations: exhaustive minimax over all upper-set questions,
canonical downward-closure profiles, and the alternating-chain dynamic program.
It also constructs a balanced strategy tree from the shorter closure chain and
checks the color returned on every point.

The exhaustive minimax recurrence is
\[
 d_A(S)=
 \begin{cases}
 0,&S\text{ is monochromatic},\\
 1+\displaystyle\min_U\max\{d_A(S\cap U),d_A(S\setminus U)\},&\text{otherwise},
 \end{cases}
\]
where $U$ ranges over upper sets splitting $S$ nontrivially. States are encoded
as bit masks, and the recursion is memoized. This recurrence does not use the
closure formula being tested.

\subsection{Scope of the computational evidence}

Finite upper-set topologies are not convergent sequences and do not model the
Hausdorff accumulation behavior of ordinal intervals. Accordingly, the finite
checks do \emph{not} establish the infinite-space counterexample, the
Cantor--Bendixson formula, or its transfinite consequences. Those results follow
from the proofs in \cref{sec:small,sec:height,sec:countable}. The computation
instead checks the general finite game/closure/alternation mechanism in a
large family where a direct independent minimax computation is possible.

\section{What is settled and what is not}

The topological-sum identity \eqref{eq:question} is false as stated. The
counterexample can be expressed using the ordinary ordinal intervals
$[0,\omega]$ and $[0,\omega\cdot2]$, and the one-move defect is sharp at every
positive finite level. The proof also yields exact computations for every
Hausdorff space of finite Cantor--Bendixson height and every countable scattered
Hausdorff space.

The two central distinctions are structural. First, the number of alternating
layers is different from the number of adaptive questions: they are related
by a ceiling logarithm. Second, the minimum over the two orientations is
different from the pair of oriented lengths. Taking a disjoint union can force
a common orientation and increase the minimum length by one; when that passes
a power of two, it costs a full additional query.

No transfinite version of \eqref{eq:logdepth} is asserted. In particular, the
symbol $\infty$ in the finite profile is not an ordinal rank and cannot simply
be inserted into an ordinal logarithm. Nothing here determines the games at
arbitrary uncountable lengths or resolves the other problems posed in
\cite{CCS}. Those are separate questions.

The research claim should be read at its demonstrated scale: an explicit
negative solution to an explicitly stated question, together with a complete additional
classification on a natural order-topological class. The proofs are intended
to be independently checkable; their priority and presentation can be assessed
separately from their mathematical content.

\section{The research question and its relationship to ProveIt}
\label{fo:sec:scope}

\subsection{From a membership bit to a finite label}
The natural object is a known labeling $c:X\to\Sigma$ of a topological space.
A point $x\in X$ is hidden. An algorithm may ask any question of the form
``is $x\in U$?'', where $U$ is open. Questions may depend on previous answers.
The algorithm must eventually report $c(x)$, not necessarily identify $x$.
For two labels this is a set-membership problem. With more labels, different
components may demand incompatible orders of resolving the labels.

Chiozini, Csern\'ak, and Soukup introduced the topological cut-and-choose
framework and asked whether the set-membership number of a topological sum
always equals the supremum of its component values \cite{CCS}. The ProveIt
report \emph{Open-query games on ordinal spaces} gives a binary counterexample
and develops a finite alternating-layer normal form \cite{Repo}. The present
article starts from that direction but does not repeat its binary
Cantor--Bendixson classification.

We address the following finite-output questions. How much extra information
can synchronization across components require? Does the efficient binary
optimization survive a third label? Can a finite non-Hausdorff obstruction be
realized in familiar compact metrizable spaces? Which information, beyond a
single optimal-depth number, composes correctly under topological sums?

The answers are the sharp bound $(q-1)m+1$, a binary/ternary complexity
dichotomy, and the word profile introduced below. The compact realization
result shows that the synchronization effect is not merely an artifact of
finite specialization orders.

\section{A normal form for arbitrary topological spaces}
\label{fo:sec:normal}

Fix a nonempty space $X$ and a finite alphabet $\Sigma$, of size $q\ge1$.
No separation axiom is assumed.

\begin{definition}[Depth, layers, and admissible words]
For $c:X\to\Sigma$, let $\depth(X,c)$ be the minimum worst-case number of open
questions in a finite correct deterministic decision tree, or $\infty$ if none
exists. A word $w=w_1\cdots w_m\in\Sigma^m$ is \emph{admissible} if there is a
map $\lambda:X\to\{1,\ldots,m\}$ satisfying
\begin{equation}\label{fo:eq:layer}
 c(x)=w_{\lambda(x)},\qquad
 U_j:=\{x:\lambda(x)\ge j\}\text{ is open for every }j.
\end{equation}
The fibers of $\lambda$ are its ordered layers. Empty layers are allowed.
Write $\W(X,c)$ for the set of admissible words, and set
\[
 \layers(X,c)=\min\{\len{w}:w\in\W(X,c)\},
\]
with the minimum of the empty set interpreted as $\infty$.
\end{definition}

In a minimal representation there are no empty layers. Also, two consecutive
layers carrying the same color can be merged: the remaining suffixes are still
open. Thus a shortest admissible word is \emph{reduced}, meaning that adjacent
letters are distinct. This reduction is not deletion of all repeated letters;
for example, $010$ is reduced.

\begin{theorem}[Tree--layer normal form]\label{fo:thm:normal}
For every $(X,c)$, the minimum number of nonempty leaves of a finite correct
open-query tree equals $\layers(X,c)$. When this number is finite,
\begin{equation}\label{fo:eq:depth-log}
 \depth(X,c)=\ceil{\log_2\layers(X,c)}.
\end{equation}
In particular, a finite correct tree exists if and only if an admissible word
exists.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Order the leaves of a query tree recursively, placing the entire no-subtree
before the entire yes-subtree. We show that the union of any suffix of its
ordered leaf cells is open. The assertion is proved by induction on the tree,
with the current possible-point set given its subspace topology.

At the root, let the queried open set be $U$. If a suffix is contained entirely
in the yes-subtree, induction expresses it as $U\cap V$ for an open set $V$ of
the current space. If the suffix includes part of the no-subtree, it contains
the whole yes-subtree and has the form
\[
 U\cup((X\setminus U)\cap V)=U\cup V,
\]
again open. The same argument works in every current subspace. The one-leaf
case is immediate. Removing empty leaf cells preserves the suffix property.
Correctness makes each remaining leaf monochromatic. Its leaf index therefore
defines a layering, so $\layers(X,c)$ is at most the number of nonempty leaves.

Conversely, given a layering with $m$ nonempty layers, locate its index by
balanced binary search. At an interval of candidate indices, split the interval
as evenly as possible and query the open suffix $U_j$ beginning at the split.
This produces a correct tree with $m$ leaves and depth at most
$\ceil{\log_2m}$.

A binary tree of depth $d$ has at most $2^d$ leaves, so the first direction gives
$\layers(X,c)\le2^d$ for any depth-$d$ correct tree. The balanced construction
supplies the opposite depth inequality and proves \eqref{fo:eq:depth-log}.
\end{proof}

The theorem does not say that every optimal-depth tree has minimum leaf count.
It says that minimum leaf count contains exactly the information needed to
compute optimal worst-case depth. The class of all open sets is important:
the proof uses closure under unions and finite intersections.

\subsection{A fixed-word closure test}

The following supplies a topological test without reference to a query tree.
For a proposed word $w=w_1\cdots w_m$, define closed sets by
\begin{equation}\label{fo:eq:closure}
 G_0=X,\qquad
 G_{t+1}=\cl_X\bigl(G_t\setminus c^{-1}(w_{m-t})\bigr)
 \quad(0\le t<m).
\end{equation}
Thus we try to remove the top color, then the next color, retaining all limit
points that cannot yet be discarded.

\begin{proposition}[Greedy closed-set elimination]\label{fo:prop:closure}
The word $w$ is admissible if and only if $G_m=\varnothing$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Suppose $\lambda$ witnesses admissibility. Put
$R_t=\{x:\lambda(x)\le m-t\}$, so $R_t$ is closed. Inductively $G_t\subseteq
R_t$: after excluding color $w_{m-t}$, all remaining points of $R_t$ lie in the
closed set $R_{t+1}$. Taking closure therefore still stays in $R_{t+1}$.
Consequently $G_m\subseteq R_m=\varnothing$.

Conversely, suppose $G_m=\varnothing$. Give a point removed at step $t+1$ the
index $m-t$. The removed set $G_t\setminus G_{t+1}$ has color $w_{m-t}$ because
$G_{t+1}$ contains every point of $G_t$ of another color. These removed sets
partition $X$. The suffix beginning at index $j$ is
$X\setminus G_{m-j+1}$, which is open. This is the required layering.
\end{proof}

The proposition is a finite colored elimination statement. It should not be
confused with a classification of all transfinite partition hierarchies.

\section{Posets and shortest common supersequences}
\label{fo:sec:posets}

Give a poset $P$ its upper Alexandrov topology: $U$ is open exactly when
$x\in U$ and $x\le y$ imply $y\in U$. A map $\lambda:P\to[m]$ has open suffixes
exactly when it is order-preserving. This observation turns topology into a
word-constrained monotone-map problem.

For a finite nonempty chain $x_1<\cdots<x_r$, read the word
$c(x_1)\cdots c(x_r)$ and collapse each maximal run of equal letters to one
letter. Let $\C(P,c)$ denote the collection of all resulting reduced words.
Write $u\subseq v$ when $u$ is a subsequence of $v$, using a strictly increasing
map of positions. For a word family $\mathcal F$, $\scs(\mathcal F)$ is the
minimum length of a common supersequence, or $\infty$ when no finite common
supersequence exists.

\begin{theorem}[Exact chain-language representation]\label{fo:thm:chains}
For any poset, finite or infinite, and any finite word $v$,
\begin{equation}\label{fo:eq:profile-chain}
 v\in\W(P,c)
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 u\subseq v\text{ for every }u\in\C(P,c).
\end{equation}
Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{fo:eq:scs}
 \layers(P,c)=\scs(\C(P,c)),\qquad
 \depth(P,c)=\ceil{\log_2\scs(\C(P,c))}
\end{equation}
whenever the right-hand side is finite.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
An admissible map is weakly increasing along a chain. At a color change its
index must increase strictly, since one word position has only one color.
Selecting one point per color run embeds the reduced chain word into $v$.

For the converse, greedily embed each reduced finite chain word into $v$.
For $x\in P$, consider all finite chains ending at $x$, including the singleton
chain. Their greedy last positions belong to the finite set
$\{j:v_j=c(x)\}$. They are nonempty in number and all exist by hypothesis.
Let $\lambda(x)$ be their maximum.

If $x<y$, take a chain ending at $x$ whose greedy last position is
$\lambda(x)$ and append $y$. If the last color repeats, reducing the word leaves
the last greedy position unchanged; otherwise that position moves forward.
The extended chain ends at $y$, so $\lambda(y)\ge\lambda(x)$. Also
$v_{\lambda(x)}=c(x)$. Thus $\lambda$ is a color-respecting order-preserving map,
which proves admissibility. The formulas follow from Theorem~\ref{fo:thm:normal}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{A linear-time feasibility test on a finite DAG}

Let the order be the reachability relation of an acyclic directed graph with
$n$ vertices and $e$ edges, supplied in a topological order. The input may
contain just cover edges, all strict comparisons, or any generating DAG.
For a finite word $v$, write
\[
 \operatorname{next}_a(b)=\min\{j\ge b:v_j=a\},
\]
where the minimum is $\infty$ if the set is empty. Process the vertices in
order and compute
\begin{equation}\label{fo:eq:greedy}
 b(x)=\max\bigl(\{1\}\cup\{f(y):y\in\pred(x)\}\bigr),\qquad
 f(x)=\operatorname{next}_{c(x)}(b(x)).
\end{equation}
Here $\pred(x)$ consists of incoming neighbors, not necessarily all predecessors.
If a value is infinite the test rejects.

\begin{proposition}[Least feasible map]\label{fo:prop:greedy}
If the test succeeds, $f$ is the pointwise least order-preserving map compatible
with $v$ and $c$. It succeeds exactly when $v$ is admissible. With a next-position
table, its running time is $O(q\len{v}+n+e)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
If $g$ is any compatible monotone map, induction gives $f(y)\le g(y)$ at
already processed vertices. Hence $b(x)\le g(x)$, and $g(x)$ is an occurrence
of $c(x)$. The first possible such occurrence is at most $g(x)$. This proves
minimality and shows that rejection precludes any compatible map. When all
values are finite, monotonicity holds on every edge and hence on reachability.
The recurrence uses one scan of the graph after constructing the next-position
table backwards through the word.
\end{proof}

The weak inequality $j\ge b(x)$ is essential. Comparable points of the same
color may use the same layer. Replacing it by $j>b(x)$ would compute a different
problem and would make even a constant-colored long chain unnecessarily hard.

\begin{corollary}[Disjoint chains]\label{fo:cor:chain-union}
If $P$ is a disjoint union of finite chains whose reduced color words are
$w_1,\ldots,w_r$, then
\[
 \layers(P,c)=\scs(w_1,\ldots,w_r).
\]
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Every chain lies in a single component. Its reduced word is a subsequence of
the component's reduced word. Thus the component words already impose all the
constraints in Theorem~\ref{fo:thm:chains}.
\end{proof}

\section{Compact metrizable realizations of word profiles}
\label{fo:sec:compact}

Finite upper-set spaces are generally not Hausdorff. We now show that their
word obstructions can survive in compact countable metrizable spaces.
The result concerns the entire admissible-word profile, not only one numerical
invariant.

Define $\Tw_1$ to be a singleton. Recursively, $\Tw_m$ is the one-point
compactification of the topological sum of countably many copies of $\Tw_{m-1}$.
Denote the new point by $\infty_m$. In particular, $\Tw_2$ is a convergent sequence
with its limit.

\begin{lemma}[The convergent towers]\label{fo:lem:towers}
Each $\Tw_m$ is countable, compact, and metrizable. Its last nonempty
Cantor--Bendixson derivative is $\Tw_m^{(m-1)}=\{\infty_m\}$ for $m\ge2$;
$\Tw_1^{(0)}=\Tw_1$ is also a singleton. A neighborhood of $\infty_m$ contains all
but finitely many entire copies of $\Tw_{m-1}$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
A compact subset of a topological sum of compact components meets only finitely
many components: the components themselves form an open cover. The assertion
about neighborhoods follows directly from the one-point compactification.
Countability and compactness follow by induction and this neighborhood
property.

For metrizability, choose a metric $d_{m-1}\le1$ on each copy. Label copies by
positive integers $n$. Within copy $n$, use $2^{-n}d_{m-1}$; give each of its
points distance $2^{-n}$ from $\infty_m$; and give points in distinct copies
$n,k$ distance $2^{-n}+2^{-k}$. This is a metric inducing precisely the stated
topology. Each copy is open and closed. Taking derivatives inside a copy agrees
with its own derivatives, while $\infty_m$ remains a limit point as long as
infinitely many nonempty copies remain. Induction gives the derivative formula.
\end{proof}

For a reduced word $w=a_1\cdots a_m$, color $\Tw_m$ recursively: give
$\infty_m$ color $a_1$, and give every copy of $\Tw_{m-1}$ the coloring for
$a_2\cdots a_m$. For $m=1$, color its single point $a_1$. Call this coloring
$c_w$. Equivalently, a point of Cantor--Bendixson rank $r$ has color $a_{m-r}$.

\begin{theorem}[Exact compact word realization]\label{fo:thm:compact}
For every reduced word $w$ and every finite word $v$,
\begin{equation}\label{fo:eq:compact-profile}
 v\in\W(\Tw_{\len{w}},c_w)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad w\subseq v.
\end{equation}
In particular, $\layers(\Tw_m,c_w)=m$ and
$\depth(\Tw_m,c_w)=\ceil{\log_2m}$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The word $w$ itself is admissible. Put the top point in layer $1$, and use the
recursive suffix layering, shifted by one, in every component. Suffixes starting
after layer $1$ are unions of open subsets of the open components; the first
suffix is the whole space. Embedding these layer indices in any supersequence
$v$ preserves the openness of suffixes, so $w\subseq v$ implies admissibility.

For the converse, induct on $m$. The singleton case is immediate. Suppose
$\lambda$ witnesses admissibility of $v$ on $\Tw_m$, and set
$j=\lambda(\infty_m)$, so $v_j=a_1$. The open suffix
$\{x:\lambda(x)\ge j\}$ contains $\infty_m$. By Lemma~\ref{fo:lem:towers}, it
contains an entire copy of $\Tw_{m-1}$. On that copy the restricted layering uses
only indices $j,\ldots,\len{v}$. The induction hypothesis embeds
$a_2\cdots a_m$ in $v_j\cdots v_{\len{v}}$. Since $a_2\ne a_1=v_j$, the first
position of this embedding is strictly greater than $j$. Prefixing position
$j$ embeds $a_1\cdots a_m=w$ into $v$, as required.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Compact realization of any finite colored poset]
Every finite colored poset has the same admissible-word profile as some compact
countable metrizable colored space of finite Cantor--Bendixson height.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
There are finitely many reduced chain words. Take the finite topological sum of
one compact realization for each such word. A word is admissible on this sum
exactly when it is admissible on every component: restriction proves necessity,
and componentwise gluing proves sufficiency. Theorem~\ref{fo:thm:compact} and
Theorem~\ref{fo:thm:chains} now give equality of profiles. A finite sum of compact
countable metrizable spaces has the same properties.
\end{proof}

This construction is an existence theorem and can be exponentially large if
one lists all chain words of a poset. For a given list of chain words, however,
it preserves that list directly as a succinct description of the compact
instance.

\section{The extremal supersequence lemma}
\label{fo:sec:universal}

The exact constants come from a small extremal word problem. Let
$\A_{q,m}(S)$ consist of all reduced words of length $m\ge1$ over $\Sigma$
whose first letter lies in a fixed nonempty set $S\subseteq\Sigma$. Write
$s=\len{S}$ and $\A_{q,m}=\A_{q,m}(\Sigma)$.

\begin{theorem}[Restricted-start universal words]\label{fo:thm:universal}
For $q\ge2$ and $1\le s\le q$,
\begin{equation}\label{fo:eq:universal}
 \scs(\A_{q,m}(S))=s+(q-1)(m-1).
\end{equation}
In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{fo:eq:universal-all}
 \scs(\A_{q,m})=(q-1)m+1.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Order the alphabet cyclically, with the $s$ letters of $S$ first, and repeat this
order indefinitely. Greedily embedding a word in $\A_{q,m}(S)$ puts its first
letter at a position at most $s$. Every next letter differs from its predecessor
and therefore occurs within the next $q-1$ positions. The prefix of length
$s+(q-1)(m-1)$ is thus a common supersequence.

For the lower bound, let $v$ be any common supersequence. Choose a first letter
in $S$ whose first occurrence is latest. Its position is at least $s$. Having
chosen a prefix ending with letter $a$ at its greedy position $j$, consider the
next occurrences after $j$ of all $q-1$ letters different from $a$. Every such
occurrence exists while the chosen prefix has length less than $m$: otherwise
that prefix followed by the missing letter could be extended to a word in
$\A_{q,m}(S)$ that does not embed in $v$.

These $q-1$ next occurrences occupy different positions. Choose the latest;
its distance from $j$ is at least $q-1$. Continuing this adversarial choice gives
a reduced word of length $m$ whose greedy last position is at least
$s+(q-1)(m-1)$. Its existence forces the same lower bound on $\len{v}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Bounded color alternation is exactly finite solvability]
\label{fo:cor:height}
For a nonempty colored poset, let $h_c$ be the supremum of lengths of reduced
finite chain words. A finite correct open-query tree exists if and only if
$h_c<\infty$. In that case
\begin{equation}\label{fo:eq:height-bound}
 h_c\le\layers(P,c)\le(q-1)h_c+1
 \qquad(q\ge2).
\end{equation}
The upper bound is sharp for every $q\ge2$ and $h_c\ge1$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Every chain word must embed into an admissible word, giving the lower bound.
If all reduced chain words have length at most $h_c$, extend each shorter one
to a reduced word of length $h_c$. The universal word from
Theorem~\ref{fo:thm:universal} embeds all of them, so Theorem~\ref{fo:thm:chains}
gives the upper bound. A disjoint union of chains carrying every word of
$\A_{q,h_c}$ attains equality. Unbounded lengths preclude any finite word.
\end{proof}

The lower bound in \eqref{fo:eq:height-bound} can be supplemented by the number of
colors actually present. Neither bound alone determines the answer: chain
words of the same length may synchronize in very different ways.

\section{Sharp Cantor--Bendixson bounds and exact tower invariants}
\label{fo:sec:CB}

For a space $X$, let $X^{(0)}=X$ and let $X^{(r+1)}$ be the nonisolated-point
subspace of $X^{(r)}$. Suppose $X^{(h)}\ne\varnothing$ and
$X^{(h+1)}=\varnothing$, where $h$ is a nonnegative integer. The
Cantor--Bendixson rank of $x$ is the unique $r$ with
$x\in X^{(r)}\setminus X^{(r+1)}$.

\begin{theorem}[A sharp finite-height layer bound]\label{fo:thm:CB}
Let $X$ be a nonempty Hausdorff space of last derivative rank $h<\infty$.
Let $c:X\to\Sigma$, where $q=\len{\Sigma}\ge2$, and let
$s=\len{c(X^{(h)})}$ be the number of colors appearing on its last nonempty
derivative. Then
\begin{equation}\label{fo:eq:CB-bound}
 \layers(X,c)\le(q-1)h+s,
 \qquad
 \depth(X,c)\le\ceil{\log_2((q-1)h+s)}.
\end{equation}
For every $q\ge2$, $h\ge0$, and $1\le s\le q$, the layer bound is attained by
a compact countable metrizable colored space having exactly $s$ points in its
last nonempty derivative, with distinct colors on those points.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof of the upper bound]
Choose a cyclic order of the alphabet whose first $s$ entries are the colors
on $X^{(h)}$. Let $v$ be the prefix of the repeated order of length
$M=s+h(q-1)$. Give a point of rank $h$ the first occurrence of its color in $v$;
its index is at most $s$.

For a point of rank $r<h$, put
\[
 b_r=s+(h-1-r)(q-1)
\]
and assign it the first occurrence of its color at or after $b_r$. The interval
$[b_r,b_r+q-1]$ has $q$ positions and contains the whole alphabet, so this
assignment exists and lies in $[1,M]$. For two consecutive lower ranks, the
maximum permitted index at the higher rank equals the minimum permitted index
at the lower rank. The corresponding comparison also holds between rank $h$
and rank $h-1$: their boundary is $s$.

Every point $x$ of rank $r$ has an open neighborhood $V_x$ satisfying
$V_x\cap X^{(r)}=\{x\}$, because it is isolated in $X^{(r)}$. Every other point
of $V_x$ has smaller rank and hence an assigned index at least that of $x$.
Thus $V_x\subseteq\{y:\lambda(y)\ge\lambda(x)\}$. Each suffix of $\lambda$ is
a union of such neighborhoods, and is open. This proves admissibility of $v$.
For $h=0$, the same argument simply orders the $s$ colors of the discrete space.
The depth estimate follows from Theorem~\ref{fo:thm:normal}.
\end{proof}

To prove sharpness we need colorings richer than a single rank word. For
$a\in\Sigma$ and $m\ge2$, color the top point of $\Tw_m$ by $a$. List every
reduced word $u$ of length $m-1$ whose first letter differs from $a$, and use
infinitely many component copies with coloring $c_u$ for each listed word.
There are finitely many word types and countably many copies. Denote the
resulting coloring by $b_{a,m}$. Put $b_{a,1}=a$ on $\Tw_1$.

\begin{lemma}[All words with one prescribed initial color]\label{fo:lem:root-profile}
For every $m\ge1$,
\[
 \W(\Tw_m,b_{a,m})
 =\{v:w\subseq v\text{ for every }w\in\A_{q,m}(\{a\})\}.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For $m=1$ the assertion is the singleton case. For $m\ge2$, suppose $v$ is
admissible and its layering gives the top point index $j$, with $v_j=a$.
The open suffix beginning at $j$ contains all but finitely many component
copies. Since each word type $u$ occurs infinitely often, one entire copy of
each type lies in that suffix. Theorem~\ref{fo:thm:compact} embeds $u$ into
$v_j\cdots v_{\len{v}}$, and its first letter differs from $a$, so this embedding
starts after $j$. Prefixing $j$ embeds every word $au$ in the required family.

Conversely, suppose every $au$ embeds into $v$. Let $j$ be the first occurrence
of $a$ in $v$. In an embedding of any $au$, the initial $a$ can be replaced by
this earlier occurrence, so every $u$ embeds after $j$. Use such an embedding
and Theorem~\ref{fo:thm:compact} to place the layers of every component after $j$;
place the top point at $j$. Suffixes beginning at most at $j$ are the whole
space, while later suffixes are unions of open subsets of components. This is
an admissible layering.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Completion of the sharpness proof of Theorem~\ref{fo:thm:CB}]
Let $S$ be a set of $s$ alphabet letters and take one copy of
$(\Tw_{h+1},b_{a,h+1})$ for each $a\in S$. For $h=0$ these are $s$ singletons.
The last derivative consists of their $s$ top points. By
Lemma~\ref{fo:lem:root-profile}, the word profile of the sum requires exactly the
words of $\A_{q,h+1}(S)$ as subsequences. Its minimum word length is
$s+(q-1)h$ by Theorem~\ref{fo:thm:universal}.
\end{proof}

Now take a supremum over all colorings, in contrast to the fixed-coloring
invariant $\depth(X,c)$:
\[
 \smq(X):=\sup_{c:X\to\Sigma}\depth(X,c).
\]
The alphabet has $q$ available colors; a coloring need not use all of them.

\begin{theorem}[Exact finite-output invariant of convergent towers]
\label{fo:thm:tower-invariant}
For all integers $m,t\ge1$ and $q\ge2$,
\begin{equation}\label{fo:eq:tower-invariant}
 \boxed{\displaystyle
 \smq\!\left(\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{t}\Tw_m\right)
 =\ceil{\log_2\bigl((q-1)(m-1)+\min(q,t)\bigr)}.}
\end{equation}
In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{fo:eq:one-tower}
 \smq(\Tw_m)=\ceil{\log_2\bigl(1+(q-1)(m-1)\bigr)}.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The last nonempty derivative is at rank $m-1$ and has $t$ points. Every coloring
uses at most $\min(q,t)$ colors there. The upper bound is therefore
Theorem~\ref{fo:thm:CB}. For the lower bound, choose $\min(q,t)$ distinct top
colors and the corresponding colorings $b_{a,m}$. If extra copies remain,
duplicate any one of those colored components. Duplicating its word constraint
does not change the intersection of word profiles. The sharpness construction
in Theorem~\ref{fo:thm:CB} gives exactly the claimed layer count and hence depth.
\end{proof}

For $q=2$, \eqref{fo:eq:tower-invariant} is
$\ceil{\log_2m}$ for one tower and $\ceil{\log_2(m+1)}$ for two or more,
consistent with the binary repository result \cite{Repo}. The theorem above
supplies the finite-alphabet extension for this compact family, including its
worst-case colorings and explicit proofs.

\begin{writenote}[Checked against Part~I]
The binary result is \cref{thm:height} applied to $\bigsqcup_{i=1}^t\Tw_m$
(last derivative rank $m-1$, $t$ terminal points). At $m=2$, $t=2$ the value~2
is Part~I's counterexample \eqref{eq:small}; since $\Tw_{2^k}\cong K_k$, the
values $k$ and $k+1$ at $m=2^k$ are \eqref{eq:sharp}. The formula agrees with
all rows of Part~I's recorded tables (\cref{fo:sec:crosswalk}, items~3 and~4).
\end{writenote}

\begin{remark}[A limitation and a useful distinction]
Theorem~\ref{fo:thm:CB} gives a sharp \emph{uniform bound over a class of spaces};
it does not assert that every coloring attains it, or that every Hausdorff
space of the specified height has the same multi-output invariant.
Theorem~\ref{fo:thm:tower-invariant} is an exact statement for the defined towers
and their finite sums. When $m\ge2$, its formula shows that summing these
uncolored towers can increase $\smq$ by at most one, even though fixed
colorings can exhibit the larger increase established next.
Indeed, if $A=(q-1)(m-1)+1$, then
$A\le(q-1)(m-1)+\min(q,t)\le A+q-1\le2A$.
\end{remark}

\section{Sharp synchronization under topological sums}
\label{fo:sec:sums}

\begin{proposition}[Profiles compose by intersection]\label{fo:prop:sum-profile}
For any nonempty family of nonempty colored spaces, with the same alphabet,
\begin{equation}\label{fo:eq:sum-profile}
 \W\!\left(\bigsqcup_{i\in I}X_i,\bigsqcup_{i\in I}c_i\right)
 =\bigcap_{i\in I}\W(X_i,c_i).
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Restrict a layering of the sum to each component for one inclusion. For the
other, take a layering of each component with the same word. Their maps glue,
and every glued suffix is open because its restriction to each open component
is open. One can choose component layerings by any such witnesses; alternatively
Proposition~\ref{fo:prop:closure} provides canonical layers for the prescribed word.
\end{proof}

A scalar such as minimum depth or minimum length loses this compatibility
information. Even the collection of shortest words alone need not be used as a
substitute for the full admissibility profile: a longer locally admissible word
may be useful for synchronization.

\begin{theorem}[Sharp layer synchronization]\label{fo:thm:sum-layers}
Let $q\ge2$ and $m\ge1$. If $\layers(X_i,c_i)\le m$ for every $i$, then
\begin{equation}\label{fo:eq:sum-layers}
 \layers\!\left(\bigsqcup_i X_i,\bigsqcup_i c_i\right)\le(q-1)m+1.
\end{equation}
For every $q,m$ the constant is best possible. Sharp examples can be chosen as
finite disjoint unions of $m$-point chains, or as finite disjoint unions of
copies of the compact countable metrizable space $\Tw_m$ with fixed rank-word
colorings.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Each component admits a reduced word of length at most $m$. Every such word
embeds into a reduced word of length $m$ and hence into the single universal
word of length $(q-1)m+1$ in Theorem~\ref{fo:thm:universal}. Inserting empty layers
according to this embedding makes the universal word admissible on every
component. Proposition~\ref{fo:prop:sum-profile} gives the upper bound, with no
restriction on the number of components.

For sharpness, take one component for each $w\in\A_{q,m}$, either the chain
carrying $w$ or the compact colored tower $(\Tw_m,c_w)$. Each component has exactly
$m$ layers. By Corollary~\ref{fo:cor:chain-union} or
Theorem~\ref{fo:thm:compact}, the union's profile is exactly the common
supersequence profile of $\A_{q,m}$, whose minimum length is $(q-1)m+1$.
There are $q(q-1)^{m-1}$ components.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Exact maximal extra query cost]\label{fo:thm:sum-depth}
For $q\ge2$ and $d\ge0$, if every component has fixed-coloring depth at most $d$,
then
\begin{equation}\label{fo:eq:sum-depth}
 \depth\!\left(\bigsqcup_iX_i,\bigsqcup_ic_i\right)
 \le d+\ceil{\log_2q}.
\end{equation}
Equality is attainable for every pair $(q,d)$, both by finite colored posets and
by finite families of compact countable metrizable colored spaces.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Use Theorem~\ref{fo:thm:normal} and Theorem~\ref{fo:thm:sum-layers} with $m=2^d$.
If $k=\ceil{\log_2q}$, then $2^{k-1}\le q-1<2^k$, so
\[
 2^{d+k-1}<(q-1)2^d+1\le2^{d+k}.
\]
Thus the ceiling of its logarithm is exactly $d+k$. The sharp examples of
Theorem~\ref{fo:thm:sum-layers} have each component depth exactly $d$ and union
depth exactly $d+k$.
\end{proof}

There is a simple independent intuition for the upper depth bound. Synchronize
component trees for $d$ rounds, taking at each round the union of the appropriate
component queries. After that transcript, the surviving points in each
component have one known color, but different components may have different
colors. Queries that are unions of whole components resolve the remaining
$q$ possibilities in at most $\ceil{\log_2q}$ rounds. The layer theorem does more:
it finds the exact extremal number of layers and proves the depth loss sharp.

For the supremum-over-colorings invariant, the same upper estimate holds if
$\smq(X_i)\le d$ for all $i$. It is sharp on finite upper-topology chains of
$2^d$ points: their worst-case layer count is $2^d$, attained by an alternating
two-color word. The compact sharpness construction in Theorem~\ref{fo:thm:sum-depth}
is a statement about its \emph{specified colorings}. It does not assert that
$\smq(\Tw_{2^d})=d$ for $q>2$; Equation~\eqref{fo:eq:one-tower} gives the different,
correct value.

\subsection{The first genuinely three-color example}
\label{fo:sec:three-colors}

Take six two-point chains with color words
\[
 01,\quad02,\quad10,\quad12,\quad20,\quad21.
\]
Each needs one question. The word $01201$ is a common supersequence, with the
following embeddings:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}cccccc@{}}
\toprule
$01$ & $02$ & $10$ & $12$ & $20$ & $21$\\
\midrule
$(1,2)$ & $(1,3)$ & $(2,4)$ & $(2,3)$ & $(3,4)$ & $(3,5)$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
No word of length four contains all six ordered pairs. Such a word must use all
three colors, and at least two of the colors occur only once; only one order of
those two colors can then be a subsequence. Hence the sum has exactly five
layers and exactly three questions. In particular, the binary ``one extra
question'' bound is false for fixed three-color targets.

Replace each chain $ab$ by a convergent sequence whose limit has color $a$ and
all isolated points have color $b$. Theorem~\ref{fo:thm:compact} preserves every
word constraint. This gives the same one-to-three increase on a compact
countable metrizable space that is a finite sum of convergent sequences.

The six constraints are deletion-minimal for this three-query obstruction.
More generally, if the ordered pair $ba$ is deleted from the full $q$-color
pair family, let $R$ list all colors except $a,b$. The word $RabR$ has length
$2q-2$ and contains every remaining ordered pair. For $q=3$ this is a four-layer,
two-query solution after deleting any one component.

\section{A binary/ternary complexity dichotomy}
\label{fo:sec:complexity}

A finite input poset is given by a generating DAG or by its order relation, with
an explicit color at every vertex. The decision problems ask whether
$\layers(P,c)\le k$ or whether $\depth(P,c)\le d$, with an integer budget supplied
as part of the input.

\subsection{The binary case}
For colors $0,1$, let $r_a$ be the maximum length of a reduced chain word
beginning with $a$, or zero if that color never occurs. Every reduced binary
word alternates. An alternating master word starting with $a$ embeds a reduced
chain word beginning with $a$ as soon as it has enough positions; embedding a
word beginning with $1-a$ costs one additional initial position.

\begin{proposition}[Exact binary formula]\label{fo:prop:binary}
For a nonempty finite binary-colored poset,
\begin{equation}\label{fo:eq:binary}
 \layers(P,c)=
 \min\{\max(r_0,r_1+1),\ \max(r_1,r_0+1)\}.
\end{equation}
Both $\layers$ and $\depth$ are polynomial-time computable.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
A shortest master word is reduced and therefore alternating. The two possible
initial colors give the two displayed lengths, by the preceding observation
and Theorem~\ref{fo:thm:chains}. To compute the $r_a$, process the transitive order
in a linear extension. For each vertex $x$ and each starting color $a$, store
the largest reduced-chain length beginning with $a$ and ending at $x$. Extending
a chain from $y<x$ increases the value by $1$ precisely when $c(y)\ne c(x)$.
This is a polynomial dynamic program; using an explicit order relation costs
$O(n^2)$ after it is available. Finally apply Theorem~\ref{fo:thm:normal}.
\end{proof}

This is the binary phenomenon already present in the repository report
\cite{Repo}, now expressed in the chain-language notation. It is included to
make the contrast with three colors precise, not as a new binary theorem.

\begin{writenote}[Where it is in Part~I]
\Cref{thm:posets} and its quadratic-time corollary. Part~I's $a_b$ reads a
chain from its greatest element, $r_a$ here from its least; both formulas give
$1+\min(a_0,a_1)$ layers (\cref{fo:sec:crosswalk}, item~2).
\end{writenote}

\subsection{The imported hardness theorem}
We use the following established result and no other external hardness
assumption beyond ordinary NP-completeness terminology.

\begin{theorem}[Lagoutte--Tavenas, modified SCS; external input]
\label{fo:thm:MSCS}
Over three letters, deciding whether a finite family of words has a common
supersequence of length at most $k$ is NP-complete even when no input word has
identical consecutive letters and no input word begins with a designated
letter, which may be called $2$.
\end{theorem}

This is Corollary~6 of \cite{LT}; its strict-budget convention is equivalent by
subtracting one from the budget. Its relationship to earlier typed-DAG fusion
work of Darte is discussed there. We do not reprove that hardness theorem or
claim that the reduced-word restriction is new.

Corollary~\ref{fo:cor:chain-union} immediately transfers this result to the layer
budget. The depth budget requires care: simply rounding $\log_2 k$ loses the
exact threshold. The next lemma supplies the missing step.

\begin{lemma}[Common-prefix cancellation]\label{fo:lem:prefix}
For any finite word $p$ and any nonempty finite family of finite words,
\begin{equation}\label{fo:eq:prefix}
 \scs(pw_1,\ldots,pw_r)=\len{p}+\scs(w_1,\ldots,w_r).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Prefixing $p$ to a shortest common supersequence proves the upper bound.
For the reverse bound, let $v$ contain every $pw_i$. Greedily embed $p$ into
$v$, and let its last position be $j$. For any embedding of $pw_i$, the prefix
$p$ finishes at a position at least $j$, so the embedded copy of $w_i$ lies
entirely after $j$. Consequently the suffix of $v$ after $j$ is a common
supersequence of all $w_i$. Since $j\ge\len{p}$, this gives the lower bound.
For the empty prefix the assertion is immediate.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Three colors suffice for NP-completeness]
\label{fo:thm:NP}
For every fixed alphabet size $q\ge3$, each of the following is NP-complete:
deciding $\layers(P,c)\le k$, and deciding $\depth(P,c)\le d$.
The result holds already for disjoint unions of chains.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
First consider membership in NP. Every $n$-point colored poset has a layering
of at most $n$ positions, obtained by listing its vertices in a linear
extension. A word of length at most $n$ together with a color-respecting
monotone map to its positions is a polynomial certificate. Its correctness is
checked on the generating edges. For the depth problem, test whether its length
is at most $2^d$. If $d\ge\ceil{\log_2n}$, the answer is automatically yes;
otherwise the integer $2^d$ is at most $2n$ and causes no encoding problem.
The same certificate proves membership for a layer budget.

Layer hardness follows directly from Theorem~\ref{fo:thm:MSCS} by making a chain
for each input word. For depth hardness, take a modified-SCS instance
$(w_1,\ldots,w_r,k)$ over $\{0,1,2\}$ whose words do not begin with $2$.
Delete empty words. Trivial budgets are decidable directly and can be mapped
to fixed yes- or no-instances, so assume $1\le k<N$, where
$N=\sum_i\len{w_i}$.

Set
\[
 d=\ceil{\log_2k},\qquad B=2^d,\qquad t=B-k.
\]
If $t>0$, choose the alternating word over $\{0,2\}$ of length $t$ ending in
$2$; call it $p$. If $t=0$, let $p$ be empty. Every word $pw_i$ is reduced,
including across the joining boundary, because no $w_i$ starts with $2$.
Let $P'$ be the disjoint union of chains carrying the words $pw_i$. By
Corollary~\ref{fo:cor:chain-union} and Lemma~\ref{fo:lem:prefix},
\[
 \layers(P',c')=t+\scs(w_1,\ldots,w_r).
\]
Therefore
\begin{align*}
 \depth(P',c')\le d
 &\Longleftrightarrow \layers(P',c')\le B\\
 &\Longleftrightarrow \scs(w_1,\ldots,w_r)\le B-t=k.
\end{align*}
The output has $N+rt$ vertices. Since $t<k\le N$, its size is polynomial in
the input size. This is an exact many-one reduction for the depth threshold.
The three used colors can be viewed as a subset of any larger fixed alphabet.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The same hardness on succinct compact instances}

The compact realization has an algorithmic consequence if the representation
of an infinite space is specified rather than left implicit. Define a
\emph{tower-list instance} to be a finite list of reduced words
$w_1,\ldots,w_r$, denoting
\[
 \bigsqcup_{i=1}^r(\Tw_{\len{w_i}},c_{w_i}).
\]
Its input size is the total word length, not the cardinality of the infinite
underlying space. The allowed open sets are still all open sets of that space.

\begin{corollary}[Compact rank-word instances]
For a fixed alphabet of at least three letters, deciding an exact finite layer
budget or an exact finite query-depth budget on tower-list instances is
NP-complete.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Theorem~\ref{fo:thm:compact} and Proposition~\ref{fo:prop:sum-profile} identify the
entire admissible-word profile with the common supersequence profile of the
input list. Thus the same certificates and the same padding reduction apply.
\end{proof}

This is a precisely defined succinct-input result, not a claim about an
unspecified computational presentation of arbitrary topological spaces.

\section{Efficient approximation and exact algorithms}
\label{fo:sec:algorithms}

\subsection{Cyclic master words}
Fix a cyclic order of the $q$ letters. For each of its $q$ rotations, greedily
map the input poset into the corresponding infinite periodic word using
\eqref{fo:eq:greedy}. Next occurrences are computed by modular arithmetic, so no
infinite object is stored. Let $M_r$ be the largest position used for rotation
$r$. Return the shortest of the resulting prefixes, with its greedy rank map,
and perform balanced suffix queries.

\begin{theorem}[Additive approximation in query count]\label{fo:thm:approx}
For a finite nonempty $q$-colored poset, $q\ge2$, the cyclic algorithm runs in
$O(q(n+e))$ word operations and returns an admissible prefix of length at most
\begin{equation}\label{fo:eq:approx-layers}
 M_{\cyc}\le 1+(q-1)(\layers(P,c)-1).
\end{equation}
Its query tree has depth at most
\begin{equation}\label{fo:eq:approx-depth}
 \depth(P,c)+\ceil{\log_2(q-1)}.
\end{equation}
For two colors it is exact. For three colors it is within one additional
question of optimal.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $w$ be a shortest admissible word of length $L$. It is reduced. Choose the
cyclic rotation starting with its first letter. Greedily embed $w$ into that
periodic word. Its first position is $1$, and every successive letter advances
by at most $q-1$, giving a last position at most $1+(q-1)(L-1)$.
Composing this word embedding with a layering for $w$ produces a compatible
monotone map into the periodic prefix. The greedy map is pointwise least by
Proposition~\ref{fo:prop:greedy}, so its last position is no larger. Minimizing
across rotations proves \eqref{fo:eq:approx-layers}.

Since $M_{\cyc}\le(q-1)L$, taking logarithms and ceilings gives
\[
 \ceil{\log_2M_{\cyc}}
 \le\ceil{\log_2L}+\ceil{\log_2(q-1)}.
\]
The normal form converts this to the depth estimate. For $q=2$, the length
bound is $M_{\cyc}\le L$, forcing equality with the optimum.

Each rotation requires one graph scan. Every rank is at most $qn$, since at
most $n$ successive vertex assignments can increase a rank, each by at most
$q$. The ranks therefore occupy $O(\log(qn))$ bits. This proves the stated
word-operation bound; bit-operation costs include the corresponding integer
arithmetic factor.
\end{proof}

With alphabet $\{0,\ldots,q-1\}$ and rotation starting with $a$, the next
position of color $c$ at or after a 1-based lower bound $b$ is
\[
 b+\bigl(c-a-(b-1)\bmod q\bigr),
\]
where the remainder is in $\{0,\ldots,q-1\}$. This is the implementation used
in the verifier.

Empty positions may be deleted, and adjacent same-color occupied layers may
then be merged, before building a query tree. These improvements only help;
the theorem does not depend on applying them. The raw prefix-length bound is
sharp for this particular algorithm: a chain following the reverse cyclic
order has its first match at position $1$ in the best rotation and each later
match $q-1$ positions farther along. For example, the chain $2102$ has optimum
length $4$ but uses positions $1,3,5,7$ in the best $012$-cycle rotation.
Deleting its unused positions recovers the optimum, so this example is
\emph{not} a lower bound against improved approximation algorithms.

\subsection{Exact search on ideals}
Let $I$ be an order ideal, interpreted as the points already placed into early
layers. Reading color $a$ can process all points whose as-yet-unprocessed
predecessors have that color. More precisely, define
\begin{equation}\label{fo:eq:ideal-transition}
 T_a(I)=I\cup\{x\in P:\mathord\downarrow x\setminus I\subseteq c^{-1}(a)\}.
\end{equation}
It is an ideal. It can be computed by a topological scan that adds a vertex of
color $a$ as soon as all its predecessors have been added.

\begin{proposition}[Exact ideal automaton]\label{fo:prop:ideal}
Starting at $\varnothing$, a word is admissible exactly when the successive
transitions $T_a$ reach $P$. Breadth-first search of this finite state graph
therefore returns a shortest admissible word. If $J(P)$ is the number of order
ideals, the running time is $O(qJ(P)(n+e))$, with at most $J(P)\le2^n$ states.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
After a word prefix has been read, process every vertex as early as its color
and predecessors allow. Equation~\eqref{fo:eq:ideal-transition} is precisely the
maximal possible extension using the next single color: a point can be
included if and only if every still-unprocessed point below it can be included
in this same monochromatic layer. Induction on the word length shows that this
process contains the processed ideal of any other compatible partial layering.
Thus it reaches all vertices if and only if some complete layering exists.
Breadth-first search minimizes the number of transitions. Each of the $q$
transitions per reached state takes one graph scan.
\end{proof}

If a chain cover with chain lengths $n_1,\ldots,n_r$ is supplied, every ideal
intersects each chain in a prefix. Consequently
\[
 J(P)\le\prod_{i=1}^{r}(n_i+1).
\]
This gives a polynomial algorithm for each fixed number of covering chains,
including the familiar product-state algorithm for a fixed list of strings.
It is an XP-type bound in that parameter, not an assertion of fixed-parameter
tractability with an exponent independent of $r$.

The verifier uses exhaustive legal-question minimax as a separate algorithm.
For a nonempty possible-point mask $A$ that is not monochromatic,
\begin{align*}
 D(A)&=1+\min_{U}\max\{D(A\cap U),D(A\setminus U)\},\\
 L_{\rm tree}(A)&=\min_U\{L_{\rm tree}(A\cap U)+L_{\rm tree}(A\setminus U)\},
\end{align*}
where the minimum runs over open sets splitting $A$ into two nonempty parts.
A monochromatic nonempty mask has depth $0$ and one leaf. The ideal automaton
does not call these recurrences, making their agreement a useful independent
finite check of the normal form.

\section{Finite obstructions and bounded-budget decidability}
\label{fo:sec:obstructions}

\begin{theorem}[Small finite witnesses of a failed layer budget]
\label{fo:thm:obstruction}
For any poset $P$, finite or infinite, with $q$ colors, if
$\layers(P,c)>m$ for some $m\ge1$, there is an induced finite colored subposet
$Q$ with
\begin{equation}\label{fo:eq:obstruction}
 \len{Q}\le(m+1)q^m,\qquad \layers(Q,c|_Q)>m.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For each word $v\in\Sigma^m$, Theorem~\ref{fo:thm:chains} gives a reduced finite
chain word not embedding into $v$. Stop its greedy embedding at the first
failure. Each previous letter uses a strictly later position, so failure occurs
within at most $m+1$ letters. Selecting the corresponding chain points gives an
obstructing chain of at most that size.

Take the union of one such chain for each of the $q^m$ words. If the induced
subposet admitted a word of length at most $m$, append empty positions to obtain
one of length exactly $m$. Its stored obstructing chain would then embed,
a contradiction. This proves the bound.
\end{proof}

In particular, a bounded finite query budget on an arbitrary Alexandrov poset
is determined by its finite colored subposets. For a depth budget $d$, one may
put $m=2^d$ in \eqref{fo:eq:obstruction}. The numerical bound is an upper bound,
not a claim of optimality or of a polynomial kernel.

On a finite DAG, enumerating the $q^{2^d}$ words of length $2^d$ and applying
Proposition~\ref{fo:prop:greedy} gives an exact algorithm with time
\[
 O\!\left(q^{2^d}(q2^d+n+e)\right).
\]
Thus the problem is fixed-parameter tractable in the joint parameters $q,d$;
for fixed $q$, it is fixed-parameter tractable in the allowed query depth.
This does not conflict with NP-completeness when the depth is part of an
unbounded input.

\begin{proposition}[Finite witnesses for sum incompatibility]
If every finite subfamily of a family of colored spaces has sum layer count
at most $m$, then the entire sum has layer count at most $m$. If the bound fails,
it fails already for a subfamily of at most $q^m$ components.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
There are only $q^m$ candidate words of length exactly $m$. If none is admissible
on the whole sum, choose for each candidate a component on which it fails,
using Proposition~\ref{fo:prop:sum-profile}. Those at most $q^m$ components already
exclude every candidate. Padding an admissible shorter word shows that they
also exclude every word of length at most $m$.
\end{proof}

The two finite-witness statements have different domains. The induced-subposet
statement is not a general finite-subspace theorem for Hausdorff spaces. Every
finite subspace of a Hausdorff space is discrete, so it can have at most $q$
necessary color layers, while the compact towers above have arbitrarily large
layer complexity. Their obstructions lie in accumulation, not in finite
Hausdorff subspaces.

\section{Proof dependencies, scope, and formalization}
\label{fo:sec:audit}

\subsection{Proof dependency audit}
The proofs form a short dependency chain. Tree flattening and balanced suffix
search prove the normal form. The poset representation follows from monotone
maps and finite greedy embeddings; compact realization follows from cofinite
component neighborhoods. The adversarial next-occurrence argument proves the
universal-word constant. These feed the synchronization theorem and the exact
tower formula. Closed-set elimination supplies an independent topological
normal-form test.

The only imported mathematical result needed for the NP-completeness claim is
Theorem~\ref{fo:thm:MSCS}. The reduction to depth is proved here, including all
boundary and input-size conditions. None of the topological or sharp-bound
theorems depends on that complexity-theoretic input. No unproved conjecture is
used as a theorem. The assertion that zero additive error cannot be guaranteed
in polynomial time for three colors is, as usual, conditional on
$\PT\ne\NP$.

Indeed, Theorem~\ref{fo:thm:approx} gives one extra query for three colors. A
uniform guarantee of fewer than one extra query would, by integrality, be an
exact algorithm and would contradict Theorem~\ref{fo:thm:NP} if $\PT\ne\NP$.
In this precise integer-additive sense, the three-color approximation guarantee
is optimal under that assumption. No analogous optimality claim is made for
the bound $\ceil{\log_2(q-1)}$ when $q\ge4$.

\subsection{Relationship to the cited work}
The binary repository layer framework is the direct starting point. Its
binary counterexamples, binary alternating-chain algorithm, and binary
Cantor--Bendixson results are not claimed anew. The finite-output exact tower
formula, sharp synchronization family, and rounding-safe complexity transfer
are the extensions developed here.

The SCS connection also has precursors in typed-graph fusion, as documented in
\cite{LT}; a mere change of vocabulary would not be a priority claim. Likewise,
Selivanov's work places multi-valued partitions within a much broader
descriptive-set-theoretic setting \cite{Selivanov}. The present article is
specifically about finite worst-case open-query budgets, explicit word
certificates, and sharp constants. An independent comparison with the full
partition-hierarchy and typed-fusion literature remains appropriate before
submission as a novelty-certified paper.

\subsection{A concrete proof-assistant plan}
The finite part has a small trusted mathematical interface. A useful Lean
formalization would proceed in the following order.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Define finite words, subsequence embeddings, reduced words, and
color-respecting monotone maps to finite chains. Verify the greedy next-position
recurrence and its least-map property.
\item Formalize finite binary query trees over a topology and the no-before-yes
leaf order. Prove the suffix-open induction, then construct balanced suffix
trees with the integer bound $m\le2^d$ rather than analytic logarithms.
\item Prove the chain-language equivalence, the universal-word upper bound,
and the adversarial lower-bound witness. These yield synchronization with
purely finite arithmetic at the final step.
\item Add a certified checker for a supplied word and rank map. A search
program can remain untrusted: its positive certificates require only color
agreement and monotonicity along input edges. Negative certificates can use
an explicit failed chain for each candidate word or a verified ideal-state
search, depending on the budget.
\item Formalize the convergent towers and their neighborhood property, then
their rank-word profiles, the finite-height interval construction, and the
exact tower invariant. The topological definitions should be kept separate
from executable finite certificates.
\end{enumerate}
The present archive contains no Lean file and makes no claim that any of these
steps has been kernel-checked. The Python assertions are reproducible evidence
about finite instances, not replacements for that formalization.

\begin{writenote}[Status in ProveIt]
None of these steps has been carried out in ProveIt, for Part~II or for
Part~I: no Lean or Rocq file of the repository mentions open-query games, and
the Lean library in the enclosing \path{SetTheory/Cardinals} directory is
about large cardinals.
\end{writenote}

\section{Further research questions and proposed directions}
\label{fo:sec:questions}

The following questions define a continuation program. They are not assertions
that every formulation is a previously published open problem; their literature
status should be checked individually before a priority claim.

\begin{question}[Exact invariants beyond convergent towers]
For a general, possibly nonmetrizable or noncompact Hausdorff space of finite
Cantor--Bendixson height, what local topological conditions make the uniform
bound $(q-1)h+s$ attainable? Can the invariant be characterized by the word
profiles that accumulate at its nonisolated points?
\end{question}
The upper-bound proof uses only rank neighborhoods. Sharpness requires enough
incompatible color patterns, and the tower construction supplies them by
repeating each type cofinally. Separating those two ingredients is a concrete
way to extend the classification without assuming that rank alone is always
sufficient.

\begin{writenote}[Answered at $q=2$; open for $q\ge3$]
For two labels Part~I answers both parts of this question. On every Hausdorff
space of last derivative rank $h<\omega$ the bound $h+s$ is attained for $s=1$
(the parity target of \cref{lem:parity}) and, when $\lvert X^{(h)}\rvert\ge2$,
for $s=2$ (the lower-bound proof of \cref{thm:height}); no condition beyond
the Hausdorff property is needed, and $\operatorname{sm}_2(X)=\sm(X)$ depends only on $h$ and on
whether $X^{(h)}$ is a singleton, by \eqref{eq:height}. The question stands for
$q\ge3$.
\end{writenote}

\begin{question}[Small sharp synchronization families]
What is the smallest number of reduced words of length at most $m$ whose common
supersequence length is $(q-1)m+1$? What is the smallest number needed merely
to force the maximal depth $\ceil{\log_2((q-1)m+1)}$?
\end{question}
Our full family has $q(q-1)^{m-1}$ words. For $m=2$ the complete ordered-pair
family is deletion-critical for its extremal layer count. For larger $m$,
minimizing the obstruction family would substantially reduce both finite and
compact examples.

\begin{question}[Optimal finite obstruction bounds]
Determine the least function $B(q,m)$ such that every failure of an $m$-layer
budget on a poset is witnessed by at most $B(q,m)$ points. How far can the bound
$(m+1)q^m$ be improved, and which colored posets are minimal obstructions?
\end{question}
The proof supplies explicit chains per candidate word but does not share their
vertices efficiently. Critical SCS families are a natural first test class.

\begin{question}[Width and cover-graph parameters]
For three colors, can the ideal-state algorithm be improved from an
$(n+1)^r$-type bound for an $r$-chain cover to a fixed-parameter algorithm with
parameter $r$? Which cover-graph restrictions permit polynomial exact
optimization when width is unbounded?
\end{question}
The finite-depth parameter is already fixed-parameter tractable by the word
enumeration above. Width is different: long chains do not by themselves cause
hardness, but synchronization among many chains does.

\begin{question}[One-extra-query algorithms with four or more colors]
Does every fixed $q\ge4$ admit a polynomial-time algorithm within one question
of optimal, or is a larger additive gap necessary under an appropriate
complexity assumption?
\end{question}
The cyclic construction gives $\ceil{\log_2(q-1)}$ extra questions. Exact
three-color hardness only excludes zero error; it does not prove the cyclic
bound optimal for larger alphabets. Improved SCS approximation or a direct
query-tree method could give a better result.

\begin{question}[Compact profile representations]
Can the full word profile of a finite colored DAG be realized by a compact
metrizable tower description of polynomial size without listing every chain
word? Which sharing operations on descriptions preserve the profile exactly?
\end{question}
The current construction is exact but can duplicate exponentially many chains.
A compositional representation of shared suborders would connect the topology
more directly to symbolic verification and succinct optimization.

\begin{question}[Expected query cost and nonuniform costs]
When points have a probability distribution, or queries have different costs,
what replaces the layer-count logarithm? Can one optimize jointly over
admissible layerings and ordered coding trees without enumerating all words?
\end{question}
Balancing by number of layers minimizes a worst-case bound, not expected cost.
The flattening theorem still supplies an ordered structure, but the masses of
the layers and the choice of word become essential data.

\begin{question}[Finite-output transfinite games]
What analogue of the word profile describes well-founded, non-uniformly bounded
query trees and games of ordinal length? Which synchronization laws survive at
limit stages?
\end{question}
Neither $\ceil{\log_2 L}$ nor finite common-supersequence padding should be
transferred mechanically to ordinal lengths. The treatment must specify the
winning condition at limits and the kind of trees being ranked.

\begin{question}[Restricted query bases and noisy answers]
How do the results change when only a designated basis of open sets may be
queried, or when finitely many answers may be incorrect? What closure conditions
on a query family retain a useful ordered-layer normal form?
\end{question}
The proof of Theorem~\ref{fo:thm:normal} identifies the exact operations to
investigate: finite intersections in a yes-branch and unions across a no-branch.
Without these operations, the current equivalence need not persist.

\section{Conclusion}
The information needed to combine finite-output open-query algorithms is not
just their depths. It is the set of color words in which their possible answers
can be arranged with open suffixes. For posets this profile is an exact chain
supersequence constraint; for compact convergent towers the same constraints
are imposed by accumulation.

This representation yields three quantitative conclusions. First, the sharp
uniform sum bound is $(q-1)m+1$ layers, with maximal depth increase
$\ceil{\log_2q}$. Second, finite Cantor--Bendixson height gives the sharp bound
$(q-1)h+s$, and the worst-case invariant of a finite sum of convergent towers is
computed exactly by \eqref{fo:eq:tower-invariant}. Third, the introduction of a
third label changes exact finite optimization from polynomial time to
NP-completeness while still permitting a one-extra-query algorithm. All three
conclusions have explicit witnesses and constructive proofs, and the finite
part admits small, independently checkable certificates.

\section{Why countable compact Hausdorff spaces are scattered}\label{app:compact}

This appendix supplies the fact used after \cref{cor:allordinals} without
requiring the full ordinal classification of countable compact spaces.

\begin{lemma}
A nonempty compact Hausdorff space with no isolated points has at least
$2^{\aleph_0}$ points.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Construct nonempty open sets $U_s$ indexed by finite binary strings. Start with
the whole space. Inside each $U_s$, select two distinct points; this is possible
because no nonempty open set is a singleton. By regularity, choose nonempty
open neighborhoods $U_{s0},U_{s1}$ with disjoint closures contained in $U_s$.

For each infinite binary string $z$, the compact sets
$\overline{U_{z\upharpoonright n}}$ are nonempty and nested, so their intersection
is nonempty. Choose one point from each intersection. Distinct binary strings
split at some level and hence give points in disjoint closed sets. This produces
an injection of $2^\N$ into the space.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}
Every countable compact Hausdorff space is scattered.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
If a space is not scattered, some nonempty subspace $B$ has no isolated points.
Its closure $F$ has no isolated points either: an isolated point of $F$ would,
by density, belong to $B$ and be isolated in $B$. In a compact Hausdorff space,
$F$ is compact Hausdorff. The preceding lemma would make $F$ uncountable, a
contradiction.
\end{proof}

This proof also shows why no additional set-theoretic hypothesis such as the
continuum hypothesis is needed. Uncountability of $2^\N$ is sufficient.

\section{A proof audit and implementation checklist}

The following points isolate possible sources of off-by-one or topology errors.

\paragraph{Zero questions.}
A constant target has $L=1$, not $L=0$, on a nonempty space. Thus its depth is
$\ceil{\log_2 1}=0$. The empty space is assigned $\sm=0$ separately.

\paragraph{Derivative rank.}
The integer $h$ is the index of the \emph{last nonempty} derivative, not the
height. The height is $h+1$. A convergent sequence has $h=1$ and height $2$.

\paragraph{First closure.}
The general estimate is $F_n\subseteq X^{(n-1)}$ for $n\geq1$, not
$F_n\subseteq X^{(n)}$. The extra first step is exactly what creates the two
oriented lengths $h+1$ and $h+2$ for a parity target.

\paragraph{Hausdorff separation.}
The lower bound with two terminal points uses disjoint open neighborhoods.
The finite-height theorem is therefore stated for Hausdorff spaces. Its proof
is not silently applied to arbitrary $T_0$ posets.

\paragraph{Arbitrary answers.}
The binary-search proof wins on every answer sequence, including one whose
state becomes empty. In the $\omega$-length certification strategy, an
all-no certification path has empty final intersection. There is no hidden
assumption that the Hider fixes a point beforehand.

\paragraph{Infinite sums.}
In the oriented finite-profile formula, an unbounded supremum of integers is
recorded as $\infty$, meaning ``no finite decomposition''. The separate
certification argument is needed to obtain the actual ordinal value $\omega$.

\paragraph{Independent checking.}
The minimax implementation enumerates all open queries. It does not import the
closed-layer answer as its return value. The mathematical proof and the
finite computational check are deliberately distinct.
