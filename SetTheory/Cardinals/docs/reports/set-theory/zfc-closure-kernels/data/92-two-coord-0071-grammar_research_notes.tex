\section{A separate search for the missing grammar state}
\label{sec:grammar-search}
The routed machine has 128 binary sequence-decoder states and 117 other
working states.  A grammar with at most 127 decoder states, the same caller
contracts, and maximum source-path zero run at most six would therefore
provide a direct route to 244 working states.  This statement is a state
accounting implication, not a correctness certificate for an unbuilt
candidate.  The literal table, register-machine correspondence, parametric
instruction contracts, whole-frame composition and blank initialization
would still have to be checked.

We searched the same closed-atom semantic words using four additional
classes of proposals.  A coordinated deletion rewrites every immediate
caller of a selected grammar state before collecting unreachable rules.
A coordinated context proposal rewrites the top one or two splits of an
existing state word to satisfy the projection equations of a chosen
microcode caller.  A semantic spelling proposal replaces a substring of
the large axiom-constructor word by an existing atomic macro with the same
finest closed-atom expansion.  Finally, a semantic deletion removes a
state private to the axiom constructor and its dependent ancestors, and
reparses the resulting frontier using unaffected states and equivalent
atomic macros.  These proposals preserve the intended source semantics
by construction, but the independent grammar checker remains the authority
for accepting an output object.

For frontier reparsing, dynamic programming minimizes the sum of newly
introduced binary concatenations while assigning cost zero to an already
available state or atom.  The sum can count a newly shared rule more than
once.  Consequently this is a useful proposal heuristic, not an exact
minimum-grammar algorithm.  The final live-rule count is recomputed after
each proposal.

\subsection{The routing-only state and an alternative encoding basin}
In the frozen 128-rule candidate, the closure of the 14 source entry roots
contains 127 rules.  The closure of those roots and all caller routes
contains one additional rule.  Write
\[
 A=\mathtt{eijkKC},\quad B=\mathtt{qkjG},\quad
 C=\mathtt{VqkjGqkjGVqkjG},\quad D=\mathtt{VqkjGS}.
\]
The additional rule spells $ABCD$ and has children $AB$ and $CD$.
The two callers which replace old framework state 177 share that rule.
One caller requires projections $01\mapsto B$ and $11\mapsto D$; the other
requires $00\mapsto A$ and $10\mapsto C$.

\paragraph{A forced-word observation.}
If the two callers use the same binary concatenation state $u$, those four
projection equations force $u=((A,B),(C,D))$ and hence force its word to be
$ABCD$.  Indeed, the first two projections of its left child force that
child's ordered children to be $A,B$, and the two projections of its right
child force its ordered children to be $C,D$.  This conclusion uses the
literal semantic-word grammar, not a lower bound for general Turing
machines.  Giving the callers distinct existing states can avoid the
extra word.

A finite examination of the frozen state's words found that the caller
requiring $00\mapsto A$, $10\mapsto C$ could use only two state words even
at the string-pattern level: $ABCD$ itself and the complete 702-symbol
axiom-constructor word.  This motivated an alternative encoding in which
the axiom-constructor root is also that caller's routing state.  Its long
prefix and suffix were reconstructed by frontier reparsing; a finite
rotation beam then repaired a 131-rule candidate from maximum zero run
seven to six.  The repair reached zero offending nodes after 6,694
neighbor evaluations.  The independent local grammar audit accepted the
131-rule object, corresponding to 248 working states under the ordinary
six-zero scanner.  Subsequent optimization reduced its grammar to 129
rules in the initial experiment.  These objects are alternate search
starting points and do not improve the published 245-state candidate.

\subsection{Why a suffix-only zero condition can be misleading}
For a finite full binary tree with edge labels zero on the left and one
on the right, the maximum length of a zero run anywhere in a root-to-leaf
word equals the maximum length of a terminal zero run among all such
words.  To prove the nontrivial inequality, take any zero run ending at a
node and continue from that node along left edges until reaching a leaf.
The resulting terminal zero run is at least as long.  The reverse
inequality is immediate.

It follows that imposing a five-zero terminal bound on every source leaf
is exactly the same restriction as imposing a five-zero global bound.
A bound only on selected instruction types can be weaker; its benefit
must be demonstrated using those types.  In the frozen candidate, every
source leaf with a six-zero suffix is an increment or decrement: there
are 49 decrement sites and one increment site.  The 99 failing guarded
instruction cases of the second scanner contraction consist of the two
branches of those 49 decrements and the single increment case.  Thus the
observed failure pattern does not by itself provide a weaker grammar
constraint than the five-zero objective for this source tree.

\subsection{A conditional obstruction from caller 183 and a constructive escape}
The five-zero objective has a structural obstruction in a commonly visited
routing basin.  Let
\[
 C=\mathtt{VqkjGqkjGVqkjG},\qquad B=\mathtt{qkjG},\qquad
 E=\mathtt{VqkjG}.
\]
Old framework caller 183 on reading one requires \emph{both} projections
$01\mapsto B$ and $11\mapsto E$.  In the original routed grammar its target
is $C$.  Retaining this choice is not compatible with the five-zero
objective, irrespective of how many grammar states are allowed, provided
the original axiom-constructor spelling is retained.

Here is the precise conditional statement and its proof.  For an expanded
binary expression $T$, let $\lambda(T)$ be the number of initial left
edges before its first instruction leaf.  The immutable atomic definitions
contain
\[
 e=(\operatorname{dec}(1),\ldots),\qquad
 V=(\mathtt{eijk},l),\qquad P=(C,\mathtt{eijkKCeijkfgW}).
\]
The first identity gives $\lambda(e)=1$.  Every concatenation parse of the
four-symbol word $\mathtt{eijk}$ starts with $e$, so it has leading length
at least two.  Hence $\lambda(V)\geq3$.  The equation $C.01=B$ forces the
left child of $C$ to have the form $(x,B)$, where $x$ is a nonempty prefix
of $C$ and therefore starts with $V$.  It follows that
\[
 \lambda(C)=2+\lambda(x)\ \geq\ 2+\lambda(V)\ \geq\ 5,
 \qquad \lambda(P)=1+\lambda(C)\ \geq\ 6.
\]
The original 702-symbol axiom-constructor word contains $P$.  Its expanded
source consequently has a zero run of length at least six.  This proves
the conditional obstruction for the orientation-preserving, one-parse-per-
semantic-word grammar model.  The finite immutable source identities,
the two caller equations and the occurrence of $P$ are checked by
\texttt{check\_routing183\_obstruction.py}; the accompanying JSON records
the exact source hash.  This is a human mathematical proof with checked
finite premises, not a proof-assistant theorem or a lower bound on
arbitrary Turing machines.

The existing shared routing-only state $u=((A,B),(C,D))$ cannot simply
replace caller 183's target.  Although $u.01=B$, it has
$u.11=D=\mathtt{VqkjGS}$, whereas this caller requires
$E=\mathtt{VqkjG}$.  This second equation blocks the apparently free reuse.
The obstruction can instead be escaped by giving caller 183 a fresh
routing word whose left and right children end at $B$ and $E$, and then
reparsing the source word $C$ with first child $V$.

A finite construction from a 135-rule, two-offender grammar found six
such repair plans with 137 grammar rules and maximum zero run exactly
five.  The recorded representative routes caller 183 to
$\mathtt{jGqkjGqkjGVqkjG}$ and splits the source word $C$ as
$(V,\mathtt{qkjGqkjGVqkjG})$.  Its independent local audit passes all source,
orientation and caller constraints.  The 137-rule object has ordinary
state accounting $137+117=254$; with the proposed second scanner
contraction its accounting would be $137+116=253$.  No corresponding full
machine was built or claimed.  The significance is that the five-zero
constraint is feasible once this caller coupling is removed.

A final 300-second hard-five-zero search started from this construction,
used the original 14 source roots with no bootstrap locks, and targeted
128 rules.  It completed 6,473,735 proposals with seed 181061 and reduced
the grammar to 135 rules, with maximum zero run five and depth 52.
The independent audit accepted all 6,460 ordered source instruction
leaves with no six-zero suffix.  Its ordinary state accounting is 252;
the proposed additional scanner contraction would give 251.  No full
table for this grammar was built.  Across the ten completed randomized
runs in this lane, 54,653,830 proposals were recorded.  The exact results,
seeds, proposal counts, source hashes and checkpoint hashes are in
\texttt{search-summary.json}.  These time-bounded searches do not
establish a minimum grammar size.

The conditional proof also isolates two other legitimate directions:
change the semantic spelling to unfold the problematic $P$, or allow
multiple decoder states with the same semantic word but different parse
trees.  The latter would separate a source role from a routing role but
must count both states explicitly.  Neither possibility is excluded by
the conditional obstruction or by the unsuccessful compression runs.

\subsection{Recorded scope}
The search logs distinguish completed time-bounded runs from runs stopped
to reallocate computation.  Proposal counts are not counts of distinct
grammars and are not exhaustive certificates.  Saved objects marked
\texttt{ABSTRACT\_ROUTING\_GRAMMAR\_ONLY} are not complete machine proofs.
The local audits attached to selected outputs validate the finite routing,
orientation, state-accounting and AST conditions only.  None of the
negative searches proves a state lower bound or rules out a different
source formalism.
