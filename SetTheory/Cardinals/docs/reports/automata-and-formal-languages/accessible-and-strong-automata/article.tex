\begin{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Part I} (accessible automata).
For each fixed integer alphabet size $k\ge2$, let $a_n$ count rooted accessible complete deterministic transition structures on $n$ states, up to state isomorphism, with no chosen accepting states. We prove an expansion to every fixed order of $a_n/S(kn+1,n)$. An exact first-failure decomposition has two relevant boundaries: $n-h$ fixed produces a contractive renewal kernel, while $h$ fixed introduces finitely many exact small-state counts at each order. Independent geometric waiting times control the left tail; a typical-coupon conditioning argument suppresses the middle; and a uniform-sum integral controls all right-endpoint remainders. A patched trial sequence converts coefficient matching into a rigorous asymptotic theorem. We give a finite coefficient algorithm, the first three corrections, an elementary saddle carrier, and Lambert--Newton inversion with integer threshold brackets. The leading equivalent is classical. This article supplies a self-contained derivation and higher-order extension; it makes no exhaustive claim of publication priority.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Part II} (strongly connected automata).
For complete deterministic transition structures with a fixed alphabet of $k\ge2$ named letters, we construct every fixed inverse-power correction to the classical strong-connectivity count. A positive restricted-growth-word model yields a renewal equation with two different boundary scales. Uniform estimates and a weighted contraction prove the expansion with a remainder at each fixed order; a direct factorial-convolution argument transfers it through the classical formal logarithm to strongly connected structures. Finite Gaussian and Fuss--Catalan moment calculations produce the coefficients without fitting. We give third-order formulas, smooth and qualified integer inverses, and a self-contained connected cyclic-lift derivation of Liskovets's exact unrooted divisor formula. Each fixed divisor has an all-order expansion, while a uniform bound controls the omitted divisor sectors. These sectors are resolved only with larger sectors kept exact: a finite algebraic truncation of the identity sector cannot resolve an exponentially smaller automorphism contribution. The leading equivalents and exact enumeration identities are classical. Historical priority of the higher-order refinement remains qualified by incomplete access to older sources.
\end{abstract}

\part{Accessible automata (manuscript 09)}

\section{Objects and main result}\label{asa:acc:sec:objects}
Fix $k\ge2$. An object consists of a finite state set, a distinguished initial state, and one total transition map for each of $k$ individually distinguished input letters. Every state must be reachable from the initial state. Isomorphisms preserve the initial state and each input letter. We do not choose an accepting-state subset, and do not quotient by permutations of the letters. The resulting sequences are OEIS A006689 for $k=2$ and A006690 for $k=3$ \cite{oeis2,oeis3}.

A rooted accessible structure has trivial state automorphism group: every state is the image of the root under some word, and an automorphism fixes each such image. Consequently the number on labels $[n]$ with root fixed to $1$ is $(n-1)!a_n$, while allowing an arbitrary labeled root gives $n!a_n$. Choosing any accepting-state subset multiplies these counts by $2^n$. These conventions are important when comparing formulas in the literature.

Write $S(m,n)$ for a Stirling number of the second kind, and set
\begin{equation}\label{asa:acc:eq:constants}
\begin{gathered}
T_n=S(kn+1,n),\qquad d=k-1,\\
t=k(1-e^{-t})>0,\qquad v=t/k,\qquad \rho=e^{-t}=1-v,\\
c=1-k\rho=kv-k+1,\qquad q=\rho v^d,\qquad \beta=e^{-d}/q.
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
The function $k(1-e^{-t})-t$ is strictly concave, initially increasing, and eventually negative, so it has precisely one positive zero. Equivalently
\[
t=k+W_0(-ke^{-k}),\qquad v=1+\frac{W_0(-ke^{-k})}{k},
\]
where $W_0$ is the principal Lambert branch. All limits below hold for fixed $k$ and fixed requested expansion order. We exclude $k=1$ and make no growing-alphabet uniformity claim.

\begin{theorem}\label{asa:acc:thm:main}
There are effectively computable constants $c_j=c_j(k)$, $c_0=c$, such that for every fixed integer $J\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{asa:acc:eq:main}
\frac{a_n}{S(kn+1,n)}=\sum_{j=0}^{J}\frac{c_j}{n^j}+O_{k,J}(n^{-J-1}).
\end{equation}
Each coefficient is obtainable by finite rational operations and differentiation in $k,v$, together with finitely many exact values $a_h(k)$. Only $h$ with $dh+1\le j$ can affect $c_j$. In particular
\begin{equation}\label{asa:acc:eq:c1}
c_1=\frac{k\rho v}{2c}.
\end{equation}
The small-state boundary first affects $c_k$; for $k=2$ it contributes the term $-cv^2$ to $c_2$.
\end{theorem}

The leading constant and leading enumeration scale are classical, due to Korshunov and their later reformulation and simplification by Bassino--Nicaud and Lebensztayn \cite{korshunov1978,bassino,lebensztayn}. The principal contribution here is the two-boundary all-fixed-orders argument, including finite-state forcing and inversion. The bounded literature audit in Section~\ref{asa:acc:sec:provenance} does not establish priority.

\section{An exact first failure identity}
Scan outgoing transitions in discovery order of states and in the fixed order of input letters. Including the root as the first symbol gives a restricted-growth word of length $kn+1$: its first occurrence labels are $1,2,\ldots,n$ in that order. Let $Y_m$ be the number of distinct symbols in the first $m$ positions. Accessibility is equivalent to
\begin{equation}\label{asa:acc:eq:checkpoint}
Y_{kh+1}\ge h+1\qquad(0\le h<n).
\end{equation}
Indeed, after processing $h$ discovered states, another state must already be waiting unless all $n$ states have been processed. Conversely these inequalities ensure the row-by-row exploration is defined throughout and reaches all states.

All restricted-growth words with $n$ symbols are counted by $T_n$. If \eqref{asa:acc:eq:checkpoint} first fails at $h$, the prefix has exactly $h$ symbols: the previous checkpoint forces at least $h$, and failure forces at most $h$. This prefix is an accessible $h$-state encoding. Its suffix of length $k(n-h)$ must introduce $n-h$ new symbols, without further accessibility restrictions. Put
\begin{equation}\label{asa:acc:eq:R}
R_h(r)=\frac{(kr)!}{r!}[z^{kr}]e^{hz}(e^z-1)^r\qquad(r\ge0).
\end{equation}
The factor $1/r!$ removes the order-independent labels of the new symbols. Existing symbols are individually distinguished. This proves
\begin{equation}\label{asa:acc:eq:renewal}
T_n=a_n+\sum_{h=1}^{n-1}a_h R_h(n-h).
\end{equation}
For example, at $k=2$, the cases $n=2,3$ are $15=12+3$ and $301=216+25+60$.

The following equivalent forms will be useful:
\begin{align}
R_h(r)&=\sum_{j=r}^{kr}\binom{kr}{j}h^{kr-j}S(j,r),\label{asa:acc:eq:suffix-sum}\\
&=\mathsf h_{dr}(h,h+1,\ldots,h+r),\label{asa:acc:eq:homogeneous}\\
&=\frac1{r!}\sum_{i=0}^{r}(-1)^i\binom ri(h+r-i)^{kr}.\label{asa:acc:eq:finite-diff}
\end{align}
Here $\mathsf h_m$ is the complete homogeneous symmetric polynomial of degree $m$. To see \eqref{asa:acc:eq:homogeneous}, the ordinary generating function for appending a suffix introducing exactly $r$ new symbols is
\[
x^r\prod_{j=0}^{r}(1-(h+j)x)^{-1}.
\]
The $r$ first occurrences supply the factors $x$, with $h+j$ symbols available between the $j$th and $(j+1)$st first occurrences. Equation~\eqref{asa:acc:eq:finite-diff} follows directly from \eqref{asa:acc:eq:R}.

Define
\begin{equation}\label{asa:acc:eq:kernel}
b_n=\frac{a_n}{T_n},\qquad K(n,h)=\frac{T_hR_h(n-h)}{T_n}.
\end{equation}
Thus $0\le b_n\le1$, $b_1=1$, and
\begin{equation}\label{asa:acc:eq:b-renewal}
b_n+\sum_{h=1}^{n-1}K(n,h)b_h=1.
\end{equation}
There is no $h=0$ term. The kernel describes an unrestricted prefix event; multiplying it by $b_h$ imposes accessibility of that prefix.

\section{The fixed saddle and its expansion}\label{asa:acc:sec:saddle}
Let $A(z)=e^z-1$. We use only the multipliers $g_0(z)=z^{-1}$ and $g_h(z)=z^{kh}e^{hz}/A(z)^h$ for fixed integers $h\ge1$. They are analytic and nonzero near $t$, and the canceled-integrand argument below controls the rest of the contour. Use the coefficient-integral notation
\[
I_g(n)=[z^{kn}]A(z)^n g(z),\qquad g_0(z)=z^{-1}.
\]
For the multipliers used below the product has a well-defined Laurent expansion at zero, so this coefficient is unambiguous. The positive saddle of $\log A(z)-k\log z$ is $t$. Write
\begin{equation}\label{asa:acc:eq:cumulants}
\kappa_m=\left.(z\partial_z)^m\log A(z)\right|_{z=t};\qquad
\kappa_1=k,\quad \kappa_2=kc>0.
\end{equation}
These are the cumulants of a positive-Poisson random variable $Z_t$, with
\[
\Prob(Z_t=j)=\frac{t^j}{j!A(t)},\quad j\ge1.
\]
In particular, the exact coefficient interpretation is
\[
T_n=\frac{(kn+1)!}{n!}\frac{A(t)^n}{t^{kn+1}}
\Prob(Z_{t,1}+\cdots+Z_{t,n}=kn+1).
\]

\begin{lemma}\label{asa:acc:lem:saddle}
For every fixed $J\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{asa:acc:eq:T-expansion}
T_n=C_T\beta^n n^{dn+1/2}
\left(\sum_{j=0}^{J}t_jn^{-j}+O_{k,J}(n^{-J-1})\right),
\qquad C_T=\frac1{v\sqrt{2\pi c}},\quad t_0=1.
\end{equation}
For these specified multipliers, the analogous expansion for $I_g(n)$ has leading factor
\[
\frac{A(t)^nt^{-kn}g(t)}{\sqrt{2\pi n\kappa_2}}.
\]
All coefficients are effectively obtained by Gaussian moments.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
On the circle $z=te^{i\theta}$, expand
\[
\log A(te^{i\theta})-\log A(t)-ik\theta
=-\frac{\kappa_2\theta^2}{2}+\sum_{m\ge3}\frac{\kappa_m(i\theta)^m}{m!}.
\]
The coefficients of $A$ are positive and its support has span $1$. Hence $|A(te^{i\theta})|<A(t)$ for $0<|\theta|\le\pi$; compact arcs away from zero are exponentially negligible. Near zero, the real part is at most $-C\theta^2$. Set $\epsilon=n^{-1/2}$ and $\theta=\epsilon u/\sqrt{\kappa_2}$. Taylor expansion on a shrinking central window, with a Gaussian-dominated remainder, gives the coefficient of $\epsilon^{2j}$ in
\begin{equation}\label{asa:acc:eq:Gaussian}
\E\left[
\frac{g(te^{i\epsilon Z/\sqrt{\kappa_2}})}{g(t)}
\exp\left\{\sum_{m\ge3}
\frac{\kappa_m(i\epsilon Z/\sqrt{\kappa_2})^m}{m!\epsilon^2}\right\}\right],
\end{equation}
where $Z$ is standard normal, $\E Z^{2j}=(2j-1)!!$, and odd moments vanish. This is a formal rule for each finite coefficient, not an expectation requiring convergence of the infinite series. To obtain order $J$, only finitely many terms are used. Taylor's formula, integrated against the Gaussian bound, gives the stated $O(n^{-J-1})$ remainder.

Finally $T_n=((kn+1)!/n!)I_{g_0}(n)$. Applying factorial Stirling expansions yields \eqref{asa:acc:eq:T-expansion}. For a completely finite rule, let $P_0(X)$ be the series generated by \eqref{asa:acc:eq:Gaussian} for $g_0$ and let $B_m(x)$ denote a Bernoulli polynomial. Then
\begin{equation}\label{asa:acc:eq:tau-algorithm}
\tau(X):=\sum_{j\ge0}t_jX^j
=P_0(X)\exp\left\{\sum_{j\ge1}\frac{(-1)^{j+1}}{j(j+1)}
\left(\frac{B_{j+1}(2)}{k^j}-B_{j+1}(1)\right)X^j\right\}.
\end{equation}
Every series here is truncated at the requested order. Simplification of the leading factor using $t=kv$ and $A(t)=v/\rho$ gives $C_T$ and $\beta$ in \eqref{asa:acc:eq:T-expansion}.
\end{proof}

A detail needed for arbitrary fixed $k$ is worth making explicit. Later $g$ contains $A(z)^{-h}$; this quotient need not be analytic throughout $|z|\le t$, because nonzero zeros of $e^z-1$ may lie there. The local expansion only requires analyticity near positive $t$. Off-central bounds must be applied to the canceled product
\[
A(z)^{n-h}z^{kh}e^{hz},
\]
which is analytic and has the required strict modulus gap for fixed $h$ and $n>h$. No global boundedness of the meromorphic quotient is assumed.

\section{Three region bounds}\label{asa:acc:sec:three-region}
\begin{proposition}\label{asa:acc:prop:tails}
For each fixed $H\ge1$, there exist $a,\varepsilon,\eta>0$ such that
\begin{align}
\sum_{H\le h\le an}K(n,h)&=O(n^{-dH-1}),\label{asa:acc:eq:left-tail}\\
\sum_{an\le h\le(1-\varepsilon)n}K(n,h)&=O(e^{-\eta n}),\label{asa:acc:eq:middle-tail}\\
K(n,n-r)&\le C e^{-\eta r}\quad(1\le r\le\varepsilon n).\label{asa:acc:eq:right-tail}
\end{align}
Constants may depend on $k,H$. Rounding interval endpoints has no effect.
\end{proposition}

\subsection{The left boundary on an independent product space}
Take independent uniform coupon draws from $n$ labels. Let $G_i$ be the number of additional draws from the $(i-1)$st discovery through the $i$th discovery. Then $G_1=1$ and the $G_i$ are independent geometric variables on $\{1,2,\ldots\}$ with success probability $(n-i+1)/n$. This follows from the iid strong Markov property at discovery times; the conditional waiting law depends only on the number already collected.

On this product space with coordinatewise order, both events
\[
\mathcal A=\{G_1+\cdots+G_{h+1}\le kh+1\},\qquad
\mathcal B=\{G_1+\cdots+G_n\le kn+1\}
\]
are decreasing. Harris association gives $\Prob(\mathcal A\mid\mathcal B)\ge\Prob(\mathcal A)$, or the reverse inequality for the complement of $\mathcal A$. One elementary proof of the association used here applies the one-variable identity
\[
\operatorname{Cov}(f(U),g(U))=\tfrac12\E[(f(U)-f(U'))(g(U)-g(U'))]\ge0
\]
for two monotone functions in the same direction, then inducts over independent coordinates by conditional covariance. Thus the argument also applies to unbounded geometric coordinates and decreasing indicators.

Conditioning $\mathcal B$ gives a uniform surjection of length $kn+1$ onto $n$ labels. Each partition into $n$ blocks has exactly $n!$ labelings; its restricted-growth encoding is therefore uniform. Consequently
\begin{align}
K(n,h)&=\Prob(Y_{kh+1}=h\mid\mathcal B)\notag\\
&\le\Prob(Y_{kh+1}\le h\mid\mathcal B)
\le\Prob(Y_{kh+1}\le h)
\le \binom nh(h/n)^{kh+1}=:u_h.\label{asa:acc:eq:coupon-left}
\end{align}
The last bound is a union bound over $h$-subsets of the labels. Direct division gives
\[
\frac{u_{h+1}}{u_h}
=\frac{n-h}{h+1}\left(\frac{h+1}{h}\right)^{kh+1}
\left(\frac{h+1}{n}\right)^k
\le 2e^k\left(\frac{h+1}{n}\right)^d.
\]
Choose $a$ small enough that this is at most $1/2$ for $h\le an$ and large $n$. Then the left-tail sum is at most $2u_H=O(n^{-dH-1})$. The same ratio argument, after multiplying by $(n/h)^p$ for any fixed $p\ge0$, gives
\begin{equation}\label{asa:acc:eq:left-weighted}
\sum_{H\le h\le an}K(n,h)(n/h)^p=O(n^{p-dH-1}).
\end{equation}
The association is on independent waiting times. It is not a claim that fixed-time occupancy indicators form an independent product space.

\subsection{A typical coupon count removes the compact middle}
Choose an integer $M=n/v+O(1)$ and draw coupons uniformly from $M$ labels. Conditional on $Y_{kn+1}=n$, each partition of $[kn+1]$ into $n$ blocks has exactly $\fall{M}{n}$ labelings. It induces the same uniform partition law and hence the same $K(n,h)$.

The exact conditioning probability is
\begin{equation}\label{asa:acc:eq:conditioning}
\Prob_M(Y_{kn+1}=n)=\frac{\fall{M}{n}T_n}{M^{kn+1}}=\Theta(n^{-1/2}).
\end{equation}
The estimate follows by inserting \eqref{asa:acc:eq:T-expansion} and Stirling's formula for $M!/(M-n)!$. The exponential rate cancels because $v=1-e^{-kv}$; rounding $M$ by $O(1)$ shifts its saddle by only $O(1)$ and changes the positive limiting prefactor by at most constant factors.

For $h/n=x$ in a fixed compact subinterval of $(0,1)$,
\begin{equation}\label{asa:acc:eq:profile}
\E_M Y_{kh+1}-h
=n\left(\frac{1-e^{-kvx}}v-x\right)+O(1).
\end{equation}
The bracket is strictly concave and vanishes at $0$ and $1$. On $[a,1-\varepsilon]$ it is bounded below by a positive constant. The number of occupied labels changes by at most one when one draw is replaced. Bounded differences therefore gives
\[
\Prob_M(Y_{kh+1}\le h)\le \exp(-\eta n)
\]
uniformly on that compact interval. Divide by \eqref{asa:acc:eq:conditioning} and sum over at most $n$ values of $h$. Polynomial factors are absorbed by reducing $\eta$, proving \eqref{asa:acc:eq:middle-tail}. Notice that here the conditioning is at a typical coupon count, rather than on the exponentially unlikely complete collection from $n$ labels.

\subsection{Exponential domination at the right boundary}
By \eqref{asa:acc:eq:homogeneous}, all variables in $R_{n-r}(r)$ are at most $n$, and the number of monomials is $\binom{kr}{r}$. Thus
\[
R_{n-r}(r)\le\binom{kr}{r}n^{dr}.
\]
The two-sided leading bounds from Lemma~\ref{asa:acc:lem:saddle}, uniform for $n-r\ge(1-\varepsilon)n$, imply
\begin{align*}
K(n,n-r)&\le C\binom{kr}{r}\beta^{-r}
(1-r/n)^{d(n-r)+1/2}\\
&\le C\binom{kr}{r}q^r\exp(C_kr^2/n).
\end{align*}
The function $(1-v)v^d$ takes its maximum $d^d/k^k$ at $v=d/k$, and our root lies to the right of that point. Hence $q<d^d/k^k$. The binomial theorem with weights $1/k$ and $d/k$ gives $\binom{kr}{r}\le(k^k/d^d)^r$. Choosing $\varepsilon$ small absorbs $\exp(C_k\varepsilon r)$ into the strict geometric decay. This proves \eqref{asa:acc:eq:right-tail}.

\section{The endpoint kernel and its contraction}
\subsection{An exact uniform sum identity}
Set $w_r=\binom{kr}{r}q^r$ for $r\ge0$. For $r\ge1$, apply the fundamental theorem of calculus $r$ times to \eqref{asa:acc:eq:finite-diff}. If $U_1,\ldots,U_r$ are independent uniform random variables on $[0,1]$, the result is
\begin{equation}\label{asa:acc:eq:uniform}
\frac{R_{n-r}(r)}{\binom{kr}{r}n^{dr}}
=\E\left(1-\frac{U_1+\cdots+U_r}{n}\right)^{dr}.
\end{equation}
Consequently, exactly,
\begin{equation}\label{asa:acc:eq:uniform-coeff}
\frac{R_{n-r}(r)}{\binom{kr}{r}n^{dr}}
=\sum_{j=0}^{dr}(-1)^j\fall{dr}{j}
[z^j]\left(\frac{e^z-1}{z}\right)^r n^{-j}.
\end{equation}
The bracketed generating function is the moment-generating function of $U_1+\cdots+U_r$. At fixed $j$ its coefficient is a polynomial in $r$ of degree at most $j$, because it is $\exp(r\log((e^z-1)/z))$. Thus the coefficient in \eqref{asa:acc:eq:uniform-coeff} is polynomial in $r$ of degree at most $2j$.

Since $0\le\sum U_i\le r$, the absolute value of the $j$th coefficient is at most $(dr^2)^j/j!$. The absolute remainder after order $J$ is bounded by
\begin{equation}\label{asa:acc:eq:uniform-remainder}
\frac{(dr^2/n)^{J+1}}{(J+1)!}\exp(dr^2/n).
\end{equation}
This quantitative estimate justifies the polynomial coefficient calculus and summation of endpoint remainders, rather than merely asserting a fixed-$r$ expansion.

Combining \eqref{asa:acc:eq:uniform-coeff} with the all-order saddle expansion gives
\begin{equation}\label{asa:acc:eq:W}
K(n,n-r)\sim w_r\sum_{j\ge0}W_j(r)n^{-j},\qquad W_0(r)=1.
\end{equation}
Each $W_j$ is polynomial in $r$, with coefficients rational in $k,v$. For $r\le C\log n$, a truncation after $J$ has a remainder bounded by $n^{-J-1}w_re^{\delta r}$ times a fixed polynomial in $r$, with $\delta>0$ as small as needed. This follows from \eqref{asa:acc:eq:uniform-remainder}, Taylor bounds on $(1-r/n)^{d(n-r)+1/2}$, and the uniform one-variable remainder in \eqref{asa:acc:eq:T-expansion}. The tail $r>C\log n$ is smaller than any prescribed fixed inverse power when $C$ is chosen large enough, by \eqref{asa:acc:eq:right-tail}.

\subsection{The mass is strictly less than one}\label{asa:acc:sec:mass}
Let $y=1+qy^k$ be the branch satisfying $y(0)=1$. Lagrange inversion gives the Fuss--Catalan series
\[
y=\sum_{r\ge0}\frac1{dr+1}\binom{kr}{r}q^r.
\]
Thus $F(q):=\sum_{r\ge0}\binom{kr}{r}q^r=y+dqy'$. Differentiating the implicit equation yields
\[
F(q)=\frac{y}{k-dy}.
\]
Our value of $q$ lies below the branch point and corresponds to $y=1/v<k/d$, so
\begin{equation}\label{asa:acc:eq:mass}
F(q)=\frac1c,\qquad
\theta:=\sum_{r\ge1}w_r=\frac{1-c}{c}=\frac{k\rho}{1-k\rho}.
\end{equation}
In fact $\theta<1$. Evaluate $f(s)=k(1-e^{-s})-s$ at $s=\log(2k)$:
\[
f(\log(2k))=k-\tfrac12-\log(2k)>0.
\]
It is positive at $k=2$ and increasing thereafter. The positive zero $t$ lies to its right, so $k\rho=ke^{-t}<1/2$, $c>1/2$, and \eqref{asa:acc:eq:mass} is a strict contraction.

\section{A finite all orders coefficient algorithm}\label{asa:acc:sec:algorithm}
All power series in this section are formal expansions at $X=0$. No convergence in $X\ne0$ is claimed. Define
\begin{align}
H_r(X)&=\mathsf h_{dr}(1-rX,1-(r-1)X,\ldots,1),\notag\\
\mathcal K_r(X)&=q^r H_r(X)(1-rX)^{1/2}
\exp\{d[(X^{-1}-r)\log(1-rX)+r]\}\notag\\
&\hspace{30mm}\cdot\frac{\tau(X/(1-rX))}{\tau(X)}.\label{asa:acc:eq:formal-kernel}
\end{align}
The apparent $X^{-1}$ singularity is removable. This is the formal series for $K(n,n-r)$, and its constant term is $w_r$.

For fixed $h$, let $D_h(X)$ be the expansion of $a_hR_h(n-h)/T_n$. Put
\[
g_h(z)=\frac{z^{kh}e^{hz}}{A(z)^h}.
\]
Direct coefficient extraction gives
\begin{equation}\label{asa:acc:eq:small-ratio}
\frac{R_h(n-h)}{T_n}
=\frac{n!}{(n-h)!}\frac{(kn-kh)!}{(kn+1)!}
\frac{I_{g_h}(n)}{I_{g_0}(n)}.
\end{equation}
The explicit finite product for its factorial prefactor is
\begin{equation}\label{asa:acc:eq:small-factorial}
X^{dh+1}k^{-kh-1}
\frac{\prod_{i=0}^{h-1}(1-iX)}{\prod_{j=0}^{kh}(1+(1-j)X/k)}.
\end{equation}
Since $g_h(t)/g_0(t)=k^{kh+1}v^{dh+1}$, Lemma~\ref{asa:acc:lem:saddle} gives
\begin{equation}\label{asa:acc:eq:D-leading}
D_h(X)=a_hv^{dh+1}X^{dh+1}(1+O(X)).
\end{equation}
The all-order saddle rule \eqref{asa:acc:eq:Gaussian} applied to the ratio in \eqref{asa:acc:eq:small-ratio} computes all further terms. The canceled-integrand caveat in Section~\ref{asa:acc:sec:saddle} applies.

The coefficient series $C(X)=\sum_{j\ge0}c_jX^j$ is specified by
\begin{equation}\label{asa:acc:eq:formal-renewal}
C(X)+\sum_{r\ge1}\mathcal K_r(X)C\left(\frac{X}{1-rX}\right)
+\sum_{h\ge1}D_h(X)=1.
\end{equation}
At each order $j$, only finitely many $h$ enter. The unknown $c_j$ has coefficient $1+\theta=1/c$, so the recursion is uniquely solvable. At order zero it gives $c_0=c$.

Although the $r$-sum is infinite, it reduces to finitely many exact moments. Define
\begin{equation}\label{asa:acc:eq:moments}
M_\ell=\sum_{r\ge1}r^\ell w_r,\qquad
M_0=1/c-1,\qquad M_\ell=(q\partial_q)^\ell(1/c)\ (\ell\ge1).
\end{equation}
In $v$-coordinates, with $k$ fixed,
\begin{equation}\label{asa:acc:eq:moment-D}
q\partial_q=-\frac{\rho v}{c}\partial_v.
\end{equation}
Each fixed coefficient of \eqref{asa:acc:eq:formal-renewal} is a finite linear combination of these moments, since the endpoint coefficients are polynomials in $r$.

A finite implementation to requested order $J$ therefore performs the following steps:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Compute cumulants through the finite order required by \eqref{asa:acc:eq:Gaussian}, then $t_0,\ldots,t_J$ via \eqref{asa:acc:eq:tau-algorithm}.
\item Compute exact $a_h$ for $1\le h\le\lfloor(J-1)/d\rfloor$, and truncate the corresponding $D_h$ at order $J$.
\item Expand \eqref{asa:acc:eq:formal-kernel} using \eqref{asa:acc:eq:uniform-coeff}, retaining polynomials in $r$ through order $J$.
\item Replace every power of $r$ in the endpoint sum by the appropriate moment \eqref{asa:acc:eq:moments}, and solve \eqref{asa:acc:eq:formal-renewal} coefficient by coefficient.
\end{enumerate}
The implementation reconstructs each order-$j$ endpoint polynomial from its values at $r=0,1,\ldots,2j$. This finite interpolation is justified by a degree bound, not by fitting count asymptotics. The normalized suffix coefficient of grade $m$ has degree at most $2m$, while the log-phase coefficient has degree at most $m+1\le2m$ for $m\ge1$. Products preserve degree at most twice the total grade. The shifted earlier $C$ coefficients obey the same bound. Thus the order-$j$ residual polynomial has degree at most $2j$, and $2j+1$ distinct nodes determine it exactly in symbolic arithmetic. The supplied implementation evaluates these finite operations at high precision; its decimal outputs are neither exact rational expressions nor interval certificates.

One exact small-count recurrence, with $L_n=(n-1)!a_n$, is
\begin{equation}\label{asa:acc:eq:labelled}
L_n=n^{kn}-\sum_{h=1}^{n-1}\binom{n-1}{h-1}n^{k(n-h)}L_h,
\qquad L_1=1.
\end{equation}
It follows by choosing the set of states reachable from root $1$, taking an accessible structure on it, and assigning all outgoing transitions on the remaining states arbitrarily. Thus the algorithm does not require previously tabulated counts.

\section{Why the coefficient expansion is a theorem}\label{asa:acc:sec:closure}
We now establish the error in Theorem~\ref{asa:acc:thm:main} without assuming an asymptotic expansion of $a_n$ in advance. Fix $J$ and choose an integer $H$ with $dH+1>J+1$. Form the explicitly patched trial sequence
\begin{equation}\label{asa:acc:eq:patch}
f_J(h)=\begin{cases}
b_h,&1\le h<H,\\
\sum_{j=0}^{J}c_jh^{-j},&h\ge H.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
This patch is a proof device. It asserts no canonical interpolation of the integer sequence.

In the renewal equation for $f_J$, the finitely many $h<H$ terms are exact small-state forcing. Expand them through order $J$ by \eqref{asa:acc:eq:small-ratio}. Discard the remaining left tail by \eqref{asa:acc:eq:left-tail}, and the compact middle by \eqref{asa:acc:eq:middle-tail}. In the right endpoint, cut at $r=C\log n$, use the uniform expansion \eqref{asa:acc:eq:W}, and then restore its infinite moment sums. Equation~\eqref{asa:acc:eq:right-tail} and the quantified remainder \eqref{asa:acc:eq:uniform-remainder} make these operations valid with error $O(n^{-J-1})$. The finite coefficients vanish by \eqref{asa:acc:eq:formal-renewal}. Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{asa:acc:eq:residual}
f_J(n)+\sum_{h=1}^{n-1}K(n,h)f_J(h)-1=O(n^{-J-1}).
\end{equation}

Set $e_n=b_n-f_J(n)$ and $p=J+1$. Subtracting gives
\begin{equation}\label{asa:acc:eq:error-rec}
e_n=-\sum_{h=H}^{n-1}K(n,h)e_h+O(n^{-p}).
\end{equation}
Take a sufficiently large fixed $N\ge H$. The finitely many $H\le h<N$ contribute $O(n^{-dH-1})$, absorbed into the forcing. Moreover
\begin{equation}\label{asa:acc:eq:weighted-contraction}
\sum_{h=N}^{n-1}K(n,h)(n/h)^p\longrightarrow\theta<1.
\end{equation}
At the left endpoint \eqref{asa:acc:eq:left-weighted} gives $O(n^{p-dN-1})$, which vanishes after enlarging $N$. The middle stays exponentially negligible under the polynomial weight. At the right endpoint the weight is bounded and tends to $1$ for each fixed $r$, so dominated convergence with \eqref{asa:acc:eq:right-tail} gives $\sum_{r\ge1}w_r$.

Choose $\theta'<1$ bounding the weighted sum for all sufficiently large $n$, and let $B$ bound the scaled forcing. Strong induction proves $n^p|e_n|\le C$: choose $C$ to cover the finite initial segment and $B/(1-\theta')$, then \eqref{asa:acc:eq:error-rec} gives $n^p|e_n|\le\theta'C+B\le C$. This proves \eqref{asa:acc:eq:main} at every fixed order. In particular, the small-state boundary is retained in the proof; replacing $b_h$ by a truncated large-$h$ formula for all $h$ would generally give the wrong coefficients.

\section{The first three corrections}\label{asa:acc:sec:corrections}
All $c_j$ below are absolute coefficients in $a_n/T_n$. They are not relative corrections after additionally dividing by $c$.

The first saddle coefficient is
\begin{equation}\label{asa:acc:eq:t1}
t_1=\frac{13}{12k}-\frac1{12}-\frac1{2\kappa_2}
-\frac{\kappa_3}{2\kappa_2^2}
+\frac{\kappa_4}{8\kappa_2^2}
-\frac{5\kappa_3^2}{24\kappa_2^3}.
\end{equation}
The offset-one/cubic-cumulant term has a negative sign. From \eqref{asa:acc:eq:uniform-coeff}, the normalized suffix begins
\[
1-\frac{dr^2}{2n}
+\frac{dr^2(dr-1)(3r+1)}{24n^2}+O_r(n^{-3}).
\]
Combining it with the $T_{n-r}/T_n$ expansion cancels the quadratic terms at first order, giving
\begin{equation}\label{asa:acc:eq:W12}
W_1(r)=-\frac r2,\qquad
W_2(r)=\frac{k(k-1)r^3}{24}-\frac{(k+2)r^2}{24}+t_1r.
\end{equation}
Hence
\begin{align}
c_1&=\frac{c^2M_1}{2}=\frac{k\rho v}{2c},\notag\\
c_2&=-c^2\left\{\frac{k(k-1)}{24}M_3-\frac{k+2}{24}M_2+t_1M_1\right\}
-\frac{cc_1M_1}{2}-\ind_{\{k=2\}}cv^2.\label{asa:acc:eq:c2}
\end{align}
For a direct check of the last term, $a_1=1$ and
\begin{equation}\label{asa:acc:eq:h1-exact}
R_1(n-1)=S(kn-k+1,n).
\end{equation}
This follows by differentiating $A(z)^n$ in \eqref{asa:acc:eq:R}. It also follows from the suffix encoding. Its contribution is $v^kn^{-k}(1+O(n^{-1}))$, so the small-state correction first enters at order $k$.

Here is an explicit factored formula for $c_3$ avoiding an unwieldy expanded rational expression. Obtain $t_2$ from \eqref{asa:acc:eq:tau-algorithm}; only cumulants through $\kappa_6$ are needed. Define
\begin{align*}
R_1^*&=-dr^2/2,&
R_2^*&=dr^2(dr-1)(3r+1)/24,\\
R_3^*&=-dr(dr-1)(dr-2)r^2(r+1)/48,\\
\ell_1&=dr^2/2-r/2,&
\ell_2&=dr^3/6-r^2/4+t_1r,\\
\ell_3&=dr^4/12-r^3/6+t_1r^2+(2t_2-t_1^2)r.
\end{align*}
Then
\begin{equation}\label{asa:acc:eq:W3}
W_3(r)=R_3^*+R_2^*\ell_1+R_1^*(\ell_2+\ell_1^2/2)
+\ell_3+\ell_1\ell_2+\ell_1^3/6.
\end{equation}
The small-state forcing at order three is
\begin{equation}\label{asa:acc:eq:D3}
\Delta_3=\begin{cases}
v^2(-\tfrac12+\kappa_3/\kappa_2^2)+12v^3,&k=2,\\
v^3,&k=3,\\
0,&k\ge4.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
For $k=2$, the first term is the next coefficient of $D_1$, and $12v^3$ is the leading term of $D_2$, since $a_2(2)=12$. For $k=3$, only $D_1$ enters. Coefficient matching now gives
\begin{equation}\label{asa:acc:eq:c3}
c_3=-c\left\{\sum_{r\ge1}w_r
\left[cW_3(r)+c_1\left(W_2(r)+\frac{r^2}{2}\right)
+\frac32c_2r\right]+\Delta_3\right\}.
\end{equation}
The sum is a finite moment evaluation by \eqref{asa:acc:eq:moments}, so \eqref{asa:acc:eq:c3} is an explicit finite formula. It was independently derived from Gaussian cumulants and both boundaries, rather than fitted to numerical residuals.

\section{An elementary carrier and inverse counting}\label{asa:acc:sec:inverse}
Multiplication of Theorem~\ref{asa:acc:thm:main} and Lemma~\ref{asa:acc:lem:saddle} gives
\begin{equation}\label{asa:acc:eq:a-carrier}
a_n=C_a\beta^n n^{dn+1/2}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{J}\alpha_jn^{-j}
+O(n^{-J-1})\right),\qquad C_a=\frac{\sqrt c}{v\sqrt{2\pi}},
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_0=1$ and
\begin{equation}\label{asa:acc:eq:alpha}
\alpha_j=c^{-1}\sum_{i=0}^{j}c_it_{j-i},\qquad
\alpha_1=t_1+c_1/c.
\end{equation}
This form involves only elementary functions of $n$, with fixed constants depending on $k$.

\subsection{Lambert seed and two explicit corrections}
Let $y=\log X$ and define
\begin{equation}\label{asa:acc:eq:Lambert}
x=\frac{y}{dW_0(\beta^{1/d}y/d)},\qquad
L=d\log x+d+\log\beta,\qquad A=\tfrac12\log x+\log C_a.
\end{equation}
The seed solves $dx\log x+x\log\beta=y$. Put
\begin{equation}\label{asa:acc:eq:deltas}
\delta_0=-A/L,\qquad
\delta_1=-\frac{(d/2)\delta_0^2+\delta_0/2+\alpha_1}{xL}.
\end{equation}
Taylor expansion of the logarithm of \eqref{asa:acc:eq:a-carrier} yields, for the smooth large solution of the corresponding truncated scale,
\begin{equation}\label{asa:acc:eq:inverse-explicit}
n=x+\delta_0+\delta_1+O\bigl(x^{-2}(\log x)^{-1}\bigr).
\end{equation}
More intrinsically, when $X=a_n$, the right side reproduces the integer index $n$ with this error. This statement does not require any canonical interpolation of the sequence $a_n$.

For detail, $\delta_0=O(1)$ cancels $A$ against the first derivative $L$. At the next order, the quadratic term of $dx\log x+x\log\beta$ is $d\delta_0^2/(2x)$, the change in $\frac12\log x$ is $\delta_0/(2x)$, and the first relative correction contributes $\alpha_1/x$. Equation~\eqref{asa:acc:eq:deltas} cancels their sum. The remaining logarithmic residual is $O(x^{-2})$; division by the derivative $L\asymp\log x$ proves \eqref{asa:acc:eq:inverse-explicit}.

\subsection{Rigorous integer threshold brackets}
For fixed $J\ge0$, define the smooth model
\begin{equation}\label{asa:acc:eq:FJ}
F_J(x)=C_a\beta^x x^{dx+1/2}\sum_{j=0}^{J}\alpha_jx^{-j}.
\end{equation}
It is positive and strictly increasing for all sufficiently large $x$. Let $x_J(X)$ be the unique sufficiently large solution of $F_J(x)=X$.

\begin{theorem}\label{asa:acc:thm:inverse}
For every fixed $J$, there exist constants $C_J,X_J^*>0$ such that, whenever $X\ge X_J^*$,
\begin{equation}\label{asa:acc:eq:bracket}
\left\lceil x_J(X)-\frac{C_Jx_J(X)^{-J-1}}{\log x_J(X)}\right\rceil
\le \min\{n\ge1:a_n\ge X\}
\le
\left\lceil x_J(X)+\frac{C_Jx_J(X)^{-J-1}}{\log x_J(X)}\right\rceil.
\end{equation}
The constants and the starting cutoff are existential; the present proof does not certify numerical thresholds. If the ceilings agree, they give the exact inverse. Otherwise the neighboring exact counts decide it.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Equation~\eqref{asa:acc:eq:a-carrier} implies
$\log a_n-\log F_J(n)=O(n^{-J-1})$, while
$(\log F_J)'(x)\sim d\log x$. Thus a displacement
$C_Jx^{-J-1}/\log x$ on either side of the model root dominates the logarithmic count error for a sufficiently large constant. The mean value theorem then shows that every integer below the left displaced root has $a_n<X$, and every integer at or above the right displaced root has $a_n\ge X$. Values a bounded distance away are covered directly by the derivative bound, and more distant large values by monotonicity. Indeed
$a_{n+1}/a_n\sim\beta e^d n^d\to\infty$, so the actual count is eventually strictly increasing. The finitely many earlier values are irrelevant once $X$ exceeds their maximum. Taking ceilings proves \eqref{asa:acc:eq:bracket}, including the possibility that $X$ lies arbitrarily close to an exact count.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Finite Newton inversion to any fixed order}
Apply Newton's method to $\log F_J(x)-\log X$, starting at the Lambert seed \eqref{asa:acc:eq:Lambert}. Its initial error is $O(1)$. On a bounded neighborhood of the root,
\[
\frac{(\log F_J)''(x)}{(\log F_J)'(x)}=O\bigl((x\log x)^{-1}\bigr).
\]
After any fixed $s\ge1$ iterations, the model-root error is therefore
\begin{equation}\label{asa:acc:eq:Newton}
O\bigl((x\log x)^{-(2^s-1)}\bigr).
\end{equation}
Choose $2^s-1\ge J+1$ to make this no larger than the scale of \eqref{asa:acc:eq:bracket}. A sufficiently enlarged bracket constant absorbs this numerical-model error. These statements distinguish inversion of a smooth asymptotic model from exact integer inversion; an unqualified ceiling of a truncated inverse is not generally valid.

\section{Provenance and the boundary of the novelty claim}\label{asa:acc:sec:provenance}
The classical leading asymptotic is explicitly attributed to Korshunov in Bassino--Nicaud and Lebensztayn. Lebensztayn simplifies its generalized-binomial constant. Amri--Chassaing develop the coupon representation and conditioned-profile approach; the waiting-time association here retains the dependence on an arbitrary fixed lower cutoff, which is needed for all orders. Chassaing--Flin--Zevio give a newer fluid-profile treatment whose automata application still states the classical leading equivalent \cite{impatient,pascal}.

The present formulation uses $S(kn+1,n)$ rather than $nS(kn,n)$. In Lebensztayn's convention, accepting-state choices produce a factor $2^n$ and the leading constant is $E_k=c/v$. Since $S(kn+1,n)/(nS(kn,n))\to1/v$, the leading equivalents agree exactly after removing $2^n$. A different normalization is not a new leading asymptotic.

Korshunov's 1976 paper already gives fixed-alphabet leading equivalents and also addresses varying alphabet sizes and the logarithmic connectivity window \cite{korshunov1976}. Those regimes are explicitly outside the novelty scope here. His 78-page 1978 Russian survey was identified, but its full text was not obtained for direct inspection in the bounded search, and the 1986 strongly-connected paper likewise remains outside an exhaustive audit. The result should therefore be described as a newly developed self-contained derivation and higher-order extension, with priority unresolved until that literature is checked. An absent OEIS asymptotic entry does not establish novelty.

\section{Further questions}\label{asa:acc:sec:further}
The following directions lie beyond the results proved here; listing them is not an exhaustive claim about what remains unpublished.

An effective version of the remainder estimates would be particularly useful. Tracking constants in the saddle bounds, the three-region estimates, and the weighted contraction could produce explicit values of the starting index and remainder constant for each fixed $(k,J)$. Coupled with interval evaluation of the finite coefficient formulas, this would turn the existential inverse brackets into certified numerical threshold computations.

The present argument fixes the order before taking $n\to\infty$. It does not control the growth of $c_j$ as $j\to\infty$. Understanding that growth could reveal whether useful optimal truncation or exponentially improved approximations are available, and how the two boundaries influence such refinements.

Uniform higher-order estimates when $k$ varies with $n$ require additional analysis of the $k$-dependence of the saddle, tail bounds, and finite-state forcing. The exponentially small quantity $\rho$ also requires care in numerical evaluation. Classical leading growing-alphabet regimes and connectivity transitions are already present in Korshunov's work; the potential extension here concerns uniform higher corrections and their rigorous error control, not rediscovery of those leading transitions.

\section{Objects and classical enumeration}\label{asa:str:sec:objects}
Fix $k\ge2$, and put $d=k-1$. A transition structure on a finite state set $V$ is a tuple $(f_1,\ldots,f_k)$ of maps $V\to V$. The letters are named and are not permuted by isomorphisms. Loops and repeated destinations are allowed. Strong connectivity means that for every ordered pair of states some word in the maps takes the first state to the second. There is no set of terminal states and no output alphabet in this model.

Let $L_n$ count strongly connected structures on $[n]$, and define
\[
 B_n=\frac{L_n}{n!},\qquad R_n=\frac{L_n}{(n-1)!}=nB_n,
 \qquad U_n=\#\{\text{unrooted isomorphism classes on $n$ states}\}.
\]
Here $R_n$ counts rooted isomorphism classes. Indeed, an automorphism fixing a root fixes every word-successor and hence every state. Thus the action on labeled structures with a distinguished root is free. The quantity $B_n$ is a symmetry-weighted count, and need not be an integer; in general $U_n\ne B_n$.

\subsection{Auxiliary recurrence and logarithm}
The classical Liskovets--Robinson auxiliary numbers $A_n$ satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{asa:str:eq:aux}
 A_n=n^{kn}-\sum_{h=1}^{n-1}\binom nh n^{k(n-h)}A_h.
\end{equation}
Set $P_n=A_n/n!$ and $P(x)=\sum_{n\ge1}P_nx^n$. The exact strong-count identity is
\begin{equation}\label{asa:str:eq:log}
 \sum_{n\ge1}B_nx^n=-\log(1-P(x)).
\end{equation}
These identities are prior work \cite{L71,R85}; all series here are formal, with radius of convergence zero. Differentiating \eqref{asa:str:eq:log} gives the useful triangular recurrence
\begin{equation}\label{asa:str:eq:Brec}
 B_n=P_n+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\left(1-\frac jn\right)P_jB_{n-j}.
\end{equation}
Equivalently, entirely in integer arithmetic,
\begin{equation}\label{asa:str:eq:Lrec}
 nL_n=nA_n+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}(n-j)\binom nj A_jL_{n-j}.
\end{equation}
The first binary values are
\[
 (L_1,\ldots,L_5)=(1,9,296,20958,2554344),\quad
 (U_1,\ldots,U_5)=(1,6,52,892,21291).
\]

\section{Main expansion theorem}\label{asa:str:sec:main}
Let $t>0$ be the unique positive solution of
\begin{equation}\label{asa:str:eq:saddle}
 t=k(1-\ee^{-t}),
\end{equation}
and write
\begin{equation}\label{asa:str:eq:constants}
 v=\frac tk,\quad \rho=1-v=\ee^{-t},\quad c=1-k\rho,
 \quad q=\rho v^d,\quad \beta=\frac{\ee^{-d}}q,
 \quad C=\sqrt{\frac c{2\pi}}.
\end{equation}
The positive solution can also be written $t=k+W_0(-k\ee^{-k})$. The other real branch gives $t=0$ and is not the required saddle. Let $T^{\circ}_n=S(kn,n)$ denote a Stirling number of the second kind.

\begin{theorem}[All fixed orders]\label{asa:str:thm:main}
For every fixed $k\ge2$ and integer $J\ge0$, there are effectively computable real coefficients $p_j,b_j,\tau_j,\alpha_j$ such that
\begin{align}
 \frac{P_n}{T^{\circ}_n}&=\sum_{j=0}^Jp_jn^{-j}+O_{k,J}(n^{-J-1}),\label{asa:str:eq:Pexp}\\
 \frac{B_n}{T^{\circ}_n}&=\sum_{j=0}^Jb_jn^{-j}+O_{k,J}(n^{-J-1}),\label{asa:str:eq:Bexp}\\
 T^{\circ}_n&=\frac{\beta^nn^{dn-1/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi c}}
 \left(\sum_{j=0}^J\tau_jn^{-j}+O_{k,J}(n^{-J-1})\right),\label{asa:str:eq:Texp}\\
 B_n&=C\beta^nn^{dn-1/2}
 \left(\sum_{j=0}^J\alpha_jn^{-j}+O_{k,J}(n^{-J-1})\right).\label{asa:str:eq:absB}
\end{align}
Here $p_0=b_0=c$, $\tau_0=\alpha_0=1$, and
\begin{align}
 p_1&=-\frac{k\rho v}{2c}-\ind_{k=2}cv,\label{asa:str:eq:p1}\\
 b_1&=-\frac{k\rho v}{2c}-\ind_{k=2}cv^2,\label{asa:str:eq:b1}\\
 \alpha_j&=\frac1c\sum_{i=0}^jb_i\tau_{j-i}.\label{asa:str:eq:alpha}
\end{align}
Multiplication by $n$ and $n!$ gives the corresponding expansions of $R_n$ and $L_n$. The same expansion \eqref{asa:str:eq:absB} holds for $U_n$ with the same coefficients. All assertions fix $k$ and the requested order; no uniform growing-$k$ theorem is asserted.
\end{theorem}
The nonidentity contributions to $U_n$ are smaller than every inverse power relative to $B_n$. Section~\ref{asa:str:sec:sectors} gives an exact separation and quantitative tail estimates. They do not make a finite truncation of \eqref{asa:str:eq:absB} an exponentially improved approximation.

The first small-state correction occurs at $n^{-d}$, one order earlier than in the ordinary accessible-automata checkpoint model. In the binary case it already changes $p_1$ and $b_1$. Ignoring this boundary gives the wrong first correction.

\section{A positive strict checkpoint model}\label{asa:str:sec:positive}
A restricted-growth word encodes a set partition by numbering its blocks in order of first appearance. Let $P'_n$ count such words of length $kn$ with exactly $n$ symbols and with distinct-symbol counts $Y_m$ satisfying
\begin{equation}\label{asa:str:eq:strict}
 Y_{kh}\ge h+1\qquad(1\le h<n).
\end{equation}
Consider an arbitrary length-$kn$ word over $n$ labeled symbols. There is a first checkpoint $h\in[1,n]$ with $Y_{kh}\le h$. At that first failure $Y_{kh}=h$: for $h>1$ the preceding checkpoint contains at least $h$ symbols, and at $h=1$ the nonempty word has at least one. Its prefix satisfies all earlier strict constraints. Choosing and ordering its $h$ symbols gives $\binom nh h!P'_h$ prefixes, while the remaining $k(n-h)$ positions are arbitrary. Hence
\[
 n^{kn}=\sum_{h=1}^n\binom nh h!P'_h n^{k(n-h)}.
\]
Comparison with \eqref{asa:str:eq:aux} proves $P'_n=P_n$. In particular $P_n$ is a nonnegative integer.

Apply first failure instead to the $T^{\circ}_n$ restricted-growth words using exactly $n$ symbols. Define
\begin{equation}\label{asa:str:eq:Rhr}
 \mathcal R_h(r)=\frac{(kr)!}{r!}[z^{kr}]\ee^{hz}(\ee^z-1)^r.
\end{equation}
The $r=n-h$ still unseen symbols must all appear in the suffix, while the $h$ old symbols are available without restriction. It follows that
\begin{equation}\label{asa:str:eq:renewal}
 T^{\circ}_n=P_n+\sum_{h=1}^{n-1}P_h\mathcal R_h(n-h).
\end{equation}
\begin{mergenote}
The suffix count \eqref{asa:str:eq:Rhr} is Part~I's $R_h(r)$ of
\eqref{asa:acc:eq:R}, with the same meaning (the $h$ old symbols are free,
the $r$ new ones must all appear), so $\mathcal R_h(r)=R_h(r)$. Manuscript~64
then derives, as manuscript~09 does, its complete-homogeneous form
$\mathcal R_h(r)=h_{dr}(h,h+1,\ldots,h+r)$, which is
\eqref{asa:acc:eq:homogeneous}; its inclusion--exclusion form
$\mathcal R_{n-r}(r)=\frac1{r!}\sum_{j=0}^r(-1)^{r-j}\binom rj(n-r+j)^{kr}$,
which is \eqref{asa:acc:eq:finite-diff} at $h=n-r$ with $i=r-j$; and, by
applying the fundamental theorem of calculus $r$ times to this finite
difference, the exact positive formula below, which is
\eqref{asa:acc:eq:uniform} with $V_i$ for $U_i$. These derivations are
printed once, in Part~I.
\end{mergenote}
\begin{equation}\label{asa:str:eq:uniform}
 \frac{\mathcal R_{n-r}(r)}{\binom{kr}r n^{dr}}
 =\E\left(1-\frac{V_1+\cdots+V_r}{n}\right)^{dr},
 \qquad V_i\overset{\mathrm{iid}}\sim\mathrm{Uniform}[0,1].
\end{equation}
Thus the endpoint expression is a finite polynomial in $1/n$.

Put
\begin{equation}\label{asa:str:eq:kernel}
 K(n,h)=\frac{T^{\circ}_h\mathcal R_h(n-h)}{T^{\circ}_n},\qquad z_n=P_n/T^{\circ}_n.
\end{equation}
Then \eqref{asa:str:eq:renewal} becomes
\begin{equation}\label{asa:str:eq:zrenew}
 z_n+\sum_{h=1}^{n-1}K(n,h)z_h=1,\qquad 0\le z_n\le1.
\end{equation}
Both the positivity and the two distinct endpoint regimes will be needed for a remainder theorem.

\section{Uniform estimates at both boundaries}\label{asa:str:sec:boundaries}
\begin{lemma}[Three ranges]\label{asa:str:lem:ranges}
For every fixed integer $H\ge1$, there are positive constants $a,\varepsilon,\eta$ and $C$, depending only on fixed parameters, such that
\begin{align}
 \sum_{H\le h\le an}K(n,h)&=O(n^{-dH}),\label{asa:str:eq:left}\\
 \sum_{an\le h\le(1-\varepsilon)n}K(n,h)&=O(\ee^{-\eta n}),\label{asa:str:eq:middle}\\
 K(n,n-r)&\le C\ee^{-\eta r}\quad(1\le r\le\varepsilon n).\label{asa:str:eq:right}
\end{align}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
\begin{mergenote}
Manuscript~64 proves this lemma by the argument of Proposition~\ref{asa:acc:prop:tails}
(Part~I, Section~\ref{asa:acc:sec:three-region}), with the checkpoint $kh+1$
replaced by $kh$ and $kn+1$ by $kn$. That argument is printed once, in Part~I;
here are the changes, with manuscript~64's displays.
\end{mergenote}
\emph{Left endpoint.} Draw independent uniform coupons from $n$ types.
Conditioning on $Y_{kn}=n$ makes their equality partition uniform over the
$T^{\circ}_n$ possibilities, so
\[
 K(n,h)=\Prb(Y_{kh}=h\mid Y_{kn}=n).
\]
The waiting times $G_i$ are those of Part~I (independent geometric, $G_1=1$);
the two decreasing events are now $\{G_1+\cdots+G_{h+1}\le kh\}$ and
$\{G_1+\cdots+G_n\le kn\}$. Product-measure association, followed by
complementation of the first event, and the union bound over $h$-subsets of
coupon types give
\begin{align*}
 K(n,h)&\le\Prb(Y_{kh}\le h\mid Y_{kn}=n)\\
 &\le\Prb(Y_{kh}\le h)
 \le\binom nh(h/n)^{kh}=:u_h ,
\end{align*}
with the same ratio bound $u_{h+1}/u_h\le2\ee^k((h+1)/n)^d$ through a
sufficiently small fixed left interval. Choosing $a$ so that this is at most
$1/2$ and summing a geometric tail from $H$ proves \eqref{asa:str:eq:left},
since now $u_H=O(n^{-dH})$ (in Part~I, $O(n^{-dH-1})$). Association is used
for the independent waiting times, not for occupancy indicators.

\emph{Middle range.} Use $M=n/v+O(1)$ coupon types, conditioned on exactly
$n$ types appearing in $kn$ draws; the conditional partition law is
unchanged, and
\[
 \Prb_M(Y_{kn}=n)=\frac{(M)_{\underline n}T^{\circ}_n}{M^{kn}}
 =\Theta(n^{-1/2}),
\]
by the saddle estimate \eqref{asa:str:eq:Texp} at leading order and
Stirling's formula. The profile $\E_M Y_{kh}-h$ is, up to its $O(1)$ term, the one of
\eqref{asa:acc:eq:profile}, and bounded differences, division by the
conditioning probability and summation over at most $n$ indices prove
\eqref{asa:str:eq:middle} exactly as in Part~I.

\emph{Right endpoint.} By \eqref{asa:str:eq:uniform},
$\mathcal R_{n-r}(r)\le\binom{kr}r n^{dr}$, and the leading saddle estimate,
with uniform upper and lower bounds for all sufficiently large indices,
gives $K(n,n-r)\le C\binom{kr}r q^r\exp(C_kr^2/n)$. Since $(1-v)v^d$
decreases on $(d/k,1)$ and $v>d/k$, $q<d^d/k^k$ while
$\binom{kr}r\le(k^k/d^d)^r$; taking $\varepsilon$ small leaves a strict
exponential gap and proves \eqref{asa:str:eq:right}.
\end{proof}
The saddle estimate used here is established independently in the next section, so the argument is not circular.

\section{Saddle expansions and a finite coefficient algorithm}\label{asa:str:sec:algorithm}
Let $D=z\,\mathrm d/\mathrm d z$ and define cumulants
\[
 \kappa_m=D^m\log(\ee^z-1)\big|_{z=t}.
\]
Then $\kappa_1=k$ and $\kappa_2=kc>0$. Since
\[
 T^{\circ}_n=\frac{(kn)!}{n!}[z^{kn}](\ee^z-1)^n,
\]
the positive saddle at $t$ gives \eqref{asa:str:eq:Texp}. In particular
\begin{equation}\label{asa:str:eq:tau1}
 \tau_1=\frac{k^{-1}-1}{12}
 +\frac{\kappa_4}{8\kappa_2^2}
 -\frac{5\kappa_3^2}{24\kappa_2^3}.
\end{equation}
All higher coefficients are specified below by finite operations.

\subsection{Finite Gaussian rule}
For a multiplier $g$ analytic near $t$, formally expand in a variable $u$ the expression
\begin{equation}\label{asa:str:eq:gaussian}
 \frac{g(t\ee^{\ii uZ/\sqrt{\kappa_2}})}{g(t)}
 \exp\left(\sum_{m=3}^{2J+2}
 \frac{\kappa_m\ii^mZ^m}{m!\kappa_2^{m/2}}u^{m-2}\right)
 \pmod{u^{2J+1}}.
\end{equation}
Apply the Gaussian functional $\mathcal G(Z^{2a})=(2a)!/(2^aa!)$ and $\mathcal G(Z^{2a+1})=0$. The coefficient of $u^{2j}$ is the normalized saddle coefficient $s_j(g)$. Only finitely many Taylor derivatives and polynomial operations enter. For $g=1$, multiplying $\sum s_j(1)X^j$ by the Stirling factorial-ratio series
\begin{equation}\label{asa:str:eq:stirlrat}
 \exp\left(\sum_{a\ge1}
 \frac{B_{2a}}{2a(2a-1)}(k^{1-2a}-1)X^{2a-1}\right)
\end{equation}
and truncating gives $\tau(X)=\sum\tau_jX^j$. The $B_{2a}$ in \eqref{asa:str:eq:stirlrat} are Bernoulli numbers, not strong counts.

These formal operations describe actual asymptotics for $g=1$ and for the fixed-$h$ multipliers
\begin{equation}\label{asa:str:eq:gh}
 g_h(z)=\frac{z^{kh}\ee^{hz}}{(\ee^z-1)^h}.
\end{equation}
Here is the required global justification. The positive coefficients of $\ee^z-1$ have span one, hence on $|z|=t$ its modulus is strictly smaller than $\ee^t-1$ away from the positive saddle. For $g_h$, use the canceled entire integrand
\[
 (\ee^z-1)^{n-h}z^{kh}\ee^{hz},
\]
not a global bound on a potentially meromorphic multiplier. For fixed $h$, its off-central integral has the same strict exponential gap. On a shrinking central arc, Taylor's theorem in the exponent and amplitude, followed by Gaussian domination, gives a remainder of any prescribed fixed order; the intermediate arcs are exponentially small or smaller than all powers. This is the usual one-dimensional positive saddle proof and directly validates the finite rule \eqref{asa:str:eq:gaussian}. Local analyticity alone would not justify an unrestricted global assertion for arbitrary $g$; none is used here.

\subsection{The right endpoint as a formal series}
Set $X=1/n$. The expansion of $T^{\circ}_{n-r}/T^{\circ}_n$ at fixed $r$ is
\begin{align}
 E_r(X)={}&q^rX^{dr}(1-rX)^{-1/2}\notag\\
 &\quad\cdot\exp\left(d\left[(X^{-1}-r)\log(1-rX)+r\right]\right)
 \frac{\tau(X/(1-rX))}{\tau(X)}.\label{asa:str:eq:Er}
\end{align}
The apparent negative power in the exponent is removable. Define
\begin{equation}\label{asa:str:eq:Kr}
 H_r(X)=h_{dr}(1-rX,1-(r-1)X,\ldots,1),\qquad
 K_r(X)=X^{-dr}E_r(X)H_r(X).
\end{equation}
The constant coefficient of $K_r$ is
\[
 w_r=\binom{kr}r q^r.
\]
In particular
\begin{align}
 K_r(X)&=w_r\{1+W_1(r)X+W_2(r)X^2+W_3(r)X^3+\cdots\},\label{asa:str:eq:W}\\
 W_1(r)&=\frac r2,\notag\\
 W_2(r)&=\frac{kd}{24}r^3+\frac{10-k}{24}r^2+\tau_1r,\notag\\
 W_3(r)&=\frac{kd}{16}r^4+\frac{6-k}{16}r^3
 +\frac32\tau_1r^2+(2\tau_2-\tau_1^2)r.\notag
\end{align}
At order $j$, $W_j(r)$ is a polynomial of degree at most $2j$. The exact endpoint moments explain both polynomiality and uniformity. From \eqref{asa:str:eq:uniform}, the coefficient of $n^{-j}$ in its expectation is the one of \eqref{asa:acc:eq:uniform-coeff}, and its tail after degree $J$ is bounded by \eqref{asa:acc:eq:uniform-remainder}.
\begin{mergenote}
Manuscript~64 prints both displays again: the coefficient
$(-1)^j\fall{dr}{j}[u^j]((\ee^u-1)/u)^r$ and the tail bound
$(dr^2/n)^{J+1}\exp(dr^2/n)/(J+1)!$. They are Part~I's, with $u$ for $z$.
\end{mergenote}
\noindent The saddle ratio has polynomial-times-exponential bounds of the same form. Therefore for $r\le A\log n$, any fixed-order error is bounded by
$n^{-J-1}w_r\ee^{\delta r}$ times a fixed polynomial in $r$, where $\delta$ can be smaller than the exponential margin. For larger $r$, choose $A$ sufficiently large and apply \eqref{asa:str:eq:right}. This justifies summing every fixed-order expansion over the right endpoint.

\subsection{Exact moments and the small-state boundary}
The relevant Fuss--Catalan identity is
\begin{equation}\label{asa:str:eq:fuss}
 F(q):=\sum_{r\ge0}\binom{kr}r q^r=\frac1c.
\end{equation}
Indeed, the small solution of $y=1+qy^k$ is $y=1/v$, and differentiating its Lagrange series gives \eqref{asa:str:eq:fuss}.
\begin{mergenote}
This is the computation of Part~I, Section~\ref{asa:acc:sec:mass}, which
prints the Lagrange series and $F(q)=y/(k-dy)$; the moments
\eqref{asa:str:eq:moments} below and their differential operator are
\eqref{asa:acc:eq:moments} and \eqref{asa:acc:eq:moment-D} (written $M_\ell$
there).
\end{mergenote}
\noindent All required right-endpoint sums are finite linear combinations of
\begin{equation}\label{asa:str:eq:moments}
 M_a=\sum_{r\ge1}r^a\binom{kr}r q^r,
 \qquad q\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d q}=-\frac{\rho v}{c}\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d v}.
\end{equation}
Apply this differential operator repeatedly to $1/c$, subtracting $1$ for $M_0$. Thus no numerical truncation of an infinite endpoint sum is needed. For example $M_1=k\rho v/c^3$.

Let $D_h(X)$ denote the expansion of $P_h\mathcal R_h(n-h)/T^{\circ}_n$ for fixed $h$. It is computed from
\begin{equation}\label{asa:str:eq:smallratio}
 \frac{\mathcal R_h(n-h)}{T^{\circ}_n}
 =\frac{n!}{(n-h)!}\frac{(kn-kh)!}{(kn)!}
 \frac{I_{g_h}(n)}{I_1(n)},\qquad
 I_g(n)=[z^{kn}](\ee^z-1)^ng(z).
\end{equation}
The factorial factor is the exact rational expansion
\[
 X^{dh}k^{-kh}
 \frac{\prod_{i=0}^{h-1}(1-iX)}{\prod_{a=0}^{kh-1}(1-aX/k)}.
\]
Since $g_h(t)=k^{kh}v^{dh}$, it follows that
\begin{equation}\label{asa:str:eq:Dh}
 D_h(X)=P_hv^{dh}X^{dh}(1+O(X)).
\end{equation}
Only $h\le\lfloor J/d\rfloor$ contributes through degree $J$.

\subsection{Finite recursion for every coefficient}
Define $P_*(X)=\sum_{j\ge0}p_jX^j$ coefficientwise by
\begin{equation}\label{asa:str:eq:Pformal}
 P_*(X)+\sum_{r\ge1}K_r(X)P_*\left(\frac X{1-rX}\right)
 +\sum_{h\ge1}D_h(X)=1.
\end{equation}
This is a formal boundary-expansion identity, not an identity of convergent functions at $X\ne0$. At degree $j$ the unknown $p_j$ is multiplied by $F(q)=1/c$; all other terms use lower $p_i$, finitely many exact $P_h$, and the moments \eqref{asa:str:eq:moments}. Hence it uniquely and effectively determines every coefficient. Each $p_j$ is rational in $k,v$ and finitely many exact auxiliary counts.

An explicit finite implementation is now available: compute exact $P_h$ through $\lfloor J/d\rfloor$; compute $\kappa_m$ through $2J+2$; use \eqref{asa:str:eq:gaussian}--\eqref{asa:str:eq:stirlrat} for $\tau$ and the fixed-$h$ ratios; construct $W_j$ using exact endpoint moments (or polynomial interpolation at the proved degree bound $2j$); reduce every right-endpoint sum to \eqref{asa:str:eq:moments}; solve \eqref{asa:str:eq:Pformal} in increasing degree. Section~\ref{asa:str:sec:transfer} supplies the finite final step for $b_j$.

\section{A remainder theorem for the strict model}\label{asa:str:sec:remainder}
The formal recursion alone does not prove an asymptotic theorem. The following contraction completes that step.

First the limiting mass of the right endpoint is strictly less than one:
\begin{equation}\label{asa:str:eq:theta}
 \theta:=\sum_{r\ge1}w_r=\frac{1-c}{c}<1.
\end{equation}
\begin{mergenote}
The mass $\theta$ is Part~I's \eqref{asa:acc:eq:mass}: the right-endpoint
weights $w_r$ do not depend on the normalization. Manuscript~64 checks
$\theta<1$ explicitly by the argument printed after \eqref{asa:acc:eq:mass}
($k(1-e^{-s})-s>0$ at $s=\log(2k)$, so $t>\log(2k)$, $k\rho<1/2$ and
$c>1/2$). The contraction argument below is that of
Section~\ref{asa:acc:sec:closure} with the exponent $dH+1$ of Part~I's left
tail replaced by $dH$.
\end{mergenote}

Fix $J$, and choose $H$ with $dH>J+1$. Patch the trial sequence to the exact values $z_h$ for $h<H$, and to $\sum_{j=0}^Jp_jn^{-j}$ thereafter. The exact small-state terms are \eqref{asa:str:eq:smallratio}. Lemma~\ref{asa:str:lem:ranges}, the quantitative endpoint expansions, and \eqref{asa:str:eq:Pformal} show that this trial has residual $O(n^{-J-1})$ in \eqref{asa:str:eq:zrenew}. Thus its difference $e_n$ from the true sequence satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{asa:str:eq:error}
 e_n=-\sum_{h=H}^{n-1}K(n,h)e_h+O(n^{-J-1}).
\end{equation}
For a sufficiently large fixed $N\ge H$,
\begin{equation}\label{asa:str:eq:weighted}
 \sum_{h=N}^{n-1}K(n,h)(n/h)^{J+1}\longrightarrow\theta<1.
\end{equation}
At the right endpoint this follows from exponential domination. In a compact middle the exponential bound wins over the polynomial weight. At the left endpoint the ratio proof of \eqref{asa:str:eq:left}, with its weight included, gives $O(n^{J+1-dN})$, which tends to zero after increasing $N$.

The finitely many terms $H\le h<N$ in \eqref{asa:str:eq:error} are $O(n^{-dH})$. By \eqref{asa:str:eq:weighted} the remaining weighted kernel is eventually at most a constant $\theta'<1$. Choose a bound for $n^{J+1}|e_n|$ that dominates the finitely many starting values and the residual constant divided by $1-\theta'$. Strong induction then preserves this bound for every subsequent $n$. This proves \eqref{asa:str:eq:Pexp} with its stated remainder.

\section{Rigorous transfer through the formal logarithm}\label{asa:str:sec:transfer}
A strongly connected structure has positive indegree at every vertex. There are $n!S(kn,n)$ assignments of the $kn$ named outgoing arcs that hit every vertex. Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{asa:str:eq:boundB}
 0\le B_n\le T^{\circ}_n.
\end{equation}
We use this simple combinatorial bound with \eqref{asa:str:eq:Brec}, avoiding uncontrolled powers of a divergent formal series.

\begin{lemma}[Factorial convolution]\label{asa:str:lem:convolution}
For fixed $H\ge1$,
\begin{equation}\label{asa:str:eq:convolution}
 \sum_{H\le j\le n-H}\frac{T^{\circ}_jT^{\circ}_{n-j}}{T^{\circ}_n}=O(n^{-dH}).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Enlarge constants in the leading estimate \eqref{asa:str:eq:Texp} so that two-sided bounds hold for all positive indices. The summand is bounded by a constant times
\[
 \sqrt{\frac n{j(n-j)}}
 \left(\frac jn\right)^{dj}
 \left(1-\frac jn\right)^{d(n-j)}.
\]
For $H\le j\le an$, direct division at consecutive $j$ bounds the ratio by $C_d((j+1)/n)^d$. Choose $a$ small enough for a geometric decrease. The first term is $O(n^{-dH})$. The opposite endpoint is identical by reflection. On the intervening compact interval the entropy $-x\log x-(1-x)\log(1-x)$ is bounded below, giving an exponential gap.
\end{proof}
In \eqref{asa:str:eq:Brec}, small $j$ contribute through $E_j$, while the opposite endpoint $j=n-h$ contributes $(h/n)B_hP_{n-h}$. Hence the formal strong series $B_*(X)=\sum b_jX^j$ is given by
\begin{align}
 B_*(X)={}&P_*(X)
 +\sum_{j\ge1}P_j(1-jX)E_j(X)
 B_*\left(\frac X{1-jX}\right)\notag\\
 &+\sum_{h\ge1}hB_hXE_h(X)
 P_*\left(\frac X{1-hX}\right).\label{asa:str:eq:Bformal}
\end{align}
Every $E_j$ starts at degree $dj\ge1$. Thus each $b_j$ is obtained directly from known $p_i$ and lower $b_i$; only finitely many indices enter a fixed order. Both endpoints are essential.

To prove the remainder, patch a trial $\sum_{j=0}^Jb_jn^{-j}$ at finitely many initial indices. Equations \eqref{asa:str:eq:convolution} and \eqref{asa:str:eq:Bformal}, the proved expansion for $P_n/T^{\circ}_n$, and a choice $dH>J+1$ give residual $O(n^{-J-1})$. The normalized error recurrence has nonnegative kernel
\[
 \left(1-\frac jn\right)P_j\frac{T^{\circ}_{n-j}}{T^{\circ}_n}.
\]
After weighting by $(n/(n-j))^{J+1}$ and restricting $n-j\ge N$, its sum tends to zero. Indeed, the small-$j$ mass is $O(n^{-d})$; near the opposite endpoint it is $O(n^{J-dN})$ after the same geometric ratio estimate; the middle has an exponential gap. Choose $N$ large enough, bound the weighted kernel by a constant less than one, and repeat the strong induction of Section~\ref{asa:str:sec:remainder}. This proves \eqref{asa:str:eq:Bexp}. Equations \eqref{asa:str:eq:Texp} and \eqref{asa:str:eq:alpha} give \eqref{asa:str:eq:absB}.

\section{Smooth inverses and qualified integer thresholds}\label{asa:str:sec:inverse}
All four models have an expansion of the form
\begin{equation}\label{asa:str:eq:generalmodel}
 N_n=C_0\gamma^nn^{an+s}
 \left(1+\sum_{j=1}^J a_jn^{-j}+O(n^{-J-1})\right).
\end{equation}
The parameters are
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{ccccc}
\toprule
Model&$a$&$\gamma$&$s$&$C_0$\\
\midrule
$B_n,U_n$&$d$&$\beta$&$-1/2$&$C$\\
$R_n$&$d$&$\beta$&$1/2$&$C$\\
$L_n$&$k$&$\beta/\ee$&$0$&$\sqrt c$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
For the first three models $a_j=\alpha_j$. For $L_n$, multiply the $\alpha$ series by the ordinary Stirling series
\[
 \exp\left(\sum_{r\ge1}\frac{B_{2r}}{2r(2r-1)}X^{2r-1}\right),
\]
so in particular $a_1=\alpha_1+1/12$.

Let $y=\log X$ for a large target $X$, and set
\begin{align}
 x&=\frac{y}{aW_0(\gamma^{1/a}y/a)},\label{asa:str:eq:xbase}\\
 \Lambda&=a\log x+a+\log\gamma,\qquad A=s\log x+\log C_0,\notag\\
 \delta_0&=-A/\Lambda,\qquad
 \delta_1=-\frac{(a/2)\delta_0^2+s\delta_0+a_1}{x\Lambda}.\label{asa:str:eq:delta}
\end{align}
At exact data $X=N_n$,
\begin{equation}\label{asa:str:eq:inverse2}
 n=x+\delta_0+\delta_1+O\left(\frac1{x^2\log x}\right).
\end{equation}
Indeed \eqref{asa:str:eq:xbase} solves $a x\log x+x\log\gamma=y$. Expand the logarithm of \eqref{asa:str:eq:generalmodel} at $x+\delta_0+\delta_1$. The first correction cancels $A$, and the second cancels the terms of size $1/x$; the remaining logarithmic error is $O(x^{-2})$, while the derivative is $\Lambda+O(x^{-1})\asymp\log x$. The same Taylor calculation recursively gives every fixed inverse order, or a finite number of Newton steps achieves a requested order.

\begin{proposition}[Threshold brackets]\label{asa:str:prop:ceil}
For fixed $J$, let $x_J(X)$ be the eventually unique large solution of
\[
 F_J(x)=X,\qquad
 F_J(x)=C_0\gamma^xx^{ax+s}\sum_{j=0}^Ja_jx^{-j},\quad a_0=1.
\]
There are constants $C_J$ and a sufficiently large target threshold such that
\begin{align}
 \left\lceil x_J-C_J\frac{x_J^{-J-1}}{\log x_J}\right\rceil
 &\le \min\{n:N_n\ge X\}\notag\\
 &\le
 \left\lceil x_J+C_J\frac{x_J^{-J-1}}{\log x_J}\right\rceil.
 \label{asa:str:eq:ceiling}
\end{align}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The model is positive and strictly increasing for large real $x$. The true adjacent ratio is asymptotic to $\gamma\ee^a n^a$, so the sequence too is eventually increasing. The fixed-order relative error is $O(n^{-J-1})$; dividing its logarithm by the logarithmic derivative $\asymp\log n$ brackets the crossing by the two perturbed real roots in \eqref{asa:str:eq:ceiling}. Increase the constant to cover these root estimates uniformly. For sufficiently large $X$, all finitely many earlier terms are below the target.
\end{proof}
The constants and cutoffs in this proposition are existential, not numerically certified by the supplied experiments. If the ceilings differ, exact neighboring counts settle the index. There is no unqualified rule to take the ceiling of an asymptotic inverse. Although $B_n$ is rational rather than generally integral, its threshold index is well defined.

\section{Exact cyclic automorphism sectors}\label{asa:str:sec:sectors}
Define the Jordan totient
\[
 J_a(\ell)=\ell^a\prod_{p\mid\ell}(1-p^{-a})
 =\sum_{b\mid\ell}\mu(\ell/b)b^a.
\]
The classical Liskovets formula is
\begin{equation}\label{asa:str:eq:unrooted}
 U_n=\sum_{\ell\mid n}\frac1\ell B_{n/\ell}J_{dn/\ell+1}(\ell).
\end{equation}
Its identity-permutation term is exactly $B_n$. For completeness, the next proof derives the connected cyclic-lift restriction responsible for the Jordan factor. It is a proof of the existing identity, not a new exact enumeration formula \cite{L71,L98}.

\subsection{Fixed divisor expansions}
For $m=n/\ell$, write the exact positive sector
\begin{equation}\label{asa:str:eq:sector}
 \mathcal D_\ell(n)=\ind_{\ell\mid n}\,
 \ell^{dm}B_m\prod_{p\mid\ell}(1-p^{-dm-1}).
\end{equation}
Thus $U_n=\sum_{\ell\mid n}\mathcal D_\ell(n)$ and $\mathcal D_1(n)=B_n$.
For each fixed $\ell$, as $n\to\infty$ through multiples of $\ell$, Theorem~\ref{asa:str:thm:main} gives
\begin{align}
 \mathcal D_\ell(n)={}&C\sqrt{\frac\ell n}(\beta n^d)^{n/\ell}
 \prod_{p\mid\ell}(1-p^{-dn/\ell-1})\notag\\
 &\quad\cdot\left\{\sum_{j=0}^J\alpha_j(\ell/n)^j
                 +O_{k,J,\ell}(n^{-J-1})\right\}.\label{asa:str:eq:sectorasymp}
\end{align}
Keeping the finite Jordan product exact retains further ordinary-exponential factors within a sector. Expanding that product gives the finite signed decomposition
\[
 \prod_{p\mid\ell}(1-p^{-dm-1})
 =\sum_{a\mid\rad(\ell)}\frac{\mu(a)}a\,a^{-dm}.
\]
Consequently its individual exponential bases are $\beta(n/a)^d$, raised to the power $n/\ell$. No fixed-$\ell$ expansion is applied when $m$ remains bounded.

\subsection{A uniform bound for all omitted divisors}
\begin{theorem}[Divisor tail]\label{asa:str:thm:tail}
Let $\mathcal O_n$ be any subset of divisors of $n$ omitted from the exact sum, and suppose it is nonempty. Set
\[
 M=\max_{\ell\in\mathcal O_n}\frac n\ell,\qquad H_n=\beta n^d.
\]
For all sufficiently large $n$, with a constant independent of the omitted set and of $M$,
\begin{equation}\label{asa:str:eq:actualtail}
 0\le\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal O_n}\mathcal D_\ell(n)
 \le C'_k M^{-1/2}H_n^M.
\end{equation}
If no divisors are omitted, the tail is exactly zero. For any fixed cutoff $L\ge1$,
\begin{equation}\label{asa:str:eq:cutofftail}
 U_n-\sum_{\substack{\ell\mid n\\\ell\le L}}\mathcal D_\ell(n)
 =O_{k,L}\left(n^{-1/2}(\beta n^d)^{n/(L+1)}\right).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
From \eqref{asa:str:eq:boundB} and the saddle bound, enlarged to all $m\ge1$,
\[
 B_m\le T^{\circ}_m\le C_k\beta^m m^{dm-1/2}.
\]
Since the Jordan product lies in $(0,1]$, \eqref{asa:str:eq:sector} implies
$\mathcal D_\ell(n)\le C_km^{-1/2}H_n^m$. Enlarge the sum over omitted quotients to all integers $1\le m\le M$. For $H_n\ge2$,
\[
 \sum_{m=1}^Mm^{-1/2}H_n^m\le C M^{-1/2}H_n^M
\]
uniformly in $M$: in the upper half use $m^{-1/2}\le\sqrt2M^{-1/2}$ and a geometric sum; in the lower half use $\sum_{m\le M/2}m^{-1/2}\le2\sqrt M$ and the exponential factor $H_n^{-M/2}$. This proves \eqref{asa:str:eq:actualtail}.

For the cutoff statement enlarge instead to all integers $m\le\lfloor n/(L+1)\rfloor$ and apply the same bound with this larger endpoint, which is comparable to $n$ for fixed $L$. The exponent can be enlarged to $n/(L+1)$. This yields \eqref{asa:str:eq:cutofftail}, including cases where the next actual divisor tends to infinity with $n$.
\end{proof}
The prefactor at the \emph{actual} largest quotient is $M^{-1/2}$, not $n^{-1/2}$. A factor $n^{-1/2}$ is justified in the fixed-cutoff bound because the enlarged endpoint is comparable to $n$.

\subsection{Even and prime state counts}
For even $n$, the leading nonidentity term is exactly the $\ell=2$ sector:
\begin{align}
 U_n-B_n={}&2^{dn/2}B_{n/2}(1-2^{-dn/2-1})\notag\\
 &+O_k\left(n^{-1/2}(\beta n^d)^{n/3}\right).\label{asa:str:eq:even}
\end{align}
The remainder relative to that exact leading sector is
\[
 O_k((\beta n^d)^{-n/6})
 =\exp\left(-\frac d6n\log n+O_k(n)\right).
\]
The first term has its own all-order relative expansion by \eqref{asa:str:eq:sectorasymp}. But its error after a fixed algebraic truncation is larger than the possible $\ell=3$ scale. Divisibility cannot be suppressed: no $\ell=2$ correction is asserted along odd $n$.

For a prime $n$, since $B_1=1$, the exact answer is simply
\begin{equation}\label{asa:str:eq:prime}
 U_n-B_n=\frac{J_{d+1}(n)}n=n^d-\frac1n.
\end{equation}
This example also shows why large divisors require exact small-index counts rather than a fixed-divisor asymptotic substitution.

\subsection{One letter and marked-state corollaries}
Although $k=1$ lies outside the expansion theorem, its strong structures are directed cycles and $B_m=1/m$. Formula \eqref{asa:str:eq:unrooted} reduces to
\[
 U_n=\frac1n\sum_{\ell\mid n}\varphi(\ell)=1,
\]
as it should. This is a useful check of the normalization.

If each state carries one of $f$ named colors, a coloring fixed by an automorphism of type $\ell^m$ is constant on its cycles and has $f^m$ choices. Hence
\begin{equation}\label{asa:str:eq:colored}
 U_n^{(f)}=\sum_{\ell\mid n}\frac{f^{n/\ell}}\ell
 B_{n/\ell}J_{dn/\ell+1}(\ell).
\end{equation}
A terminal-state subset is the special case $f=2$. In particular its unrooted count is generally not $2^nU_n$. The fixed-sector formulas and uniform tail proof apply with $\beta$ replaced by $f\beta$. This is a marked-state corollary of the classical Burnside mechanism, not an extra assumption in the main model.

\subsection{What the separation does and does not resolve}
The exact finite decomposition, the fixed-divisor expansions, and the uniform omitted-sector bound rigorously isolate automorphism contributions when all larger sectors are retained exactly. The main count $B_n$ is itself available from exact recurrences. A finite inverse-power approximation to $B_n$ has an error much larger than the $\ell=2$ sector. Accordingly, summing finitely truncated algebraic expansions of sectors is not claimed to be exponentially improved. No Borel summability, optimal truncation theorem, or complete description of beyond-all-orders contributions intrinsic to $B_n$ is established here.

\section{Further questions}\label{asa:str:sec:further}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Can the weighted contraction and saddle estimates be made numerically explicit enough to produce certified remainder constants and practical integer-inverse thresholds? Interval versions of the coefficient computations would be a natural first step.
\item How do $p_j,b_j,\alpha_j$ grow with $j$? Gevrey bounds, a sharp least term, and exponentially improved remainders would be needed before algebraic truncations could be compared to automorphism sectors.
\item What beyond-all-orders contributions belong to the identity count $B_n$ itself? They are logically separate from the exact Burnside sectors and cannot be inferred from them.
\item Can one obtain a joint range for $k=k(n)$ and truncation order? As $k$ increases the small parameter $\rho$ becomes exponentially small, so numerical stability alone is not an asymptotic uniformity proof.
\item Can the positive strict checkpoint argument extend to nonuniform letter probabilities, partially defined maps, or constrained transition multiplicities while retaining a tractable two-boundary kernel?
\item A complete reading of the 1978 and 1986 Korshunov sources, and of the original 1971 Liskovets text, would sharpen the historical boundary between existing higher corrections and the constructive refinement developed here.
\end{enumerate}

\section{An independent analytic left tail bound}\label{asa:acc:app:left}
For completeness, the left-tail estimate can also be proved without association. At the saddle $t$, the numerator coefficient in \eqref{asa:acc:eq:R} is represented by a sum of $h$ independent Poisson$(t)$ variables and $n-h$ independent positive-Poisson$(t)$ variables. The latter distribution has maximum point mass at most $C/\sqrt{n-h}$: Fourier inversion bounds the integral by a Gaussian near zero and a strict geometric modulus bound away from zero. The denominator positive-Poisson sum at $kn+1$ has mass bounded below by $C'/\sqrt n$ by Lemma~\ref{asa:acc:lem:saddle}. Thus uniformly for $h\le an$, $a<1$,
\[
K(n,h)\le C T_h\frac{(k(n-h))!}{(kn+1)!}
\frac{n!}{(n-h)!}\,t^{kh+1}v^{-h}.
\]
Using $T_h\le h^{kh+1}/h!\le e^h h^{dh+1}$ and elementary factorial bounds gives
\[
K(n,h)\le C\frac hn\left[C_k(h/n)^d\right]^h.
\]
For sufficiently small $a$, successive terms decrease at a uniformly geometric rate (apply the logarithmic derivative, or a direct ratio bound). Summing from $H$ yields $O(n^{-dH-1})$, and multiplication by $(n/h)^p$ yields $O(n^{p-dH-1})$. This independently supplies exactly the left-tail input needed for the weighted contraction.

\subsection{A connected cyclic lift proof}
\paragraph{Automorphisms have equal-length cycles.}
An automorphism $\sigma$ commutes with each $f_i$, and therefore with
every composition of the named maps.  If $\sigma$ fixes a vertex $v$,
it fixes every vertex reachable from $v$.  Strong connectivity then
forces $\sigma$ to be the identity.  The same argument applies to
every element of the automorphism group, so its action on the vertices
is free.  In particular, if $\sigma$ has order $\ell$ and the
$\sigma$-orbit of $v$ has length $t$, then $\sigma^t$ fixes $v$ and
hence is the identity.  Thus $\ell\mid t$; since also $t\mid\ell$,
we have $t=\ell$.  Consequently any permutation fixing a strongly
connected structure has cycle type $\ell^m$, where $n=\ell m$.

\paragraph{Commuting maps are cyclic-voltage lifts.}
Fix a permutation $\sigma$ of cycle type $\ell^m$.  Label its cycles
by $[m]$ and choose one origin in each cycle, once and for all.  These
choices identify the vertex set with $[m]\times C_\ell$, where
$C_\ell=\mathbb Z/\ell\mathbb Z$ and
$\sigma(v,t)=(v,t+1)$.  A map commuting with $\sigma$ has the unique
form
\[
 f_i(v,t)=(q_i(v),t+a_{v,i}),
 \qquad q_i:[m]\longrightarrow[m],\quad a_{v,i}\in C_\ell.
\]
Thus a fixed $k$-tuple consists of a quotient $Q=(q_1,\ldots,q_k)$
and one voltage $a_e\in C_\ell$ on each of its $km$ named arcs.
If the lift is strongly connected, its quotient is strongly connected.
We now fix one strongly connected quotient and count exactly which
of its voltage assignments yield strongly connected lifts.

\paragraph{The directed connectivity issue.}
For a strongly connected quotient, every arc of the lift can be
traversed backwards by some directed path, even though its literal
reverse need not be an arc.  Indeed, consider a quotient arc
$e:u\to v$ and choose a directed quotient path $P:v\to u$.
The closed directed walk $W=eP$ has a total voltage $h\in C_\ell$.
Starting with a lifted arc
\[
 (u,t)\longrightarrow(v,t+a_e),
\]
follow the lift of $P$, reaching $(u,t+h)$.  If $q$ is the additive
order of $h$ (with $q=1$ when $h=0$), then following $W$ a further
$q-1$ times reaches $(u,t+qh)=(u,t)$.  This constructs the required
directed return path.  It follows that the weak components of the
lift are precisely its strong components: an undirected path can
be replaced, edge by edge, by directed paths in either direction.
In particular, weak connectivity and strong connectivity are
equivalent for these finite lifts of a strongly connected quotient.

\paragraph{Tree normalization and the voltage subgroup.}
Choose a spanning tree $T$ of the underlying undirected multigraph
of $Q$, retaining the actual named quotient arc corresponding to
each of its $m-1$ edges.  The arcs need not point away from a root.
For a voltage assignment $a$, there is a unique function
$g:[m]\to C_\ell$, with $g(v_0)=0$ at a fixed root $v_0$, such that
\[
 g(v)-g(u)=a_e
 \qquad\text{for every tree arc }e:u\to v.
\]
Existence and uniqueness follow by propagation along the tree,
using a minus sign when a tree edge is traversed against its arc
orientation.  The coordinate change $(v,t)\mapsto(v,t-g(v))$
replaces the voltages by
\[
 b_e=a_e+g(u)-g(v),\qquad e:u\to v,
\]
so every tree voltage becomes zero.  This is an isomorphism of the
lifts.  Moreover, the assignment
\[
 (a_e)_{e\in E(Q)}\longleftrightarrow
 \bigl((g(v))_{v\ne v_0},(b_e)_{e\notin T}\bigr)
\]
is a bijection: for arbitrary data on the right, set $b_e=0$ on
$T$ and recover $a_e=b_e-g(u)+g(v)$.  There are
\[
 r=km-(m-1)=(k-1)m+1
\]
non-tree arcs, including loops and any unused parallel arcs.

Let $H\leq C_\ell$ be the subgroup generated by their normalized
voltages $b_e$.  The weak component of $(v_0,t)$ consists exactly of
\[
 \{(v,t+h):v\in[m],\ h\in H\}.
\]
For one inclusion, traversing an arc in either direction changes
the second coordinate by an element of $H$, since tree voltages
are zero.  For the converse, zero-voltage tree paths connect every
$(v,s)$ to $(v_0,s)$.  Traversing a tree path to the tail of a
non-tree arc, then that arc, and then a tree path back to the root
changes the root coordinate by its voltage; reverse traversal
realizes its negative.  Concatenation realizes every element of
$H$.  By the preceding directed-connectivity argument, the lift
is therefore strongly connected if and only if
\[
 \langle b_e:e\notin T\rangle=C_\ell.
\]
Equivalently, it has $[C_\ell:H]$ strong components, one for each
coset of $H$.  This also proves the criterion when $m=1$, for
which the tree is empty.

\paragraph{Counting the generating tuples.}
An $r$-tuple $(b_1,\ldots,b_r)\in C_\ell^r$ generates $C_\ell$
if and only if no prime divisor $p$ of $\ell$ divides all its
coordinates.  For a set of distinct prime divisors with product
$d$, exactly $(\ell/d)^r$ tuples have all coordinates divisible
by $d$.  Inclusion--exclusion gives
\[
 \#\{(b_1,\ldots,b_r):\langle b_1,\ldots,b_r\rangle=C_\ell\}
 =\sum_{d\mid\operatorname{rad}(\ell)}\mu(d)(\ell/d)^r
 =J_r(\ell).
\]
For $\ell=1$ the same count is $1$.  The $m-1$ free values of $g$
are independent of this generation condition.  Thus every fixed
strongly connected labeled quotient on $[m]$ has exactly
\begin{equation}
 \ell^{m-1}J_{(k-1)m+1}(\ell)
 \label{asa:str:eq:strong-connected-lift-count}
\end{equation}
strongly connected voltage assignments.  No division by gauge
transformations is taken here: these assignments are distinct
labeled maps on the fixed vertex set.  Tree normalization is a
bijection used to count them, not a quotient by isomorphism.

\paragraph{Burnside's lemma.}
The symmetric group $S_n$ acts on strongly connected labeled
$k$-tuples by simultaneous conjugation.  Its orbits are the desired
isomorphism classes.  A permutation of type $\ell^m$ has
\[
 \operatorname{Fix}(\sigma)
 =L_m\,\ell^{m-1}J_{(k-1)m+1}(\ell)
\]
fixed structures, by the quotient-and-lift count above.  All other
cycle types have no fixed strongly connected structures.  There
are $n!/(\ell^m m!)$ permutations of type $\ell^m$.  Burnside's
lemma consequently gives
\begin{align*}
 U_n
 &=\frac1{n!}\sum_{\ell m=n}
   \frac{n!}{\ell^m m!}\,
   L_m\ell^{m-1}J_{(k-1)m+1}(\ell)\\
 &=\sum_{\ell\mid n}
   \frac{B_{n/\ell}}{\ell}J_{(k-1)n/\ell+1}(\ell),
\end{align*}
which proves \eqref{asa:str:eq:unrooted}.
