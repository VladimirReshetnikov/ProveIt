\begin{abstract}
For languages $A,B$, let $\mathcal I_k(A,B)$ consist of the words formed by splitting one word of $A$ into at most $k$ pieces and inserting those pieces, in order, into one word of $B$. The minimum required number of pieces defines an insertion-degree spectrum. We give a negative answer to the proposed assertion that this spectrum must be an initial interval. A ternary example has spectrum $\{1,3\}$ and satisfies
\[
 \mathcal I_1(A,B)=\mathcal I_2(A,B)=\{a,b,c\}^5\setminus\{abcba\},
 \qquad \mathcal I_3(A,B)=\{a,b,c\}^5.
\]
The five-letter exceptional word has the smallest possible length for any gap witness. More generally, we construct finite homogeneous languages realizing every prescribed finite spectrum containing $1$, and even prescribe the number of exceptional words added at each degree greater than $1$. Thus arbitrarily long plateaus can precede renewed growth. A binary example with spectrum $\{1,2,4\}$ shows that two letters already suffice; all of its singleton source-pair spectra are initial intervals. In contrast, the iteration-depth spectrum has no gaps for arbitrary languages, by a shortest-derivation argument. Complete proofs accompany the constructions. Independent exhaustive enumeration and dynamic programming verify the small examples; the accompanying code requires only Python's standard library.
\end{abstract}

\section{The problem and the outcome}

The question investigated here is whether an increasing hierarchy of bounded interleavings can pause and subsequently grow. It is elementary to state, concerns finite words rather than encodings of computations, and admits exact finite experiments. It also makes a useful distinction between a resource bound and the number of applications of a fixed operation.

Hughes~\cite[Sections 10--11]{Hughes2026} formulates an insertion-degree no-gap conjecture, its singleton restriction, a finite representation-erasure problem, and a separate iteration-depth conjecture. The cited version is arXiv:2608.27755v1, submitted 27 August 2026. Searches by title and conjecture terminology on 20 September 2026 found that version and no subsequent resolution; this is a search record, not a guarantee of priority.

Here is the precise answer developed in this note. Among pairs of nonempty finite languages, the possible insertion-degree spectra are \emph{exactly} the finite subsets of the positive integers containing $1$. There is no additional interval condition. Moreover, these spectra can be realized with every word of $A$ having one common length and every word of $B$ having another common length. Infinite languages, ambiguous grammars, and unbounded word lengths are unnecessary.

The proof is constructive. It first makes almost every output word easy: a local defect produces a contiguous source factor, hence insertion degree $1$. The few outputs with no local defect are rigid, explicitly designated words. Their unique source-position assignments determine their degrees. Selecting those assignments selects the spectrum.

Three complementary constructions make the result transparent. Section~\ref{sec:ternary} gives a small counterexample that can be checked without a computer. Sections~\ref{sec:delay}--\ref{sec:realization} give the general theorem. Section~\ref{sec:binary} supplies a binary counterexample and a finite certificate demonstrating that source-pair ambiguity can erase an entire intermediate degree even when each individual pair has no gap.
[Added 29 September 2026, batch 41: this paragraph describes Part~I. Part~II, from Section~\ref{ids:bp:sec:intro} on, proves a fixed-alphabet version of the realization theorem: over $\{a,b\}$ and with the single inserted word $a^m$, every finite spectrum containing~$1$ occurs, with prescribed multiplicities above degree~$1$ (Theorem~\ref{ids:bp:thm:profile-main}).]

The iteration-depth problem has the opposite answer. Once a word is reached by a shortest sequence of insertions, every intermediate word on that sequence must have the corresponding shortest depth. This statement does not require finiteness, regularity, or an empty-word assumption.

\section{Definitions and elementary bounds}\label{sec:definitions}

Let $\Sigma$ be a finite alphabet, $\Sigma^*$ its free monoid, and $\eps$ the empty word. The length of a word $w$ is $|w|$. Concatenation is written by juxtaposition. A \emph{factor} is contiguous; a \emph{subsequence} need not be.

\begin{definition}
For $A,B\subseteq\Sigma^*$ and $k\geq1$, define
\begin{align}
 \ins{k}{A}{B}=\{\,&x_0y_1x_1\cdots y_kx_k:\notag\\[-0.4em]
 &y_1\cdots y_k\in A,\quad x_0\cdots x_k\in B,\quad x_i,y_j\in\Sigma^*\,\}.
 \label{eq:insertion}
\end{align}
The inserted source is $A$ and the target source is $B$. All factors may be empty. The shuffle is
\[
 A\sh B=\bigcup_{k\geq1}\ins{k}{A}{B}.
\]
For $w\in A\sh B$, put
\[
 d_{A,B}(w)=\min\{k\geq1:w\in\ins{k}{A}{B}\},
 \qquad \spec{A}{B}=\{d_{A,B}(w):w\in A\sh B\}.
\]
\end{definition}

The allowance of empty factors is important. It makes $\ins{k}{A}{B}\subseteq\ins{k+1}{A}{B}$, and it ensures that ``at most $k$ pieces'' and the displayed $k$-factor definition agree. The degree is oriented: the number counted is the number of runs belonging to $A$, not the number of runs of both sources combined.

\subsection{Assignment masks}

For fixed words $u,v$, a shuffle representation can be recorded by a mask over $\{A,B\}$. Reading the $A$-positions in order gives $u$, and reading the $B$-positions gives $v$. For instance,
\[
 \begin{array}{c|ccccc}
 \text{output}&a&b&c&b&a\\
 \text{mask}&A&B&A&B&A
 \end{array}
 \qquad\text{represents }u=aca,\quad v=bb.
\]
A nonempty maximal block of $A$'s is an $A$-run.

\begin{lemma}[Run characterization]\label{lem:runs}
For a nonempty inserted word, the minimum insertion degree of an output is the minimum number of $A$-runs among all its valid source pairs and assignment masks. An empty inserted word contributes degree $1$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
A factorization in~\eqref{eq:insertion} has at most $k$ nonempty $A$-runs: empty target pieces may cause two displayed inserted pieces to merge. Conversely, the maximal $A$-runs of any mask give a factorization of the required form. Minimize in both directions. When the inserted word is empty there are no $A$-runs, but the definition starts at degree $1$.
\end{proof}

For fixed lengths $m=|u|$ and $n=|v|$, there are exactly $\binom{m+n}{m}$ masks. Different masks, and even different source pairs, can produce the same unlabelled output. It is essential to minimize over these representations instead of merely counting the runs in a convenient one. Source-position masks are a particular instance of the trajectory viewpoint on shuffle; the broader framework is developed in~\cite{Mateescu1998}.

\begin{proposition}[Elementary restrictions]\label{prop:bounds}
The following statements hold.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),nosep]
\item If $A$ and $B$ are nonempty, then $1\in\spec{A}{B}$.
\item If $d_{A,B}(w)=r\geq2$, then $|w|\geq2r-1$.
\item If $A\subseteq\Sigma^m$, $B\subseteq\Sigma^n$, and $m>0$, then every degree is at most $\min(m,n+1)$.
\item Over a one-letter alphabet, nonempty language pairs have spectrum $\{1\}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For (i), choose $u\in A,v\in B$; the concatenation $uv$ has a one-piece representation. For (ii), a minimal mask with $r$ $A$-runs contains at least $r$ $A$-positions and at least $r-1$ separating $B$-positions. For (iii), there are at most $m$ nonempty $A$-runs and at most $n+1$ gaps around the target letters. For (iv), every shuffle of $a^i$ and $a^j$ is the concatenation $a^{i+j}$ and therefore has degree $1$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Gaps and plateaus are equivalent}

The spectrum records exactly when new output words appear:
\begin{equation}
 k\in\spec{A}{B}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \ins{k-1}{A}{B}\subsetneq\ins{k}{A}{B}\qquad(k\geq2).
 \label{eq:new-degree}
\end{equation}
Thus $\ins{k}{A}{B}=\ins{k+1}{A}{B}$ says only that degree $k+1$ is absent. It says nothing purely set-theoretic about later degrees. The no-gap conjecture supplies precisely the missing assertion; the constructions below disprove it.

\section{A five-letter counterexample}\label{sec:ternary}

Let $\Sigma=\{a,b,c\}$, and set
\begin{equation}
 F=\{aba,abb,abc,acb,bba,bca,bcb,cba\},\qquad
 A=\Sigma^3\setminus F,\qquad B=\Sigma^2.
 \label{eq:ternary}
\end{equation}
Thus $|A|=19$ and $|B|=9$. Explicitly,
\begin{align*}
 A=\{&aaa,aab,aac,aca,acc,baa,bab,bac,bbb,bbc,\\
     &bcc,caa,cab,cac,cbb,cbc,cca,ccb,ccc\}.
\end{align*}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:ternary}
For the languages in~\eqref{eq:ternary},
\begin{align*}
 \ins{1}{A}{B}=\ins{2}{A}{B}&=\Sigma^5\setminus\{abcba\},\\
 \ins{k}{A}{B}&=\Sigma^5\qquad(k\geq3).
\end{align*}
Consequently $\spec{A}{B}=\{1,3\}$.
\end{theorem}

\subsection{All other words have a one-piece representation}

A five-letter word belongs to $\ins{1}{A}{B}$ exactly when one of its three length-three factors belongs to $A$. Indeed, remove that contiguous factor; the remaining two letters form a word of $B$. Conversely, a one-piece insertion exhibits such a factor.

We therefore need to find the five-letter words all of whose length-three factors belong to $F$. Regard a triple $xyz\in F$ as a directed edge from $xy$ to $yz$. The complete edge table is
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{cl}
\toprule
Vertex & Outgoing vertices\\
\midrule
$ab$ & $ba,bb,bc$\\
$ac$ & $cb$\\
$bb$ & $ba$\\
$bc$ & $ca,cb$\\
$cb$ & $ba$\\
$ba,ca$ & none\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Other two-letter vertices have no outgoing edges. The only path consisting of three edges is
\[
 ab\longrightarrow bc\longrightarrow cb\longrightarrow ba.
\]
It spells $abcba$. Thus every other five-letter word has an $A$-factor and has degree $1$. This proves the first equality in
\[
 \ins{1}{A}{B}=\Sigma^5\setminus\{abcba\}
 \subseteq\ins{2}{A}{B}\subseteq\ins{3}{A}{B}\subseteq\Sigma^5.
\]

\subsection{The exceptional word requires three pieces}

The distinct length-three subsequences of $abcba$ are
\[
 abc,\ abb,\ aba,\ acb,\ aca,\ bcb,\ bca,\ bba,\ cba.
\]
Exactly one is in $A$: $aca$. Its occurrence is unique, at positions $1,3,5$. The complementary source is $bb$, so its mask is $ABABA$ and has three $A$-runs. There is no alternative source pair or alignment that could lower this cost. Hence
\[
 d_{A,B}(abcba)=3.
\]
All output words have length five, so this completes the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:ternary}.

\begin{corollary}[Minimum possible witness length]
Five is the smallest length of a word that can witness a gap in an insertion-degree spectrum.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Degree $1$ cannot be missing for a nonempty pair. Any gap must therefore have a word of degree at least $3$ above it, whose length is at least $5$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:bounds}. The example attains this bound.
\end{proof}

This does not assert that $19$ and $9$ are minimum language sizes. The exact optimality claim concerns witness length only.

\section{An arbitrarily delayed final word}\label{sec:delay}

The small example suggests a more systematic device: make all but one full-length word contain an easy contiguous source factor.

Fix $r\geq2$, put $N=2r-1$, and use the ordered alphabet
\[
 \Sigma_r=\{1,2,\ldots,N\}.
\]
These integers denote individual alphabet symbols, not decimal strings. Let
\[
 W_r=1\,2\,\cdots\,N,
 \qquad U_r=1\,3\,5\,\cdots\,N.
\]
Define
\begin{align}
  A_r&=\{u\in\Sigma_r^r:u\text{ is not strictly increasing}\}\cup\{U_r\},\notag\\
 B_r&=\Sigma_r^{r-1}.
 \label{eq:one-block}
\end{align}

\begin{lemma}[A local defect supplies a factor]\label{lem:defect}
Every word $w\in\Sigma_r^N$ other than $W_r$ has a length-$r$ factor that is not strictly increasing.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
A strictly increasing word of length $N$ over an $N$-letter ordered alphabet is necessarily $W_r$. Thus $w\neq W_r$ has an adjacent pair $w_i\geq w_{i+1}$. Any adjacent pair in a word of length at least $r\geq2$ is contained in a factor of length $r$: extend the pair to the left and right within the word until the length is $r$. That factor is not strictly increasing.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[One-word delay]\label{thm:delay}
For every $r\geq2$, the pair in~\eqref{eq:one-block} satisfies
\begin{equation}
 \ins{k}{A_r}{B_r}=
 \begin{cases}
  \Sigma_r^N\setminus\{W_r\},&1\leq k<r,\\
  \Sigma_r^N,&k\geq r.
 \end{cases}
 \label{eq:delay}
\end{equation}
In particular, $\spec{A_r}{B_r}=\{1,r\}$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For $w\neq W_r$, Lemma~\ref{lem:defect} provides a contiguous factor in $A_r$. Deleting it leaves $r-1$ symbols, automatically a word of $B_r$. Hence $d_{A_r,B_r}(w)=1$.

Every subsequence of $W_r$ is strictly increasing. The only length-$r$ subsequence accepted by $A_r$ is therefore $U_r$. Since all letters of $W_r$ are distinct, this subsequence has exactly one alignment, using positions $1,3,\ldots,2r-1$. Its complement is in $B_r$, and its mask has exactly $r$ $A$-runs. Thus $d_{A_r,B_r}(W_r)=r$. All shuffles have length $N$, and the displayed formula follows.
\end{proof}

The language sizes and stage sizes are explicit:
\begin{align*}
 |A_r|&=(2r-1)^r-\binom{2r-1}{r}+1,\\
 |B_r|&=(2r-1)^{r-1},\\
 |\ins{k}{A_r}{B_r}|&=(2r-1)^{2r-1}-1\quad(1\leq k<r).
\end{align*}
For $r=3$, this gives an especially simple ordered-alphabet construction with $|A_3|=116$, $|B_3|=25$, and a single exceptional word among $3125$ possible outputs. Section~\ref{sec:ternary} uses fewer symbols and fewer source words, but the ordered construction scales immediately.

\begin{corollary}[No fixed plateau test is sound]\label{cor:plateau}
There is no constant $h\geq1$ such that, for all finite language pairs, observing $h$ consecutive equalities in the degree hierarchy guarantees permanent stabilization.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Take $r=h+2$. Then stages $1$ through $h+1$ are equal, giving $h$ consecutive equalities, but stage $h+2$ adds $W_r$.
\end{proof}

This is a statement about tests based solely on a fixed number of repeated stages, not an undecidability claim. For explicitly finite input languages, the elementary length bound still supplies a termination bound.

\section{Every finite spectrum can be realized}\label{sec:realization}

We now prescribe several exceptional outputs independently. Disjoint alphabet blocks prevent one exception from supplying a representation of another.

\begin{theorem}[Finite delay-profile realization]\label{thm:profile}
Let $t_1,\ldots,t_q$ be any nonempty finite list of integers at least $2$, and choose $m\geq\max_i t_i$. There are a finite alphabet $\Sigma$, finite languages
\[
 A\subseteq\Sigma^m,\qquad B=\Sigma^{m-1},
\]
and distinct words $W_1,\ldots,W_q\in\Sigma^{2m-1}$ such that
\begin{equation}
 \ins{k}{A}{B}
 =\Sigma^{2m-1}\setminus\{W_i:t_i>k\}
 \qquad(k\geq1).
 \label{eq:profile}
\end{equation}
Thus $\spec{A}{B}=\{1,t_1,\ldots,t_q\}$, and the number of words first added at degree $k\geq2$ is exactly $|\{i:t_i=k\}|$.
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Choosing positions with a prescribed number of runs}

Set $N=2m-1$. For $1\leq t\leq m$, define a subset of positions
\begin{equation}
 J_{m,t}=\{1,\ldots,m-t+1\}
 \cup\{m-t+1+2j:1\leq j\leq t-1\}.
 \label{eq:positions}
\end{equation}
It has $(m-t+1)+(t-1)=m$ elements. Its largest position is $m+t-1\leq N$. Its first $m-t+1$ elements form one interval, and each of its remaining $t-1$ elements is separated from the preceding selected position by one unselected position. Hence its indicator mask has exactly $t$ runs.

For $m=4$, three examples are
\[
 \begin{array}{c|c|c}
 t&J_{4,t}&\text{mask}\\
 \hline
 2&\{1,2,3,5\}&AAABABB\\
 3&\{1,2,4,6\}&AABABAB\\
 4&\{1,3,5,7\}&ABABABA
 \end{array}
\]
This explicit position formula avoids any existential choice in the construction.

\subsection{The alphabet and the languages}

Use $q$ disjoint ordered blocks
\[
 C_i=\{c_{i,1},\ldots,c_{i,N}\},\qquad
 \Sigma=C_1\mathbin{\dot\cup}\cdots\mathbin{\dot\cup}C_q,
\]
and define
\[
 W_i=c_{i,1}c_{i,2}\cdots c_{i,N}.
\]
Call a word \emph{block-increasing} if all its letters belong to one block $C_i$ and their second indices are strictly increasing. Let $U_i$ be the subsequence of $W_i$ at the positions $J_{m,t_i}$. Finally set
\begin{equation}
 A=\{u\in\Sigma^m:u\text{ is not block-increasing}\}
   \cup\{U_1,\ldots,U_q\},
 \qquad B=\Sigma^{m-1}.
 \label{eq:profile-construction}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Proof of the realization theorem}

Suppose $w\in\Sigma^N$ is not one of the $W_i$. If every adjacent pair of $w$ belonged to a common block and increased within that block, all letters of $w$ would lie in one block and form a strictly increasing sequence. Since its length is the block size $N$, it would be that block's $W_i$, a contradiction.

Therefore $w$ contains an adjacent pair that either crosses between blocks or fails to increase. Extend that pair to a length-$m$ factor. This factor is not block-increasing, belongs to $A$, and leaves a complementary word of length $m-1$ in $B$. Consequently,
\[
 w\notin\{W_1,\ldots,W_q\}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad d_{A,B}(w)=1.
\]

Now fix $W_i$. Every one of its subsequences is block-increasing within $C_i$. The only such length-$m$ word accepted by $A$ is $U_i$: the other exceptional words $U_j$ use disjoint alphabets. The letters of $W_i$ are distinct, so the alignment of $U_i$ is unique. Its selected positions are exactly $J_{m,t_i}$ and have $t_i$ runs. The complementary word has length $m-1$, so it belongs to $B$. Hence
\[
 d_{A,B}(W_i)=t_i.
\]
These two calculations prove~\eqref{eq:profile}.\qed

\begin{corollary}[Complete classification for finite languages]\label{cor:classification}
A set $S\subseteq\N$ is the insertion-degree spectrum of a pair of nonempty finite languages over some finite alphabet if and only if $S$ is finite and $1\in S$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Necessity follows because a shuffle of two finite languages is finite and because degree $1$ is always attained. If $S=\{1\}$, take $A=\{a\}$ and $B=\{\eps\}$. Otherwise list the elements of $S\setminus\{1\}$ as $t_1,\ldots,t_q$ and apply Theorem~\ref{thm:profile} with $m=\max S$.
\end{proof}

The empty spectrum is realized by an empty source language. Thus, allowing empty inputs, the possible finite spectra are exactly $\varnothing$ and the finite sets containing $1$.

\subsection{Sizes and an example}

The construction has $qN$ alphabet symbols. Exactly $q\binom Nm$ length-$m$ words are block-increasing; we add back $q$ selected words. Therefore
\begin{align*}
 |A|&=(qN)^m-q\binom Nm+q,\qquad |B|=(qN)^{m-1},\\
 |\ins{k}{A}{B}|&=(qN)^N-|\{i:t_i>k\}|.
\end{align*}

For example, choose the desired spectrum $\{1,2,4\}$, use two seven-letter blocks, and set $m=4$. From the first block select positions $1,2,3,5$; from the second select $1,3,5,7$. All length-seven outputs except the two increasing block words have degree $1$. The first exceptional word appears at degree $2$, degree $3$ adds nothing, and the second appears at degree $4$.

Repeated values in the delay list are also meaningful. The list $(2,3,3,4)$ adds one exceptional word at degree $2$, two at degree $3$, and one at degree $4$. Thus the theorem controls the positive-degree increments, not merely their support.

\section{Explicit grammars and automata}

Although the displayed finite sets may be large, their descriptions need not enumerate every accepted word. This section gives direct language descriptions for the realization theorem.

Let $E$ be the set of \emph{bad adjacent pairs}: ordered symbol pairs that either come from different blocks or fail to increase within a common block. The non-block-increasing part of $A$ is exactly
\begin{equation}
 A_{\mathrm{bad}}=
 \bigcup_{j=0}^{m-2}\Sigma^j E\Sigma^{m-j-2}.
 \label{eq:regex}
\end{equation}
Thus $A=A_{\mathrm{bad}}\cup\{U_1,\ldots,U_q\}$ is given by a finite regular expression, and $B=\Sigma^{m-1}$ is even simpler. The equivalence follows because a word is block-increasing exactly when every adjacent pair is good.

For a grammar description, use a nonterminal $X$ with productions $X\to c$ for $c\in\Sigma$. Let $P_0\to\eps$ and $P_j\to XP_{j-1}$ for $1\leq j\leq m-1$. A nonterminal $E_0$ has productions $E_0\to cd$ for bad pairs $(c,d)\in E$. Then take
\begin{align*}
 S_A&\to P_jE_0P_{m-j-2} &&(0\leq j\leq m-2),\\
 S_A&\to U_i &&(1\leq i\leq q),\\
 S_B&\to P_{m-1}.&
\end{align*}
This is a finite acyclic context-free grammar for the required finite languages. It may have several parses when a word contains several bad pairs; that ambiguity is irrelevant to the insertion-degree definition. Regularity follows directly from~\eqref{eq:regex}, independently of the grammar presentation.

Writing $Q=|\Sigma|$, the grammar uses $O(m+Q^2+qm)$ total right-hand-side symbols when the exceptional words are written explicitly. A direct automaton for $A_{\mathrm{bad}}$ tracks the length read, the preceding alphabet symbol, and whether a bad pair has occurred. Accepting at length $m$ in the bad state recognizes $A_{\mathrm{bad}}$. A finite trie for the $U_i$ supplies the exceptional part. These are construction bounds, not claims of minimal grammar or automaton size.

For each fixed $k$, one may also recognize $\ins{k}{A}{B}$ by a finite automaton that nondeterministically assigns each input symbol to an $A$-automaton or a $B$-automaton, remembers the previous source, and counts starts of $A$-runs up to $k$. This automaton is finite whenever the source automata are finite. The gap phenomenon is therefore not caused by stepping outside regular languages.

\section{A binary counterexample and representation erasure}\label{sec:binary}

The parametric theorem uses an alphabet that grows with the prescribed profile. A separate small example demonstrates that a binary alphabet already supports a gap.
[Added 29 September 2026, batch 41: Part~II removes the growing alphabet altogether: every finite spectrum containing~$1$ is realized over $\{a,b\}$ (Corollary~\ref{ids:bp:cor:classification}).]
Set
\begin{equation}
 A=\{aaba,aabb,abbb,bbaa,bbba,bbbb\},
 \qquad B=\{aaa,aba,bab\}.
 \label{eq:binary}
\end{equation}
All shuffles have length seven, and each source pair has $\binom74=35$ assignment masks.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:binary}
For~\eqref{eq:binary}, the degree counts are
\[
 \begin{array}{c|rrrr}
 k&1&2&3&4\\\hline
 |\{w:d_{A,B}(w)=k\}|&57&53&0&1.
 \end{array}
\]
The unique degree-four word is $bababab$. Every singleton pair $(u,v)\in A\times B$ has an initial-interval spectrum.
\end{theorem}

A finite certificate is given in Appendix~\ref{app:binary}, together with complete machine-readable degree and source-pair tables. The proof is an exhaustive check of $18\cdot35=630$ masks followed by minimization over equal outputs. It has also been checked independently by the dynamic program in Section~\ref{sec:algorithms}. The analytical realization theorem does not depend on this finite computation.

There is a particularly short certificate for the absence of degree $3$. Across the eighteen singleton source pairs, exactly twenty-three distinct words have singleton minimum degree $3$. Appendix~\ref{app:binary} gives a source pair and a mask with at most two $A$-runs for each of those words. Therefore every candidate language-level degree-three word loses that degree to a competing representation. The remaining degree-four witness does not.

For $bababab$, a source-pair check leaves only $(bbbb,aaa)$; its unique mask is $ABABABA$. To see the exclusion explicitly, a source $aba$ would leave either $abbb$ or $bbba$ by letter counts. The only possible projections of those forms leave respectively $baa$ and $aab$, not $aba$. A source $bab$ would leave either $aabb$ or $bbaa$; their possible projections leave respectively $bba$ and $abb$, not $bab$. The other source combinations fail letter counts. Thus the high-degree witness survives.

\begin{corollary}
Competing source-pair representations can create a gap even when every singleton source-pair spectrum is an initial interval. Moreover, two is the minimum alphabet size admitting any insertion-degree gap.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The first assertion is exactly the binary example and its pair table, answering the finite representation-erasure question affirmatively. The example proves the binary upper bound; the unary impossibility is Proposition~\ref{prop:bounds}(iv).
\end{proof}

This does not disprove the singleton no-gap conjecture: the source languages in the counterexample have six and three words. It disproves the inference from good behavior of every individual source pair to good behavior of their union.

\section{Iteration depth has no gaps}\label{sec:depth}

Now hold the operation fixed and count applications rather than pieces. Put
\[
 X_0=B,\qquad X_{m+1}=A\sh X_m,
\]
and, for a word reachable at some finite stage, define
\[
 \iota_{A,B}(w)=\min\{m\geq0:w\in X_m\},\qquad
 \mathcal T(A,B)=\{\iota_{A,B}(w):w\in\bigcup_{m\geq0}X_m\}.
\]
No monotonicity of the exact stages $X_m$ is assumed. For example, $A=\{a\}$ and $B=\{\eps\}$ give $X_m=\{a^m\}$.

\begin{lemma}[Shortest-prefix lemma]\label{lem:prefix}
Let $R$ be any binary relation on a set $V$, and let $B\subseteq V$. If a vertex $v$ has shortest $R$-path length $r$ from $B$, there is a vertex of shortest path length $j$ for every $0\leq j\leq r$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Choose a shortest path $v_0Rv_1R\cdots Rv_r=v$ with $v_0\in B$. Its prefix reaches $v_j$ in $j$ steps. A path to $v_j$ of length $\ell<j$, followed by the suffix $v_jR\cdots Rv_r$, would reach $v$ in $\ell+r-j<r$ steps. This contradicts the choice of the path. Thus $v_j$ has shortest length $j$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Iteration-depth no-gap theorem]\label{thm:depth}
For arbitrary languages $A,B$, $\mathcal T(A,B)$ is an initial interval of $\Nzero$, possibly empty or infinite. Equivalently,
\[
 r\in\mathcal T(A,B)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 \{0,1,\ldots,r\}\subseteq\mathcal T(A,B).
\]
The same statement holds when each insertion is restricted to any one fixed degree bound.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Take $V=\Sigma^*$ and let $xRy$ mean $y\in A\sh\{x\}$. The least number of relation steps from $B$ is exactly $\iota_{A,B}$, so apply Lemma~\ref{lem:prefix}. For a fixed degree bound $k$, replace the relation by $y\in\ins{k}{A}{\{x\}}$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{A uniform degree bound along a finite derivation}

One formulation of the depth parameter minimizes $m$ subject to the existence of a single finite $k$ usable in every step of an $m$-step derivation. This gives the same depth as unrestricted shuffle at each step. One direction is immediate. For the other, a finite derivation uses finitely many assignment masks, hence finitely many degrees $k_1,\ldots,k_m$. Their maximum $K$ bounds every step, since empty pieces pad a representation to degree $K$. The case $m=0$ uses any $K\geq1$.

Thus Theorem~\ref{thm:depth} applies to that formulation as well. Its argument does not require a largest word in either language. It also handles $B=\varnothing$, when the spectrum is empty, and $A=\varnothing$ with $B\neq\varnothing$, when the spectrum is $\{0\}$.

\subsection{Why the degree hierarchy behaves differently}

For an autonomous iteration $Y_{m+1}=F(Y_m)$, an equality $Y_m=Y_{m+1}$ immediately gives equality at every later stage, simply by applying $F$. If cumulative reachability is wanted, use
\[
 C_m=\bigcup_{j=0}^mX_j,\qquad C_{m+1}=C_m\cup(A\sh C_m).
\]
Shuffle distributes over unions in its second argument, so this is another autonomous iteration.

By contrast, $\ins{k}{A}{B}$ increases the admissible number of source runs in a \emph{single} shuffle. It is not obtained by applying one fixed extension operator to the previous unlabelled output set. Minimizing over representations may discard the source information needed to extend a particular witness. The counterexamples show that this loss of information can hide every witness at one cost while preserving a witness at a later cost.

\section{Exact algorithms and reproducibility}\label{sec:algorithms}

\subsection{Exhaustive assignment enumeration}

For each $u\in A$, $v\in B$, enumerate every $|u|$-element subset of the $|u|+|v|$ output positions. Merge the words according to that mask, count its $A$-runs, and retain the smallest count for each output word. A second minimum over source pairs gives the language-level degree.

With $m=|u|$ and $n=|v|$, this takes
\[
 O\left((m+n)\binom{m+n}{m}\right)
\]
character operations per source pair, aside from ordinary dictionary costs. The implementation normalizes the empty-inserted-word case to degree $1$ and treats an absent output as nonmembership, not as degree zero.

The raw masks are witnesses only of upper bounds. Exactness comes from exhaustive coverage of every mask and every source pair. This distinction is particularly important in a problem whose obstruction is competing representations.

\subsection{An independent dynamic program}

For a fixed triple $(u,v,w)$ with $|u|=m$, $|v|=n$, and $|w|=m+n$, define $D(i,j,s)$ as the minimum number of $A$-runs after matching the first $i$ letters of $u$ and $j$ letters of $v$ against the prefix of $w$ of length $i+j$. The value $s\in\{A,B\}$ records the most recently used source; the starting state is treated as $B$.

Initialize $D(0,0,B)=0$ and all other states to $+\infty$. If $u_{i+1}=w_{i+j+1}$, relax
\[
 D(i+1,j,A)\gets\min\bigl(D(i+1,j,A),D(i,j,s)+[s\neq A]\bigr).
\]
If $v_{j+1}=w_{i+j+1}$, relax
\[
 D(i,j+1,B)\gets\min\bigl(D(i,j+1,B),D(i,j,s)\bigr).
\]
At the end, take $\min_sD(m,n,s)$ and normalize zero to one. Infinite cost means that $w$ is not a shuffle of the fixed pair.

\begin{proposition}
The dynamic program computes the exact singleton insertion degree in $O((m+1)(n+1))$ time and space.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Every transition consumes exactly the next letter of one source and the next letter of the output. Paths therefore correspond bijectively to valid assignment masks. A transition into source $A$ charges one exactly at the start of an $A$-run. The state graph is acyclic because $i+j$ increases at every transition. Induction in that sum proves the recurrence computes minimum path cost. There are $2(m+1)(n+1)$ states and at most two outgoing transitions per state.
\end{proof}

Minimizing the dynamic-programming result over all pairs gives a second algorithm for $d_{A,B}(w)$. The verifier compares these two implementations for \emph{every} word in the complete small output universes, including words outside the shuffle.

\subsection{How the counterexample search was encoded}

The initial search used Boolean variables $a_u,b_v$ selecting words into $A$ and $B$. For each potential output $w$, precompute its exact singleton degree for every potential pair. Define
\[
 Q_{w,k}=\bigvee_{d_{u,v}(w)\leq k}(a_u\wedge b_v).
\]
To forbid degree $g$, impose $Q_{w,g}\Rightarrow Q_{w,g-1}$ for every output. To require a larger degree, require at least one $w$ with $Q_{w,M}\wedge\neg Q_{w,g}$, where $M$ is the length-based maximum degree. These constraints express the actual minima, not merely a chosen mask's run count.

A Z3 search suggested the small examples; the general construction was subsequently proved without relying on the solver. The archive includes a portable SMT-LIB instance and its generator. Neither Z3 nor the discovery search is needed to run the exact verifier. No minimality claim about the total number of source words rests on the solver's output.

\section{What has and has not been settled}

The insertion-degree no-gap assertion is false, already for finite regular languages. The finite representation-erasure question has an affirmative answer: the binary certificate exhibits the required source-pair behavior. The general finite-spectrum realization theorem strengthens the negative answer from an isolated gap to an arbitrary finite profile. Separately, the iteration-depth no-gap assertion is true for arbitrary languages.

The unrestricted singleton insertion-degree question remains outside the proofs in this note. The arbitrary-spectrum construction intentionally exploits multiple possible source words and cannot be read as a singleton counterexample. Similarly, the argument does not classify infinite spectra over a fixed finite alphabet, and it does not establish which finite spectra can all be realized over one fixed binary alphabet.
[Added 29 September 2026, batch 41: the last point is settled in Part~II. Every finite set of positive integers containing~$1$ is the spectrum of a pair of finite binary languages, with the inserted language the single unary word $\{a^m\}$ (Theorem~\ref{ids:bp:thm:profile-main}, Corollary~\ref{ids:bp:cor:classification}). The unrestricted singleton question and the classification of infinite spectra over a fixed finite alphabet remain open (Questions~\ref{ids:bp:q:singleton} and~\ref{ids:bp:q:infinite}).]

Several concrete problems are left by these results. One can seek smaller source languages for a specified spectrum, determine the least alphabet needed to realize $\{1,r\}$ as $r$ grows, or study descriptions by small deterministic automata rather than by cardinality. The proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:profile} also suggests investigating classes where competing source-pair representations are controlled strongly enough to restore an interval theorem. These are research directions, not additional claims of unresolved status in the published literature.
[Added 29 September 2026, batch 41: Part~II answers the least-alphabet question: for every $r\geq2$ the spectrum $\{1,r\}$ is realized over two letters, and no fewer suffice (Corollary~\ref{ids:bp:cor:classification}). It answers the smallest-source question for the inserted language only, which it reduces to one word; the minimum size of the target language stays open (Question~\ref{ids:bp:q:target}), as do small automaton descriptions (Question~\ref{ids:bp:q:automata}) and interval-restoring classes (Question~\ref{ids:bp:q:structure}). Its quadratic length bound (Theorem~\ref{ids:bp:thm:length-main}) holds only in its full-coverage class, not for every binary spectrum.]

The central lesson is specific: an insertion-degree spectrum is a minimum over representations, and taking that minimum can erase intermediate costs. The same phenomenon does not affect shortest derivation depth, because a cheaper prefix could always be substituted into a derivation. This difference accounts for the opposite answers without appealing to general undecidability machinery.

\subsection{The answer in brief}\label{ids:bp:sec:brief}

Part~I realizes arbitrary finite insertion-degree spectra over alphabets that
grow with the requested profile (Theorem~\ref{thm:profile}) and leaves the
fixed binary alphabet open (Section~10). Part~II proves that every finite
subset of the positive integers containing~$1$ is realizable over $\{a,b\}$,
even with the inserted language equal to the single word $\{a^m\}$. More
strongly, any prescribed finite multiplicities of degrees $2,\ldots,m$ can be
realized, with every other output having degree~$1$. Both inputs are finite
and homogeneous, and their shuffle is an entire fixed-letter-count class.

The proof turns a binary word into its vector of $a$-gap lengths. Insertion
degree becomes the minimum support size of a nonnegative vector difference.
Separated exceptional vectors protect the desired degrees, while a
quantitative escape lemma makes every other output a one-piece insertion. This
mechanism requires no search oracle or unproved singleton conjecture.

Part~II also proves an exact extremal result. With $A=\{a^m\}$, if the shuffle
is an entire binary fixed-letter-count class, every output but one has
degree~$1$, and the remaining output has degree $m\geq3$, then its length is
at least $m^2$. For every $m\geq6$, the construction attains $m^2$.
\emph{The full-coverage hypothesis is essential to the scope of this bound: it
is not a lower bound on the length of an arbitrary binary word of insertion
degree~$m$, for which the elementary bound is $2m-1$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:bounds}(ii)).}

Each individual source pair nevertheless has the same gap-free degree
histogram. Part~II computes that histogram and proves its real-rootedness in
the balanced case, while the combined language can have arbitrary missing
degrees and almost entirely nonreal histogram zeros. Full proofs, an explicit
14-symbol example, exact verification code, executed receipts, and further
research questions are included.

\subsection{The repository question and an attribution}\label{ids:bp:sec:question}

The manuscript read Part~I at the pin as follows. Part~I proves arbitrary
finite-spectrum realization with an alphabet that grows with the requested
profile (Theorem~\ref{thm:profile}), and supplies a binary example with
spectrum $\{1,2,4\}$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:binary}). It explicitly leaves open the
classification over a fixed binary alphabet and the least alphabet size for
spectra $\{1,r\}$ (Section~10). The earlier realization theorem is not claimed
as new here.

Part~II proves a stronger fixed-alphabet statement. The inserted language can
contain just one word, consisting entirely of copies of one letter; all the
source-pair ambiguity can be confined to the target language. The target
words have the same length and the same number of each letter. Moreover, one
can prescribe not only which degrees occur but the exact number of words of
each degree above~$1$.

Hughes formulates the general insertion-degree no-gap conjecture, its
both-singleton restriction, and a finite representation-erasure problem in
Section~11 of~\cite{Hughes2026}. The same source already proves that a unary
inserted word and a single arbitrary target word have an initial-interval
spectrum \cite[Theorem~9, page~9]{Hughes2026}. Part~II uses and reproves its
gap-distribution observation; \emph{the unary-source singleton interval
theorem is not a new contribution of this addition}.

The new result is that an arbitrary finite spectrum can arise by taking the
minimum over a finite family of these particularly well-behaved singleton
pairs, while keeping the inserted word fixed. It is a uniform strengthening
of the representation-erasure phenomenon already exhibited in Part~I
(Section~\ref{sec:binary}). It is not a resolution of the unrestricted
both-singleton conjecture.

\section{Definitions and main statements}\label{ids:bp:sec:main}

The oriented insertion degree is that of Section~\ref{sec:definitions}.

\subsection{Fixed-letter-count classes}
Write $\Nzero=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}$ and, for $g\geq1$, set
\[
 \bpC{T}{g}=\{\mathbf c=(c_1,\ldots,c_g)\in\Nzero^g:
                   c_1+\cdots+c_g=T\}.
\]
Encode a gap vector by
\begin{equation}
 \bpgap{\mathbf c}=a^{c_1}b a^{c_2}b\cdots b a^{c_g},\qquad
 \bpW{T}{g}=\{\bpgap{\mathbf c}:\mathbf c\in\bpC{T}{g}\}.
 \label{ids:bp:eq:gap-word}
\end{equation}
There are exactly $g-1$ separators, even when some gap lengths are zero.
Consequently the encoding is one-to-one, and $\bpW{T}{g}$ is the entire set of
binary words with $T$ copies of $a$ and $g-1$ copies of $b$. In particular,
\begin{equation}
 |\bpW{T}{g}|=\binom{T+g-1}{g-1}.
 \label{ids:bp:eq:class-size}
\end{equation}
This is often called a Parikh class; only the explicit description above is
needed in the proofs.

\begin{theorem}[Arbitrary binary profiles with one unary inserted word]
\label{ids:bp:thm:profile-main}
Let $m\geq2$, and let $(t_0,\ldots,t_{q-1})$ be a nonempty finite list with
$2\leq t_i\leq m$. There are an integer $T$, a finite language
$B\subseteq\bpW{T-m}{m}$, and distinct words $W_0,\ldots,W_{q-1}$ in
$\bpW{T}{m}$ such that, for $A=\{a^m\}$,
\begin{equation}
 \ins{k}{A}{B}=\bpW{T}{m}\setminus\{W_i:t_i>k\}
 \qquad(k\geq1).
 \label{ids:bp:eq:profile-main}
\end{equation}
In particular $A\sh B=\bpW{T}{m}$, $d_{A,B}(W_i)=t_i$, and every other
output has degree~$1$. Each $W_i$ is produced by exactly one target word of
$B$, although it can have more than one position mask for that target.
\end{theorem}

An explicit formula for $T$ and every word of $B$ is given in
Section~\ref{ids:bp:sec:construction}. Repeated entries in the list are
allowed and control multiplicities, not merely the support of the spectrum.

\begin{corollary}[Fixed-binary classification]
\label{ids:bp:cor:classification}
The spectra of pairs of nonempty finite binary languages are exactly the
finite subsets of $\N$ containing~$1$. Every such set is attained with the
inserted language a singleton $\{a^m\}$. If the spectrum contains a degree
greater than~$1$, two is its minimum possible alphabet size, and one is the
minimum possible cardinality of the nonempty inserted language.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
A shuffle of finite languages is finite. Concatenating any chosen source pair
shows that degree~$1$ occurs. Conversely, list the elements of
$S\setminus\{1\}$ and apply Theorem~\ref{ids:bp:thm:profile-main} with
$m=\max S$. For $S=\{1\}$ use $A=\{a\}$, $B=\{b\}$. Over a one-letter alphabet
every shuffle output is the concatenation of its sources, so every degree
is~$1$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:bounds}(iv)). Finally, a nonempty language
cannot have fewer than one word.
\end{proof}
Allowing empty input languages adds precisely the empty spectrum, as in
Part~I. In particular, for every $r\geq2$ the least alphabet realizing
$\{1,r\}$ has two letters, which answers the least-alphabet question of
Part~I's Section~10; the smallest-source question there is answered for the
inserted language only (one word), not for the target language
(Question~\ref{ids:bp:q:target}).

\begin{theorem}[Sharp isolated-witness length in the full-coverage class]
\label{ids:bp:thm:length-main}
Let $m\geq3$. Suppose $A=\{a^m\}$, $B\subseteq\bpW{T-m}{g}$ is nonempty,
and
\[
 A\sh B=\bpW{T}{g}.
\]
If exactly one output has degree greater than~$1$, and its degree is $m$,
then every output has length
\begin{equation}
 N=T+g-1\geq m^2.
 \label{ids:bp:eq:sharp-main}
\end{equation}
For every $m\geq6$, equality is attained with $g=m$ and
$T=m^2-m+1$. Thus the minimum length in this class is exactly $m^2$ for
all $m\geq6$.
\end{theorem}

The full-coverage and singleton-unary-source hypotheses are part of the
extremal theorem. The assertion is \emph{not} a universal $m^2$ lower bound
for a word of insertion degree $m$: without these hypotheses the elementary
length bound is only $2m-1$.

\section{Gap vectors and the minimum over representations}
\label{ids:bp:sec:gaps}

For vectors, inequalities are coordinatewise, and
$\supp(\mathbf z)=\{j:z_j\ne0\}$.

\begin{lemma}[Gap-support formula]
\label{ids:bp:lem:gap}
Fix $m\geq1$, $g\geq1$, and $\boldsymbol\beta\in\Nzero^g$. A word
$\bpgap{\mathbf c}$ is a shuffle of $a^m$ and $\bpgap{\boldsymbol\beta}$ if and
only if
\[
 \boldsymbol\beta\leq\mathbf c,\qquad
 \sum_j(c_j-\beta_j)=m.
\]
For every such output,
\begin{equation}
 d_{\{a^m\},\{\bpgap{\boldsymbol\beta}\}}(\bpgap{\mathbf c})
   =|\supp(\mathbf c-\boldsymbol\beta)|.
 \label{ids:bp:eq:singleton-gap}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The inserted source supplies no $b$. Hence all $b$'s come from the target,
in their original order. The $j$th output gap retains the target's $\beta_j$
copies of $a$ and receives exactly $c_j-\beta_j$ new copies. This proves the
necessary coordinate and sum conditions; they are sufficient by placing the
new copies in those gaps.

Each gap receiving new copies contains at least one inserted-source run.
Distinct gaps are separated by a target $b$, so those runs cannot merge.
Conversely, within each receiving gap put all its new copies consecutively.
This gives exactly one run per receiving gap and proves the minimum formula.
The argument minimizes over all masks for the fixed target; it does not
assume that a mask is unique.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Language-level gap optimization]
\label{ids:bp:cor:gap-language}
Let $\bpE\subseteq\bpC{T-m}{g}$ and
$B=\{\bpgap{\boldsymbol\beta}:\boldsymbol\beta\in \bpE\}$. For
$\mathbf c\in\bpC{T}{g}$,
\begin{equation}
 d_{\{a^m\},B}(\bpgap{\mathbf c})
 =\min_{\substack{\boldsymbol\beta\in \bpE\\\boldsymbol\beta\leq\mathbf c}}
       |\supp(\mathbf c-\boldsymbol\beta)|,
 \label{ids:bp:eq:language-gap}
\end{equation}
where an empty minimum means the word is not a shuffle output. In particular,
it has degree~$1$ precisely when
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf c-m\mathbf e_j\in \bpE
 \quad\hbox{for some $j$ with $c_j\geq m$}.
 \label{ids:bp:eq:one-run}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Take the minimum of \eqref{ids:bp:eq:singleton-gap} over the target words. A
nonnegative vector of total $m$ has support one exactly when it is
$m\mathbf e_j$.
\end{proof}

The distinction between \eqref{ids:bp:eq:singleton-gap} and
\eqref{ids:bp:eq:language-gap} is the entire source of the examples below. A
union of target languages takes a pointwise minimum of costs, not a union of
the sets of their minimum costs.

\subsection{The exact singleton histogram}
\begin{proposition}[Singleton degree counts]
\label{ids:bp:prop:histogram}
For every fixed target gap vector $\boldsymbol\beta\in\Nzero^g$, the number
of distinct outputs of the pair $(a^m,\bpgap{\boldsymbol\beta})$ whose minimum
degree is $r$ is
\begin{equation}
 h_{m,g,r}=\binom gr\binom{m-1}{r-1}
 \qquad(1\leq r\leq\min(m,g)).
 \label{ids:bp:eq:histogram}
\end{equation}
These counts are independent of $\boldsymbol\beta$. The total number of
outputs is $\binom{m+g-1}{g-1}$, and the spectrum is
$\{1,\ldots,\min(m,g)\}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Every output corresponds to a unique weak composition
$\boldsymbol\delta=\mathbf c-\boldsymbol\beta$ of $m$ into $g$ parts.
To have support $r$, choose its $r$ positive coordinates in $\binom gr$ ways
and distribute $m$ positively over them in $\binom{m-1}{r-1}$ ways.
The encoding by gap vectors is injective, so these count distinct words rather
than masks. Summing counts all weak compositions of $m$.
\end{proof}

The spectrum conclusion is Hughes's unary-source singleton theorem
\cite[Theorem~9]{Hughes2026}; the displayed counts are its elementary
counting refinement. The same proof works with an arbitrary fixed sequence of
non-$a$ separator letters, not only repeated $b$'s.

\section{A geometric realization principle}
\label{ids:bp:sec:geometry}

We first isolate the geometry needed to protect exceptional outputs. This
also makes the construction adaptable to other parameter choices.
For equal-sum vectors, define
\begin{equation}
 \Delta(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)
   =\sum_j\bppos{x_j-y_j}
   =\frac12\sum_j|x_j-y_j|.
 \label{ids:bp:eq:distance}
\end{equation}
The second equality follows by equating total positive and negative parts.
Consequently $\Delta$ is a metric on each fixed-sum class.

\begin{lemma}[A target below an exceptional vector is nearby]
\label{ids:bp:lem:near}
Let $\mathbf c,\mathbf v\in\bpC{T}{g}$ and
$\boldsymbol\gamma\in\Nzero^g$ have total $m$. If
$\mathbf c-\boldsymbol\gamma\geq0$ and
$\mathbf c-\boldsymbol\gamma\leq\mathbf v$, then
\[
 \Delta(\mathbf c,\mathbf v)\leq m.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For each coordinate, $(c_j-v_j)_+\leq\gamma_j$. Sum.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[One-piece escape]
\label{ids:bp:lem:escape}
Let $\mathbf v\in\bpC{T}{g}$ and assume
\begin{equation}
 T>g(m-1),\qquad
 \rho_j(\mathbf v):=\sum_{k\ne j}\bppos{v_k-m+1}>m
 \quad(1\leq j\leq g).
 \label{ids:bp:eq:escape-hypotheses}
\end{equation}
For every $\mathbf c\in\bpC{T}{g}$ with $\mathbf c\ne\mathbf v$, there
exists $j$ such that $c_j\geq m$ and
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf c-m\mathbf e_j\not\leq\mathbf v.
 \label{ids:bp:eq:escape}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Call $j$ eligible if $c_j\geq m$. At least one coordinate is eligible,
otherwise $T\leq g(m-1)$. Also some coordinate $i$ satisfies $c_i>v_i$,
because the two vectors have the same total and are distinct.

If at least two coordinates are eligible, choose an eligible $j\ne i$.
Subtracting $m$ there leaves the excess at $i$, proving
\eqref{ids:bp:eq:escape}. Suppose instead that $j$ is the unique eligible
coordinate. If an excess $c_i>v_i$ occurs at an $i\ne j$, subtraction at $j$
again preserves it. The remaining case has
\[
 c_k\leq\min(v_k,m-1)\quad(k\ne j).
\]
Equality of totals then gives
\[
 c_j-v_j=\sum_{k\ne j}(v_k-c_k)
 \geq\sum_{k\ne j}\bppos{v_k-m+1}=\rho_j(\mathbf v)>m.
\]
Thus $c_j-m>v_j$, and subtracting at $j$ still leaves an excess.
These cases exhaust all possibilities.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Why both hypotheses matter]
\label{ids:bp:rem:escape-guardrail}
Large values of $\rho_j$ do not by themselves imply that an eligible coordinate
exists. For $m=g=6$, the vector $(12,12,1,1,1,1)$ satisfies $\rho_j>6$ for
every $j$, but has total $28$. The other vector $(5,5,5,5,4,4)$ has the same
total and no coordinate at least~$6$. It admits no one-piece insertion of
$a^6$ at all. The strict total-size condition in
\eqref{ids:bp:eq:escape-hypotheses} cannot be dropped. This obstruction is the
reason the sharp construction becomes quadratic rather than remaining linear
in $m$.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[Separated exceptional vectors]
\label{ids:bp:thm:geometric}
Let $m\geq1$, $g\geq2$, $q\geq1$, and let distinct vectors
$\mathbf v_0,\ldots,\mathbf v_{q-1}\in\bpC{T}{g}$ satisfy
\eqref{ids:bp:eq:escape-hypotheses} and
\begin{equation}
 \Delta(\mathbf v_i,\mathbf v_h)>2m\qquad(i\ne h).
 \label{ids:bp:eq:separation}
\end{equation}
Choose $\boldsymbol\delta_i\leq\mathbf v_i$ of total $m$, with
$t_i=|\supp(\boldsymbol\delta_i)|\geq2$, and put
$\boldsymbol\beta_i=\mathbf v_i-\boldsymbol\delta_i$.
Define
\begin{align}
 \bpE_{\rm safe}
 &=\{\boldsymbol\beta\in\bpC{T-m}{g}:
          \boldsymbol\beta\not\leq\mathbf v_i\text{ for every }i\},
 \label{ids:bp:eq:safe}\\
 \bpE&=\bpE_{\rm safe}\cup\{\boldsymbol\beta_0,\ldots,
                         \boldsymbol\beta_{q-1}\},\qquad
 B=\{\bpgap{\boldsymbol\beta}:\boldsymbol\beta\in \bpE\}.
 \label{ids:bp:eq:targets}
\end{align}
Then $A=\{a^m\}$ and $B$ satisfy
\[
 \ins{k}{A}{B}=\bpW{T}{g}\setminus
                 \{\bpgap{\mathbf v_i}:t_i>k\}
 \quad(k\geq1).
\]
Each exceptional output $\bpgap{\mathbf v_i}$ has the unique target
$\bpgap{\boldsymbol\beta_i}$ in $B$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
All possible shuffles have the asserted letter counts. We determine the
minimum degree of every vector in $\bpC{T}{g}$.

\emph{Exceptional vectors.} No element of $\bpE_{\rm safe}$ lies below
$\mathbf v_i$. For $h\ne i$, the inequality
$\boldsymbol\beta_h\leq\mathbf v_i$, together with
$\mathbf v_h-\boldsymbol\beta_h=\boldsymbol\delta_h$ of total $m$,
would imply $\Delta(\mathbf v_h,\mathbf v_i)\leq m$ by
Lemma~\ref{ids:bp:lem:near}. This contradicts separation. Thus the only
accepted target below $\mathbf v_i$ is $\boldsymbol\beta_i$, and
Lemma~\ref{ids:bp:lem:gap} gives minimum degree $t_i$. The same argument shows
that the special targets are distinct and proves uniqueness of the target
word.

\emph{Every other vector.} Fix $\mathbf c$ distinct from all exceptional
vectors. A center $\mathbf v_i$ will be called an obstruction for $\mathbf c$
if some eligible coordinate $j$ has
$\mathbf c-m\mathbf e_j\leq\mathbf v_i$. By Lemma~\ref{ids:bp:lem:near}, each
such center lies at distance at most $m$ from $\mathbf c$. There is at most
one obstructing center: two would violate \eqref{ids:bp:eq:separation} by the
triangle inequality.

There is at least one eligible coordinate because $T>g(m-1)$. If no center
is an obstruction, any eligible subtraction lies in $\bpE_{\rm safe}$. If
$\mathbf v_i$ is the sole obstruction, Lemma~\ref{ids:bp:lem:escape} gives an
eligible subtraction not below that center. It cannot be below any other
center, because that would make the other center an obstruction as well.
Again it lies in $\bpE_{\rm safe}$. In both cases
Corollary~\ref{ids:bp:cor:gap-language} gives degree~$1$.

Every vector has now been assigned an accepted representation, and its
minimum degree has been proved. This establishes the entire hierarchy.
\end{proof}

\section{Explicit binary profiles and exact sizes}
\label{ids:bp:sec:construction}

The next parameter choices turn Theorem~\ref{ids:bp:thm:geometric} into a
completely explicit recipe. They keep the alphabet fixed and give the sharp
isolated length for all sufficiently large $m$.

\subsection{Base vectors and separated translates}
For $m\geq2$, put $\Lambda=2m+1$ and define a vector $\mathbf b_m$ with $m$
coordinates by
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf b_m=
 \begin{cases}
 (4,4),&m=2,\\
 (4,4,4),&m=3,\\
 (2m+\eta_m,\,2m,\,1,\ldots,1),&m\geq4,
 \end{cases}
 \qquad \eta_m=\max(0,m^2-6m+3).
 \label{ids:bp:eq:base}
\end{equation}
Let $T_0=\sum_j b_{m,j}$. Thus
\begin{equation}
 T_0=
 \begin{cases}
 8,&m=2,\\
 12,&m=3,\\
 \max(5m-2,m^2-m+1),&m\geq4.
 \end{cases}
 \label{ids:bp:eq:T0}
\end{equation}
Given a profile list $(t_0,\ldots,t_{q-1})$, set
\begin{align}
 \mathbf v_i&=\mathbf b_m+\Lambda i\mathbf e_1+\Lambda(q-1-i)\mathbf e_2,
       &&0\leq i<q,\label{ids:bp:eq:centers}\\
 T&=T_0+\Lambda(q-1),\qquad N=T+m-1,\label{ids:bp:eq:length-formula}\\
 \boldsymbol\delta_i
   &=(m-t_i+1,\underbrace{1,\ldots,1}_{t_i-1},
                          \underbrace{0,\ldots,0}_{m-t_i}),
       \qquad\boldsymbol\beta_i=\mathbf v_i-\boldsymbol\delta_i.
 \label{ids:bp:eq:deficits}
\end{align}
Use \eqref{ids:bp:eq:safe}--\eqref{ids:bp:eq:targets} with these vectors.
Equations \eqref{ids:bp:eq:base}--\eqref{ids:bp:eq:deficits} and the
membership condition are an explicit specification of every target word; no
existential choices remain.

\begin{lemma}[The parameters meet every hypothesis]
\label{ids:bp:lem:parameters}
The vectors in \eqref{ids:bp:eq:centers} and \eqref{ids:bp:eq:deficits}
satisfy all the hypotheses of Theorem~\ref{ids:bp:thm:geometric}, with $g=m$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Every center has total $T$ and positive coordinates. For $m=2$, $T_0=8>2$
and each $\rho_j$ of the base equals $3>2$. For $m=3$, $T_0=12>6$ and
each $\rho_j$ equals $2(4-3+1)=4>3$.

For $m\geq4$, $T_0\geq m(m-1)+1$. The first two base coordinates are
at least $2m$. After any one coordinate is excluded from the sum defining
$\rho_j$, at least one of these two remains. It contributes at least
$2m-m+1=m+1>m$. Increasing coordinates preserves all these inequalities,
so every translated center satisfies the escape hypotheses.

For distinct indices,
\[
 \Delta(\mathbf v_i,\mathbf v_h)=\Lambda|i-h|>2m.
\]
Each deficit has total $m$ and exactly $t_i$ positive coordinates. Its first
coordinate is at most $m-1$, while the first coordinate of the center is at
least $m$. Every other positive deficit coordinate is one and every center
coordinate is positive. Thus $\boldsymbol\delta_i\leq\mathbf v_i$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{ids:bp:thm:profile-main}]
Apply Theorem~\ref{ids:bp:thm:geometric} to
Lemma~\ref{ids:bp:lem:parameters}, taking $W_i=\bpgap{\mathbf v_i}$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Exact language cardinalities}
\begin{proposition}[Input and output sizes]
\label{ids:bp:prop:sizes}
For the explicit construction, define
\begin{equation}
 \kappa_m=
 \begin{cases}
 3,&m=2,\\
 10,&m=3,\\
 (m+4)2^{m-3},&m\geq4.
 \end{cases}
 \label{ids:bp:eq:K}
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{align}
 |A|&=1,\qquad |B|=\binom{T-1}{m-1}-q(\kappa_m-1),
                         \label{ids:bp:eq:input-count}\\
 |A\sh B|&=\binom{N}{m-1},\qquad
 |\ins{k}{A}{B}|=\binom{N}{m-1}-|\{i:t_i>k\}|.
                         \label{ids:bp:eq:output-count}
\end{align}
Every target word has length $N-m$, and every output has length $N$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
At total $T-m$, let
$\operatorname{Box}_i=\{\boldsymbol\beta\in\bpC{T-m}{m}:
\boldsymbol\beta\leq\mathbf v_i\}$. These sets are pairwise disjoint.
Indeed, if $\boldsymbol\beta\leq\mathbf v_i,\mathbf v_h$, then on the
coordinates where $v_{i,j}>v_{h,j}$ one has
$v_{i,j}-v_{h,j}\leq v_{i,j}-\beta_j$. Summing bounds
$\Delta(\mathbf v_i,\mathbf v_h)$ by $m$, contradicting separation.

Count $\operatorname{Box}_i$ through deficits $\boldsymbol\delta=\mathbf v_i-
\boldsymbol\beta$ of total $m$. For $m=2$ and $m=3$ none of their coordinates
is capped below $m$, so the counts are $\binom31=3$ and $\binom52=10$.
For $m\geq4$, the first two deficit coordinates are unrestricted, while the
remaining $m-2$ lie in $\{0,1\}$. Hence
\[
 |\operatorname{Box}_i|=\sum_{s=0}^{m-2}\binom{m-2}{s}(m-s+1)
 =(m+1)2^{m-2}-(m-2)2^{m-3}=\kappa_m.
\]
The safe set removes all $q\kappa_m$ vectors and the construction restores
one in each box, proving \eqref{ids:bp:eq:input-count}. The exceptional
targets are distinct and were all removed before restoration. The output
formulas follow from Theorem~\ref{ids:bp:thm:profile-main} and
\eqref{ids:bp:eq:class-size}. Letter counts give the length statements.
\end{proof}

For one prescribed degree $m$, representative sizes are as follows. No
minimality is asserted in the first four rows.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}rrrrr@{}}
\toprule
$m$ & $T_0$ & output length $N$ & $|B|$ & $|A\sh B|$\\
\midrule
2 & 8 & 9 & 5 & 9\\
3 & 12 & 14 & 46 & 91\\
4 & 18 & 21 & 665 & 1,330\\
5 & 23 & 27 & 7,280 & 17,550\\
6 & 31 & 36 & 142,427 & 376,992\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
For $m\geq6$, the length for an arbitrary $q$-entry profile is
\begin{equation}
 N=m^2+(2m+1)(q-1).
 \label{ids:bp:eq:large-m-length}
\end{equation}
The exact $m=5$ count in the table is
$\binom{22}{4}-(36-1)=7315-35=7280$.

\subsection{All possible finite higher-degree count sequences}
\begin{corollary}[Arbitrary higher-degree multiplicities]
\label{ids:bp:cor:multiplicity}
Fix $m\geq2$ and nonnegative integers $q_2,\ldots,q_m$, not all zero.
There are finite binary languages as above for which
\[
 |\{w:d_{A,B}(w)=r\}|=q_r\quad(2\leq r\leq m),
\]
and every other output has degree~$1$. Consequently the degree-enumerating
polynomial is
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{Deg}_{A,B}(z)=\left(\binom{N}{m-1}-q\right)z
                         +\sum_{r=2}^m q_rz^r,
 \qquad q=\sum_{r=2}^m q_r.
 \label{ids:bp:eq:aggregate-polynomial}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Repeat the entry $r$ exactly $q_r$ times in the profile list and apply
Theorem~\ref{ids:bp:thm:profile-main} and Proposition~\ref{ids:bp:prop:sizes}.
\end{proof}
The degree-one coefficient is determined by the construction. The theorem
does not prescribe that coefficient independently. The entirely zero
higher-degree profile is already represented by any nonempty pair with
spectrum $\{1\}$.

\section{Sharp quadratic size for an isolated high degree}
\label{ids:bp:sec:sharp}

The optimized parameter choice is forced by a combinatorial obstruction,
not merely suggested by finite experiments.

\subsection{Outputs with no long enough gap}
\begin{lemma}[A capped-composition obstruction]
\label{ids:bp:lem:capped}
Assume $A=\{a^m\}$, $B\subseteq\bpW{T-m}{g}$, and
$A\sh B=\bpW{T}{g}$. The number of outputs with degree greater than~$1$
is at least
\begin{equation}
 [x^T](1+x+\cdots+x^{m-1})^g.
 \label{ids:bp:eq:cap-coeff}
\end{equation}
If $m\geq2$ and $0<T<g(m-1)$, this coefficient is at least $g$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Each vector with every coordinate at most $m-1$ lacks an eligible one-run
deletion in \eqref{ids:bp:eq:one-run}. Full coverage guarantees it is
nevertheless an output, so its degree exceeds one. The coefficient counts
exactly those vectors.

For the second assertion, the case $g=1$ has coefficient one. For $g\geq2$,
form a vector by filling coordinates greedily to
$m-1$, then using a possible remainder, then zeros. At an interior total it
is not constant. Permuting its coordinates yields at least $g$ distinct
vectors: if a value has multiplicity $s$ with $1\leq s<g$, the number of
permutations is at least $\binom gs\geq g$. Each permutation still has the
required total and coordinate cap.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The boundary total cannot attain high degree}
\begin{proposition}[Universal lower bound in the specified class]
\label{ids:bp:prop:lower}
Under the hypotheses of Theorem~\ref{ids:bp:thm:length-main}, one has
\[
 g\geq m,\qquad T\geq g(m-1)+1,\qquad N\geq gm\geq m^2.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
An inserted word of length $m$ can occupy at most $g$ gaps, so an output of
degree $m$ forces $g\geq m$. Also $T\geq m$ because the nonempty target
language contains $T-m$ copies of $a$.

If $T<g(m-1)$, Lemma~\ref{ids:bp:lem:capped} supplies at least $g\geq m\geq3$
outputs of degree greater than~$1$, contrary to uniqueness. Thus
$T\geq g(m-1)$. Suppose equality holds. The vector
\[
 \mathbf v_*=(m-1,m-1,\ldots,m-1)
\]
has no eligible one-run deletion, so it must be the unique exceptional
vector. Consider its distinct neighbor
\[
 \mathbf c=(m,m-2,m-1,\ldots,m-1).
\]
It has the same total, hence is an output, and must have degree~$1$. Only
its first gap has length at least $m$. Its only possible one-run target is
therefore
\[
 \boldsymbol\beta=(0,m-2,m-1,\ldots,m-1),
\]
which must be in the target set. But $\boldsymbol\beta\leq\mathbf v_*$ and
\[
 \mathbf v_*-\boldsymbol\beta=(m-1,1,0,\ldots,0).
\]
By Lemma~\ref{ids:bp:lem:gap}, this target gives the exceptional output a
representation of degree~$2$, contradicting its degree $m\geq3$. Equality in
the total is impossible. Finally $N=T+g-1\geq gm\geq m^2$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{An explicit optimal family}
For $m\geq6$, take $q=1$ and $t_0=m$. The center is
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf v=(m^2-4m+3,\ 2m,\ \underbrace{1,\ldots,1}_{m-2}),
 \qquad T=m^2-m+1.
 \label{ids:bp:eq:optimal-center}
\end{equation}
The first coordinate is at least $2m$ because $m^2-6m+3\geq0$ for all
integers $m\geq6$. The special target gap vector is
$\boldsymbol\beta_* = \mathbf v-(1,\ldots,1)$. In ordinary word notation,
\begin{align}
 W_*&=a^{m^2-4m+3}b a^{2m}(ba)^{m-2},
                    \label{ids:bp:eq:optimal-word}\\
 V_*&=a^{m^2-4m+2}b a^{2m-1}b^{m-2}.
                    \label{ids:bp:eq:optimal-target}
\end{align}
Let $B$ contain $V_*$ and every target word whose gap vector has total
$(m-1)^2$ and is not coordinatewise below $\mathbf v$.
Then $W_*$ is the unique degree-$m$ output, all other words with $T$ copies
of $a$ and $m-1$ copies of $b$ have degree~$1$, and the common output length
is $m^2$.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{ids:bp:thm:length-main}]
The lower bound is Proposition~\ref{ids:bp:prop:lower}. Equations
\eqref{ids:bp:eq:optimal-center}--\eqref{ids:bp:eq:optimal-target} are the
$q=1$ instance of Theorem~\ref{ids:bp:thm:profile-main}, whose hypotheses were
proved in Lemma~\ref{ids:bp:lem:parameters}. Its length is
$T+m-1=m^2$, giving equality for every $m\geq6$.
\end{proof}

This theorem minimizes over \emph{all} finite target languages in the stated
full-coverage class, not only over targets arising from the separated-center
construction. It also permits arbitrary numbers of separator letters in the
lower bound. For $m\geq6$, any equality case necessarily has $g=m$ and
$T=m^2-m+1$, since both inequalities $N\geq gm\geq m^2$ must be equalities.
No classification of the target languages at equality is claimed.

\section{A fully explicit fourteen-symbol gap}
\label{ids:bp:sec:example}

The balanced $m=3$ instance is particularly easy to inspect. Set
\begin{equation}
 A=\{aaa\},\qquad
 B=\{a^ib a^jb a^k:i+j+k=9,\ \max(i,j,k)\geq5\}
        \cup\{a^3ba^3ba^3\}.
 \label{ids:bp:eq:worked}
\end{equation}
There are $\binom{11}{2}=55$ nonnegative triples of total~$9$. Exactly ten
are coordinatewise at most $(4,4,4)$, since subtracting them from $(4,4,4)$
gives the ten weak compositions of~$3$ into three parts. Removing those ten
and restoring $(3,3,3)$ gives $|B|=46$.

\begin{theorem}[The worked example]
\label{ids:bp:thm:example}
For \eqref{ids:bp:eq:worked}, with $W=a^4ba^4ba^4$,
\[
 \ins{1}{A}{B}=\ins{2}{A}{B}=\bpW{12}{3}\setminus\{W\},
 \qquad \ins{k}{A}{B}=\bpW{12}{3}\quad(k\geq3).
\]
There are $90$ degree-one outputs and one degree-three output. Every one of
the $46$ singleton source pairs has exactly three degree-one, six degree-two,
and one degree-three outputs.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For $W$, any valid target is coordinatewise at most $(4,4,4)$. Among the
accepted targets, only $(3,3,3)$ has that property. Its deficit is $(1,1,1)$,
which forces degree three.

For any other vector $\mathbf c$ of total $12$, the escape lemma applies to
$\mathbf v=(4,4,4)$ with $m=g=3$: the total exceeds $3(3-1)$, and each
$\rho_j$ is~$4>3$. It gives a coordinate with $c_j\geq3$ for which
$\mathbf c-3\mathbf e_j\not\leq(4,4,4)$. That target has a coordinate at
least five and belongs to $B$, giving degree one. Thus all
$\binom{14}{2}=91$ output words occur with the stated degrees.
The singleton counts are \eqref{ids:bp:eq:histogram} at $m=g=3$.
\end{proof}

inserted $a$'s from its second gap to obtain the target $(5,1,3)$. On the

\section{Identical real-rooted singleton histograms, arbitrary aggregate gaps}
\label{ids:bp:sec:roots}

There is a stronger algebraic contrast than the absence of a singleton gap.
Every target in the $g=m$ construction has the same polynomial
\begin{equation}
 H_m(z)=\sum_{r=1}^m\binom mr\binom{m-1}{r-1}z^r.
 \label{ids:bp:eq:H}
\end{equation}
We give a short self-contained proof that all its zeros are real. This is a
classical orthogonal-polynomial type argument; no priority is asserted for
the real-rootedness fact itself. Its role here is to delimit what can survive
minimization over source representations.

\subsection{A Rodrigues polynomial}
For $n\geq0$, define
\begin{equation}
 \Phi_n(x)=\frac{1}{n!x}\frac{d^n}{dx^n}
                  \bigl[x^{n+1}(1-x)^n\bigr].
 \label{ids:bp:eq:Rodrigues}
\end{equation}
The apparent singularity at zero is removable: every differentiated monomial
still contains a factor $x$. Expansion gives
\begin{equation}
 \Phi_n(x)=\sum_{j=0}^n(-1)^j\binom nj\binom{n+j+1}{n}x^j,
 \label{ids:bp:eq:J-expansion}
\end{equation}
so it has degree exactly $n$.

\begin{lemma}
\label{ids:bp:lem:orthogonal}
For $n\geq1$, $\Phi_n$ has $n$ distinct zeros, all in $(0,1)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For every polynomial $p$ of degree less than $n$, integration by parts $n$
times gives
\[
 \int_0^1 x\Phi_n(x)p(x)\,dx
 =\frac1{n!}\int_0^1
      \frac{d^n}{dx^n}[x^{n+1}(1-x)^n]p(x)\,dx=0.
\]
Every boundary term vanishes, because derivatives of order at most $n-1$
of $x^{n+1}(1-x)^n$ vanish at both endpoints. The remaining integral contains
$p^{(n)}=0$.

Suppose $\Phi_n$ had fewer than $n$ sign-changing zeros in $(0,1)$. Let $p$ be
the product of $x-r$ over those zeros, once each. Its degree is less than
$n$, while $\Phi_np$ has one constant sign on $(0,1)$ apart from its finitely
many zeros and is not identically zero. Multiplying by $x>0$ and integrating
cannot give zero, a contradiction. Thus there are at least $n$ sign-changing
zeros there. Degree $n$ forces exactly $n$, all simple.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Finite polynomial identity]
\label{ids:bp:lem:mobius}
For $n=m-1$,
\begin{equation}
 H_m(z)=z(1-z)^n \Phi_n\!\left(\frac{z}{z-1}\right).
 \label{ids:bp:eq:H-J}
\end{equation}
The right side is interpreted after polynomial cancellation at $z=1$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Using \eqref{ids:bp:eq:J-expansion}, the coefficient of $z^k$ in
$(1-z)^n\Phi_n(z/(z-1))$ is
\[
 \sum_{j=0}^k(-1)^{k-j}\binom nj\binom{n+j+1}{n}\binom{n-j}{k-j}.
\]
Since $\binom nj\binom{n-j}{k-j}=\binom nk\binom kj$, this becomes
\[
 \binom nk\sum_{j=0}^k(-1)^{k-j}\binom kj\binom{n+j+1}{n}
 =\binom nk\binom{n+1}{k+1}.
\]
For the last equality, apply the forward-difference identity
$\binom{y+1}{n}-\binom yn=\binom y{n-1}$ successively $k$ times, starting
at $y=n+1$. These are exactly the coefficients of $H_m(z)/z$ in
\eqref{ids:bp:eq:H}, proving the identity.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Real-rooted singleton histograms]
\label{ids:bp:thm:realroots}
For $m\geq2$, $H_m$ has a simple zero at $0$ and $m-1$ distinct negative
real zeros.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Each zero $x\in(0,1)$ of $\Phi_{m-1}$ gives the distinct negative zero
$z=x/(x-1)$ in \eqref{ids:bp:eq:H-J}. The additional zero at $0$ is simple
because $[z]H_m=m$. There are $m$ zeros in total, so these exhaust its
degree.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The aggregate can have almost no real zeros}
For an isolated degree $m$, \eqref{ids:bp:eq:aggregate-polynomial} reduces to
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{Deg}_{A,B}(z)=\mu z+z^m,\qquad \mu=\binom{N}{m-1}-1>0.
 \label{ids:bp:eq:two-term}
\end{equation}
Apart from zero, its roots solve $z^{m-1}=-\mu$. If $m$ is odd, all $m-1$
nonzero roots are nonreal. If $m$ is even, exactly one is negative real and
$m-2$ are nonreal. Thus the proportion of nonreal roots tends to one as
$m\to\infty$, even though \emph{each} singleton source-pair polynomial is
exactly the same real-rooted polynomial $H_m$.

Already $m=3$ gives the coefficient sequence $(\mu,0,1)$, which fails
log-concavity. Thus neither a general log-concavity nor a no-internal-zero
conclusion survives this minimization.
This is not a counterexample to a theorem about sums of real-rooted
polynomials: the language-level polynomial is not their sum. It counts each
output once at its cheapest degree, an operation that retains neither the
singleton coefficient sequence nor its real zeros.

\section{Algorithms, succinct descriptions, and certificates}
\label{ids:bp:sec:algorithms}

\subsection{Membership without enumerating the target language}
Although $|B|$ can be large, the vector description is short. A target word is
parsed into its gap vector $\boldsymbol\beta$, checked to have $m-1$
separators and total $T-m$, and then accepted exactly if
\[
 \boldsymbol\beta\in\{\boldsymbol\beta_i:0\leq i<q\}
 \quad\hbox{or}\quad
 \forall i\ \boldsymbol\beta\not\leq\mathbf v_i.
\]
A direct implementation uses $O(N+qm)$ elementary operations on the explicit
word and the stored vectors. This is a membership-algorithm bound, not a
claim that a minimal deterministic finite automaton has that size.

The safe-set test has an even simpler form for the translates
\eqref{ids:bp:eq:centers}. If some $j\geq3$ has $\beta_j>b_{m,j}$, the vector
is safe. Otherwise, domination by some center is equivalent to the existence
of an integer $i$ in the interval
\begin{equation}
 \max\left(0,\left\lceil\frac{\beta_1-b_{m,1}}\Lambda\right\rceil\right)
 \ \leq\ i\ \leq\
 \min\left(q-1,q-1-\left\lceil\frac{\beta_2-b_{m,2}}\Lambda\right\rceil\right).
 \label{ids:bp:eq:interval-test}
\end{equation}
The interval is empty precisely when the vector is safe. This follows by
solving the first two coordinate inequalities for $i$; the remaining
coordinate inequalities have already been checked. For $m=2$ there are no
remaining coordinates. Ceiling division must retain its mathematical meaning
for negative numerators.

All target languages are finite and hence regular: a trie of their words is
one explicit finite automaton. The concise membership test should not be
confused with a minimal-state construction, and no unproved polynomial DFA
state bound is used.

\subsection{Computing a minimum degree}
For an arbitrary finite gap-vector target set,
\eqref{ids:bp:eq:language-gap} is an exact algorithm. An alternative
enumerates weak compositions $\boldsymbol\delta$ of $m$ and tests whether
$\boldsymbol\delta\leq\mathbf c$ and
$\mathbf c-\boldsymbol\delta\in \bpE$. Ordering the compositions by support
size permits stopping at the first accepted one. For $g=m$, at most
$\binom{2m-1}{m-1}$ candidates are tested.

For the constructed languages there is a faster certified route. Compare the
output vector with the $q$ centers. Equality with a center gives its declared
degree, with the uniqueness proof certifying minimality. For any other output,
try the at most $m$ vectors $\mathbf c-m\mathbf e_j$ with $c_j\geq m$.
The escape argument guarantees that one is safe. Direct comparisons give an
$O(qm^2)$ arithmetic-operation bound, in addition to reading the word. The
successful target and chosen gap are an explicit degree-one certificate.

\subsection{A literal-word dynamic program}\label{ids:bp:sec:literal-dp}
For independent verification, it is useful to avoid the gap lemma entirely.
Fix literal words $u,v,w$ with $|w|=|u|+|v|$. A state $(i,j,\ell)$ records
how many letters of $u$ and $v$ have been consumed and whether the preceding
output letter was assigned to $u$ ($\ell=1$) or not ($\ell=0$). Initialize
$(0,0,0)$ at cost zero. At output position $i+j$, a transition taking the
next matching letter of $u$ adds $1-\ell$ to the cost and sets $\ell=1$;
a transition taking the next matching letter of $v$ adds zero and sets
$\ell=0$. Keep the smallest cost at every state.

Induction on the consumed output prefix shows that the state cost is exactly
the smallest number of inserted-source runs among its compatible partial
masks. Hence the minimum terminal cost is the desired degree for nonempty
$u$. There are $O((|u|+1)(|v|+1))$ states and constant work per outgoing
transition. This program can be compared directly with enumeration of the
$\binom{|u|+|v|}{|u|}$ position masks, providing a check by a different
algorithm. [Merge note: this is the dynamic program of
Section~\ref{sec:algorithms}, with $\ell=1$ for Part~I's state $s=A$ and
$\ell=0$ for $s=B$; Part~I proves its exactness in the unnumbered proposition
there.]

\subsection{Specific failure modes excluded by the proofs}
The proof does not rely on a uniquely chosen position mask; only the target
word for an exceptional output is unique. It does not transfer an interval
property through a union of targets; it calculates the resulting minimum.

Part~II.

\section{Further research questions and topics}
\label{ids:bp:sec:questions}

The following program separates the cited both-singleton question from
new questions suggested by the present construction. Except where a source
is explicitly named, these are proposed research problems rather than claims
about an exhaustive literature consensus. Together with the questions of
Part~I's Section~10 that Part~II leaves open, they are the report's current
open list.

\begin{question}[The unrestricted both-singleton conjecture]
\label{ids:bp:q:singleton}
Does $\spec{\{u\}}{\{v\}}$ always form an initial interval for arbitrary
words $u,v$? This is Hughes's Conjecture~2 \cite[Section~11]{Hughes2026}.
The present construction leaves it untouched: it keeps $u=a^m$ fixed but
uses multiple targets. A useful next case is a two-run inserted word such as
$a^pb^q$, where the single-skeleton gap model no longer applies unchanged.
\end{question}

\begin{question}[How small can the target language be?]
\label{ids:bp:q:target}
For a prescribed spectrum, or prescribed higher-degree multiplicities, what
is the minimum $|B|$ when $A$ is a singleton? The inserted-language size here
is optimal, but optimality of the target counts in
\eqref{ids:bp:eq:input-count} is not established. In particular, how many
targets are needed to erase all intermediate degrees below a surviving degree
$m$? A lower bound must account for overlaps between the sets of outputs a
single target makes cheaper.
\end{question}

\begin{question}[Small isolated full-coverage witnesses]
\label{ids:bp:q:small}
Determine the exact minimum length in Theorem~\ref{ids:bp:thm:length-main} for
$m=3,4,5$. The proved lower bounds are $9,16,25$, while this construction
gives upper bounds $14,21,27$. Varying the number of gaps, rather than only
the center within $g=m$ gaps, may matter. No optimality of the small examples
is asserted here.
\end{question}

\begin{question}[Classification at the sharp bound]
\label{ids:bp:q:classification}
For $m\geq6$, classify the pairs attaining $N=m^2$. The lower-bound proof
forces $g=m$ and $T=m^2-m+1$, but does not force the center shape
\eqref{ids:bp:eq:optimal-center}. Which vectors permit one isolated degree-$m$
output? Which target words are mandatory in every realizing language?
\end{question}

\begin{question}[Optimal packing for many exceptions]
\label{ids:bp:q:packing}
For $q>1$, can the added length $(2m+1)(q-1)$ in
\eqref{ids:bp:eq:large-m-length} be reduced by placing centers in more than
two varying coordinates? The relevant objects are fixed-sum integer vectors
whose radius-$m$ obstruction neighborhoods do not overlap. Separation greater
than $2m$ is sufficient; determining when overlapping neighborhoods can be
handled without introducing unintended degrees is a sharper problem.
\end{question}

\begin{question}[Removing full coverage]
\label{ids:bp:q:coverage}
Without requiring $A\sh B$ to fill an entire fixed-letter-count class, how
short can a binary witness to spectrum $\{1,m\}$ be with $A=\{a^m\}$?
The general lower bound is $2m-1$, while the present family uses $m^2$ for
$m\geq6$. The quadratic lower bound proved here does not answer this
question. A construction of length $O(m)$ would require avoiding the many
short-gap words, rather than making them one-piece outputs.
\end{question}

\begin{question}[Descriptional complexity]
\label{ids:bp:q:automata}
What are optimal deterministic or nondeterministic automaton sizes for these
languages and their bounded-insertion stages? The arithmetic membership test
is compact, but this alone is not a state bound. One can ask for a family
with arbitrary prescribed plateaus and a target automaton whose state count
is polynomial in the profile parameters.
\end{question}

\begin{question}[Structural hypotheses restoring regular degree shapes]
\label{ids:bp:q:structure}
Which overlap restrictions on target shuffle sets force an interval spectrum,
log-concavity, or real-rooted degree polynomials? Pairwise source
separability eliminates the minimization conflict, but is much stronger than
necessary. Bounds on the number of target representations per output, or
laminarity of their low-cost regions, may offer intermediate conditions.
\end{question}

\begin{question}[Infinite spectra over a fixed alphabet]
\label{ids:bp:q:infinite}
Classify the spectra of infinite binary language pairs. A fixed inserted
word $a^m$ bounds all degrees by $m$, so an unbounded spectrum requires
unbounded inserted length. Combining finite constructions across lengths is
not automatic: cross-shuffles can introduce new degrees. A successful
construction needs a mechanism controlling those interactions without a
growing alphabet.
\end{question}

\begin{question}[Formal verification and weighted extensions]
\label{ids:bp:q:formal}
A Lean development could start with finite vectors, the support formula,
Lemmas~\ref{ids:bp:lem:near} and~\ref{ids:bp:lem:escape}, and the
full-coverage lower bound. These are finite combinatorial statements and
require no real analysis. A separate extension could assign different costs
to insertion runs in different gaps, or track several inserted colors. Which
finite sets of costs or vectors of costs admit analogous profile
realizations?
\end{question}

\section{Conclusion of Part~II}
\label{ids:bp:sec:conclusion}
The fixed-binary classification is as unrestricted as the earlier
variable-alphabet classification: finiteness and the unavoidable degree~$1$
are the only conditions. Even one unary inserted word suffices. Its individual
source pairs have identical, gap-free, real-rooted degree histograms, but
minimization over targets can erase any chosen intermediate degrees and can
prescribe every higher-degree multiplicity.

The gap-vector representation makes the mechanism explicit. Coordinatewise
exclusion protects a designated expensive output; distance separation prevents
other designated targets from reducing its cost; and a one-piece escape
lemma makes all remaining outputs cheap. The same representation exposes a
sharp extremal threshold: an isolated degree $m$ in a full binary
fixed-letter-count class needs $m^2$ symbols, and that threshold is attained
for every $m\geq6$. These are the proposed additions to the repository's
insertion-degree program.

\section{A reproducible construction blueprint}
\label{ids:bp:sec:blueprint}
Given $m\geq2$ and a list $t_0,\ldots,t_{q-1}\in\{2,\ldots,m\}$,
compute $\Lambda$, $\mathbf b_m$, $\mathbf v_i$, $T$, and
$\boldsymbol\beta_i$ from Section~\ref{ids:bp:sec:construction}. The
following pseudocode is a direct implementation of the target definition.
\begin{lstlisting}
def target_member(beta):
    if len(beta) != m or min(beta) < 0:
        return False
    if sum(beta) != T - m:
        return False
    if beta in special_targets:
        return True
    for v in centers:
        if all(beta[j] <= v[j] for j in range(m)):
            return False
    return True
\end{lstlisting}
Enumerating weak compositions of $T-m$ and applying this predicate lists all
of $B$. Enumeration is not required to use the mathematical definition or
the membership algorithm. To obtain the actual binary word from a vector,
join the strings $a^{\beta_j}$ with a single $b$ between consecutive gaps.
Zero gaps must be retained, not deleted from the vector.

\section{The binary finite certificate}\label{app:binary}

For each source pair in~\eqref{eq:binary}, the table records the number of \emph{distinct} output words whose singleton minimum has the indicated value. It is obtained by enumerating all thirty-five masks, not by counting masks as distinct outputs. Every row has nonzero counts at every degree from $1$ through its maximum.

Across these rows, the union of the singleton degree-three words contains twenty-three words. For each, the next table provides a competing source pair and a mask of cost at most two. Reading positions marked $A$ or $B$ reconstructs the displayed sources, so each row is directly checkable.

These tables certify the missing degree as follows. A language-level degree-three output would have a singleton representation of minimum degree exactly three for some pair.
