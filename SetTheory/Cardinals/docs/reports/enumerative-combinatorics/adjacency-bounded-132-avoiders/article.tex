\begin{abstract}
Let $a_n^{(m)}$ count permutations $\pi\in S_n$ avoiding the pattern $132$ and
satisfying $|\pi_{i+1}-\pi_i|\le m$. Nadler conjectured that their exponential
growth constants increase strictly from $1$ to $4$. Mayama and Akita established
a finite-state enumeration, the existence of the growth constants,
non-strict monotonicity, and convergence to $4$. We prove the missing strict
inequality for every adjacency bound. The argument embeds each of the two
irreducible cyclic state components at bound $m$ into a proper principal
submatrix of its counterpart at bound $m+1$ by increasing both finite endpoint
thresholds by one. Perron--Frobenius comparison then gives strict growth in
\emph{each} component, independently of which component dominates.

A separate counting argument gives
\[
 4-\alpha_m=\frac{2\log m+4\log\log m+O(1)}{m}.
\]
It compares the full class with a scalar Catalan majorant and an injectively
encoded family of variable-length skew blocks. A truncated-series root lemma
also bounds the possible constant term. Complete proofs, exact generating
functions for $m\le4$, integer sequences, and independently checked rational
Perron certificates accompany the report.

\emph{Addition of 28 September 2026 (batch 38).} Part~II, from a later
manuscript, proves $\alpha_m<\beta_m$ for the root $\beta_m^{-1}$ of
$\sum_{k=1}^mC_{k-1}x^k=1$ and $\beta_m-\alpha_m=O((\log m)^2/m^2)$; hence the
constant term converges, $m(4-\alpha_m)-2\log m-4\log\log m\to2\log\pi$, and
the growth deficit has an expansion to every inverse-logarithmic order.

\emph{Addition of 29 September 2026 (batch 43).} Part~III, from a further
manuscript, takes the bound near its trivial value, $m=n-d$. The deficit
$n-M_n$ of the largest adjacent jump of a uniform $132$-avoider converges in
total variation, at the sharp rate $n^{-1/2}$, to an explicit algebraic law
with $\mathbb P(D>k)\sim3/\sqrt{\pi k}$; uniformly,
$a_n^{(n-d)}/C_n=3/\sqrt{\pi d}+O(d^{-3/2}+n^{-1/2})$. This answers the joint
limit in $n$ and $m$ near the boundary only; the bulk regime stays open.
\end{abstract}

\section{The problem and the results}
\label{sec:results}
A permutation $\pi\in S_n$ avoids $132$ when there are no indices $i<j<k$ for
which $\pi_i<\pi_k<\pi_j$. Fix an integer $m\ge1$ and put
\[
\Aclass_n^{(m)}=\{\pi\in\Av_n(132):|\pi_{i+1}-\pi_i|\le m
                  \text{ for }1\le i<n\},\qquad
 a_n^{(m)}=|\Aclass_n^{(m)}|.
\]
We set $a_0^{(m)}=1$ and use the ordinary generating function
\[
 A_m(x)=\sum_{n\ge0}a_n^{(m)}x^n.
\]
The growth constant is
\begin{equation}
 \alpha_m=\lim_{n\to\infty}(a_n^{(m)})^{1/n}.
 \label{eq:alpha}
\end{equation}
The order of limits matters: $n\to\infty$ is taken for each fixed $m$, and
only afterwards do we let $m\to\infty$. The trivial identity
$a_n^{(m)}=C_n$ for $m\ge n-1$ does not by itself settle this iterated limit.
Here $C_n=\binom{2n}{n}/(n+1)$ is the $n$th Catalan number.
[Added 29 September 2026, batch 43: Part~III studies the joint regime next
to that identity, $m=n-d$ with $d$ fixed or $d=o(n)$
(\cref{lj:thm:main,lj:thm:tail-main}); it does not bear on the iterated
limit taken here.]

\subsection{Which part was still open?}
Nadler's Conjecture~2 in Section~5.2 of \cite{Nadler} asks for the existence of
\eqref{eq:alpha}, strict increase $1=\alpha_1<\alpha_2<\cdots<4$, and limit $4$.
Mayama and Akita subsequently proved rationality for all $m$, existence of the
limit, non-strict increase, and $4-\alpha_m=O(\log m/m)$
\cite[Theorems~2.4, 4.12, and 4.14]{MayamaAkita}. Their monotonicity theorem
states $\alpha_m\le\alpha_{m+1}$, not strict inequality.

Merely finding additional permutations at bound $m+1$ is insufficient:
sequences $2^n$ and $2^n+1$ differ strictly at every index but have identical
exponential growth. The needed argument must show that the extra freedom
changes the exponential rate.

\begin{theorem}[Strict increase]\label{thm:strict-main}
For every integer $m\ge1$,
\[
 \alpha_{m+1}>\alpha_m.
\]
In the two-component spectral representation described below, both component
rates increase strictly: for every $m\ge2$,
\[
 \lambda_U(m+1)>\lambda_U(m),\qquad
 \lambda_V(m+1)>\lambda_V(m).
\]
Consequently Nadler's growth-constant conjecture holds in full.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Two-term approach to the Catalan limit]\label{thm:asympt-main}
As $m\to\infty$ through integers,
\begin{equation}
 4-\alpha_m=\frac{2\log m+4\log\log m+O(1)}{m}.
 \label{eq:main-asympt}
\end{equation}
In particular, $4-\alpha_m\sim2\log m/m$. All logarithms in this report are
natural logarithms.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[A window for the next constant]\label{thm:window-main}
Define $E_m=m(4-\alpha_m)-2\log m-4\log\log m$ for $m\ge2$. Then
\begin{equation}
 2\log\pi-4\log2
 \ \le\ \liminf_{m\to\infty}E_m
 \ \le\ \limsup_{m\to\infty}E_m
 \ \le\ 2\log\pi.
 \label{eq:constant-window}
\end{equation}
No convergence of $E_m$ is asserted.
\end{theorem}
\noindent[Added 28 September 2026, batch 38: the last sentence describes the
report at its pinned state \texttt{6c9175a54}. Part~II proves that $E_m$
converges to the upper endpoint, $E_m\to2\log\pi$
(\cref{crem:thm:main-quantitative,crem:cor:constant}).]

The strict comparison uses a finite-state embedding. The asymptotic results
use scalar comparison series and do not require deciding which finite-state
component has the larger rate. The distinction is important: the all-$m$
comparison of $\lambda_U(m)$ with $\lambda_V(m)$ is a separate question.

\section{The exact endpoint recursion}
\label{sec:endpoints}
This section develops the endpoint representation of Mayama and Akita
\cite[Section~2]{MayamaAkita}, including the counting details needed later.
It is included to make the subsequent proofs independently readable.

\subsection{Decomposition at the maximum}
If $\pi=L\,n\,R$ avoids $132$, every entry of $L$ exceeds every entry of $R$:
otherwise an entry of $L$, then $n$, then an entry of $R$ forms $132$.
Conversely, if this separation holds and both standardized sides avoid
$132$, their concatenation with $n$ avoids $132$.

Suppose $R$ is nonempty and the maximum is at position $k$. Then $L$ has
length $k-1$, its values are $n-k+1,\ldots,n-1$, and $R$ has values
$1,\ldots,n-k$. The jump from $n$ to the first entry of $R$ is at least $k$.
Therefore
\begin{equation}
 R\ne\varnothing\quad\Longrightarrow\quad 1\le k\le m.
 \label{eq:max-position}
\end{equation}
The alternative $R=\varnothing$ means appending the maximum to an avoider
of length $n-1$.

\subsection{Cumulative endpoint thresholds}
Let
\[
 B_m=\{0,1,\ldots,m-1,\infty\}.
\]
For $p,q\in B_m$, let $T_{p,q}^{(m)}(n)$ count the permutations in
$\Aclass_n^{(m)}$ satisfying
\[
 n-\pi_1\le p,\qquad n-\pi_n\le q.
\]
A threshold $\infty$ imposes no restriction. Define
\[
 F_{p,q}^{(m)}(x)=\sum_{n\ge1}T_{p,q}^{(m)}(n)x^n.
\]
A negative threshold gives the zero series, and $\infty-k=\infty$.
We suppress the superscript $(m)$ where it is fixed.

For $k\ge2$ define the nonnegative integer
\begin{equation}
 c_{k,p}=\bigl|\{\sigma\in\Av_{k-1}(132):k-\sigma_1\le p\}\bigr|,
 \qquad c_{1,p}=1
 \label{eq:c-def}
\end{equation}
for nonnegative or infinite $p$; for negative $p$ set $c_{k,p}=0$.
These coefficients do not otherwise depend on $m$. Two properties will be
crucial:
\begin{equation}
 c_{k,p+1}\ge c_{k,p},\qquad c_{k,\infty}=C_{k-1}.
 \label{eq:c-monotone}
\end{equation}
For finite $p\ge0$ and $k\ge2$, an explicit formula is
\begin{equation}
 c_{k,p}=\sum_{j=1}^{\min(p,k-1)}
 \frac{j}{k-1}\binom{2k-j-3}{k-2}.
 \label{eq:c-formula}
\end{equation}
A generating-function proof of this formula appears in
\cref{sec:block-weights}; no numerical fitting is involved.

\begin{proposition}[Endpoint recurrence]\label{prop:endpoint}
For all $p,q\in B_m$,
\begin{equation}
 F_{p,q}=x+\sum_{k=1}^m c_{k,p}x^kF_{m-k,q-k}
                   +xF_{p-1,m-1}.
 \label{eq:endpoint}
\end{equation}
Moreover $A_m(x)=1+F_{\infty,\infty}(x)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The singleton permutation contributes $x$ to every nonnegative threshold
state. For length at least two, first suppose $R\ne\varnothing$ in the
maximum decomposition. By \eqref{eq:max-position}, $k\le m$.
The left block has $k-1$ values in an interval of that length, so its internal
jumps, and its jump to $n$, are automatically at most $m$. Its first-entry
restriction gives $c_{k,p}$ choices; for $k=1$ the first entry is $n$ and the
left block is empty.

The right block has length $n-k$. Writing its first and last entries as
$R_1$ and $R_{n-k}$, the two remaining restrictions are exactly
\[
 (n-k)-R_1\le m-k,\qquad
 (n-k)-R_{n-k}\le q-k.
\]
The first inequality is the jump bound from $n$ to $R_1$; the second is the
last-entry threshold for the whole permutation. This gives
$c_{k,p}x^kF_{m-k,q-k}$.

If $R=\varnothing$, remove the final maximum. The first deficiency increases
by one when the maximum is appended, giving threshold $p-1$ for the smaller
permutation. Its last deficiency must be at most $m-1$ to permit the last
jump. The final entry of the whole permutation is $n$, so any nonnegative
$q$ is satisfied. This is $xF_{p-1,m-1}$. The cases are disjoint and reversible.
\end{proof}

Let $W_m(x)$ have rows and columns indexed by $B_m^2$, with
\begin{align}
 (W_m)_{(p,q),(r,s)}={}&
  \sum_{k=1}^m c_{k,p}x^k
       \mathbf1_{(r,s)=(m-k,q-k)}\notag\\
 &+x\,\mathbf1_{(r,s)=(p-1,m-1)}.
 \label{eq:matrix}
\end{align}
Invalid negative thresholds are omitted. Rows are the left-hand states of
\eqref{eq:endpoint}; columns are the right-hand states. Thus
\begin{equation}
 F=x\one+W_m(x)F,
 \qquad F=x(I-W_m(x))^{-1}\one.
 \label{eq:matrix-system}
\end{equation}
Every entry of $W_m$ has zero constant term, so the inverse is well-defined
as a formal power series. This also proves rationality and gives an exact
algorithm for computing $A_m$.

\section{Cyclic components and growth constants}
\label{sec:components}
The following fixed-$m$ structure is established in
\cite[Proposition~2.7 and Theorem~4.12]{MayamaAkita}. We supply elementary
component and transfer-graph arguments to make the spectral input explicit.

\subsection{The two relevant components}
For $m\ge2$ put
\begin{align}
 U_m&=\{(p,\infty):0\le p\le m-1\},\label{eq:U}\\
 V_m&=\{(p,q):0\le q<p\le m-1\}
       \ \cup\ \{(p,m-1):0\le p\le m-2\}.
       \label{eq:V}
\end{align}
Their sizes are $m$ and $(m-1)(m+2)/2$. Write $W_{U_m}$ and $W_{V_m}$ for the
corresponding principal matrices. A directed edge means a nonzero term in a
row of \eqref{eq:matrix}, directed from its row state to its column state.

\begin{lemma}[Component structure]\label{lem:components}
At every real $x>0$, both $W_{U_m}(x)$ and $W_{V_m}(x)$ are irreducible.
The only other cyclic component of the full state graph is the singleton
$I_m=(\infty,m-1)$, whose internal matrix is $(x)$. Each of these three
components is reachable from $(\infty,\infty)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Within $U_m$, every state has a $k=1$ edge to $(m-1,\infty)$. From that state,
the $k=1,\ldots,m$ edges reach every state of $U_m$, since
$c_{k,m-1}>0$. Hence $U_m$ is strongly connected.

For $V_m$, call the states with $q<p$ lower states and the states
$(p,m-1)$ top states. Every lower state has an append edge to a top state.
Every top state has a $k=1$ edge to
\[
 e=(m-1,m-2).
\]
It remains to show that $e$ reaches every state. For $m=2$ this is the
immediate two-state cycle. Suppose $m\ge3$. Appending at $e$ reaches
$(m-2,m-1)$; its split edges reach all lower states $(p,p-1)$, $1\le p\le m-1$.
Their append edges reach every top state. Inductively, suppose all lower
states of gap at most $d-1$ are reachable, where $2\le d\le m-1$.
The state $(m-1,m-d)$ is then reachable. Its split edge with $k=m-p$ reaches
$(p,p-d)$ for every $d\le p\le m-1$. Thus every lower gap is reached.

For finite $q$, every valid split edge ends at a lower state, because
$(m-k)-(q-k)=m-q>0$. Every valid append edge with finite $p$ ends at a top
state. Therefore a finite--finite state outside $V_m$ cannot be on a cycle.
A state $(\infty,q)$ with finite $q$ has append target $I_m$ and split
targets in $V_m$; among these states only $I_m$ has a self-loop. No edge
returns to $(\infty,\infty)$. Edges with second coordinate $\infty$ stay
in $U_m$ or leave it by an append edge, and cannot return from a finite
second coordinate. This exhausts all possibilities.

Finally, the $k=1$ split from $(\infty,\infty)$ reaches $U_m$, its append
edge reaches $I_m$, and a $k=1$ split from $I_m$ reaches $e\in V_m$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The strict matrix comparison used in the proof}
We use the Perron--Frobenius theorem for finite nonnegative matrices: an
irreducible matrix has positive left and right eigenvectors at its spectral
radius; a primitive matrix has powers asymptotic to a positive rank-one
matrix times the corresponding power of that radius.

\begin{lemma}[Proper principal comparison]\label{lem:PF}
Let $M$ be an irreducible nonnegative matrix and let $S$ be a nonempty proper
subset of its indices. If $B=M[S,S]$, then $\spr(B)<\spr(M)$.
If $0\le N\le B$ entrywise, then $\spr(N)<\spr(M)$ as well.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Take a nonnegative nonzero Perron vector $v$ of $B$ and extend it by zeros to
a vector $w$ on all indices of $M$. Then $Mw\ge\spr(B)w$. There is strict
inequality in at least one coordinate: the support of $w$ is nonempty and
proper, and irreducibility supplies an edge from its complement to that
support. If $u$ is a positive left Perron vector of $M$, multiplication by
$u^{\mathsf T}$ gives
\[
 \spr(M)u^{\mathsf T}w>\spr(B)u^{\mathsf T}w.
\]
Since $u^{\mathsf T}w>0$, the first assertion follows. Entrywise monotonicity
of the spectral radius gives $\spr(N)\le\spr(B)$; for example, this
monotonicity follows by comparing powers and then taking matrix-norm roots.
\end{proof}

For $C=U,V$, there is a unique $r_C(m)>0$ satisfying
\begin{equation}
 \spr(W_{C_m}(r_C(m)))=1,
 \qquad \lambda_C(m)=r_C(m)^{-1}.
 \label{eq:component-root}
\end{equation}
Indeed the spectral radius is continuous, tends to zero as $x\downarrow0$,
and tends to infinity as $x\to\infty$ because the component has a positive
cycle. If $y>x>0$, every monomial has degree at least one, so
$W_{C_m}(y)\ge(y/x)W_{C_m}(x)$. Its positive spectral radius is therefore
strictly increasing.

\begin{proposition}[Fixed-bound growth]\label{prop:growth}
For $m\ge2$ the limit \eqref{eq:alpha} exists and
\begin{equation}
 \alpha_m=\max\{\lambda_U(m),\lambda_V(m)\}.
 \label{eq:alpha-max}
\end{equation}
Also $\lambda_U(m)>1$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The state $(m-1,\infty)$ has an internal self-loop of weight $x$.
At $x=1$ its singleton principal matrix is $(1)$, and it is a proper
principal matrix of $W_{U_m}(1)$. By \cref{lem:PF}, the latter has spectral
radius greater than one. Thus $r_U(m)<1$ and $\lambda_U(m)>1$.

To justify the actual limit rather than only a limsup, replace each edge
of weight $c x^k$ by a directed path of $k$ unit-length edges with total
multiplicity $c$. Use fresh intermediate vertices for every edge. Let $H$
be the resulting finite nonnegative adjacency matrix, and let $b$ be one
on original endpoint states and zero on intermediate states. With $o$ the
state $(\infty,\infty)$, \eqref{eq:matrix-system} gives
\[
 a_n^{(m)}=e_o^{\mathsf T}H^{n-1}b\qquad(n\ge1).
\]
For an expanded irreducible component with Perron eigenvalue $\lambda$,
eliminating the intermediate coordinates of a positive eigenvector gives
$z=W_{C_m}(1/\lambda)z$. Its eigenvalue is therefore $\lambda_C(m)$.
The expanded singleton component has eigenvalue one. The component
classification and $\lambda_U(m)>1$ show that $\spr(H)$ is the right-hand
side of \eqref{eq:alpha-max}.

The expanded $U_m$ component is primitive, since it is strongly connected
and has a length-one loop. For $m\ge3$, the expanded $V_m$ component has
closed walks of lengths two and five. The first is
\[
 e\ \xrightarrow{1}\ (m-2,m-1)\ \xrightarrow{1}\ e.
\]
For the second, split with $k=2$ on the middle state and then append and
split with $k=1$:
\[
 e\xrightarrow{1}(m-2,m-1)
 \xrightarrow{2}(m-2,m-3)
 \xrightarrow{1}(m-3,m-1)
 \xrightarrow{1}e.
\]
The labels are edge lengths. All coefficients are positive, including
when $m=3$. Their greatest common divisor is one, so this component is
primitive. When $m=2$, $V_m$ is a two-cycle with rate one and is not dominant.

A dominant primitive component is reachable from $o$, and $b$ is positive
on its original states. Fixing an entrance path and then applying the
primitive Perron theorem supplies $a_n^{(m)}\ge c\spr(H)^n$ for all
sufficiently large $n$, with $c>0$. A finite-matrix power bound gives
$a_n^{(m)}\le C n^D\spr(H)^n$ for some fixed $C,D$ (or, equivalently, an
upper bound by $(\spr(H)+\eps)^n$ for each $\eps>0$).
Taking $n$th roots proves \eqref{eq:alpha-max} and the existence of the limit.
\end{proof}

\section{A shift of endpoint thresholds proves strict increase}
\label{sec:strict}
The key comparison preserves the split length $k$, not merely the
underlying unweighted graph. Define
\[
 \phi(p)=\begin{cases}p+1,&p<\infty,\\ \infty,&p=\infty,\end{cases}
 \qquad \shift(p,q)=(\phi(p),\phi(q)).
\]
The next lemma is the essential new observation.

\begin{lemma}[Coefficientwise shift embedding]\label{lem:shift}
For every $m\ge2$ and $C=U,V$, the map $\shift$ embeds $C_m$ into a proper
subset of $C_{m+1}$. Under this identification,
\begin{equation}
 W_{C_m}(x)\preceqc
 W_{C_{m+1}}(x)[\shift(C_m),\shift(C_m)]
 \label{eq:embedding}
\end{equation}
coefficientwise in every matrix entry.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
A state $(p,\infty)$ of $U_m$ maps to $(p+1,\infty)$ of $U_{m+1}$; the
state $(0,\infty)$ of the latter is missing. A lower state $(p,q)$ of $V_m$
maps to a lower state $(p+1,q+1)$ of $V_{m+1}$. A top state $(p,m-1)$ maps
to $(p+1,m)$, a top state at the new bound. The new top state $(0,m)$ is
missing, so that inclusion is also proper.

Consider a split term at row $(p,q)$ of the old matrix, with column
$(m-k,q-k)$ and coefficient $c_{k,p}x^k$. At the new row $\shift(p,q)$,
the split with the same $k$ has column
\[
 (m+1-k,\phi(q)-k)=\shift(m-k,q-k),
\]
whenever the old term is valid. Its coefficient is
$c_{k,\phi(p)}x^k\ge c_{k,p}x^k$. For $p=\infty$ the coefficients are equal.
For a valid append term, the old column $(p-1,m-1)$ maps to
\[
 (\phi(p)-1,m),
\]
which is exactly the new append column, and the weight remains $x$.
Each term is preserved with at least its old multiplicity. Adding terms
with the same row and column proves the coefficientwise assertion.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{thm:strict-main}]
Fix $m\ge2$, $C\in\{U,V\}$, and a positive real $x$. By
\cref{lem:shift,lem:components,lem:PF},
\begin{align*}
 \spr(W_{C_m}(x))
 &\le \spr\bigl(W_{C_{m+1}}(x)[\shift(C_m),\shift(C_m)]\bigr)\\
 &<\spr(W_{C_{m+1}}(x)).
\end{align*}
At $x=r_C(m)$ the first quantity is one. Strict increase of the new spectral
radius as a function of $x$ gives $r_C(m+1)<r_C(m)$, hence
$\lambda_C(m+1)>\lambda_C(m)$.

Choose a component $C$ realizing the maximum at bound $m$. Then
\[
 \alpha_{m+1}\ge\lambda_C(m+1)>\lambda_C(m)=\alpha_m.
\]
This works even if the dominant component changes between the two bounds.

For $m=1$, any adjacent difference is $\pm1$, and reversing direction would
repeat a value. The only permutations of length $n\ge2$ are the increasing
and decreasing ones, both avoiding $132$. Thus $\alpha_1=1$.
At $m=2$, in the order $(0,\infty),(1,\infty)$,
\[
 W_{U_2}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}0&x\\x^2&x\end{pmatrix},\qquad
 \det(I-W_{U_2}(x))=1-x-x^3.
\]
Its positive root is below one, so $\alpha_2\ge\lambda_U(2)>1$. This covers
the remaining step.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Strictness comes from a \emph{proper principal extension of an irreducible
component}. It does not require finding a strictly larger entry inside the
old image. This is useful for the $U$ component, where many coefficients
are preserved exactly. Nor is a numerical lower bound on the gap between
consecutive rates needed.
\end{remark}

\section{Scalar upper bounds and injective skew-block lower bounds}
\label{sec:scalar}
We next obtain two scalar comparison series whose root asymptotics have
the same first two terms. Write $P\preceqc Q$ for coefficientwise inequality
of formal power series with nonnegative coefficients.

\subsection{A Catalan majorant}
Define
\begin{equation}
 R_m(x)=x+\sum_{k=1}^m C_{k-1}x^k.
 \label{eq:R}
\end{equation}
The separate initial $x$ accounts for appending a maximum, whereas the
$k=1$ term in the sum accounts for a maximum in the first position with a
nonempty right block. Thus the coefficient of $x$ in $R_m$ is two.

\begin{proposition}[Scalar upper bound]\label{prop:upper}
Let $q_m>0$ be the unique positive root of $R_m(q_m)=1$. Then
\begin{equation}
 A_m(x)\preceqc 1+\frac{x}{1-R_m(x)},\qquad
 \alpha_m\le q_m^{-1}<4.
 \label{eq:upper}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The maximum decomposition, with all endpoint restrictions discarded, gives
for $n\ge2$
\[
 a_n^{(m)}\le a_{n-1}^{(m)}+
 \sum_{k=1}^{\min(m,n-1)}C_{k-1}a_{n-k}^{(m)}.
\]
The upper endpoint $n-1$ is essential: the right block in a split must be
nonempty. Starting with $a_1^{(m)}=1$, induction yields the generating-function
majorant. Its radius is $q_m$: the denominator is nonzero for $|x|<q_m$
by positivity, and it vanishes at $q_m$ without numerator cancellation.
Cauchy--Hadamard therefore gives $\alpha_m\le q_m^{-1}$.

Finally,
\[
 R_m(1/4)<\frac14+\sum_{k\ge1}C_{k-1}4^{-k}
          =\frac14+\frac12=\frac34<1.
\]
Hence $q_m>1/4$ and the strict upper bound by four follows.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Variable-length blocks with recoverable boundaries}
Fix $d\ge1$ and $m>d$. An admissible block of length $k$, where
$1\le k\le m-d$, is a permutation $\beta\in\Av_k(132)$ such that
\[
 \beta_k=k,\qquad k-\beta_1\le d.
\]
There are $c_{k,d}$ such blocks: for $k\ge2$, deleting the last maximum
leaves the permutations counted in \eqref{eq:c-def}; the singleton contributes
$c_{1,d}=1$.

For a sequence of blocks, assign consecutive value intervals in decreasing
order. That is, every entry of an earlier block exceeds every entry of a
later block. This operation is their \emph{skew sum}. Let
\begin{equation}
 K_{m,d}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{m-d}c_{k,d}x^k.
 \label{eq:K}
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Injective block lower bound]\label{prop:lower}
Let $s_{m,d}>0$ be the unique positive root of $K_{m,d}(s_{m,d})=1$. Then
\begin{equation}
 \frac{1}{1-K_{m,d}(x)}\preceqc A_m(x),\qquad
 s_{m,d}^{-1}\le\alpha_m\le q_m^{-1}.
 \label{eq:root-sandwich}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
A skew sum of $132$-avoiding blocks avoids $132$. If three indices are not
all in one block, their first and last indices lie in different blocks,
so the first value exceeds the last; this is incompatible with $132$.
Within a block, the largest possible jump is $k-1<m$.

For a boundary, let the earlier block have length $k$ and let the next
block have first-entry deficiency $j\le d$. The earlier block ends at its
maximum. Since the two value intervals are consecutive and decreasing,
the boundary jump is exactly $k+j\le m$. This also covers a singleton next
block, where $j=0$.

It remains to prove injectivity; this is not automatic for variable-length
concatenations. A skew cut in a permutation is a proper cut for which every
value on the left exceeds every value on the right. A block ending at its
maximum has no internal skew cut, because its maximum lies to the right
of every proper cut. Every actual boundary between the constructed blocks
is a skew cut. Any other proposed cut is internal to a block and is not a
skew cut. Consequently the complete set of skew cuts recovers exactly the
block boundaries, and standardization recovers each block. Distinct block
sequences cannot yield the same permutation.

The empty sequence and all nonempty sequences of admissible blocks are
therefore counted by $1+K+K^2+\cdots=(1-K)^{-1}$ and inject into the desired
class. Its radius is $s_{m,d}$ by the same positivity argument as above,
which proves the growth lower bound.
\end{proof}

For $d=1$ this becomes particularly simple:
\begin{equation}
 K_{m,1}(x)=x+\sum_{k=2}^{m-1}C_{k-2}x^k.
 \label{eq:K1}
\end{equation}
This one-parameter subfamily already proves \cref{thm:asympt-main}.
Allowing arbitrary fixed $d$ sharpens the bounded remainder in
\cref{thm:window-main}.

\subsection{Exact block weights and their Catalan tail}
\label{sec:block-weights}
Let $h(k,j)$ count blocks of length $k\ge2$ ending at their maximum and
having first entry $k-j$, where $1\le j\le k-1$. Put
\[
 \Cat(x)=\sum_{n\ge0}C_nx^n
 =\frac{1-\sqrt{1-4x}}{2x},\qquad \Cat(x)=1+x\Cat(x)^2.
\]

\begin{lemma}[First-entry generating function]\label{lem:firstentry}
For each fixed $j\ge1$,
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{k\ge j+1}h(k,j)x^k=x^{j+1}\Cat(x)^j,
 \qquad
 h(k,j)=\frac{j}{k-1}\binom{2k-j-3}{k-2}.
 \label{eq:h}
\end{equation}
Consequently
\begin{equation}
 K_{\infty,d}(x):=\sum_{k\ge1}c_{k,d}x^k
   =x\sum_{j=0}^d(x\Cat(x))^j.
 \label{eq:Kinfty}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Delete the final maximum from such a block. Its first entry is $a=k-j$.
The $j-1$ larger entries $a+1,\ldots,k-1$ must appear in increasing order:
a decreasing pair together with the initial $a$ would form $132$.
The smaller entries lie in the $j$ intervening slots, giving the form
\[
 a,\ \tau_0,\ a+1,\ \tau_1,\ a+2,\ldots,\ k-1,\ \tau_{j-1}.
\]
Every value in an earlier small slot must exceed every value in a later
small slot. Otherwise those two values and a separating large entry form
$132$. Each slot must itself avoid $132$. Conversely these conditions are
sufficient: triples meeting different small slots cannot form $132$, the
large entries are increasing, and any triple entirely in one small slot
is excluded by its own avoidance condition. Once the slot lengths are
specified, their value intervals are forced in decreasing order.
There are therefore $j$ independent Catalan slots and $j+1$ fixed entries
when the deleted final maximum is restored. This gives the first identity.

For completeness, put $Y=\Cat(x)-1$, so $Y=x(1+Y)^2$. For $N\ge1$,
Lagrange inversion gives
\[
 [x^N]\Cat(x)^j
 =\frac{j}{N}[u^{N-1}](1+u)^{2N+j-1}
 =\frac{j}{2N+j}\binom{2N+j}{N}.
\]
The same final expression is one for $N=0$. Substituting $N=k-j-1$ and
cancelling factorials gives the displayed formula for $h(k,j)$.
Summing over $1\le j\le d$, with the singleton added, proves
\eqref{eq:Kinfty} and \eqref{eq:c-formula}.
\end{proof}

Since $\Cat(1/4)=2$, \eqref{eq:Kinfty} yields
\begin{equation}
 B_d:=\sum_{k\ge1}c_{k,d}4^{-k}
      =\frac12\bigl(1-2^{-(d+1)}\bigr).
 \label{eq:Bd}
\end{equation}
To obtain the tail constant without singularity-analysis machinery,
compare each first-entry count directly with a Catalan number:
\begin{equation}
 \frac{h(k,j)}{C_{k-2}}
 =j\prod_{r=0}^{j-2}\frac{k-2-r}{2k-4-r}
 \longrightarrow \frac{j}{2^{j-1}}
 \qquad(k\to\infty).
 \label{eq:h-ratio}
\end{equation}
The empty product for $j=1$ is one. Stirling's formula gives
$C_n\sim4^n/(\sqrt\pi\,n^{3/2})$. For fixed $d$ it follows that
\begin{equation}
 c_{k,d}4^{-k}\sim\frac{a_d}{4\sqrt\pi}\,k^{-3/2},
 \qquad
 a_d:=\sum_{j=1}^d\frac{j}{2^{j+1}}
     =1-\frac{d+2}{2^{d+1}}.
 \label{eq:cd-tail}
\end{equation}
Both $a_d$ and $B_d$ are strictly positive for $d\ge1$, and $B_d<1$.

\section{A truncated-series root lemma}
\label{sec:root-lemma}
The following elementary estimate is the analytic step responsible for
the $\log\log m$ term. It applies to both sides of
\eqref{eq:root-sandwich}.

\begin{lemma}[A tilted $k^{-3/2}$ tail]\label{lem:tailroot}
Let $b_k\ge0$ satisfy
\[
 b_k\sim c k^{-3/2},\qquad c>0,\qquad B:=\sum_{k\ge1}b_k<1.
\]
Fix an integer $h\ge0$. For all sufficiently large $m$, let $r_m>0$ be the
unique positive solution of
\[
 \sum_{k=1}^{m-h}b_k(4r_m)^k=1.
\]
Then $r_m>1/4$ and
\begin{equation}
 m\log(4r_m)
 =\frac12\log m+\log\log m+
       \log\frac{1-B}{2c}+o(1).
 \label{eq:tailroot}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
At $r=1/4$ the truncated sum is at most $B<1$. For large enough $m$ the
polynomial is nonconstant with a positive coefficient, strictly increases
on the positive real axis, and tends to infinity. Thus the stated root
exists uniquely and exceeds $1/4$.

For a fixed real $s$, write
\[
 t=t_m(s)=\frac12\log m+\log\log m+s.
\]
We first prove the limiting profile
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{k=1}^{m-h}b_ke^{tk/m}\longrightarrow B+2ce^s.
 \label{eq:profile}
\end{equation}
The untilted truncated sum tends to $B$. It remains to evaluate the
increment from exponential tilting.

For $k\le m/2$, use $e^u-1\le ue^u$ for $u\ge0$ to obtain
\begin{align*}
 0\le\sum_{k\le m/2}b_k(e^{tk/m}-1)
 &\le \frac{t}{m}e^{t/2}\sum_{k\le m/2}k b_k\\
 &=O\left(\frac{t e^{t/2}}{\sqrt m}\right)
  =O\left(m^{-1/4}(\log m)^{3/2}\right)=o(1).
\end{align*}
Here $b_k=O(k^{-3/2})$ implies $\sum_{k\le M}k b_k=O(\sqrt M)$.

For $k>m/2$, the untilted tail is $O(m^{-1/2})=o(1)$. The assumed asymptotic
for $b_k$ holds uniformly in this range as $m\to\infty$. Put $j=m-k$.
The tilted upper tail is therefore asymptotic to
\begin{equation}
 c m^{-3/2}e^t
 \sum_{h\le j<m/2}(1-j/m)^{-3/2}e^{-tj/m}.
 \label{eq:boundary-sum}
\end{equation}
The sum in \eqref{eq:boundary-sum} is asymptotic to $m/t$.
To check this precisely, for $j\le m/\sqrt t$ the factor
$(1-j/m)^{-3/2}$ is $1+O(t^{-1/2})$ uniformly. The geometric sum without
that factor is
\[
 \sum_{j\ge h}e^{-tj/m}
 =\frac{e^{-th/m}}{1-e^{-t/m}}\sim\frac{m}{t}.
\]
The omitted part $j>m/\sqrt t$, with $j<m/2$, is bounded by a constant times
$e^{-\sqrt t}/(1-e^{-t/m})=o(m/t)$, since $(1-j/m)^{-3/2}\le2^{3/2}$.
The tail beyond $m/2$ in the geometric sum is negligible by the same
estimate. A fixed lower cutoff $h$ has no effect on the asymptotic.

Thus \eqref{eq:boundary-sum} is asymptotic to
\[
 \frac{c e^t}{\sqrt m\,t}
 =\frac{c e^s\log m}{\frac12\log m+\log\log m+s}
 \longrightarrow 2ce^s,
\]
which proves \eqref{eq:profile}.

Let $s_0=\log((1-B)/(2c))$. For each fixed $\eps>0$, the limiting value in
\eqref{eq:profile} is below one at $s=s_0-\eps$ and above one at
$s=s_0+\eps$. Monotonicity of the polynomial in $t$ traps
$m\log(4r_m)$ between $t_m(s_0-\eps)$ and $t_m(s_0+\eps)$ for all sufficiently
large $m$. Since $\eps$ is arbitrary, \eqref{eq:tailroot} follows.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Why the second logarithm appears]
The effective large-$k$ contribution comes from a boundary layer of width
about $m/t$. Each coefficient near the cutoff is of order $m^{-3/2}$,
and the exponential factor is $e^t$. Its total mass is consequently
$e^t/(\sqrt m\,t)$. Requiring a nonzero limiting mass gives
$t=\frac12\log m+\log t+O(1)$, hence the additional $\log\log m$.
The proof above supplies the constants and controls the discarded ranges.
\end{remark}

\section{The refined asymptotic and its constant window}
\label{sec:asympt}
Put
\[
 L_m=\frac12\log m+\log\log m,
 \qquad t_m^*=m\log\frac4{\alpha_m}.
\]
We apply \cref{lem:tailroot} with two different nonnegative coefficient
sequences.

For the upper-growth majorant $R_m$, write
\[
 R_m(x)=\sum_{k=1}^m b_k(4x)^k,\qquad
 b_1=\frac12,\qquad b_k=C_{k-1}4^{-k}\quad(k\ge2).
\]
Then
\[
 B=\frac34,\qquad c=\frac1{4\sqrt\pi},\qquad h=0.
\]
Therefore
\begin{equation}
 m\log(4q_m)=L_m+\log\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2}+o(1).
 \label{eq:q-asympt}
\end{equation}
For the block lower bound, take $b_k=c_{k,d}4^{-k}$ and $h=d$, holding $d$
fixed as $m\to\infty$. Equations \eqref{eq:Bd} and \eqref{eq:cd-tail} give
\begin{equation}
 m\log(4s_{m,d})=L_m+\gamma_d+o(1),
 \qquad
 \gamma_d=
 \log\!\left(
 \frac{\sqrt\pi(1+2^{-(d+1)})}{1-(d+2)2^{-(d+1)}}\right).
 \label{eq:s-asympt}
\end{equation}
The $o(1)$ in this formula is for each fixed $d$; no uniformity in a growing
$d$ is used.

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{thm:asympt-main}]
From \eqref{eq:root-sandwich},
\begin{equation}
 m\log(4q_m)\le t_m^*\le m\log(4s_{m,d}).
 \label{eq:t-sandwich}
\end{equation}
Already $d=1$, for which $\gamma_1=\log(5\sqrt\pi)$, shows
$t_m^*=L_m+O(1)$. Hence $\alpha_m=4e^{-t_m^*/m}$ and
\begin{equation}
 m(4-\alpha_m)=4m(1-e^{-t_m^*/m})
 =4t_m^*+O\!\left(\frac{(t_m^*)^2}{m}\right)
 =4t_m^*+o(1).
 \label{eq:t-conversion}
\end{equation}
Since $4L_m=2\log m+4\log\log m$, the theorem follows.
This also independently proves $\alpha_m\to4$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{thm:window-main}]
The lower side of \eqref{eq:t-sandwich}, together with
\eqref{eq:q-asympt} and \eqref{eq:t-conversion}, gives
\[
 \liminf_{m\to\infty}E_m\ge4\log(\sqrt\pi/2)
   =2\log\pi-4\log2.
\]
For every fixed $d\ge1$, the upper side gives
\[
 \limsup_{m\to\infty}E_m\le4\gamma_d.
\]
Now let $d\to\infty$ \emph{after} taking the limsup in $m$.
Because $\gamma_d\to\log\sqrt\pi$, the upper bound becomes $2\log\pi$.
The middle inequality between liminf and limsup is automatic.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The constants are not finite-$m$ error bars]
The endpoints in \eqref{eq:constant-window} are asymptotic bounds, not a
claim that every $E_m$ lies in that interval. Numerically the scalar
comparisons approach their limits slowly. Even at $m=10^6$, the best of
the four computed fixed-$d$ lower bounds gives a remainder upper bound
above $2\log\pi$. This is compatible with the theorem: a fixed $d$ has
limit $4\gamma_d$, and the root expansion also has a slowly vanishing
error.
\end{remark}

\section{What is resolved, and what remains}
\label{sec:remaining}
The strict inequality $\alpha_{m+1}>\alpha_m$ is proved for every positive
integer $m$. Together with the existing fixed-bound results, it completes
the growth-constant conjecture selected here. The scalar comparison proof
also determines the leading constant and next logarithmic term in the
approach to four.

Several nearby questions are not resolved by these arguments. The first
is whether $\lambda_V(m)>\lambda_U(m)$ for \emph{every} $m\ge5$; the reverse
inequality holds for $m=2,3,4$. This is the separate component-comparison
problem formulated in \cite[Problem~P3]{MayamaAkita}. Strict increase in
both sequences is compatible with crossings, so it cannot settle their
ordering. The finite certificates here establish the pattern only through
$m=20$.
[Added 28 September 2026, batch 38: this question remains open. Part~II
shows only that a dominant $V$ can lead by at most
$(\lambda_V(m)-\lambda_U(m))_+=O((\log m)^2/m^2)$
(\cref{crem:cor:component-gap}), which is why the ordering is invisible to
every inverse-logarithmic term; see \cref{crem:sec:future}.]

A second question is whether $E_m$ converges, and, if so, to which constant.
The scalar majorant discards endpoint compatibility, while the lower
construction permits only a special recoverable family of blocks. Their
limiting constants leave a window of width $4\log2$. Closing that gap
requires controlling more of the endpoint information, not merely taking
more terms of the same scalar expansions. A growing-$d$ analysis could
improve finite bounds, but our argument deliberately takes $d$ fixed and
then sends $d$ to infinity only after a limsup.
[Added 28 September 2026, batch 38: the preceding paragraph describes the
report at its pinned state \texttt{6c9175a54}. The question is answered in
Part~II: $E_m\to2\log\pi$ (\cref{crem:cor:constant}). As anticipated here,
the upper side keeps last-endpoint information, through a Catalan-weighted
positive vector (\cref{crem:lem:V-vector}), and the lower side lets the
cutoff grow, $d=\lceil\log_2m\rceil$, using a tail bound uniform in $d$
(\cref{crem:lem:uniform-tail,crem:thm:corridor-quantitative}).]

Finally, the minimal recurrence order and uniform denominator factorization
of $A_m(x)$ remain outside the scope of this report. The exact $m\le4$
artifacts are useful examples, not a claimed solution of that general
algebraic problem. The new strict-growth argument is valuable precisely
because it avoids needing such a formula.

\subsection{The question and the answer}
\label{crem:sec:answer}
Part~I proved $4-\alpha_m=(2\log m+4\log\log m+O(1))/m$
(\cref{thm:asympt-main}) and, for the centered remainder
$E_m=m(4-\alpha_m)-2\log m-4\log\log m$, the window
\eqref{eq:constant-window} of width $4\log2$. Its concluding discussion asks
whether $E_m$ converges and, if so, to which constant. That is the question
resolved here. It was an open question in the inspected report (Part~I), not
a claim that every open problem in the original enumeration papers has been
settled.

The older upper comparison was based on $R_m(x)=x+\sum_{k=1}^mC_{k-1}x^k$.
The extra $x$ accounts for appending a maximum when endpoint restrictions are
discarded. One cannot simply delete that term from the counting recurrence.
Instead, we retain the last-endpoint information and assign a different
positive weight to each endpoint threshold. A Catalan convolution then
incorporates the append transition without paying for it as an independent
unconstrained scalar choice. Part~I's diagnosis in \cref{sec:remaining}, that
closing the window needs more endpoint information than the scalar series
carry, is exactly what this weighting supplies on the upper side; on the
lower side a cutoff $d$ growing with $m$ is used, made legitimate by a bound
uniform in $d$.

Write
\begin{equation}
 C_j=\frac1{j+1}\binom{2j}{j},\qquad
 K_m(x)=\sum_{k=1}^m C_{k-1}x^k,
 \qquad K_m(r_m)=1,
 \qquad \beta_m=r_m^{-1}.
 \label{crem:eq:scalar-root}
\end{equation}
The polynomial $K_m$ is strictly increasing on the positive real axis. Thus
$r_m$ is well defined. Since $K_m(1/4)<\sum_{k\ge1}C_{k-1}4^{-k}=1/2$, we have
$r_m>1/4$. For $m\ge2$, $K_m(1)>1$, so $r_m<1$.

\begin{theorem}[Finite renewal upper bound]\label{crem:thm:main-upper}
For every $m\ge2$,
\[
 \alpha_m<\beta_m<4.
\]
For $m=1$, $\alpha_1=\beta_1=1$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Quantitative scalar universality]\label{crem:thm:main-quantitative}
As $m\to\infty$,
\begin{equation}
 0\le\beta_m-\alpha_m
 \le\beta_m-\lambda_U(m)
 =O\!\left(\frac{(\log m)^2}{m^2}\right),
 \label{crem:eq:main-quantitative}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_U(m)$ is the first cyclic-component growth rate of
\eqref{eq:component-root}. In particular,
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\displaystyle\lim_{m\to\infty}E_m=2\log\pi.}
 \label{crem:eq:main-constant}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

For the all-orders statement, put
\begin{equation}
 \Lambda=\tfrac12\log m,\qquad
 z=\log\Lambda+\log(2\sqrt\pi)
   =\log\log m+\tfrac12\log\pi.
 \label{crem:eq:Lz}
\end{equation}
\begin{theorem}[All inverse-logarithmic orders]\label{crem:thm:main-allorders}
There are explicitly computable polynomials $\mathcal P_j\in\mathbb Q[z]$, of
degree $j$, such that for every fixed integer $J\ge0$,
\begin{equation}
 m(4-\alpha_m)
 =4\Lambda+4z+4\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{\mathcal P_j(z)}{\Lambda^j}
 +O_J\!\left(\frac{(1+|z|)^{J+1}}{\Lambda^{J+1}}\right).
 \label{crem:eq:main-allorders}
\end{equation}
The first three are
\begin{align}
 \mathcal P_1(z)&=z-\tfrac32,\notag\\
 \mathcal P_2(z)&=-\tfrac12z^2+\tfrac52z-\tfrac{33}{8},\notag\\
 \mathcal P_3(z)&=\tfrac13z^3-3z^2+\tfrac{43}{4}z-15.
 \label{crem:eq:first-polynomials}
\end{align}
The same expansion holds with $\alpha_m$ replaced by $\lambda_U(m)$ or
$\beta_m$.
\end{theorem}

Since $4\Lambda+4z=2\log m+4\log\log m+2\log\pi$, the case $J=0$ is again
$E_m\to2\log\pi$: the upper endpoint of Part~I's window
\eqref{eq:constant-window}. Part~II refutes nothing in Part~I; it sharpens
the window to a point.

 \quad\text{with weight }c_{k,p}x^k,
 \label{crem:eq:split-edge}

 0\le c_{k,p}\le C_{k-1},\qquad

 \label{crem:eq:c-basic}

\begin{proposition}[Spectral interface]\label{crem:prop:spectral}

\begin{equation}
 \alpha_m=\max\{\lambda_U(m),\lambda_V(m)\},
 \qquad \lambda_U(m)>1.
 \label{crem:eq:spectral-interface}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

This is \cref{lem:components}, the paragraph after \cref{lem:PF}, and

every edge into unit-length edges, and the same closed walks of lengths two
and five for primitivity. Its argument for $\lambda_U(m)>1$ differs and is

\begin{proof}[Second proof of $\lambda_U(m)>1$ (manuscript~04)]

We will repeatedly use two elementary consequences of Perron--Frobenius

\begin{lemma}[Positivity tools]\label{crem:lem:positivity}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item If $M$ is irreducible and nonnegative, and $v>0$ satisfies $Mv\le v$
 with a strict inequality in at least one coordinate, then $\spr(M)<1$.
\item For any nonnegative matrix, the spectral radius is at least its
 minimum row sum, and it is monotone under entrywise increase and under
 principal extension.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For (i), multiply by a positive left Perron vector $u$:
$\spr(M)u^{\mathsf T}v=u^{\mathsf T}Mv<u^{\mathsf T}v$. For (ii), the
row-sum bound follows by iterating $M\one\ge a\one$; the monotonicity
follows by comparing powers. The mechanism of (i) is the one used in
\cref{lem:PF}.
\end{proof}

\section{The endpoint renewal comparison}
\label{crem:sec:renewal}
The essential new finite observation is a positive vector adapted to the
last threshold. Before applying it, it is useful to isolate its algebraic
mechanism.

\subsection{A renewal system with a reset}
\begin{lemma}[Renewal--reset identity]\label{crem:lem:reset}
Let $w_1,\ldots,w_{m-1}\ge0$ and $a\ge0$. On indices $0,\ldots,m-1$, let
$L$ be the strictly lower triangular matrix with $L_{q,q-k}=w_k$ for
$1\le k\le q$, and put
\[
 N=L+a\one e_{m-1}^{\mathsf T}.
\]
Define $u_0=1$, $u_n=\sum_{k=1}^n w_ku_{n-k}$ for $1\le n<m$, and
$H_q=\sum_{j=0}^q u_j$. Then
\begin{align}
 (I-L)H&=\one,\label{crem:eq:renewalH}\\
 NH&=H+(aH_{m-1}-1)\one,\label{crem:eq:reset-vector}\\
 \det(I-N)&=1-aH_{m-1}.\label{crem:eq:reset-det}
\end{align}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For each $q$,
\[
 \sum_{k=1}^q w_kH_{q-k}
 =\sum_{n=1}^q\sum_{k=1}^n w_ku_{n-k}
 =\sum_{n=1}^q u_n=H_q-1.
\]
This proves \eqref{crem:eq:renewalH}; the reset term gives
\eqref{crem:eq:reset-vector}. Since $L$ is strictly triangular,
$\det(I-L)=1$. The determinant identity for a rank-one update, or direct
elimination of $(I-L)$, gives
\[
 \det(I-N)=\det(I-L)
 \bigl(1-ae_{m-1}^{\mathsf T}(I-L)^{-1}\one\bigr)
 =1-aH_{m-1}.
\]
All identities also hold over a commutative coefficient ring when the
nonnegativity assertions are omitted.
\end{proof}

For the Catalan application, take $w_k=C_{k-1}x^k$ and $a=x$. The Catalan
recurrence gives $u_j=C_jx^j$. Thus
\begin{equation}
 H_q(x)=\sum_{j=0}^q C_jx^j,
 \qquad xH_{m-1}(x)=K_m(x).
 \label{crem:eq:H}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, the formal identity $\Cat(x)=1+x\Cat(x)^2$ gives
$(1-x\Cat(x))^{-1}=\Cat(x)$. The finite version we need is
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{k=1}^q C_{k-1}x^kH_{q-k}(x)=H_q(x)-1.
 \label{crem:eq:catalan-renewal}
\end{equation}
There is no convergence issue in this polynomial identity.

\subsection{A positive vector for the true two-endpoint component}
\begin{lemma}[Last-threshold supersolution]\label{crem:lem:V-vector}
For a state $(p,q)\in V_m$, set $v_{p,q}(x)=H_q(x)$. For every $x>0$,
\begin{equation}
 (W_{V_m}(x)v(x))_{p,q}
 \le H_q(x)-1+K_m(x).
 \label{crem:eq:V-domination}
\end{equation}
At $x=r_m$, at least one coordinate inequality is strict.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
A valid split from $(p,q)$ has $k\le q$, and its target has last threshold
$q-k$. We may bound $c_{k,p}$ by $C_{k-1}$ and include every $1\le k\le q$.
If an append edge exists, its target has last threshold $m-1$; including
such an edge even when it is absent can only increase the result. Therefore
\begin{align*}
 (W_{V_m}v)_{p,q}
 &\le\sum_{k=1}^q C_{k-1}x^kH_{q-k}(x)+xH_{m-1}(x)\\
 &=H_q(x)-1+K_m(x),
\end{align*}
by \eqref{crem:eq:catalan-renewal}. At the state $(0,m-1)$, which belongs to
$V_m$ for $m\ge2$, there is no append edge. The positive term
$xH_{m-1}(x)$ was consequently added strictly. This proves strictness, in
fact for every $x>0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{crem:thm:main-upper}]
At $x=r_m$, \cref{crem:lem:V-vector} gives $W_{V_m}v\le v$, with a strict
coordinate and $v>0$. Irreducibility and
\cref{crem:lem:positivity}(i) imply $\spr(W_{V_m}(r_m))<1$.

In $U_m$, append edges leave the component. Its row at first threshold $p$
has sum $\sum_{k=1}^m c_{k,p}r_m^k\le K_m(r_m)=1$. The row $p=0$ has sum
$r_m<1$, since only the $k=1$ coefficient survives. Thus
$\spr(W_{U_m}(r_m))<1$ by the same positivity argument, now with vector
$\one$.

Strict increase of each spectral radius gives $r_U(m),r_V(m)>r_m$. Taking
reciprocals and using \eqref{crem:eq:spectral-interface} proves
$\alpha_m<\beta_m$. The already established $r_m>1/4$ gives $\beta_m<4$.
For $m=1$, the only permutations of length at least two with jumps of
absolute value one are the increasing and decreasing permutations; a change
of direction would repeat a value. Hence $\alpha_1=1=\beta_1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Comparison with Part~I's majorant]\label{crem:rem:vs-upper}
Since $R_m=x+K_m$, one has $R_m(r_m)=1+r_m>1$, so $q_m<r_m$ and
$\beta_m<q_m^{-1}$ for every $m\ge1$: \cref{crem:thm:main-upper} is
strictly sharper than \cref{prop:upper}. At $m=100$, for instance, the
upper bound drops from $3.8581\ldots$ (\cref{tab:scalar}) to
$3.8201\ldots$ (\cref{crem:tab:certified}). Part~I's bound is
coefficientwise, \cref{crem:thm:main-upper} only spectral; see the next
remark.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[What this comparison does not say]\label{crem:rem:not-quotient}
The scalar polynomial is not asserted to be a denominator of $A_m$, and
$\det(I-W_{V_m})$ is not asserted to equal $1-K_m$. The determinant identity
\eqref{crem:eq:reset-det} belongs to the \emph{majorizing reset system}, not
to the full endpoint system. Nor does the proof assert the coefficientwise
inequality $A_m(x)\preceqc(1-K_m(x))^{-1}$; it is false
(\cref{crem:sec:m2}). Our comparison is a spectral one, witnessed by a
positive vector. These distinctions prevent a valid growth bound from being
misread as a solution of state minimization or exact enumeration.
\end{remark}

\section{A uniform lower approximation from recoverable blocks}
\label{crem:sec:blocks}
The block construction itself is that of \cref{prop:lower} in Part~I.
The additional ingredients of manuscript~04 are a component version of the lower
bound, an estimate uniform in the cutoff, and its use with a cutoff growing
with $m$.

\subsection{Block and component lower bounds}
Fix integers $1\le d<m$, and let $K_{m,d}$ and $s_{m,d}$ be as in
\eqref{eq:K} and \cref{prop:lower}:
\begin{equation}
 K_{m,d}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{m-d}c_{k,d}x^k,
 \qquad K_{m,d}(s_{m,d})=1.
 \label{crem:eq:block-root}
\end{equation}
The coefficient of $x$ is one, so a unique positive root exists.

\begin{proposition}[Block and component lower bounds]\label{crem:prop:block-lower}
For every $1\le d<m$,
\begin{equation}
 \frac1{1-K_{m,d}(x)}\preceqc A_m(x),
 \qquad s_{m,d}^{-1}\le\lambda_U(m)\le\alpha_m<\beta_m.
 \label{crem:eq:corridor}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The coefficientwise inequality is \cref{prop:lower}: skew sums of blocks
ending at their maximum, with the boundary jump $k+j\le m$, and injectivity
because the skew cuts recover the block boundaries. Manuscript~04 reproves it
by the same argument.

For the sharper component statement, restrict $W_{U_m}(x)$ to first
thresholds $p\in\{d,\ldots,m-1\}$. For every $1\le k\le m-d$, the split
target $m-k$ is in that subset and its coefficient satisfies
$c_{k,p}\ge c_{k,d}$. Every row sum of this principal submatrix is at least
$K_{m,d}(x)$. At $x=s_{m,d}$ its spectral radius is at least one. Principal
comparison (\cref{crem:lem:positivity}(ii)) implies
$\spr(W_{U_m}(s_{m,d}))\ge1$, so $r_U(m)\le s_{m,d}$ and
$\lambda_U(m)\ge s_{m,d}^{-1}$. The remaining inequalities are
\eqref{crem:eq:spectral-interface} and \cref{crem:thm:main-upper}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{First-entry counts and a uniform tail}
The first-entry formula is \cref{lem:firstentry}:
$h(k,j)=\frac{j}{k-1}\binom{2k-j-3}{k-2}$ for $1\le j<k$, with generating
function \eqref{eq:h}. Manuscript~04 reproves it with the same slot
decomposition and the same Lagrange inversion. Consequently
$c_{k,d}=\sum_{j=1}^{\min(d,k-1)}h(k,j)$ for $k\ge2$, as in
\eqref{eq:c-formula}.

\begin{lemma}[Uniform deficiency-tail estimate]\label{crem:lem:uniform-tail}
For all $d\ge1$ and $k\ge1$,
\begin{equation}
 0\le C_{k-1}-c_{k,d}
 \le\eps_d C_{k-1},
 \qquad \eps_d=(d+2)2^{-d}.
 \label{crem:eq:uniform-tail}
\end{equation}
Hence, whenever $1\le d<m$ and $\eps_d<1$,
\begin{equation}
 (1-\eps_d)K_{m-d}(x)\le K_{m,d}(x)\le K_{m-d}(x)
 \quad(x\ge0).
 \label{crem:eq:uniform-polynomial}
\end{equation}
The corresponding derivative inequalities also hold for $x\ge0$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
There is nothing to prove if $k=1$ or $k\le d+1$. For the remaining cases,
formula \eqref{eq:h} gives
\begin{equation}
 \frac{h(k,j)}{C_{k-2}}
 =j\prod_{r=0}^{j-2}\frac{k-2-r}{2k-4-r}
 \le\frac{j}{2^{j-1}}.
 \label{crem:eq:h-product}
\end{equation}
The product is empty for $j=1$. Each displayed factor is at most $1/2$.
For a missing deficiency $j\ge d+1\ge2$, we have $k\ge3$ and
\[
 \frac{C_{k-2}}{C_{k-1}}=\frac{k}{4k-6}\le\frac12.
\]
Thus $h(k,j)/C_{k-1}\le j2^{-j}$. Summing missing deficiencies and extending
the tail to infinity gives
\[
 \frac{C_{k-1}-c_{k,d}}{C_{k-1}}
 \le\sum_{j=d+1}^{\infty}\frac{j}{2^j}
 =\frac{d+2}{2^d}.
\]
For the final equality, differentiate the finite-tail geometric sum or
subtract its successive terms. Multiplying coefficientwise by $x^k$ and
summing proves \eqref{crem:eq:uniform-polynomial}. Differentiation preserves
a coefficientwise inequality between these nonnegative polynomials.
\end{proof}

The identity in \eqref{crem:eq:h-product} is Part~I's \eqref{eq:h-ratio};
the inequality is new.

\begin{remark}[Merge note: the uniform bound against Part~I's limit]
\label{crem:rem:ad}
By \eqref{eq:cd-tail} and $C_{k-1}4^{-k}\sim(4\sqrt\pi)^{-1}k^{-3/2}$, the
missing fraction $1-c_{k,d}/C_{k-1}$ tends to
$1-a_d=(d+2)2^{-(d+1)}=\eps_d/2$ as $k\to\infty$. The uniform bound
$\eps_d$ of \cref{crem:lem:uniform-tail} therefore loses at most a factor two
against the limit, the factor coming from $C_{k-2}/C_{k-1}\le\frac12$ where
the limit is $\frac14$. This observation is the merge's, not the
manuscript's.
\end{remark}

The role of uniformity is decisive. A fixed-$d$ asymptotic for $c_{k,d}$,
such as \eqref{eq:cd-tail}, would not justify substituting $d=d(m)$.
Inequality \eqref{crem:eq:uniform-tail} holds simultaneously for all $k,d$, so
the growing cutoff used below requires no interchange of unproved uniform
limits.

\section{The truncated Catalan root at all orders}
\label{crem:sec:scalar}
The remaining analysis begins with the scalar root alone. Normalize its
coefficients by
\begin{equation}
 b_k=C_{k-1}4^{-k},\qquad
 \sum_{k\ge1}b_k=\frac12,\qquad
 b_k=\frac1{4\sqrt\pi}k^{-3/2}\bigl(1+O(k^{-1})\bigr).
 \label{crem:eq:bk}
\end{equation}
The last estimate follows from Stirling's formula. In particular,
$b_k\le C_\ast k^{-3/2}$ with one absolute constant $C_\ast$. If
\begin{equation}
 t_m=m\log(4r_m),
 \label{crem:eq:tm}
\end{equation}
then
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{k=1}^m b_ke^{t_mk/m}=1,
 \qquad \beta_m=4e^{-t_m/m}.
 \label{crem:eq:tilted-root}
\end{equation}
The tail is summable but has total mass only $1/2$. A positive tilt must
supply the missing half. It does so in a thin layer near $k=m$.

\subsection{A uniform endpoint expansion}
For $a\in\R$ and $j\ge0$, write $(a)_0=1$ and $(a)_j=a(a+1)\cdots(a+j-1)$.

\begin{lemma}[Tilted Catalan endpoint expansion]\label{crem:lem:watson}
Fix $M>0$ and an integer $N\ge1$. Uniformly for
\begin{equation}
 t=\tfrac12\log m+\log\log m+s,\qquad |s|\le M,
 \label{crem:eq:t-window}
\end{equation}
as $m\to\infty$,
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{k=1}^m b_ke^{tk/m}
 =\frac12+\frac{e^t}{4\sqrt{\pi m}\,t}
 \left(\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\frac{(3/2)_j}{t^j}
       +O_{M,N}(t^{-N})\right).
 \label{crem:eq:watson}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Split the sum at $m/2$. For the lower half, the unweighted partial sum is
$1/2+O(m^{-1/2})$. Since $e^u-1\le ue^u$ for $u\ge0$, its tilt increment is
at most
\begin{align*}
 \sum_{k\le m/2}b_k(e^{tk/m}-1)
 &\le \frac{C_\ast t}{m}e^{t/2}\sum_{k\le m/2}k^{-1/2}\\
 &=O\!\left(\frac{te^{t/2}}{\sqrt m}\right)
 =O_M\!\left(m^{-1/4}(\log m)^{3/2}\right).
\end{align*}
This is smaller than every fixed negative power of $t$. Thus the lower half
contributes $1/2$ plus an error that can be absorbed into the remainder in
\eqref{crem:eq:watson}.

On the upper half, Stirling's estimate is uniform:
\[
 b_k=\frac1{4\sqrt\pi}k^{-3/2}(1+O(m^{-1})).
\]
A Riemann-sum comparison, using that $y^{-3/2}e^{ty}$ is increasing on
$[1/2,1]$ for all sufficiently large $t$, gives
\begin{align*}
 \sum_{m/2<k\le m}b_ke^{tk/m}
 &=\frac1{4\sqrt{\pi m}}
   \int_{1/2}^1 y^{-3/2}e^{ty}\,\dd y
   +O\!\left(\frac{e^t}{m^{3/2}}\right)\\
 &=\frac{e^t}{4\sqrt{\pi m}\,t}
   \int_0^{t/2}(1-v/t)^{-3/2}e^{-v}\,\dd v
   +O\!\left(\frac{e^t}{m^{3/2}}\right).
\end{align*}
A possible half-integer lower endpoint changes only the same error term.
For $0\le v\le t/2$, Taylor's theorem with a uniform remainder gives
\[
 (1-v/t)^{-3/2}
 =\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\frac{(3/2)_j}{j!}(v/t)^j
   +O_N((v/t)^N).
\]
Integrating each monomial against $e^{-v}$ and extending its upper limit to
infinity produces $j!$; the extension error is exponentially small in $t$.
The integral is therefore $\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}(3/2)_jt^{-j}+O_N(t^{-N})$.

In the window \eqref{crem:eq:t-window}, the prefactor $e^t/(\sqrt m\,t)$ is
bounded above and below by positive constants depending on $M$. The relative
Riemann error is $O(t/m)$, and all lower-half errors are smaller than any
fixed $t^{-N}$. Combining the estimates proves the uniform expansion.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Scalar root location]\label{crem:cor:scalar-location}
As $m\to\infty$,
\begin{equation}
 t_m=\tfrac12\log m+\log\log m+\tfrac12\log\pi+o(1).
 \label{crem:eq:t-leading}
\end{equation}
In particular, $t_m=O(\log m)$, $e^{t_m}=O(\sqrt m\log m)$, and
$r_m\to1/4$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
For a fixed real $s$, the $N=1$ case of \cref{crem:lem:watson} gives
\[
 \sum_{k=1}^m b_k
 \exp\!\left(\frac{k}{m}
 [\tfrac12\log m+\log\log m+s]\right)
 \longrightarrow \frac12+\frac{e^s}{2\sqrt\pi}.
\]
The limit equals one precisely at $s_0=\tfrac12\log\pi$. For every
$\delta>0$, its values at $s_0-\delta$ and $s_0+\delta$ lie strictly on
opposite sides of one. The finite sum is strictly increasing in $t$, so its
root lies between those trial values for all sufficiently large $m$. Let
$\delta\downarrow0$. The remaining assertions follow directly from
\eqref{crem:eq:t-leading} and \eqref{crem:eq:tm}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Second proof (merge note, from Part~I)]
Apply \cref{lem:tailroot} to $b_k=C_{k-1}4^{-k}$, $h=0$: then $B=\frac12$,
$c=1/(4\sqrt\pi)$, and $\log\frac{1-B}{2c}=\log\sqrt\pi$, which is
\eqref{crem:eq:t-leading}. Part~I applied the same lemma to the coefficients
of $R_m$ ($B=\frac34$), which gave the lower endpoint of its window; the
extra $x$ of $R_m$ alone accounts for the loss of $4\log2$ there, as
$4\log\frac{1-3/4}{1-1/2}=-4\log2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[An immediate resolution using the earlier window]
\label{crem:rem:immediate}
Already \cref{crem:thm:main-upper,crem:cor:scalar-location} imply
$\liminf E_m\ge2\log\pi$. Combined with the upper bound on $\limsup E_m$ in
\eqref{eq:constant-window}, this closes Part~I's question. The next section
proves a substantially stronger quantitative result and provides an
independent lower argument rather than relying on that asymptotic window.
\end{remark}

\section{A logarithmically growing cutoff gives a sharp corridor}
\label{crem:sec:quantitative}
Take, for all sufficiently large $m$,
\begin{equation}
 d=d_m=\lceil\log_2m\rceil,
 \qquad \eps_d=(d+2)2^{-d}=O(\log m/m).
 \label{crem:eq:choice-d}
\end{equation}
In particular, $d<m$, $d=O(\log m)$, and $\eps_d<1$.

\begin{lemma}[Value deficit and slope]\label{crem:lem:deficit-slope}
At the scalar root $r_m$, with $d$ as in \eqref{crem:eq:choice-d},
\begin{align}
 0\le1-K_{m,d}(r_m)
    &=O\!\left(\frac{(\log m)^2}{m}\right),
       \label{crem:eq:deficit}\\
 K_{m,d}'(r_m)&\ge c_\ast m
       \quad\text{for all sufficiently large }m,
       \label{crem:eq:slope}
\end{align}
for an absolute constant $c_\ast>0$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Set $t=t_m$. The lost last $d$ terms of $K_m(r_m)$ satisfy
\begin{align}
 \Delta_m&:=K_m(r_m)-K_{m-d}(r_m)
       =\sum_{k=m-d+1}^m b_ke^{tk/m}\notag\\
 &\le C_\ast d(m-d)^{-3/2}e^t
       =O\!\left(\frac{d\log m}{m}\right)
       =O\!\left(\frac{(\log m)^2}{m}\right),
 \label{crem:eq:last-d-tail}
\end{align}
using \cref{crem:cor:scalar-location}. Since $K_m(r_m)=1$,
\cref{crem:lem:uniform-tail} gives
\[
 K_{m,d}(r_m)\ge(1-\eps_d)(1-\Delta_m),
 \qquad 1-K_{m,d}(r_m)\le\eps_d+\Delta_m.
\]
This proves \eqref{crem:eq:deficit}.

The lower-half estimate in the proof of \cref{crem:lem:watson}, now
evaluated at $t_m$, shows
\[
 \sum_{k\le m/2}b_ke^{t_mk/m}\longrightarrow\frac12.
\]
The whole sum is one, and $\Delta_m\to0$. Consequently
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{m/2<k\le m-d}b_ke^{t_mk/m}\longrightarrow\frac12.
 \label{crem:eq:upper-mass}
\end{equation}
Every term in this range has $k>m/2$, so
\[
 K_{m-d}'(r_m)
 =\frac1{r_m}\sum_{k=1}^{m-d}k b_ke^{t_mk/m}
 \ge\frac{m}{2r_m}
       \sum_{m/2<k\le m-d}b_ke^{t_mk/m}.
\]
As $r_m\to1/4$, the right side is bounded below by a positive constant
times $m$. The derivative version of \eqref{crem:eq:uniform-polynomial},
together with $\eps_d\to0$, proves \eqref{crem:eq:slope}.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Quantitative scalar corridor]\label{crem:thm:corridor-quantitative}
With $d=\lceil\log_2m\rceil$,
\begin{equation}
 0\le s_{m,d}-r_m
    =O\!\left(\frac{(\log m)^2}{m^2}\right),
 \qquad
 0\le\beta_m-s_{m,d}^{-1}
    =O\!\left(\frac{(\log m)^2}{m^2}\right).
 \label{crem:eq:root-error}
\end{equation}
In particular, \eqref{crem:eq:main-quantitative} holds.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Since $K_{m,d}\le K_m$, we have $s_{m,d}\ge r_m$. Its derivative is
nondecreasing on the positive axis, since all coefficients are nonnegative.
Writing $s=s_{m,d}$, integration or the mean-value inequality gives
\[
 1-K_{m,d}(r_m)
 =K_{m,d}(s)-K_{m,d}(r_m)
 \ge K_{m,d}'(r_m)(s-r_m).
\]
Divide the estimate \eqref{crem:eq:deficit} by \eqref{crem:eq:slope} to
obtain the first part of \eqref{crem:eq:root-error}. Because $s,r_m\ge1/4$,
\[
 0\le r_m^{-1}-s^{-1}
 =\frac{s-r_m}{r_ms}\le16(s-r_m),
\]
which proves the second part. Finally, use the entire chain
\eqref{crem:eq:corridor}:
\[
 0\le\beta_m-\alpha_m
 \le\beta_m-\lambda_U(m)
 \le\beta_m-s_{m,d}^{-1}.
 \qedhere
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[The exact constant]\label{crem:cor:constant}
The centered remainder of \cref{thm:window-main} satisfies $E_m\to2\log\pi$.
Equivalently,
\begin{equation}
 4-\alpha_m
 =\frac{2\log m+4\log\log m+2\log\pi+o(1)}m.
 \label{crem:eq:constant-final}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
By \cref{crem:thm:corridor-quantitative} and $\beta_m=4e^{-t_m/m}$,
\begin{align}
 m(4-\alpha_m)
 &=4m(1-e^{-t_m/m})+O((\log m)^2/m)\notag\\
 &=4t_m+O((\log m)^2/m).
 \label{crem:eq:transfer}
\end{align}
Here Taylor's theorem applies since $t_m=O(\log m)$. Substitute
\eqref{crem:eq:t-leading}. This proves \eqref{crem:eq:constant-final} and
\cref{crem:thm:main-quantitative} in full.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[A one-sided constraint on component competition]
\label{crem:cor:component-gap}
Regardless of which component dominates,
\begin{equation}
 0\le\alpha_m-\lambda_U(m)
   =\bigl(\lambda_V(m)-\lambda_U(m)\bigr)_+
   =O\!\left(\frac{(\log m)^2}{m^2}\right).
 \label{crem:eq:component-gap}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}
This is deliberately a one-sided conclusion. We have not proved a
corresponding lower approximation for $\lambda_V(m)$ when it is below
$\lambda_U(m)$, and do not claim an absolute bound on
$|\lambda_V(m)-\lambda_U(m)|$ in that case. The result does show that
whenever $V$ wins, its advantage is invisible to every inverse-logarithmic
term in the scaled deficit.

\section{Inverting the scalar expansion to every logarithmic order}
\label{crem:sec:inversion}
The quantitative corridor is what makes a scalar asymptotic expansion a
statement about the actual permutation class. We now derive the
coefficients, including their error interpretation.

\subsection{Finite asymptotic equations, not a convergent series}
Introduce the \emph{formal} power series
\begin{equation}
 \Bformal(w)=\sum_{j\ge0}(3/2)_j w^j.
 \label{crem:eq:Bformal}
\end{equation}
Its coefficients grow factorially. The series has radius of convergence
zero and is never evaluated as an ordinary convergent series at $w>0$. For
each fixed $N\ge1$, instead use the finite polynomial in $1/t$
\[
 \Bformal_N(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}(3/2)_jt^{-j}.
\]
Substituting $t=t_m$ into \cref{crem:lem:watson} gives
\begin{equation}
 t_m-\log t_m+\log\Bformal_N(t_m)
 =\tfrac12\log m+\log(2\sqrt\pi)+O_N(t_m^{-N}).
 \label{crem:eq:finite-root-equation}
\end{equation}
Indeed the missing mass is $1/2$, so
$e^{t_m}\Bformal_N(t_m)/t_m=2\sqrt{\pi m}\,(1+O_N(t_m^{-N}))$. The
polynomial $\Bformal_N(t)$ is positive for $t>0$, and its logarithm is
unambiguous.

Formal logarithms are used below only as a finite coefficient-extraction
device. To compute a coefficient of degree $j$, only coefficients through
degree $j$ of \eqref{crem:eq:Bformal} are needed. Thus the divergent notation
organizes an unlimited sequence of finite, justified asymptotic statements;
it is not an analytic summation prescription.

\subsection{A triangular recursion for all coefficients}
\begin{proposition}[Coefficient recursion]\label{crem:prop:P-recursion}
Set $\mathcal P_0$ aside and define $\mathcal P_j$ recursively as follows.
At step $j\ge1$, put
\[
 T_{j-1}(u,z)=1+zu+\sum_{h=1}^{j-1}\mathcal P_h(z)u^{h+1},
\]
and define
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal P_j(z)=[u^j]\left\{
  \log T_{j-1}(u,z)
  -\log\Bformal\!\left(\frac{u}{T_{j-1}(u,z)}\right)
 \right\}.
 \label{crem:eq:P-recursion}
\end{equation}
These are well-defined rational polynomials of degree $j$, with leading
coefficient $(-1)^{j+1}/j$. For every fixed $J\ge0$,
\begin{equation}
 t_m=\Lambda+z+\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{\mathcal P_j(z)}{\Lambda^j}
 +O_J\!\left(\frac{(1+|z|)^{J+1}}{\Lambda^{J+1}}\right),
 \label{crem:eq:t-allorders}
\end{equation}
with $\Lambda,z$ as in \eqref{crem:eq:Lz}.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Write $u=1/\Lambda$ and seek $t=\Lambda T$, with
\[
 T=1+zu+\sum_{j=1}^{J}\mathcal P_j(z)u^{j+1}.
\]
Since $z=\log\Lambda+\log(2\sqrt\pi)$, substitution into the formal version
of \eqref{crem:eq:finite-root-equation} leaves the residual
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{j=1}^{J}\mathcal P_j(z)u^j-\log T
       +\log\Bformal(u/T).
 \label{crem:eq:formal-residual}
\end{equation}
The coefficient of $u^j$ in the last two terms depends only on
$\mathcal P_1,\ldots,\mathcal P_{j-1}$: $\mathcal P_j$ first enters $T$ at
order $u^{j+1}$. Cancelling the coefficient of $u^j$ therefore gives exactly
\eqref{crem:eq:P-recursion}, proving existence and uniqueness of the
recursion.

Induction shows $\deg\mathcal P_j\le j$. The only term of degree $j$ in $z$
at order $u^j$ comes from $\log(1+zu)$ and equals $(-1)^{j+1}z^j/j$. Terms
involving an earlier $\mathcal P_hu^{h+1}$ spend at least one more power of
$u$ than their possible degree in $z$; terms from $\log\Bformal(u/T)$ have
the same strict deficit. Thus the degree is exactly $j$ and its leading
coefficient has the stated value.

To justify the error, fix $J$ and take $N\ge J+2$ in the finite equation
\eqref{crem:eq:finite-root-equation}. For the trial value $t_*$ obtained by
truncating \eqref{crem:eq:t-allorders}, we have
$T=1+O_J((1+|z|)/\Lambda)$ and $z=O(\log\Lambda)$. Finite Taylor expansions
of the logarithms, with remainders uniform for $|T-1|\le1/2$, turn the formal
cancellations into the numerical residual estimate
\[
 t_*-\log t_*+\log\Bformal_N(t_*)-\Lambda-\log(2\sqrt\pi)
 =O_J((1+|z|)^{J+1}\Lambda^{-J-1}).
\]
Both $t_*$ and $t_m$ lie in a window with bounded shift of the form
\eqref{crem:eq:t-window}, by \cref{crem:cor:scalar-location} and
$\sum_{j\le J}\mathcal P_j(z)\Lambda^{-j}=o(1)$. The derivative of
$t-\log t+\log\Bformal_N(t)$ is
\[
 1-\frac1t+O_N(t^{-2}),
\]
so it is at least $1/2$ throughout the interval between them for large $m$.
Apply the mean-value theorem to the residuals of $t_*$ and $t_m$. The
$O_N(t_m^{-N})$ term is smaller than the claimed bound. This proves
\eqref{crem:eq:t-allorders}.
\end{proof}

The first four polynomials are
\begin{align}
 \mathcal P_1(z)&=z-\frac32,\notag\\
 \mathcal P_2(z)&=-\frac12z^2+\frac52z-\frac{33}{8},\notag\\
 \mathcal P_3(z)&=\frac13z^3-3z^2+\frac{43}{4}z-15,\notag\\
 \mathcal P_4(z)&=-\frac14z^4+\frac{10}{3}z^3-\frac{153}{8}z^2
              +\frac{223}{4}z-\frac{4461}{64}.
 \label{crem:eq:P-four}
\end{align}
For an independent algebraic check, the formal logarithm begins
\begin{equation}
 \log\Bformal(w)=\frac32w+\frac{21}{8}w^2
                 +\frac{69}{8}w^3+\frac{2529}{64}w^4+O(w^5).
 \label{crem:eq:logB}
\end{equation}
The bundled symbolic verifier constructs the polynomials from the recursion
and confirms that \eqref{crem:eq:formal-residual} vanishes through degree
four using exact rational polynomial arithmetic.

\subsection{Transfer to the true growth constant}
\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{crem:thm:main-allorders}]
Equation \eqref{crem:eq:transfer} gives
$m(4-\alpha_m)=4t_m+O((\log m)^2/m)$. For each fixed $J$, this error is
smaller than every fixed power of $1/\log m$, and hence is absorbed by the
remainder in \eqref{crem:eq:t-allorders}. Multiply that expansion by four.
The same proof applies to $\lambda_U(m)$ by
\eqref{crem:eq:main-quantitative}. For $\beta_m$, the direct Taylor
expansion of $4e^{-t_m/m}$ supplies the transfer.
\end{proof}

With $z=\log\log m+\frac12\log\pi$, the first three corrections can also be
written in powers of $1/\log m$ (the manuscript abbreviates $\log m$ as $H$,
which Part~II avoids):
\begin{align}
 m(4-\alpha_m)
 ={}&2\log m+4\log\log m+2\log\pi
       +\frac{8z-12}{\log m}\notag\\
 &+\frac{-8z^2+40z-66}{(\log m)^2}\notag\\
 &+\frac{\frac{32}{3}z^3-96z^2+344z-480}{(\log m)^3}
       +O\!\left(\frac{(1+|\log\log m|)^4}{(\log m)^4}\right).
 \label{crem:eq:explicit-expansion}
\end{align}
The assertion is asymptotic for each fixed truncation order. It does not
promise that adding another term improves a numerical approximation at a
particular small or moderate value of $m$.

\subsection{Two different error scales}
There are two conceptually distinct approximations. The scalar surrogate
$\beta_m$ differs from $\alpha_m$ by at most order $(\log m)^2/m^2$. A fixed
truncation of the logarithmic expansion generally has a much larger error,
of order a power of $1/\log m$ after multiplying the growth deficit by $m$.
Thus a scalar root can be numerically extremely accurate while its first few
asymptotic terms are still inaccurate.

This also explains the component issue. The proof does not need to decide
whether $\alpha_m=\lambda_U(m)$ or $\lambda_V(m)$. When $V$ is dominant,
\cref{crem:cor:component-gap} confines its extra growth to a scale smaller
than all the terms in \eqref{crem:eq:explicit-expansion}. A calculation
retaining only those terms has discarded the very information needed to
settle that component ordering.

\section{A complete small example and a useful non-implication}
\label{crem:sec:m2}
At $m=2$, order the $U$ states by $p=0,1$, and the $V$ states as
$(1,0),(0,1)$. Then
\[
 W_{U_2}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}0&x\\x^2&x\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad
 W_{V_2}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}0&x\\x&0\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Thus $\lambda_U(2)$ is the positive root of $\lambda^3-\lambda^2-1=0$, while
$\lambda_V(2)=1$ (the matrix $W_{U_2}$ also appears in the proof of
\cref{thm:strict-main}). The scalar polynomial is $K_2(x)=x+x^2$, and
$\beta_2=(1+\sqrt5)/2$.

The last-threshold vector is $v=(1,1+x)^{\mathsf T}$. At the root
$K_2(r_2)=1$,
\[
 W_{V_2}(r_2)v=
 \begin{pmatrix}r_2+r_2^2\\r_2\end{pmatrix}
 =\begin{pmatrix}1\\r_2\end{pmatrix}
 <_{\mathrm{some}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1+r_2\end{pmatrix}=v,
\]
where $<_{\mathrm{some}}$ means coordinatewise inequality with at least one
strict coordinate. The separate reset comparator of \cref{crem:lem:reset} is
\[
 N(x)=\begin{pmatrix}0&x\\x&x\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad \det(I-N(x))=1-x-x^2.
\]
This differs from $\det(I-W_{V_2}(x))=1-x^2$, illustrating exactly why a
spectral comparison is not an equality of state systems.

There is also an immediate counterexample to the tempting coefficientwise
interpretation. For length three and $m=2$, every $132$-avoider meets the
adjacency bound, so $[x^3]A_2(x)=C_3=5$ (\cref{tab:counts}). But
\[
 [x^3]\frac1{1-K_2(x)}
 =[x^3]\frac1{1-x-x^2}=3.
\]
The upper bound on exponential growth is valid even though this proposed
coefficientwise majorant is false. This example is a useful diagnostic when
transporting the renewal argument to another combinatorial class.

\section{Further research questions and proposed approaches}
\label{crem:sec:future}
The exact constant is no longer the first obstruction. The quantitative
corridor changes which questions are mathematically distinguishable and
which tools are likely to be useful. The second question below is Part~I's
first open question (\cref{sec:remaining}), now constrained by
\cref{crem:cor:component-gap}; the eighth continues Part~I's remark on
minimal recurrence orders and denominators.

\subsection{The first term beyond scalar universality}
Determine the true order and leading behavior of $\beta_m-\alpha_m$. Our
upper bound is $O((\log m)^2/m^2)$, obtained by discarding a logarithmic
number of terminal block lengths and controlling a deficiency tail
uniformly. Those losses need not be optimal. A sharper approach would keep
the compatibility between a block length and the next first deficiency
rather than impose a single worst-case cutoff. A small matrix of deficiency
classes, with a positive-vector comparison analogous to
\cref{crem:lem:V-vector}, is a concrete candidate. No asymptotic equivalent
for this defect is asserted here.

\subsection{The eventual ordering of the two components}
Resolve whether $\lambda_V(m)>\lambda_U(m)$ for every $m\ge5$, the ordering
suggested by finite computations and related to Problem P3 in
\cite{MayamaAkita}. By \cref{crem:cor:component-gap}, a proof cannot be
obtained merely by comparing their shared inverse-logarithmic expansion with
the scalar root. It must detect finer endpoint losses or construct a
comparison preserving more information than the present supersolution. One
possibility is to compare first-return operators after isolating the
large-threshold states, where long blocks are compatible with nearly all
first entries.

\subsection{The geometry of near-critical Perron vectors}
Describe the positive eigenvectors of $W_{U_m}$ and $W_{V_m}$ at their
critical roots as $m\to\infty$. The scalar tilted mass is concentrated in a
layer of width about $m/t_m$ near the maximal block length, while the chosen
cutoff is only of order $\log m$. A useful question is whether endpoint
coordinates admit a scaling limit connecting these two regimes. Such a limit
could explain the loss of the scalar upper bound and supply an analytic
route to the sign of the component gap. The present test vector is a
supersolution, not a proved approximation to the true eigenvector.

\subsection{Poles, amplitudes, and a uniform spectral gap}
For each fixed $m$, determine when the dominant pole of $A_m$ is simple and
obtain a usable expression for the leading amplitude. An equality of
component rates could interact with paths between components and alter pole
order. Our argument identifies only their maximal exponential rate. A
uniform analysis of subdominant singularities would be needed to turn
fixed-$m$ spectral information into estimates with useful error constants as
$m$ grows. This is distinct from the scalar root's own simple zero, which
follows immediately from $K_m'(r_m)>0$.

\subsection{Joint growth of length and adjacency bound}
Study $a_n^{(m(n))}/C_n$ when $m=m(n)$ grows with $n$. The fixed-$m$ limit
used here is not automatically uniform, so substituting $m(n)$ into
$\alpha_m^n$ is not justified. Candidate regimes should compare the
exponential penalty $n\log(4/\alpha_m)$ with the finite-state mixing scale
and the Catalan polynomial factor. A satisfactory result would establish an
explicit range of uniformity before proposing a transition law. The identity
$a_n^{(m)}=C_n$ when $m\ge n-1$ provides an exact boundary condition for such
a theory.
[Added 29 September 2026, batch 43: the boundary end of this direction is
answered in Part~III. For fixed $d$, $a_n^{(n-d)}/C_n$ converges to an
explicit rational limit, and uniformly for $n\ge8$, $2\le d\le n-1$,
$a_n^{(n-d)}/C_n=3/\sqrt{\pi d}+O(d^{-3/2}+n^{-1/2})$, an equivalent whenever
$d\to\infty$ and $d=o(n)$ (\cref{lj:thm:tail-main,lj:eq:fixed-window}). The
direction stays open for $n-m\asymp n$ (\cref{lj:rem:bulk}) and for any
interpolation with the fixed-$m$ regime (\cref{lj:sec:q-reconnect}).]

\subsection{Renewal comparisons for other constrained tree classes}
The reset identity \cref{crem:lem:reset} is not specifically Catalan. It
applies whenever a monotone threshold is lowered by weighted steps and
restored by a reset. Identify other pattern-avoidance or tree-enumeration
problems where the associated renewal sequence is explicitly controlled. If
a truncated scalar coefficient law has a summable power tail, an analogous
endpoint tilt should be investigated. The necessary research task is to
prove a matching combinatorial lower construction; a scalar majorant alone
does not make its asymptotic constant universal.

\subsection{Weighted statistics and multivariate perturbations}
Refine the endpoint recurrence with variables recording descents, records,
or block statistics, in the direction of Problem P4 of \cite{MayamaAkita}.
The central question is whether a positive last-threshold witness persists
under a parameter perturbation. A successful extension might permit
derivative bounds for a weighted growth rate and limit laws at fixed $m$.
Positivity and irreducibility must be checked in the perturbed system;
formal differentiation of an unproved multivariate analogue of $K_m$ would
be insufficient.

\subsection{State minimization versus a spectral majorant}
Investigate whether the renewal witness can guide an exact reduction of the
endpoint system without confusing domination with equality. Problems P1 and
P2 in \cite{MayamaAkita} concern issues such as state complexity and
rational denominators. Our $m$-state reset comparator has determinant
$1-K_m$, but it forgets first-endpoint compatibility. One concrete goal is a
characterization of the lost information as a correction operator of small
rank or controlled support. An exact operator identity would be a
substantive advance beyond the present scalar comparison.

\subsection{Certified computation and formalization}
Build an adaptive exact or interval algorithm choosing $d$ to meet a
requested error tolerance, using $K_m$ and $K_{m,d}$ directly. This would
avoid large component determinants and would certify large-$m$ growth
intervals with clear cost accounting. For formalization, the finite renewal
polynomial identity, the spectral supersolution argument, the skew-cut
injection, and the uniform deficiency-tail inequality are natural first
targets. The analytic tail splitting and all-orders inversion can then be
formalized as separate lemmas with explicit uniform quantifiers. None of
this formalization has been started.

\subsection{Points checked in the written argument}
The sharp upper bound is not obtained by dropping a legitimate append term:
that term is incorporated by the last-threshold vector. The first-entry tail
estimate is uniform in both parameters, which permits a growing cutoff. The
conversion from a polynomial value deficit to a root displacement uses a
proved derivative lower bound, not a heuristic linearization. The order of
inequalities is preserved when reciprocal roots become growth bounds and
when growth bounds become deficit bounds. Finally, the factorially divergent
formal series is used only to define finite coefficient calculations, while
each analytic statement has its own fixed-order remainder.

These checks identify the parts of the proof most worth independent
scrutiny. They do not replace that scrutiny, but they make its targets
explicit and keep finite experiments distinct from universal arguments.

\subsection{Which question is answered, and how far}
\label{lj:sec:scope}
Part~II's research direction ``Joint growth of length and adjacency bound''
(\cref{crem:sec:future}) asks for $a_n^{(m(n))}/C_n$ when $m=m(n)$ grows
with $n$, warns that the fixed-$m$ limit is not automatically uniform, asks
that ``a satisfactory result'' establish an explicit range of uniformity
before a transition law, and names the identity $a_n^{(m)}=C_n$ for $m\ge
n-1$ as a boundary condition. The report's README lists ``no joint limit in
$n$ and $m$'' among the things not claimed. Part~III settles the regime next
to that boundary condition:
\begin{itemize}
\item for each fixed $d$, $a_n^{(n-d)}/C_n\to\ljP(D\ge d)$, an explicit
 rational number (\cref{lj:eq:fixed-window});
\item uniformly for $n\ge8$ and $2\le d\le n-1$,
 $a_n^{(n-d)}/C_n=3/\sqrt{\pi d}+O(d^{-3/2}+n^{-1/2})$ with an absolute
 constant (\cref{lj:thm:tail-main}), which is an explicit range of
 uniformity of the kind Part~II asks for;
\item hence $a_n^{(n-d)}/C_n\sim3/\sqrt{\pi d}$ whenever $d\to\infty$ and
 $d=o(n)$.
\end{itemize}
It does not settle the question. When $n-m$ is a fixed fraction of $n$, the
additive error is as large as the main term and no equivalent is claimed
(\cref{lj:rem:bulk}); for fixed $m$ the growth-constant regime of Parts~I
and~II is untouched; and nothing connects the two regimes yet
(\cref{lj:sec:q-reconnect}). Part~II's direction is therefore re-scoped, not
closed: its boundary end is answered here, and its bulk and interpolation
questions remain open. Part~III refutes nothing in Parts~I and~II and uses
none of their theorems.

\section{The question, its context, and the main results}
\label{lj:sec:introduction}

\subsection{A different order of limits}
As in Part~I, a permutation $\pi\in S_n$ avoids $132$ if no three indices
$i<j<k$ satisfy $\pi_i<\pi_k<\pi_j$. Write $\Av_n(132)$ for this class, and let
\[
 C_n=\frac1{n+1}\binom{2n}{n},\qquad
 \Cat(x)=\sum_{n\ge0}C_nx^n=\frac{1-\sqrt{1-4x}}{2x}.
\]
For $n\ge2$, set
\begin{equation}
 M(\pi)=\max_{1\le i<n}|\pi_{i+1}-\pi_i|,
 \qquad D(\pi)=n-M(\pi).
 \label{lj:eq:statistics}
\end{equation}
If $\Pi_n$ is uniform on $\Av_n(132)$, write $M_n=M(\Pi_n)$ and
$D_n=D(\Pi_n)$. Thus $1\le D_n\le n-1$.

Let $a_n^{(m)}$ count the avoiders with every adjacent difference at most
$m$. The identity connecting enumeration and probability is
\begin{equation}
 \frac{a_n^{(m)}}{C_n}=\ljP(M_n\le m)
 =\ljP(D_n\ge n-m).
 \label{lj:eq:bridge}
\end{equation}
The fixed-$m$ problem studies $n\to\infty$ with $m$ unchanged. Part~III
studies the boundary $m=n-d$, and then allows $d$ to grow with $n$.
Neither problem is a substitution into the other.

Nadler initiated the adjacency-constrained enumeration and analyzed $m=2$
\cite{Nadler}. Mayama and Akita supplied a uniform endpoint-state system,
rational generating functions for every fixed $m$, and a spectral
characterization of growth \cite{MayamaAkita}. The ProveIt report
\emph{Strict growth and the Catalan limit for adjacency-bounded
132-avoiding permutations}, including its September 28 continuation, that
is, Parts~I and~II above, addresses strict growth and increasingly precise
fixed-bound growth asymptotics. Its README explicitly excludes a joint limit
in $n$ and $m$ from its claims (at the pin recorded in
\cref{lj:app:provenance}).

Our connection to that report is the same counting class and the same
maximum decomposition, not a dependence on its claimed growth-constant
estimates. The proofs below derive their own finite combinatorics. The
infinite-tree viewpoint is standard background in conditioned tree
probability \cite{JansonTrees}; here it is made into an explicit finite
counting argument. Other statistics on random $132$-avoiders have been
studied through tree and excursion methods \cite{JansonPatterns}; those
results are not substituted for the extreme-jump analysis.

\subsection{The law and the rate}
For real $0\le z\le1$, put
\begin{equation}
 s=\sqrt{1-z},\qquad r=\sqrt{\frac{1+s}{2}}.
 \label{lj:eq:s-r}
\end{equation}
Both square roots are the nonnegative ones. Around $z=0$, they denote the
analytic branches with $s(0)=r(0)=1$.

\begin{theorem}[Algebraic boundary law]\label{lj:thm:main}
There is a positive integer-valued random variable $D$ with probability
generating function
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\displaystyle
 G(z)=\ljE z^D=
 \frac{(1-s)\bigl(4+8s+3s^2-s^2r\bigr)}
      {4(1+2s)(1+s)^2}.}
 \label{lj:eq:main-pgf}
\end{equation}
Moreover,
\begin{equation}
 \ljTV(\ljLaw(D_n),\ljLaw(D))=\Theta(n^{-1/2}).
 \label{lj:eq:main-tv}
\end{equation}
With $h=\lfloor n/4\rfloor$, an explicit bound for $n\ge8$ is
\begin{equation}
 \ljTV(\ljLaw(D_n),\ljLaw(D))\le
 \min\left\{1,
 \frac{2(h+1)(2h+1)}{h+2}\frac{C_h}{4^h}
 +n(n+1)(2n+1)2^{-n/2}\right\}.
 \label{lj:eq:explicit-tv}
\end{equation}
In particular, $D_n=O_{\ljP}(1)$ and $M_n/n\to1$ in probability.
\end{theorem}

The convention for total variation is
$\ljTV(\mu,\nu)=\sup_B|\mu(B)-\nu(B)|$, equivalently half the
$\ell^1$ distance on a countable space. The explicit bound is designed for a
transparent proof rather than a small finite-$n$ constant. The matching
lower bound follows from the limiting heavy tail and the finite support
of $D_n$, as shown in \cref{lj:cor:sharp-tv}.

\begin{theorem}[Tail and joint enumeration]\label{lj:thm:tail-main}
As $k\to\infty$,
\begin{align}
 \ljP(D=k)&=\frac{3}{2\sqrt\pi}\,k^{-3/2}
          +O(k^{-5/2}),\label{lj:eq:pmf-leading}\\
 \ljP(D>k)&=\frac{3}{\sqrt\pi}\,k^{-1/2}
          +O(k^{-3/2}).\label{lj:eq:tail-leading}
\end{align}
Uniformly for integers $n\ge8$ and $2\le d\le n-1$,
\begin{equation}
 \frac{a_n^{(n-d)}}{C_n}
 =\frac{3}{\sqrt{\pi d}}+O(d^{-3/2}+n^{-1/2}),
 \label{lj:eq:uniform-joint}
\end{equation}
with an absolute implied constant. Consequently, if $d\to\infty$ and
$d=o(n)$, then
\begin{equation}
 \frac{a_n^{(n-d)}}{C_n}\sim\frac{3}{\sqrt{\pi d}},
 \qquad
 a_n^{(n-d)}\sim\frac{3}{\pi}\frac{4^n}{n^{3/2}\sqrt d}.
 \label{lj:eq:joint-equivalent}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Moment transition]\label{lj:thm:moments-main}
For each fixed real $p>0$,
\begin{align}
 0<p<\tfrac12:\quad
 &\ljE D_n^p=\ljE D^p+O(n^{p-1/2}),\label{lj:eq:small-moments}\\
 p=\tfrac12:\quad
 &\ljE\sqrt{D_n}=\frac{3}{2\sqrt\pi}\log n+O(1),
 \label{lj:eq:critical-moment}\\
 p>\tfrac12:\quad
 &\ljE D_n^p=\Theta(n^{p-1/2}).\label{lj:eq:large-moments}
\end{align}
The constants in the first and third lines may depend on $p$.
In particular,
\begin{equation}
 \ljE M_n=n-\Theta(\sqrt n),\qquad
 \operatorname{Var}(D_n)=\Theta(n^{3/2}).
 \label{lj:eq:mean-variance}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\subsection{What is and is not resolved}
The results determine the entire fixed-deficit boundary law, give a
uniform quantitative approximation to it, and establish a growing-deficit
regime. They do not determine the asymptotic equivalent of
$\ljE D_n$, nor the fraction $a_n^{(\lfloor\theta n\rfloor)}/C_n$ to
relative precision for fixed $0<\theta<1$. The additive error in
\eqref{lj:eq:uniform-joint} has the same order as its displayed term when
$d$ is proportional to $n$; it must not be advertised as an equivalent
there. The component-ordering question in the fixed-$m$ finite-state
system is also untouched.

\section{Catalan decompositions and endpoint identities}
\label{lj:sec:preliminaries}

\subsection{Decomposition at the maximum}
If $\pi=L\,n\,R$ avoids $132$, every value of $L$ is greater than every
value of $R$. Conversely, two avoiding standardized blocks with this
separation give an avoiding permutation. If $|R|=b$, the left block is
obtained by adding $b$ to each entry of an avoider of size $n-b-1$.
This proves
\[
 C_n=\sum_{b=0}^{n-1}C_bC_{n-b-1},\qquad
 \Cat(x)=1+x\Cat(x)^2.
\]
All the reconstruction operations used below are instances of this
bijection; no approximate counting is involved. (The decomposition is the
one of Part~I, \cref{sec:endpoints}, where the separation property is also
proved; manuscript~02 re-derives it independently.)

\subsection{First entries and first deficiencies}
For a nonempty avoider $\pi$, let $F(\pi)=\pi_1$. Define
\[
 P(x,z)=\sum_{n\ge1}\ \sum_{\pi\in\Av_n(132)}x^nz^{F(\pi)}.
\]
When the left block at the maximum is empty, the first value is the
maximum. When it is nonempty, its first value is shifted by the size of
the right block. Hence
\[
 P(x,z)=xz\Cat(xz)+x\Cat(xz)P(x,z),
\]
and therefore
\begin{equation}
 P(x,z)=\frac{xz\Cat(xz)}{1-x\Cat(xz)}.
 \label{lj:eq:first-gf}
\end{equation}
Replacing $x$ by $xu$ and $z$ by $u^{-1}$ gives the useful deficiency form
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{n\ge1}\sum_{\pi\in\Av_n(132)}x^nu^{n-F(\pi)}
 =\frac{x\Cat(x)}{1-ux\Cat(x)}.
 \label{lj:eq:deficiency-gf}
\end{equation}
In particular, for every $j\ge0$,
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{n\ge j+1}
 \#\{\pi\in\Av_n(132):n-F(\pi)=j\}\,x^n
 =(x\Cat(x))^{j+1}.
 \label{lj:eq:deficiency-fixed}
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}[First-entry count]\label{lj:lem:first-count}
For $1\le f\le N$,
\begin{equation}
 N_{N,f}:=\#\{\pi\in\Av_N(132):F(\pi)=f\}
 =\frac{N-f+1}{N}\binom{N+f-2}{N-1}.
 \label{lj:eq:first-count}
\end{equation}
Moreover, for $1\le t\le N$,
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\#\{\pi\in\Av_N(132):F(\pi)\le t\}}{C_N}
 \le t(N+1)(2N+1)2^{t-N-2}.
 \label{lj:eq:first-small}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The coefficient formula
\[
 [x^a]\Cat(x)^b=\frac{b}{2a+b}\binom{2a+b}{a}
\]
follows by applying Lagrange inversion to $\Cat=1+x\Cat^2$.
Apply it to \eqref{lj:eq:deficiency-fixed}, with $j=N-f$, to obtain
\eqref{lj:eq:first-count}; the two binomial forms are equal by cancellation
of factorials. For the bound, discard the prefactor, use
$\binom uv\le2^u$, and sum over $f\le t$:
\[
 \sum_{f\le t}N_{N,f}\le t2^{N+t-2}.
\]
Since the largest binomial coefficient in the expansion of $(1+1)^{2N}$
is central, $\binom{2N}{N}\ge4^N/(2N+1)$. Dividing proves the claim.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Merge note: the same count in Parts~I and~II]
\label{lj:rem:first-entry}
Deleting the final maximum of a block of length $k$ that ends at its
maximum and has first entry $k-j$ leaves an avoider of size $N=k-1$ with
first entry $f=N+1-j$. Hence $N_{N,f}=h(N+1,N+1-f)$, and
\eqref{lj:eq:first-count} is Part~I's formula \eqref{eq:h} for $h(k,j)$,
reindexed. Part~I proves it by a slot decomposition at the first entry
(\cref{lem:firstentry}), manuscript~02 by the maximum decomposition and the
bivariate series \eqref{lj:eq:first-gf}; both finish with Lagrange
inversion. The manuscript's route is kept here, because
\eqref{lj:eq:first-gf} and \eqref{lj:eq:deficiency-fixed} are used again in
\cref{lj:sec:pgf}.

The fraction bounded in \eqref{lj:eq:first-small} is also bounded by
Part~II's uniform tail estimate. With $k=N+1$ and $d=N-t\ge1$, the
difference $C_N-c_{N+1,N-t}$ counts exactly the avoiders of size $N$ with
first entry at most $t$, so \eqref{crem:eq:uniform-tail} gives
\[
 \frac{\#\{\pi\in\Av_N(132):F(\pi)\le t\}}{C_N}\le(N-t+2)\,2^{-(N-t)}
 \qquad(1\le t\le N-1).
\]
This is sharper than \eqref{lj:eq:first-small} by a factor of order $N^3$.
In the setting of \cref{lj:lem:exception}, $N-t=n-2t\ge n/2$, and
$x\mapsto(x+2)2^{-x}$ decreases for $x\ge0$, so the exceptional fraction is
at most $(n/2+2)2^{-n/2}$, which is below $1$ for every $n\ge8$; the term
$n(n+1)(2n+1)2^{2h-n}$ in the proof of \cref{lj:cor:sharp-tv} can likewise
be replaced by $(n-2h+2)2^{2h-n}$. Neither replacement changes an order of
magnitude or any statement of Part~III, which prints the manuscript's
bounds. This remark is the merge's, not the manuscript's; the bound was
checked by exhaustive enumeration for $N\le8$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Last entries}
A particularly simple decomposition occurs at the last value $\ell$.
Every entry greater than $\ell$ must precede every entry smaller than
$\ell$: otherwise the smaller entry, the greater entry, and the final
$\ell$ form $132$. Thus, uniquely,
\begin{equation}
 \pi=(\tau+\ell)\,\sigma\,\ell,
 \quad |\tau|=n-\ell,\quad |\sigma|=\ell-1,
 \label{lj:eq:last-decomposition}
\end{equation}
where $\tau$ and $\sigma$ are arbitrary avoiders of those sizes.
The converse is verified directly, or by the maximum decomposition.
Consequently,
\begin{equation}
 \#\{\pi\in\Av_n(132):\pi_n=\ell\}=C_{n-\ell}C_{\ell-1}.
 \label{lj:eq:last-count}
\end{equation}
An additional consequence will control the clipping operation:
if the last value is not the maximum, then the first value is greater
than the last value. Indeed, $\tau$ in \eqref{lj:eq:last-decomposition}
is then nonempty.

\section{A finite absorbing decomposition}
\label{lj:sec:skeleton}

\subsection{Three operations with one unexpanded block}
We use a finite decorated word, called a \emph{skeleton}, whose outer
operations end at one terminal operation. The unexpanded block will later
be filled with a large avoiding permutation. There are three operation
types:
\begin{description}[style=nextline,leftmargin=0pt,labelwidth=0pt,labelsep=0pt]
\item[$R_\beta$: a large right block.]
Here $\beta$ is any avoider of size $b\ge0$. Applied to a core $\sigma$
of size $N$, it returns
$(\beta+N)\,(N+b+1)\,\sigma$. Its cost is $b+1$.
\item[$A$: append a maximum.]
Applied to a core of size $N$, it returns $\sigma\,(N+1)$.
Its cost is $1$.
\item[$T_\tau$: a terminal finite right block.]
Here $\tau$ is any nonempty avoider of size $b\ge1$. Applied to the hole
$\sigma$ of size $N$, it returns
$(\sigma+b)\,(N+b+1)\,\tau$. Its cost is $b+1$.
\end{description}
A skeleton is a word in $R_\beta$ and $A$, followed by exactly one
$T_\tau$. Operations act from the terminal outward. Its cost $t$ is the
sum of their costs, so inserting a hole of size $N$ gives a permutation
of size $N+t$. All resulting permutations avoid $132$.

For example, the word $A R_{(1)}T_{(2,1)}$ has cost $1+2+3=6$.
The terminal will initialize the pair $(2,1)$, $R_{(1)}$ changes it to
$(4,1)$, and $A$ changes it to $(1,\infty)$. Thus this skeleton produces
a maximal possible jump, once a sufficiently large hole with a sufficiently
large first value is inserted.

\subsection{A probability measure on skeletons}
Give each individually decorated operation of cost $c$ weight $4^{-c}$.
The total weights are
\begin{equation}
 \sum_\beta4^{-(|\beta|+1)}=\frac12,
 \qquad w(A)=\frac14,
 \qquad\sum_{\tau\ne\varnothing}4^{-(|\tau|+1)}=\frac14.
 \label{lj:eq:operation-masses}
\end{equation}
Thus one can repeatedly choose an operation using these weights, stopping
when a terminal is chosen. The probability of not stopping after $j$
choices is $(3/4)^j$. Every skeleton is finite almost surely, and a
specified decorated skeleton of cost $t$ has probability $4^{-t}$.

This is a probability measure on a countable set, not a heuristic
``typical giant block'' assertion. The finite comparison in the next two
sections will connect it to the uniform permutation measure.

\subsection{Exact cost distribution}
Let $b_t$ be the number of skeletons of cost $t$. Their ordinary generating
function is
\begin{equation}
 B(x)=\sum_{t\ge0}b_tx^t
 =\frac{x(\Cat(x)-1)}{1-x\Cat(x)-x}
 =(\Cat(x)-1)^2.
 \label{lj:eq:cost-gf}
\end{equation}
For the last equality, note that
$1-x\Cat(x)-x=\Cat(x)^{-2}$ and
$x\Cat(x)^2=\Cat(x)-1$.
For $t\ge1$,
\begin{equation}
 b_t=C_{t+1}-2C_t=\frac{2(t-1)}{t+2}C_t,
 \quad b_0=b_1=0.
 \label{lj:eq:b-t}
\end{equation}
If $T$ denotes the random cost, then
\begin{equation}
 \ljE z^T=B(z/4)
 =\left(\frac{1-\sqrt{1-z}}{1+\sqrt{1-z}}\right)^2.
 \label{lj:eq:cost-pgf}
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}[Exact cost tail]\label{lj:lem:cost-tail}
For every integer $h\ge0$,
\begin{equation}
 \ljP(T>h)=\frac{2(h+1)(2h+1)}{h+2}\frac{C_h}{4^h}
 \sim\frac4{\sqrt{\pi h}}.
 \label{lj:eq:cost-tail}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Put $c_j=C_j/4^j$. The Catalan ratio is
$c_{j+1}/c_j=(2j+1)/(2j+4)$, and induction gives
$\sum_{j=0}^hc_j=2-(2h+1)c_h$.
Using $b_t4^{-t}=4c_{t+1}-2c_t$ for $t\ge1$, with the constant-term
adjustment at $t=0$, yields
\[
 \sum_{t=0}^hb_t4^{-t}
 =1-\frac{2(h+1)(2h+1)}{h+2}c_h.
\]
Stirling's formula gives the equivalent.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The endpoint state and its transformations}
The state is a pair $(d,\ell)$, where $d$ is a positive integer and
$\ell\in\N_{>0}\cup\{\infty\}$. The symbol $\infty$ records a last
entry close to the high end of a large block; it is not an actual entry
of a finite permutation. Starting at the terminal, define
\begin{align}
 T_\tau &: \quad (d,\ell)=(F(\tau),\tau_{|\tau|}),
 \label{lj:eq:state-terminal}\\
 R_\beta &: \quad (d,\ell)\longmapsto(d+|\beta|+1,\ell),
 \label{lj:eq:state-right}\\
 A &: \quad (d,\ell)\longmapsto
       (\min\{d+1,\ell\},\infty),
 \label{lj:eq:state-append}
\end{align}
where $\min(d+1,\infty)=d+1$.
Let $(D,L)$ be the resulting pair on the probability space of skeletons.
It is finite in its first coordinate almost surely and obeys
\begin{equation}
 1\le D\le T-1.
 \label{lj:eq:D-cost}
\end{equation}
This defines the candidate law probabilistically before any generating
function is simplified; coefficient nonnegativity will therefore not
need to be guessed from the radical expression.

\section{The deterministic reconstruction lemma}
\label{lj:sec:reconstruction}

The state rules need proof: an endpoint heuristic would not justify a law
for the largest jump. The following statement is the central finite lemma.

\begin{lemma}[A large hole determines the extreme jump]
\label{lj:lem:reconstruction}
Fix a skeleton $\ljskel$ of cost $t$ and state $(d,\ell)$. Insert an avoider
$\sigma$ of size $N>t$ whose first entry satisfies $F(\sigma)>t$.
Let $\pi$ be the resulting permutation of size $n=N+t$. Then
\begin{equation}
 D(\pi)=d.
 \label{lj:eq:deterministic-deficit}
\end{equation}
If $\ell<\infty$, then $\pi_n=\ell$. If $\ell=\infty$, then
$\pi_n\ge N+1$. At every intermediate reconstruction stage, the first
entry is greater than $t$ and the largest jump is at least $N+1$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Proceed from the terminal outward. Suppose the terminal decoration has
size $b\ge1$, first entry $f$, and last entry $\ell$. The reconstructed
terminal block is
\[
 (\sigma+b)\,(N+b+1)\,\tau.
\]
The jump from its maximum to $f$ has size $N+b+1-f\ge N+1$.
The jumps internal to $\sigma$ are at most $N-1$; the jump into the
maximum from the left is at most $N$; and the jumps internal to $\tau$
are at most $b-1<N+1$. Thus that downward jump is largest, its deficit
is exactly $f$, and the last value is $\ell$. The first value is
$F(\sigma)+b>t$.

Maintain the following stronger invariants. If $c$ is the cost used so
far, the current length is $N+c$, its deficit $d_c$ satisfies
$d_c\le c-1$, its first entry exceeds $t$, and its last entry is either
the recorded finite value, at most $t$, or at least $N+1$ when the state
records $\infty$. The largest jump is then at least $N+1$.

For $R_\beta$, let $b=|\beta|$ and let the old length be $a$.
The old internal maximum jump has new deficit $d_c+b+1$.
The finite left block contributes only jumps of size at most $b$,
including its boundary with the new maximum. These cannot exceed the
old maximum jump. The jump from the new maximum to the old first value
is strictly less than $a+b+1-t$, whereas the old maximum is
\[
 a-d_c=(a+b+1)-(d_c+b+1)\ge(a+b+1)-t+1,
\]
because the new accumulated cost is at most $t$.
So the old internal maximum remains largest, giving
\eqref{lj:eq:state-right}. The last entry is unchanged. The new first
entry is either the new maximum or a value exceeding $a$, hence exceeds
$t$ since $a\ge N>t$.

For $A$, appending the new maximum changes the deficit of the old
largest jump from $d_c$ to $d_c+1$. The new boundary jump has deficit
equal to the old last value. When that value is finite, the new deficit
is precisely $\min(d_c+1,\ell)$. When it is recorded as $\infty$,
the actual value is at least $N+1>t$, while $d_c+1\le t-1$; the new
jump cannot win. The last value becomes the new maximum, at least
$N+1$, and the first value is unchanged. These are
\eqref{lj:eq:state-append} and all the invariants.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Disjoint reconstruction families]\label{lj:lem:injective}
Fix a total size $n$ and a cutoff $h<n/2$. Reconstructions from distinct
skeletons of cost $t\le h$, with holes of size $n-t$, are disjoint.
For each fixed skeleton, reconstruction is injective in the hole.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
At every maximum decomposition, the block containing the hole is larger
than its finite sibling: its size is at least $n-t>t$, while the sibling
has size at most $t$. Hence it is identifiable from the final
permutation. A large right block specifies an $R$ operation. A large
left block with empty right block specifies $A$. A large left block
with nonempty finite right block specifies the terminal $T$, where
parsing stops. Each finite decoration is recovered from the actual
sibling values by standardization. The remaining large left block at
that terminal recovers the hole. Thus the whole decorated word and the
hole are uniquely determined.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The first-entry hypothesis cannot simply be deleted. A very small first
entry in the hole can create an additional large downward jump at an
outer $R$ operation. The next section bounds precisely this exceptional
set. Likewise, using only the deficit and omitting the last-entry state
would lose the clipping at an append operation.
\end{remark}

\section{Counting domination and total variation}
\label{lj:sec:tv}

\subsection{Why each short skeleton has enough finite mass}
The normalized Catalan numbers $c_j=C_j/4^j$ decrease strictly, because
$c_{j+1}/c_j=(2j+1)/(2j+4)<1$. Consequently, whenever $0\le t<n$,
\begin{equation}
 \frac{C_{n-t}}{C_n}
 =4^{-t}\frac{c_{n-t}}{c_n}\ge4^{-t}.
 \label{lj:eq:domination}
\end{equation}
The left side is the probability mass of the reconstruction family of
one fixed skeleton of cost $t$ under the uniform size-$n$ measure,
before the first-entry restriction is imposed. The right side is
exactly that skeleton's probability under the limiting construction.
This one-sided comparison is stronger for our purpose than an
unquantified local convergence statement.

\begin{lemma}[Exceptional first entries]\label{lj:lem:exception}
For $n\ge8$, $t\le\lfloor n/4\rfloor$, and $N=n-t$, the fraction of
holes in $\Av_N(132)$ with first entry at most $t$ is at most
\begin{equation}
 \omega_n=n(n+1)(2n+1)2^{-n/2}.
 \label{lj:eq:epsilon}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Apply \eqref{lj:eq:first-small}. Since $t-N=2t-n\le-n/2$ and
$t\le n$, $N\le n$, its right side is at most the displayed bound.
The deliberately enlarged prefactor avoids unnecessary finite-size
casework. If it exceeds $1$, the assertion is still valid.
\end{proof}

\subsection{A common subprobability measure}
For probability measures on a countable space, a common subprobability
measure of mass $1-\delta$ implies total-variation distance at most
$\delta$. This follows immediately from
$\ljTV(\mu,\nu)=1-\sum_x\min(\mu\{x\},\nu\{x\})$.

\begin{proof}[Proof of the rate in \cref{lj:thm:main}]
Let $h=\lfloor n/4\rfloor$ and suppose first that $\omega_n\le1$.
For each skeleton $\ljskel$ of cost $t\le h$, the good reconstruction
family has probability at least
\[
 \frac{C_{n-t}}{C_n}(1-\omega_n)
 \ge4^{-t}(1-\omega_n).
\]
The families are disjoint by \cref{lj:lem:injective}. Every good member has
deficit $d(\ljskel)$ by \cref{lj:lem:reconstruction}. The limiting deficit
law also puts the skeleton mass $4^{-t}$ at that same value.
After summing over skeletons, even when multiple skeletons yield the
same deficit, the two deficit laws have a common subprobability measure
of mass at least
\[
 (1-\omega_n)\sum_{\ljcost(\ljskel)\le h}4^{-\ljcost(\ljskel)}
 =(1-\omega_n)\ljP(T\le h).
\]
It follows that
\begin{equation}
 \ljTV(\ljLaw(D_n),\ljLaw(D))\le\ljP(T>h)+\omega_n.
 \label{lj:eq:tv-common}
\end{equation}
If $\omega_n>1$, the same displayed upper bound is trivial.
Insert the exact tail from \cref{lj:lem:cost-tail} and combine with the
universal bound $\ljTV\le1$. Stirling's formula gives
\[
 \ljP(T>\lfloor n/4\rfloor)
 =\frac8{\sqrt{\pi n}}+O(n^{-3/2}),
\]
while $\omega_n$ is exponentially small up to its polynomial
factor. This proves the upper bound in \eqref{lj:eq:main-tv} and proves
\eqref{lj:eq:explicit-tv}; the lower bound is given in
\cref{lj:cor:sharp-tv}.
Since $D$ is a proper finite-valued random variable, the laws of $D_n$
are tight, and $M_n/n=1-D_n/n\to1$ in probability.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The order is sharp; the leading constant remains open]
The cutoff $n/4$ makes every inequality transparent. After deriving the
limiting tail, \cref{lj:cor:sharp-tv} will prove the matching order and improve
the upper constant by taking a cutoff approaching $n/2$. These arguments
do not identify an exact asymptotic constant for the total-variation
error.
\end{remark}

\section{Evaluation of the algebraic probability generating function}
\label{lj:sec:pgf}

\subsection{Renewal notation}
For $0\le z\le1$, write
\begin{equation}
 q=\ljQ(z)=\frac z4\Cat(z/4)
 =\frac{1-\sqrt{1-z}}2.
 \label{lj:eq:q}
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
 z=4q(1-q),\qquad
 \frac1{1-q}=\Cat(z/4),\qquad s=1-2q.
 \label{lj:eq:q-identities}
\end{equation}
An $R_\beta$ operation adds $|\beta|+1$ to the deficit and has
weight $4^{-(|\beta|+1)}$. Its deficit-increment generating function is
therefore $q$. A sequence of such operations has generating function
\begin{equation}
 \frac1{1-q}=\sum_{b\ge0}\frac{C_b}{4^b}z^b.
 \label{lj:eq:R-renewal}
\end{equation}
Here $b$ records the \emph{sum of increments}, not the number of
operations and not the size of a single decoration.

Define two subprobability generating functions
\begin{equation}
 H(z)=\ljE[z^D;L<\infty],\qquad
 J(z)=\ljE[z^D;L=\infty],\qquad G=H+J.
 \label{lj:eq:H-J}
\end{equation}
The last-entry state is finite exactly when there is no $A$ operation:
it starts finite at the terminal, $R$ preserves it, and $A$ changes it
permanently to $\infty$.

\subsection{The finite-last part}
The terminal first-entry series is
$\tfrac14P(\tfrac14,z)$. By \eqref{lj:eq:first-gf},
\[
 P(\tfrac14,z)=\frac{z}{1+2\sqrt{1-z}}=\frac{z}{3-4q}.
\]
Adding an arbitrary outer sequence of $R$ operations gives
\begin{equation}
 H(z)=\frac{P(\tfrac14,z)}{4(1-q)}
 =\frac{q}{3-4q}.
 \label{lj:eq:H}
\end{equation}
We also need the last-entry marginal, rather than its joint relation
with $D$. From \eqref{lj:eq:last-count},
\[
 \sum_{n\ge1}\sum_{\pi\in\Av_n(132)}x^nz^{\pi_n}
 =\Cat(x)\,xz\Cat(xz).
\]
At $x=1/4$ the terminal last-entry series is $q/2$. An arbitrary
sequence of $R$ operations has total weight
$1/(1-\ljQ(1))=2$. Therefore
\begin{equation}
 \ljE[z^L;L<\infty]=q.
 \label{lj:eq:last-marginal}
\end{equation}
In particular, $H(1)=J(1)=1/2$ and
$\ljP(L=\ell)=C_{\ell-1}/4^\ell$ for finite $\ell\ge1$.

\subsection{Why the append operation is not independent}
Let
\begin{equation}
 \ljK(z)=\ljE[z^{\min(D+1,L)};L<\infty].
 \label{lj:eq:K-definition}
\end{equation}
The outermost-operation decomposition gives
\[
 J(z)=qJ(z)+\frac z4J(z)+\frac14\ljK(z).
\]
The first term is an outer $R$, the second an outer $A$ applied to an
infinite-last state, and the third an outer $A$ applied to a finite-last
state. Hence
\begin{equation}
 J(z)=\frac{\ljK(z)}{4(1-q-z/4)}
 =\frac{\ljK(z)}{4(1-q)^2}.
 \label{lj:eq:J}
\end{equation}
The dependence between $D$ and $L$ is concentrated in $\ljK$.
Replacing $L$ by an independent variable with its marginal law would
be incorrect. The next calculation retains the full dependence.

\subsection{An exact clipping calculation}
If a terminal permutation $\tau$ does not end in its maximum, then
$F(\tau)>\tau_{|\tau|}$ by \eqref{lj:eq:last-decomposition}. Every
outer $R$ only increases the first state coordinate, so clipping returns
the terminal last value. The only correction to the marginal
\eqref{lj:eq:last-marginal} arises from terminal permutations ending in
their maximum.

Write such a non-singleton terminal as $\sigma(k+1)$, where
$|\sigma|=k\ge1$, $F(\sigma)=f$, and $j=k-f$. If the outer $R$ sequence
has total increment $b$, the state before clipping is $(b+f,k+1)$.
Clipping is strict exactly when $b<j$. The singleton terminal produces
no correction. Its non-singleton correction therefore has generating
function
\begin{align}
 \ljK(z)-q
 &=\frac z{16}\sum_{j\ge1}q^{j+1}
       \sum_{0\le b<j}\frac{C_b}{4^b}(z^{b-j}-1).
 \label{lj:eq:clipping-double}
\end{align}
To see the factor $1/16$, the terminal size is $k+1$, hence its weight
is $4^{-(k+2)}$. Formula \eqref{lj:eq:deficiency-fixed}, evaluated at
$x=z/4$, sums its possible $\sigma$ to $q^{j+1}$.
The factor $z$ records the final maximum; \eqref{lj:eq:R-renewal} supplies
$C_b/4^b$.

For $0<z<1$, the geometric sums converge absolutely; in particular
$q/z=1/(4(1-q))<1$. Interchanging the sums in
\eqref{lj:eq:clipping-double} gives
\begin{align}
 \ljK(z)-q
 &=\frac z{16}\sum_{b\ge0}\frac{C_b}{4^b}q^{b+2}
      \left(\frac1{z-q}-\frac1{1-q}\right)\notag\\
 &=\frac{z(1-z)q^2\Cat(q/4)}{16(z-q)(1-q)}\notag\\
 &=(1-z)\frac{q^2\Cat(q/4)}{4(3-4q)}.
 \label{lj:eq:clipping-sum}
\end{align}
Both sides extend analytically to $z=0$. As
$q\Cat(q/4)=2(1-\sqrt{1-q})$, define
\begin{equation}
 \ljU(z)=\frac{q(1-\sqrt{1-q})}{2(3-4q)}.
 \label{lj:eq:A}
\end{equation}
We have proved the particularly useful representation
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\ljK(z)=q+(1-z)\ljU(z).}
 \label{lj:eq:K}
\end{equation}
The series $\ljU$ also has the direct tail interpretation
\begin{equation}
 [z^k]\ljU(z)=\ljP(L>k,\ D\le k-1,\ L<\infty).
 \label{lj:eq:A-probability}
\end{equation}
Indeed, for finite $L$, the tail of $\min(D+1,L)$ at $k$ differs
from the tail of $L$ precisely on this event; use the standard
probability-generating-function tail identity.

\subsection{Completion of the formula}
Combining \eqref{lj:eq:H}, \eqref{lj:eq:J}, and \eqref{lj:eq:K},
\begin{equation}
 G(z)=\frac{q}{3-4q}
 +\frac{q+(1-z)\,q(1-\sqrt{1-q})/(2(3-4q))}
       {4(1-q)^2}.
 \label{lj:eq:pgf-q}
\end{equation}
Substitute $q=(1-s)/2$ and $\sqrt{1-q}=r$. The result is
\eqref{lj:eq:main-pgf}, proving the generating-function assertion of
\cref{lj:thm:main}. This derivation also verifies $G(0)=0$ and $G(1)=1$.
The probabilistic construction has already proved that its coefficients
are nonnegative and sum to one.

\begin{remark}[Algebraicity, but not a finite constant-coefficient recurrence]
The expression lies in the field obtained from $\mathbb Q(z)$ by adjoining
$s$ and then $r$, so its algebraic degree is at most four. It is not
rational: the nonzero square-root term at $z=1$, computed next, excludes
rationality. Thus the limiting probability sequence does not satisfy an
eventual constant-coefficient linear recurrence. This is compatible with
rationality of each fixed-$m$ generating function $A_m(x)$: it concerns a
different sequence and a different limit.
\end{remark}

\section{Singularity analysis and the heavy tail}
\label{lj:sec:singularity}

\subsection{The dominant singularity is genuinely unique}
The square-root branches in \eqref{lj:eq:main-pgf} extend to
$\mathbb C\setminus[1,\infty)$. On this domain the principal value of
$s=\sqrt{1-z}$ has positive real part. Consequently $1+s$ and $1+2s$
do not vanish, and $(1+s)/2$ never meets the negative real axis or zero.
Thus $r$ is analytic there as well. In particular, $G$ is analytic in a
slit neighborhood of the closed unit disk except at $z=1$.
There are no competing unit-circle singularities or periodicity factors
hidden in the coefficient asymptotics.

Expansion of the explicit formula at $s=0$ gives
\begin{align}
 G(z)={}&1-3s
 +\left(\frac{23}{4}-\frac{\sqrt2}{8}\right)s^2
 +\left(-\frac{43}{4}+\frac{9\sqrt2}{16}\right)s^3\notag\\
 &+\left(\frac{81}{4}-\frac{99\sqrt2}{64}\right)s^4
 +\left(-\frac{155}{4}+\frac{461\sqrt2}{128}\right)s^5
 +O(s^6).
 \label{lj:eq:singular-expansion}
\end{align}
This calculation uses only
$r=2^{-1/2}(1+s)^{1/2}$ and ordinary Taylor expansion. An optional
symbolic checker in the package
(\path{code/03-largest-jump-verify_symbolic.py}) verifies the displayed
coefficients.
The term $-3\sqrt{1-z}$ is the source of both the infinite mean of $D$
and the critical exponent $1/2$.

\subsection{Coefficient extraction, including the second term}
For a fixed nonintegral $\gamma$, the exact generalized binomial formula
and the asymptotic expansion of the gamma quotient give
\begin{equation}
 [z^k](1-z)^\gamma
 =\frac{k^{-\gamma-1}}{\Gamma(-\gamma)}
 \left(1+\frac{\gamma(\gamma+1)}{2k}+O(k^{-2})\right).
 \label{lj:eq:transfer-basic}
\end{equation}
Analytic continuation in the slit neighborhood just established permits
termwise singularity transfer; this is the standard univariate transfer
principle of \cite{FlajoletOdlyzko}. Integer powers of $1-z$ are locally
analytic terms and do not contribute the displayed algebraic orders.
For completeness, the nonanalytic terms needed for the first two orders
are $(1-z)^{1/2}$ and $(1-z)^{3/2}$, whose leading coefficient constants
are respectively $-1/(2\sqrt\pi)$ and $3/(4\sqrt\pi)$.

\begin{proposition}[Two-term probability and survival expansions]
\label{lj:prop:two-term}
As $k\to\infty$,
\begin{align}
 \ljP(D=k)
 &=\frac{3}{2\sqrt\pi}\,k^{-3/2}
   +\frac{-480+27\sqrt2}{64\sqrt\pi}\,k^{-5/2}
   +O(k^{-7/2}),\label{lj:eq:pmf-two}\\
 \ljP(D>k)
 &=\frac{3}{\sqrt\pi}\,k^{-1/2}
   +\frac{-184+9\sqrt2}{32\sqrt\pi}\,k^{-3/2}
   +O(k^{-5/2}).\label{lj:eq:tail-two}
\end{align}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For the point probabilities, apply \eqref{lj:eq:transfer-basic} to the $s$
and $s^3$ terms of \eqref{lj:eq:singular-expansion}. The coefficient of
$k^{-5/2}$ is
\[
 \frac{9}{16\sqrt\pi}
 +\frac3{4\sqrt\pi}
    \left(-\frac{43}{4}+\frac{9\sqrt2}{16}\right)
 =\frac{-480+27\sqrt2}{64\sqrt\pi}.
\]
For the survival probabilities use the exact identity
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{k\ge0}\ljP(D>k)z^k=\frac{1-G(z)}{1-z}.
 \label{lj:eq:tail-gf}
\end{equation}
Writing $a_2=23/4-\sqrt2/8$ and
$a_3=-43/4+9\sqrt2/16$, its expansion begins
$3(1-z)^{-1/2}-a_2-a_3(1-z)^{1/2}+O(1-z)$.
The second coefficient is
\[
 -\frac3{8\sqrt\pi}+\frac{a_3}{2\sqrt\pi}
 =\frac{-184+9\sqrt2}{32\sqrt\pi}.
\]
Continuing the expansion two half-orders further gives the stated
remainders. The analyticity argument above supplies the hypotheses of
singularity transfer throughout.
\end{proof}


\begin{corollary}[Sharp total-variation order]
\label{lj:cor:sharp-tv}
The error in the boundary law has order exactly $n^{-1/2}$. More precisely,
\begin{equation}
 \frac3{\sqrt\pi}
 \le \liminf_{n\to\infty}\sqrt n\,
       \ljTV(\ljLaw(D_n),\ljLaw(D))
 \le \limsup_{n\to\infty}\sqrt n\,
       \ljTV(\ljLaw(D_n),\ljLaw(D))
 \le \frac{4\sqrt2}{\sqrt\pi}.
 \label{lj:eq:tv-constants}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Because $D_n\le n-1$, the event $\{n,n+1,\ldots\}$ gives
\[
 \ljTV(\ljLaw(D_n),\ljLaw(D))\ge\ljP(D\ge n)
 =\frac3{\sqrt{\pi n}}+O(n^{-3/2}).
\]
For the improved upper bound, repeat the common-submeasure proof with
any integer $2\le h<n/2$. The first-entry estimate gives
\[
 \ljTV(\ljLaw(D_n),\ljLaw(D))
 \le\ljP(T>h)+n(n+1)(2n+1)2^{2h-n}.
\]
For all sufficiently large $n$, choose
$h=\lfloor n/2-2\log_2 n\rfloor$. The second term is $O(n^{-1})$,
while the exact cost tail is
$(4\sqrt2/\sqrt\pi)n^{-1/2}(1+o(1))$.
This proves the three inequalities and completes the sharp-rate claim
in \cref{lj:thm:main}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Quantiles and limiting moments]
\label{lj:cor:quantiles}
The variable $D$ has finite moment of order $p>0$ exactly when $p<1/2$.
If $\kappa_{1-\epsilon}=\min\{k:\ljP(D\le k)\ge1-\epsilon\}$, then
\begin{equation}
 \kappa_{1-\epsilon}\sim\frac9{\pi\epsilon^2}
 \qquad(\epsilon\downarrow0).
 \label{lj:eq:quantiles}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The moment criterion follows by summing
$k^p\ljP(D=k)\sim(3/(2\sqrt\pi))k^{p-3/2}$.
For the quantile statement, bracket the survival function between
$(1\pm\eta)3/\sqrt{\pi k}$ for all sufficiently large $k$, invert these
inequalities, and then let $\eta\downarrow0$. Rounding the resulting
bounds to integers has no effect on the equivalent.
\end{proof}

\section{Joint limits for the adjacency bound}
\label{lj:sec:joint}

\subsection{A fixed distance from the maximal possible jump}
For every fixed integer $d\ge1$, \eqref{lj:eq:bridge} and total-variation
convergence give
\begin{equation}
 \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a_n^{(n-d)}}{C_n}
 =\ljP(D\ge d)
 =1-\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}[z^k]G(z).
 \label{lj:eq:fixed-window}
\end{equation}
Thus the limiting profile across the boundary consists of explicit
rational numbers. At $d=1$ the fraction is identically one. At $d=2$,
its limit is $41/48$: asymptotically $7/48$ of all avoiders contain a
jump of the largest possible size, namely $n-1$.

This is not a smooth limiting distribution for $M_n/n$. That normalized
statistic tends to the constant $1$. The nontrivial distribution is
visible only after retaining the integer distance $n-M_n$.

\subsection{A uniform estimate and its domain of relative validity}
\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{lj:thm:tail-main}]
The point and survival estimates follow from \cref{lj:prop:two-term}.
For $2\le d\le n-1$, total variation gives
\[
 \left|\frac{a_n^{(n-d)}}{C_n}-\ljP(D\ge d)\right|
 \le C_{\mathrm{TV}} n^{-1/2}
\]
for an absolute constant $C_{\mathrm{TV}}$. Also
\[
 \ljP(D\ge d)=\ljP(D>d-1)
 =\frac3{\sqrt{\pi d}}+O(d^{-3/2}).
\]
Combining these inequalities proves \eqref{lj:eq:uniform-joint}.
Dividing the error by the main term gives
\begin{equation}
 \frac{a_n^{(n-d)}}{C_n}
 =\frac3{\sqrt{\pi d}}
   \left(1+O\left(d^{-1}+\sqrt{d/n}\right)\right).
 \label{lj:eq:relative-joint}
\end{equation}
When $d\to\infty$ and $d=o(n)$ the relative error tends to zero.
Finally $C_n\sim4^n/(\sqrt\pi\,n^{3/2})$ gives the second equivalent
in \eqref{lj:eq:joint-equivalent}.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Polynomial and logarithmic boundary windows]
For any fixed $0<\alpha<1$ and $d_n=\lfloor n^\alpha\rfloor$,
\[
 \frac{a_n^{(n-d_n)}}{C_n}
 \sim\frac3{\sqrt\pi}\,n^{-\alpha/2}.
\]
Taking $d_n=\lfloor\log n\rfloor$ gives the equivalent
$3/\sqrt{\pi\log n}$. More generally, the same formula with
$d_n$ in place of these choices holds along every unbounded sublinear
integer sequence. Neither monotonicity nor regular variation of $d_n$
is required.
\end{example}

\begin{corollary}[All diverging boundary deficits are negligible]
\label{lj:cor:diverging}
If $d_n\to\infty$ and $1\le d_n\le n-1$, then
\[
 a_n^{(n-d_n)}/C_n\longrightarrow0.
\]
For every fixed $0<\theta<1$,
\[
 a_n^{(\lfloor\theta n\rfloor)}/C_n=O(n^{-1/2}).
\]
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The first assertion follows either from tightness of $D_n$ or directly
from the absolute estimate. In the second, $d_n=n-\lfloor\theta n\rfloor$
is of order $n$, and both terms of \eqref{lj:eq:uniform-joint} are
$O(n^{-1/2})$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The unresolved bulk regime]\label{lj:rem:bulk}
When $d$ is a fixed positive fraction of $n$, the total-variation error
is of the same order as the proposed leading term in
\eqref{lj:eq:uniform-joint}. The equation is still a valid absolute estimate,
but it does \emph{not} prove a relative equivalent there. In particular,
Part~III does not assert
$a_n^{(\lfloor\theta n\rfloor)}/C_n\sim
3/\sqrt{\pi(1-\theta)n}$. An additional finite-size scaling analysis would
be needed, and it could yield a different function of $\theta$.
\end{remark}

\section{The moment transition and its consequences}
\label{lj:sec:moments}

\subsection{Truncation converts total variation into moment bounds}
For two probability measures $\mu,\nu$ and a measurable function with
$0\le f\le B$, the layer-cake representation gives
\begin{equation}
 \left|\int f\,\dd\mu-\int f\,\dd\nu\right|
 \le B\,\ljTV(\mu,\nu).
 \label{lj:eq:bounded-test}
\end{equation}
Apply this to $f(x)=\min(x,n)^p$. Since $D_n<n$,
\begin{equation}
 \left|\ljE D_n^p-\ljE\min(D,n)^p\right|
 =O(n^{p-1/2}).
 \label{lj:eq:moment-comparison}
\end{equation}
The finite support of $D_n$ matters: total-variation convergence without
a rate or a truncation bound would not control these unbounded moments.

For any positive integer-valued $X$, summation by parts gives
\begin{equation}
 \ljE\min(X,n)^p
 =\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\bigl((k+1)^p-k^p\bigr)\ljP(X>k).
 \label{lj:eq:layer-cake}
\end{equation}
Using \eqref{lj:eq:tail-two} and
$(k+1)^p-k^p=pk^{p-1}+O(k^{p-2})$ for $k\ge1$,
we obtain all three moment regimes.

\subsection{Subcritical and critical moments}
\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{lj:thm:moments-main}, for $p\le1/2$]
If $0<p<1/2$, the tail of the sum \eqref{lj:eq:layer-cake} is
$O(n^{p-1/2})$, and therefore
\[
 \ljE\min(D,n)^p=\ljE D^p+O(n^{p-1/2}).
\]
Equation \eqref{lj:eq:moment-comparison} proves
\eqref{lj:eq:small-moments}.

If $p=1/2$, the summand in \eqref{lj:eq:layer-cake} is
\[
 \bigl(\sqrt{k+1}-\sqrt k\bigr)\ljP(D>k)
 =\frac3{2\sqrt\pi}\frac1k+O(k^{-2}).
\]
Consequently
\[
 \ljE\min(D,n)^{1/2}
 =\frac3{2\sqrt\pi}\log n+O(1).
\]
The error in \eqref{lj:eq:moment-comparison} is now $O(1)$, proving the
critical statement.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Supercritical moments: the order, not the constant}
\begin{proof}[Completion of the proof of \cref{lj:thm:moments-main}]
Let $p>1/2$. The sum in \eqref{lj:eq:layer-cake} is
$O(n^{p-1/2})$, so \eqref{lj:eq:moment-comparison} gives the upper bound.
For the lower bound choose a constant $C_{\mathrm{TV}}$ such that
$\ljTV(\ljLaw(D_n),\ljLaw(D))\le C_{\mathrm{TV}}n^{-1/2}$ for all sufficiently large $n$.
Fix a sufficiently small $\epsilon>0$ such that
\[
 \frac3{2\sqrt{\pi\epsilon}}>C_{\mathrm{TV}}+1.
\]
Set $d=\lfloor\epsilon n\rfloor$. The limiting tail estimate implies,
for all sufficiently large $n$,
\[
 \ljP(D\ge d)\ge
 \frac3{2\sqrt{\pi\epsilon}}n^{-1/2}.
\]
Hence $\ljP(D_n\ge d)\ge n^{-1/2}$, and
\[
 \ljE D_n^p\ge d^p\ljP(D_n\ge d)
 \ge \underline c_p n^{p-1/2}
\]
for some $\underline c_p>0$. This proves the claimed two-sided order.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Mean and variance of the largest-jump deficit]
\label{lj:cor:mean-var}
As $n\to\infty$,
\begin{equation}
 \ljE D_n=\Theta(\sqrt n),\qquad
 \ljE M_n=n-\Theta(\sqrt n),\qquad
 \operatorname{Var}(D_n)=\Theta(n^{3/2}).
 \label{lj:eq:mean-variance-cor}
\end{equation}
The same variance identity holds for $M_n$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Use \cref{lj:thm:moments-main} with $p=1,2$. The squared mean is $O(n)$,
whereas the second moment is bounded above and below by positive
constants times $n^{3/2}$. Subtracting the squared mean preserves this
order. Finally $M_n=n-D_n$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Why a bounded typical deficit and an unbounded mean coexist]
The limit has median $9$, but an infinite mean. Rare permutations with
much larger deficits carry an increasing fraction of the finite-size
mean. There is therefore no contradiction between $D_n=O_{\ljP}(1)$ and
$\ljE D_n=\Theta(\sqrt n)$. Nor can the constant in the mean be read off
from the tail constant $3/\sqrt\pi$: the range $D_n$ of order $n$ can
contribute to the mean at the same order, and that bulk range has not been
identified asymptotically in Part~III.
\end{remark}

\subsection{An independent exact check of the first mass}
\begin{proposition}[The largest possible jump]
\label{lj:prop:exact-extreme}
For $n\ge3$,
\begin{equation}
 \#\{\pi\in\Av_n(132):M(\pi)=n-1\}
 =C_{n-2}+\sum_{j=0}^{n-2}C_j.
 \label{lj:eq:extreme-exact}
\end{equation}
Consequently $\ljP(D_n=1)\to7/48$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
A jump of size $n-1$ is exactly an adjacency of the values $1$ and $n$.
If it is the ascent $1,n$, this pair must be at the end: any later entry
would form $132$ with the pair. The preceding entries, standardized,
are an arbitrary avoider of length $n-2$, giving $C_{n-2}$ choices.

If it is the descent $n,1$, decompose at the maximum. The right block
starts with $1$ and must be increasing, since a later descent together
with that first $1$ would form $132$. For each right-block length
$r\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}$, the left block is an arbitrary avoider of
length $n-r-1$. This gives the sum in \eqref{lj:eq:extreme-exact}.
The two cases are disjoint.

Finally $C_{n-2}/C_n\to1/16$. Also
$\sum_{j=0}^{n-2}C_j\sim(4/3)C_{n-2}$, as follows either by reversing
this sum and using the Catalan ratio, or by Stolz--Ces\`aro applied to
$\sum_{j=0}^N C_j$ and $C_N$. Thus the second term divided by $C_n$
tends to $1/12$. Their sum is $7/48$.
\end{proof}

\section{Further research questions}
\label{lj:sec:questions}
The results above settle the bounded and unbounded sublinear deficit
windows. They also reveal several questions whose answers would extend
the theory rather than merely recompute its constants.

\subsection{Bulk finite-size scaling}
For $0<u<1$, determine whether the limit
\begin{equation}
 \Psi(u)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sqrt n\,\ljP(D_n>\lfloor un\rfloor)
 \label{lj:eq:bulk-question}
\end{equation}
exists, and if so compute it, including its endpoint behavior.
This is a proposed problem, not a theorem or an asserted formula.
Part~III proves an upper bound on the expression, and a positive
lower bound for sufficiently small fixed $u$. It does not establish a
limit. A likely starting point is to retain the finite Catalan ratio
$C_{n-t}/C_n$ instead of replacing it by $4^{-t}$, and to analyze the
regime where both sides at a maximum decomposition are macroscopic.

\subsection{Exact constants for the mean and higher moments}
Determine whether
$n^{1/2-p}\ljE D_n^p$ converges for each $p>1/2$, especially whether
$\ljE D_n/\sqrt n$ has a limit. A bulk profile with sufficient uniform
integrability would make these constants integrals of that profile.
At the critical point, ask whether
\[
 \ljE\sqrt{D_n}-\frac3{2\sqrt\pi}\log n
\]
converges. The $O(1)$ result proved here does not imply convergence of
the centered quantity.

\subsection{The sharp total-variation constant and local corrections}
The order $n^{-1/2}$ is now proved sharp, but the interval in
\eqref{lj:eq:tv-constants} leaves the leading constant open. Does
$\sqrt n\,\ljTV(\ljLaw(D_n),\ljLaw(D))$ converge? Is the missing limiting
mass beyond the finite support the whole leading discrepancy, or does
an additional contribution arise inside the support?
Separately, for each fixed $k$, determine the first correction to
$\ljP(D_n=k)$. A pointwise expansion can be much sharper than the global
total-variation bound and would give effective finite-size estimates
for the first several adjacency thresholds.

\subsection{Ascending and descending extreme jumps jointly}
Define the largest ascent and largest descent by
\[
 M_n^+=\max_i(\Pi_n(i+1)-\Pi_n(i))_+,
 \qquad
 M_n^-=\max_i(\Pi_n(i)-\Pi_n(i+1))_+.
\]
Determine their joint limiting behavior, allowing the possibility that
one coordinate requires an extended state space or a different scale.
The current clipping step mixes an old extreme jump with a newly created
ascent, so its explicit dependence on the terminal endpoint should
provide useful information. Independence must not be assumed.

\subsection{Near-maximal jumps as a point process}
Instead of retaining only the minimum deficit, retain the multiset
\[
 \left\{n-|\Pi_n(i+1)-\Pi_n(i)|:1\le i<n\right\}
\]
near the finite end of the positive integers. Determine its joint count
law on every fixed finite set, including ties for the maximum jump.
A decorated skeleton records where a finite number of new extreme
candidates are created. It may therefore support a marked extension of
the present generating-function calculation. A proof must also control
unrecorded jumps inside the large hole.

\subsection{Weighted Catalan models}
Replace the uniform measure by weights tracking descents, leaves of the
associated binary tree, or another statistic with an explicit bivariate
Catalan generating function. Determine when an absorbing finite
skeleton still exists, whether the moment threshold remains $1/2$, and
which critical parameter values change the tail exponent. Both the
operation probabilities and the endpoint clipping law would have to be
recomputed; a formal substitution into \eqref{lj:eq:main-pgf} is not justified.

\subsection{Other avoidance classes}
Develop an analogous boundary analysis for other pattern classes, with
particular attention to classes whose recursive specifications have
several large-block types. Before treating a new pattern as a distinct
case, account for the reversal and complement symmetries that preserve
absolute adjacent differences. The difficult extension is not a mere
renaming of a symmetry-equivalent class, but a new decomposition in which
the competition between large adjacent jumps has a different structure.

\subsection{An exact sampler with controlled work}
The skeleton probability space is an exact description of the limit, but
its cost has infinite expectation by \eqref{lj:eq:cost-tail}. Sampling full
finite decorations literally can therefore be expensive on rare runs.
Design an exact sampler for $D$ that manipulates only the endpoint
information and clipping events it needs. Determine its expected bit
complexity, and compare a state-based sampler with inversion of the
rational coefficient sequence. Almost-sure termination alone is not a
finite expected-cost theorem.

\subsection{A formalization boundary}
A feasible first formal target is the deterministic reconstruction lemma:
define $132$-avoidance, the maximum decomposition, decorated skeletons,
and the endpoint state, then prove reconstruction and injectivity by
induction. Next formalize the first-entry formula and the common-submeasure
inequality. These steps isolate the combinatorial content from the later
analytic transfer theorem. A complete formalization would still need
countable probability, formal power series or analytic identities, and
asymptotic coefficient transfer; the present package does not claim to
supply them.

\subsection{Reconnect to the fixed-bound spectral questions}
\label{lj:sec:q-reconnect}
The boundary law does not decide which of the two irreducible endpoint
components controls the fixed-$m$ growth rate, nor the optimal defect
between that rate and the Catalan scalar comparison rate discussed in
Parts~I and~II (\cref{sec:remaining,crem:sec:future}). Study whether a two-parameter endpoint system can interpolate
between the fixed-$m$ spectral regime and the boundary regime here.
Any proposed interpolation must specify its uniform error before taking
a moving-bound limit; fixed-parameter rationality alone is insufficient.

\subsection{What is resolved}
The adjacency-bounded enumeration has a nontrivial joint-limit boundary
at distance $O(1)$ below $n$. Part~III identifies the entire limiting
profile there, gives a sharp-order convergence rate, and extends it
uniformly to every growing sublinear deficit window. The algebraic law
has a heavy enough tail to force a moment transition, so typical and
average deficits live on different scales. These conclusions answer a
specific missing regime in the repository's program while leaving its
fixed-bound spectral problems and the bulk finite-size profile separate.

The reusable mechanism is the common-mass comparison: a finite stopping
skeleton of cost $t$ has limiting weight $4^{-t}$, while the corresponding
finite Catalan family has at least that weight. Once exceptional first
entries are removed, the extreme statistic agrees exactly. This finite
argument simultaneously supplies convergence, its quantitative rate, and
a controlled bridge from limiting probabilities to moving-bound counts.

\section{Exact coefficient recursions}
\label{lj:app:recursions}
This section and the next two were the manuscript's Appendices~A--C. They
are numbered here as sections of Part~III, so that Part~I's appendices keep
their letters. The package computes all coefficients with rational arithmetic. To degree
$N$, begin with
\[
 q(z)=\sum_{k=1}^N\frac{C_{k-1}}{4^k}z^k+O(z^{N+1}).
\]
For a series $a(z)=1+\sum_{k\ge1}a_kz^k$, the square root
$b(z)=1+\sum_{k\ge1}b_kz^k$ is obtained from
\begin{equation}
 b_k=\frac12\left(a_k-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}b_jb_{k-j}\right).
 \label{lj:eq:sqrt-recursion}
\end{equation}
For a series $a$ with nonzero constant term, its inverse is obtained from
\begin{equation}
 b_0=a_0^{-1},\qquad
 b_k=-a_0^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^k a_jb_{k-j}.
 \label{lj:eq:inverse-recursion}
\end{equation}
Use these recursions to compute $r=\sqrt{1-q}$,
$H=q/(3-4q)$,
$\ljU=q(1-r)/(2(3-4q))$, and
$J=(q+(1-z)\ljU)/(4(1-q)^2)$. Their sum is $G$.
Only the first $N$ coefficients of each input are needed. Ordinary
convolution gives $O(N^2)$ rational arithmetic operations; this is not a
bit-complexity bound, because numerators and denominators grow.

The independent clipping computation uses instead the endpoint counts
\eqref{lj:eq:first-count} and their tails. Writing
$P(z)=P(1/4,z)=z/(1+2\sqrt{1-z})$, it extracts coefficients of $P$
without the formula for $\ljU$, subtracts finite first-entry counts,
and sums over the possible finite $R$-increments. Agreement is therefore
not merely two evaluations of the same line of code.

\section{An algebraic certificate without branch ambiguity}
\label{lj:app:algebra}
One may describe $G$ without nested radical notation by introducing
$q$ and $r$ and imposing
\begin{align}
 4q^2-4q+z&=0, & q(0)&=0,\label{lj:eq:q-algebraic}\\
 r^2-(1-q)&=0, & r(0)&=1.\label{lj:eq:r-algebraic}
\end{align}
In addition,
\begin{equation}
 8(3-4q)(1-q)^2G
 =q\bigl(15-24q+8q^2-z-(1-z)r\bigr).
 \label{lj:eq:G-certificate}
\end{equation}
Equations \eqref{lj:eq:q-algebraic}--\eqref{lj:eq:G-certificate}, with the stated
initial values, select the same analytic series as \eqref{lj:eq:main-pgf}.
Eliminating $r$ first gives
\begin{align}
 &\bigl[q(15-24q+8q^2-z)
       -8(3-4q)(1-q)^2G\bigr]^2\notag\\
 &\hspace{2cm}-q^2(1-z)^2(1-q)=0.
 \label{lj:eq:eliminate-r}
\end{align}
The resultant of \eqref{lj:eq:eliminate-r} and $4q^2-4q+z$ eliminates $q$.
Squaring can add branches, so the conditions at zero must be retained;
an arbitrary root of the eliminated polynomial is not automatically the
probability generating function. Part~III claims degree at most four,
not an independently proved irreducibility statement for a minimal
polynomial.

\section{Exact small-bound algebra}
\label{app:algebra}
For $m=3$, the certified generating function is $A_3(x)=P_3(x)/Q_3(x)$, where
\begin{align*}
 P_3(x)={}&1-x-x^2+x^3+4x^4-4x^6-3x^7-3x^8\\
          &-5x^9-3x^{10}+2x^{11}+2x^{12},\\
 Q_3(x)={}&(x-1)(x+1)

          &\hspace{12mm}\cdot(x^6+x^5+x^4+2x^3+x^2-1).
\end{align*}

 Q_4(x)={}&(x-1)\,D_U(x)\,D_V(x),\\

          &+4x^5+5x^4+3x^3+x^2-1.
\end{align*}
Both displayed full denominators have constant term one. The numerator
of $A_4$, listed from constant term upwards, has coefficient vector

The exact recurrence uses $(m+1)^2$ endpoint states and at most $m+1$
terms per state. Computing all coefficients through length $N$ therefore
uses $O(Nm^3)$ integer multiply-add operations, after coefficient
precomputation, and $O(Nm^2)$ stored integers in the supplied straightforward
implementation. These are arithmetic-operation counts, not bit-complexity
bounds. Exact values have growing bit length. A rolling buffer can reduce
storage when the complete coefficient table is not needed.

Perron certificates store integers $p,D$ for each radius endpoint $p/D$,
and a positive integer vector in the state order generated by the verifier.
Symbolic certificates store all polynomial coefficients in ascending degree.
The constant term of each common denominator is checked to be one.
The certificate identities prove infinitely many coefficients because the
formal system has a unique solution; the finite coefficient checks are
additional consistency tests.

\section{Source status and proof audit}
\label{app:status}
The literature check was performed on September 19, 2026. The inspected
primary items are the arXiv v1 versions listed below, including Nadler's
Conjecture~2 and Mayama--Akita's Theorem~4.14 and Problem~P3. The latter
paper's displayed numerical table and statement that its component comparison
was unproved were also inspected in the PDF. Targeted searches used the
paper titles and identifiers together with strict monotonicity, growth
constants, and asymptotic refinements. No later solution was located in
that search. This establishes the specific source-based status used here;
it is not an assertion that every possible publication has been searched.

The logical dependencies of the new claims are particularly short.
The strict-growth theorem uses the exact endpoint recurrence, the two
irreducible components, their root characterization, the proper-principal
Perron lemma, and the explicit shift of thresholds. This report includes
proofs of those prerequisites. The asymptotic theorem instead uses the
maximum-decomposition majorant, the injective skew-block construction,
the first-entry Catalan formula, and the truncated-tail lemma. In particular,
no numerical fit, unproved component-ordering pattern, or formal passage
from finite checks to all $m$ is used.

The work has not been formalized in a proof assistant. The integer and
polynomial certificates are executable verification of the stated finite
algebraic claims; they should not be described as a formal verification
of the analytic limiting argument or of the all-$m$ theorem.
