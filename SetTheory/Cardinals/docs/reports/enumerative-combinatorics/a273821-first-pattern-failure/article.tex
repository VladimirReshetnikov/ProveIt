\begin{abstract}
For a uniformly chosen $123$-avoiding permutation of $[n]$, let $K_n$ be the
largest number of its largest entries whose induced permutation still avoids
$132$. The generating function for the joint counts $T(n,k)$ is marked
conjectural in OEIS A273821. We prove it by inserting successive minima,
then telescope the resulting Catalan convolution to obtain a difference of
two binomial coefficients. This resolves both the posted generating-function
conjecture and the indicated growth-rate question for every nonzero fixed
column.

The exact formula yields substantially more: every column $k\ge2$ is
hypergeometric in $n$; its full inverse-power expansion is computable to any
order; and $K_n$ has an explicit discrete limit law with exponentially
weighted total-variation error $O(n^{-1})$. The fixed-column approximation
changes at $k\asymp\sqrt n$, where we prove an explicit crossover. For
$k$ proportional to $n$, two distinct exponential sectors are separated
exactly rather than hidden under an algebraic error term.

Weighting permutations by $y^{K_n}$ produces a transition at $y=2$.
Below the transition $K_n$ stays bounded; above it $n-K_n$ stays bounded;
at the transition $K_n/n$ converges to $\Beta(1,\tfrac12)$. We give an exact
critical partition function and mean, a critical window, Lambert-$W$
inverse expansions, independent computation, and a formalization and research
agenda.
\end{abstract}

\part{The pattern 132: proof of the A273821 conjecture and a critical phase transition}

\section{The question, the answer, and the scope of the claim}\label{fpf:sec:question}

\subsection{The statistic and the live OEIS question}
A permutation avoids a pattern if no subsequence, with positions increasing,
has the same relative order as that pattern. Thus $123$ avoidance forbids
three increasing entries, and $132$ avoidance forbids indices $i<j<h$ with
$\pi_i<\pi_h<\pi_j$. Write $\Av_n(123)$ for the first class.

For $\pi\in\Av_n(123)$, restrict $\pi$ to its $k$ largest values without
changing their order. Let $K(\pi)$ be the largest $k$ for which this
restriction avoids $132$. Set
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:definition}
 T(n,k)=\#\{\pi\in\Av_n(123):K(\pi)=k\},\qquad
 Z_n(y)=\sum_{k=1}^n T(n,k)y^k.
\end{equation}
The triangle is \Oeis{A273821}, introduced by David Callan in 2016
\cite{oeis273821}. As retrieved on October 1, 2026, its bivariate generating
function remains labeled conjectural; its comments also suggest growth
constant $4$ for each column other than the first. These are the two
specific posted questions answered below.

We use $C_n=\frac1{n+1}\binom{2n}{n}$ and
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:catalan}
 C(x)=\sum_{n\ge0}C_nx^n=\frac{2}{1+\sqrt{1-4x}},
 \qquad C(x)=1+xC(x)^2.
\end{equation}
Our insertion argument will itself establish the Catalan row sums, so the
proof does not assume the conjectural triangle recurrence or generating
function. All entries outside $1\le k\le n$ are zero. For a binomial
coefficient with nonnegative upper argument, a negative lower argument is
interpreted as zero.

\subsection{Principal conclusions}
The central enumeration result is
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:main-binomial}
\boxed{\displaystyle
 T(n,k)=2^{k-1}\binom{2n-2k}{n-k-1}
             -\binom{2n-k-1}{n-k-1}\quad (n>k\ge1),}
\end{equation}
with the separate diagonal value $T(n,n)=2^{n-1}$. In particular, the first
column is zero after its initial entry.

The resulting bivariate generating function is
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:main-gf}
\boxed{\displaystyle
 F(x,y):=\sum_{n\ge1}Z_n(y)x^n
 =\frac{xy}{1-2xy}
  +\frac{x^3y^2C(x)^3}{(1-2xy)(1-xyC(x))}.}
\end{equation}
This is algebraically the conjectured OEIS expression. Its present form
records the first failure of $132$ avoidance explicitly.

For every fixed $k\ge2$,
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:main-asymptotic}
 T(n,k)\sim \frac{(k-1)(k+4)}{2^{k+3}\sqrt\pi}\,
                 \frac{4^n}{n^{3/2}}.
\end{equation}
We prove a full expansion with explicit first coefficients and an all-orders
recursion, not only this leading equivalent. Moreover, for a uniform
permutation in $\Av_n(123)$,
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:main-discrete-law}
 \Prob(K_n=k)\longrightarrow p_k:=\frac{(k-1)(k+4)}{2^{k+3}}
 \quad(k\ge2),\qquad
 \sum_{k\ge2}p_ky^k=\frac{y^2(3-y)}{2(2-y)^3}.
\end{equation}

There is a second, macroscopic phenomenon. Under the probability measure
proportional to $y^{K(\pi)}$, the scaling of $K_n$ changes at $y=2$:
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:phase-summary}
\begin{array}{c|c}
 0<y<2 & K_n\text{ converges to an explicit discrete law},\\[2pt]
 y=2 & K_n/n\ \Rightarrow\ \Beta(1,\tfrac12),\\[2pt]
 y>2 & n-K_n\text{ converges to an explicit discrete law}.
\end{array}
\end{equation}
The critical normalization has the exact evaluation
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:critical-preview}
 Z_n(2)=(n+2)\binom{2n}{n}-4^n.
\end{equation}
These conclusions are consequences of the same exact first-failure
formula, not independent guesses from numerical plots.

\section{A minimum-insertion model for Catalan permutations}\label{fpf:sec:insertion}

\subsection{The active suffix}
Every nonempty permutation has a unique parent obtained by deleting its
minimum and standardizing the remaining values. Reversing this operation
builds a permutation by inserting successively smaller values.

\begin{definition}
The suffix state $d$ of a nonempty permutation is the length of its longest
terminal decreasing subsequence of \emph{consecutive positions}. Thus the
last $d$ entries decrease, and either the permutation is decreasing or the
entry immediately before them forms an ascent with the first of those $d$
entries. An insertion site is indexed by the number $j$ of old entries to
its right.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Active sites]\label{fpf:lem:active}
For a permutation in $\Av_n(123)$ with suffix state $d$, insertion of a new
minimum preserves $123$ avoidance exactly at the sites $0\le j\le d$.
The new state is $d+1$ when $j=0$, and is $j$ when $1\le j\le d$.
Consequently the state production rule is
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:production}
 d\longrightarrow 1,2,\ldots,d,d+1,
\end{equation}
with each child state occurring once.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Any newly formed increasing triple must start at the new minimum. Such a
triple exists precisely when the entries to its right contain an increasing
pair. Avoiding such a pair means that this right-hand suffix is decreasing,
which is equivalent to $j\le d$. If the minimum is appended, it lengthens
the decreasing suffix by one. Otherwise it lies just before a decreasing
suffix of length $j$ and is smaller than its first entry, so that suffix
cannot extend to the left. Its new length is exactly $j$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Descendant generating functions}
Let $D_d(x)$ count descendants of a state-$d$ permutation, with $x$ marking
the number of additional minima inserted. The particular values or earlier
shape of the permutation do not matter, because the production rule depends
only on $d$.

\begin{lemma}\label{fpf:lem:descendants}
For every $d\ge1$,
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:descendants}
 D_d(x)=C(x)^{d+1}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The production rule gives
\[
 D_d(x)=1+x\sum_{j=1}^{d+1}D_j(x).
\]
This infinite system has a unique formal-power-series solution with constant
term $1$: after the coefficients through degree $m-1$ have been determined
for every state, the equation determines the coefficient of degree $m$
for every state using only a finite sum. To verify the claimed solution,
use $xC^2=C-1$:
\[
 x\sum_{j=1}^{d+1}C^{j+1}
 =(C-1)(1+C+\cdots+C^d)=C^{d+1}-1.
\]
Uniqueness finishes the proof.
\end{proof}
The single-entry permutation has state $1$. Therefore the number of
size-$n$ permutations generated is
\[
 [x^{n-1}]D_1(x)=[x^{n-1}]C(x)^2=C_n,
\]
where the final equality follows from $xC^2=C-1$. Thus this construction
also proves the Catalan row sums.

\subsection{The two safe sites for avoiding both patterns}
Suppose the current permutation avoids both $123$ and $132$. At an active
site the entries to the right of a newly inserted minimum decrease. The new
minimum starts a $132$ occurrence exactly when there are at least two such
entries. Hence the insertions that remain safe for both patterns are
precisely $j=0$ and $j=1$.

The first appends the new minimum and increases $d$ by one; the second places
it before the last entry and resets $d$ to $1$. These two choices encode
all size-$k$ safe prefixes by binary words of length $k-1$.

\begin{lemma}\label{fpf:lem:safe-count}
Let $a_{k,d}$ be the number of permutations of size $k$ avoiding both
patterns and having suffix state $d$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:safe-count}
 a_{k,k}=1,\qquad a_{k,d}=2^{k-d-1}\quad(1\le d<k).
\end{equation}
In particular, there are $2^{k-1}$ such permutations, and for $2\le j\le k$,
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:safe-tail}
 \sum_{d=j}^k a_{k,d}=2^{k-j}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The state is $k$ only if every insertion appends. Otherwise locate the last
reset: to finish at state $d<k$ it must be followed by exactly $d-1$
appends. The preceding $k-d-1$ decisions are free. Summation gives both
remaining assertions.
\end{proof}

\section{First failure and the conjectured generating function}\label{fpf:sec:first-failure}

\subsection{The first-failure decomposition}
Once a $132$ occurrence has appeared among the largest entries, inserting
smaller entries cannot remove it. Thus $K=k<n$ is equivalent to remaining
safe through size $k$ and failing at the next insertion. At that failure
insertion the active site has $j\ge2$ entries to its right.

\begin{theorem}[Positive Catalan convolution]\label{fpf:thm:convolution}
For $n>k\ge2$,
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:convolution}
 T(n,k)=\sum_{j=2}^k 2^{k-j}[x^{n-k-1}]C(x)^{j+1}.
\end{equation}
For $n=k$, $T(n,n)=2^{n-1}$; for $n>1$, $T(n,1)=0$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For a fixed failing site $j$, a safe size-$k$ prefix permits that insertion
exactly when its state $d$ satisfies $d\ge j$. There are $2^{k-j}$ such
prefixes by Lemma~\ref{fpf:lem:safe-count}. The failing insertion gives state
$j$ by Lemma~\ref{fpf:lem:active}; the remaining $n-k-1$ insertions are arbitrary
$123$-preserving descendants and are counted by $C^{j+1}$. The parent
operation is unique, so these cases are disjoint and exhaustive. The
boundary cases follow from the safe-prefix count and the fact that a
$132$ occurrence needs three entries.
\end{proof}

For example, the row at size $5$ is
\[
(T(5,1),\ldots,T(5,5))=(0,9,10,7,16).
\]
The off-diagonal formula gives $T(5,3)=[x](2C^3+C^4)=6+4=10$.
The row sums to $42=C_5$. This example is computed from the insertion
argument, not used to infer it.

\subsection{Summation of the columns}
\begin{theorem}[Proof of the posted generating-function conjecture]
The identity \eqref{fpf:eq:main-gf} holds in $\mathbb Q[y][[x]]$. It is equal to
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:oeis-gf}
 C(x)-1+
 \frac{(1-y)(1-xy)\{1-(1-xy)C(x)\}}
 {(1-2xy)(1-y+xy^2)},
\end{equation}
the expression marked conjectural in OEIS A273821 \cite{oeis273821}.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The size generating function for column $k$ is
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:column-gf}
 G_k(x)=2^{k-1}x^k+x^{k+1}\sum_{j=2}^k2^{k-j}C(x)^{j+1},
 \qquad G_1(x)=x.
\end{equation}
The diagonal contribution sums to $xy/(1-2xy)$. In the second contribution,
write $k=j+h$ and sum the two geometric series:
\begin{align*}
 \sum_{k\ge2}y^kx^{k+1}\sum_{j=2}^k2^{k-j}C^{j+1}
 &=xC\sum_{j\ge2}(xyC)^j\sum_{h\ge0}(2xy)^h\\
 &=\frac{x^3y^2C^3}{(1-2xy)(1-xyC)}.
\end{align*}
This proves \eqref{fpf:eq:main-gf}. To compare with \eqref{fpf:eq:oeis-gf}, replace
$xC^2$ by $C-1$ and use
\[
 1-y+xy^2=(1-xyC)(1-y/C).
\]
Clearing denominators then gives identical numerators. This is an identity
of rational algebraic functions; any removable factors in the displayed
OEIS form are interpreted through the formal series \eqref{fpf:eq:main-gf}.
\end{proof}
Setting $y=1$ gives $F(x,1)=C(x)-1$, consistently with the combinatorial
row-sum proof. The derivation also shows why two apparently different
singular factors occur: $1-2xy$ records the binary safe-prefix evolution,
whereas $1-xyC$ records the active suffix at first failure.

\section{Two binomials, exact tails, and hypergeometric columns}

\subsection{A telescoping Catalan convolution}
Lagrange inversion applied to $C=1+xC^2$ gives, for $m\ge0$ and $j\ge1$,
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:ballot}
 b(m,j):=[x^m]C(x)^{j+1}
 =\frac{j+1}{2m+j+1}\binom{2m+j+1}{m}
 =\binom{2m+j}{m}-\binom{2m+j}{m-1}.
\end{equation}
For completeness, if $m>0$, set $u=C-1=x(1+u)^2$. The Lagrange formula
for $[x^m](1+u)^{j+1}$ is
$\frac{j+1}{m}[u^{m-1}](1+u)^{2m+j}$, which simplifies to the first
expression. At $m=0$ both sides are $1$.

\begin{lemma}[Telescoper]\label{fpf:lem:telescoper}
For $t_j=2^{-j}\binom{2m+j}{m}$,
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:telescoper}
 2^{-j}b(m,j)=2(t_j-t_{j+1}).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Pascal's identity gives
\[
 2\binom{2m+j}{m}-\binom{2m+j+1}{m}
 =\binom{2m+j}{m}-\binom{2m+j}{m-1}.
\]
Multiplication by $2^{-j}$ is exactly the claim.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Exact entries and cumulative counts]\label{fpf:thm:exact}
The binomial formula \eqref{fpf:eq:main-binomial} holds. In addition, for
$1\le k\le n$,
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:exact-tail}
 \boxed{\displaystyle
 S(n,k):=\sum_{h=k}^n T(n,h)
 =2^{k-1}\binom{2n-2k+1}{n-k}
  -\binom{2n-k}{n-k-1}.}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
In \eqref{fpf:eq:convolution} put $m=n-k-1$. Lemma~\ref{fpf:lem:telescoper}
telescopes the sum to $2^{k+1}(t_2-t_{k+1})$, giving
\eqref{fpf:eq:main-binomial}. For $k=1<n$, its two terms coincide, as required.

For the cumulative count, being safe through size $k$ is the only
restriction. Put $\ell=n-k$. Lemmas~\ref{fpf:lem:descendants} and
\ref{fpf:lem:safe-count} give
\[
 S(n,k)=[x^\ell]\left(C^{k+1}
           +\sum_{d=1}^{k-1}2^{k-d-1}C^{d+1}\right).
\]
With $m=\ell$, the telescoping sum over $d$ is
$2^k(t_1-t_k)$, and the remaining term is $b(\ell,k)$.
The result is
$2^{k-1}\binom{2\ell+1}{\ell}-\binom{2\ell+k}{\ell-1}$,
which is \eqref{fpf:eq:exact-tail}. It includes $k=n$ under the stated
binomial convention.
\end{proof}
In particular $S(n,1)=C_n$. The positive formula
\eqref{fpf:eq:convolution} proves positivity of the difference of binomials for
$2\le k<n$, without needing a cancellation estimate.

\subsection{A polynomial factor and a first-order recurrence}
\begin{definition}
For $k\ge2$, define
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:H}
 H_k(n)=2^{k-1}\fall{n}{k-1}-\fall{2n-k-1}{k-1},
 \qquad \fall{a}{r}=\prod_{j=0}^{r-1}(a-j).
\end{equation}
\end{definition}
\begin{theorem}\label{fpf:thm:hypergeom}
The polynomial $H_k$ has degree $k-2$, with leading coefficient
$2^{k-3}(k-1)(k+4)$. For $n>k$,
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:hypergeom}
 T(n,k)=\frac{(2n-2k)!}{(n-k-1)!\,n!}\,H_k(n).
\end{equation}
Consequently each fixed off-diagonal column is hypergeometric in $n$,
with recurrence
\begin{align}\label{fpf:eq:column-rec}
 &(n-k)(n+1)H_k(n)T(n+1,k)\notag\\
 &\hspace{1cm}=2(n-k+1)(2n-2k+1)H_k(n+1)T(n,k),\qquad n>k.
\end{align}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Factor the factorial expression in \eqref{fpf:eq:main-binomial} to obtain
\eqref{fpf:eq:hypergeom}. Both terms of $H_k$ have leading term
$2^{k-1}n^{k-1}$, so those terms cancel. Expanding the next coefficient by
summing the roots of the two products gives
$2^{k-3}(k-1)(k+4)>0$. Thus the degree is exactly $k-2$.
Taking the ratio of the factorial prefactors at $n+1$ and $n$ gives
\eqref{fpf:eq:column-rec}. Positivity follows from
Theorem~\ref{fpf:thm:convolution} and the positive prefactor.
\end{proof}
The first examples are
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:H-examples}
 H_2(n)=3,\qquad H_3(n)=14n-20,\qquad
 H_4(n)=48n^2-198n+210.
\end{equation}
This first-order structure is stronger than merely knowing that each
column is D-finite.

\subsection{Known companion sequences and their offsets}
The identifications noted in the OEIS entry become exact with the following
ranges:
\begin{align}\label{fpf:eq:companions}
 T(n,2)&=\Oeis{A000245}(n-2), && n\ge3,\notag\\
 T(n,3)&=\Oeis{A071718}(n-3), && n\ge4.
\end{align}
For the second column, \eqref{fpf:eq:column-gf} gives $G_2=2x^2+x^3C^3$;
the companion generating function is $xC^3$. For the third,
$G_3=4x^3+x^4(2C^3+C^4)$, and
\[

\]

\section{All-orders fixed-column asymptotics}

\subsection{A rational ratio to the Catalan numbers}
\begin{proposition}\label{fpf:prop:rational-ratio}
For each $k\ge2$ and $n>k$, the ratio $T(n,k)/C_n$ is rational in $n$:
\begin{align}\label{fpf:eq:rational-ratio}
 \frac{T(n,k)}{C_n}
 &=(n+1)\frac{2^{k-1}\fall{n}{k+1}\fall{n}{k-1}}
                      {\fall{2n}{2k}}\notag\\
 &\quad -(n+1)\frac{\fall{n}{k+1}}{\fall{2n}{k+1}}.
\end{align}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Divide each binomial term of \eqref{fpf:eq:main-binomial} by
$(2n)!/(n!^2(n+1))$ and cancel the factorials.
\end{proof}
The two terms on the right individually grow linearly with $n$ for fixed
$k$, but their difference has a finite limit. Resolving that cancellation
before expanding is useful both algebraically and numerically.

\subsection{An explicit coefficient engine}
Put $t=1/n$ and $S_m(q)=\sum_{r=0}^q r^m$. For $m\ge1$ define
\begin{align}\label{fpf:eq:ab}
 a_m(k)&=\frac{2^{-m}S_m(2k-1)-S_m(k)-S_m(k-2)}{m},\notag\\
 b_m(k)&=\frac{(2^{-m}-1)S_m(k)}{m},
\end{align}
and let $A_k(t)=\sum_{m\ge1}a_m(k)t^m$,
$B_k(t)=\sum_{m\ge1}b_m(k)t^m$.
Taking logarithms of the finite products in
\eqref{fpf:eq:rational-ratio} yields the local identity
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:probability-engine}
 \frac{T(n,k)}{C_n}
 =2^{-k-1}\frac{1+t}{t}
       \bigl(e^{A_k(t)}-e^{B_k(t)}\bigr).
\end{equation}
For fixed $k$, this is the Taylor expansion of a rational function at
$t=0$ after a removable singularity is canceled. The series therefore
converges for sufficiently small $|t|$, unlike the separate Stirling series
used for $C_n$.

The leading difference is
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:d1}
 d_1(k):=a_1(k)-b_1(k)=\frac{(k-1)(k+4)}4.
\end{equation}
Exponentials of either logarithmic series can be generated without repeated
symbolic exponentiation: if $E(t)=\exp(\sum_{m\ge1}\alpha_mt^m)=\sum E_jt^j$,
then
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:exp-rec}
 E_0=1,\qquad E_j=\frac1j\sum_{m=1}^j m\alpha_m E_{j-m}.
\end{equation}
This supplies every probability correction as a rational function of $k$.

To include the Catalan prefactor, write the formal Stirling logarithm
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:cat-log}
 L_C(t)=-\log(1+t)+
 \sum_{r\ge1}\frac{B_{2r}(2^{1-2r}-2)}{2r(2r-1)}t^{2r-1},
\end{equation}
where $B_{2r}$ are Bernoulli numbers with $B_2=1/6$. Stirling's expansion
\cite{dlmfgamma} gives
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:cat-exp}
 C_n\sim\frac{4^n}{\sqrt\pi n^{3/2}}e^{L_C(1/n)}
 =\frac{4^n}{\sqrt\pi n^{3/2}}
 \left(1-\frac9{8n}+\frac{145}{128n^2}
                   -\frac{1155}{1024n^3}+\cdots\right).
\end{equation}
The symbol $\sim$ here means agreement at every fixed truncation order; it
does not assert convergence of the infinite Stirling series.

\begin{theorem}[Full fixed-column expansion]\label{fpf:thm:fixed-all}
For every fixed integer $k\ge2$ and every $J\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:all-orders}
 T(n,k)=\frac{p_k}{\sqrt\pi}\frac{4^n}{n^{3/2}}
 \left(\sum_{j=0}^J\frac{c_j(k)}{n^j}+O_{k,J}(n^{-J-1})\right),
 \qquad p_k=\frac{(k-1)(k+4)}{2^{k+3}},
\end{equation}
where $c_0=1$ and the formal generating series of the coefficients is
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:all-engine}
 \sum_{j\ge0}c_j(k)t^j
 =e^{L_C(t)}\frac{(1+t)(e^{A_k(t)}-e^{B_k(t)})}{d_1(k)t}.
\end{equation}
In particular,
\begin{align}\label{fpf:eq:c12}
 c_1(k)&=-\frac{k^3-k^2-17k+36}{8(k+4)},\notag\\
 c_2(k)&=\frac{4k^5-32k^4-22k^3+578k^2-2601k+1740}{384(k+4)}.
\end{align}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Equations \eqref{fpf:eq:probability-engine} and \eqref{fpf:eq:d1} give the convergent
fixed-$k$ ratio expansion with leading coefficient
$2^{-k-1}d_1=p_k$. Multiply it by the Stirling expansion
\eqref{fpf:eq:cat-exp}. A finite Taylor remainder for the rational ratio and
the finite-order Stirling remainder give the stated error for each fixed
$J$. Applying \eqref{fpf:eq:exp-rec} gives \eqref{fpf:eq:c12}; the derivation is
also checked symbolically in the supplied program.
\end{proof}
Both natural meanings of the OEIS growth-rate comment now follow:
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:growth}
 \lim_{n\to\infty}T(n,k)^{1/n}=4,
 \qquad \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{T(n+1,k)}{T(n,k)}=4
 \quad(k\ge2).
\end{equation}

For reference, the corresponding normalized-probability expansion starts
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:prob-corr}
 \frac{T(n,k)}{C_n}
 =p_k\left(1+\frac{r_1(k)}n+\frac{r_2(k)}{n^2}+O_k(n^{-3})\right),
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align}\label{fpf:eq:r12}
 r_1(k)&=-\frac{k(k^2-k-26)}{8(k+4)},\notag\\
 r_2(k)&=\frac{k(k^4-8k^3-19k^2+158k-408)}{96(k+4)}.
\end{align}
The dependence on $k$ already warns that these fixed-column expansions
cannot be used uniformly all the way across the triangle.

\section{The discrete limiting law and an exponentially weighted error}

\begin{theorem}\label{fpf:thm:limitlaw}
Let $K_n$ be defined under the uniform measure on $\Av_n(123)$. It converges
in distribution to $K$ with probabilities $p_k$ in
\eqref{fpf:eq:main-discrete-law}. Its probability generating function, mean,
variance, and tail are
\begin{align}\label{fpf:eq:limit-data}
 P(y)&=\E y^K=\frac{y^2(3-y)}{2(2-y)^3}, && |y|<2,\notag\\
 \E K&=\frac92,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(K)=\frac{21}4,\notag\\
 \Prob(K\ge m)&=2^{-m-2}(m^2+5m+2), && m\ge1.
\end{align}
For every fixed $1<r<2$,
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:weighted-tv}
 \sum_{k\ge1}r^k
 \left|\Prob(K_n=k)-p_k\right|=O_r(n^{-1}),
\end{equation}
where $p_1=0$ and the finite-$n$ probabilities are zero for $k>n$.
Consequently all moments converge, with error $O(n^{-1})$ for each fixed
moment.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Summing the quadratic times geometric expression for $p_k$ gives $P(y)$,
with $P(1)=1$. Derivatives at $y=1$ give the two moments. Summing the same
geometric tail gives the last formula in \eqref{fpf:eq:limit-data}.

For the error assertion, set $z=\sqrt{1-4x}$ in the exact generating
function. It becomes
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:F-z}
 F(x,y)=-\frac{y(z-1)(yz^2-yz+z+1)}
                  {(yz-y+2)(yz^2-y+2)}.
\end{equation}
Fix $R<2$. Uniformly for $|y|\le R$, neither denominator in
\eqref{fpf:eq:main-gf} vanishes for $|x|\le1/4$: indeed
$|2xy|\le R/2<1$ and $|xyC(x)|\le R/2<1$. By compactness the function
admits a common slit, dented neighborhood of its dominant square-root
singularity. Its expansion at $z=0$ has coefficient $-2P(y)$ on $z$.
The next nonanalytic term is of order $z^3$; retaining further terms gives
uniform coefficient remainders. Standard square-root coefficient transfer
\cite[Chapter VI]{flajolet} therefore gives
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:uniform-pgf}
 \sup_{|y|\le R}\left|\frac{Z_n(y)}{C_n}-P(y)\right|=O_R(n^{-1}).
\end{equation}
One may also obtain the transfer constants from
$[x^n](1-4x)^\alpha
=4^n\Gamma(n-\alpha)/(\Gamma(-\alpha)\Gamma(n+1))$
for nonintegral $\alpha$; the uniform analytic remainder is handled on the
same dented contour.

Now choose $r<R<2$. Cauchy's coefficient bound applied in the variable $y$
to \eqref{fpf:eq:uniform-pgf} gives
$|\Prob(K_n=k)-p_k|\le M_R n^{-1}R^{-k}$.
Sum the convergent geometric majorant $(r/R)^k$ to prove
\eqref{fpf:eq:weighted-tv}. Polynomial moment weights are bounded by a constant
times $r^k$, which proves moment convergence and its rate.
\end{proof}
The exponential weight is useful: an unweighted pointwise limit alone would
not justify interchanging arbitrary moment sums and limits. Here it is
justified with a quantitative bound.

\section{The square-root crossover and uniform moving-column regimes}

\subsection{An exact positive factorization}
For $n>k\ge2$, put $\ell=n-k$ and write
\begin{align}\label{fpf:eq:AB-split}
 A_{n,k}&=2^{k-1}\binom{2\ell}{\ell-1}=2^{k-1}\ell C_\ell,\notag\\
 B_{n,k}&=\binom{2\ell+k-1}{\ell-1},\qquad
 T(n,k)=A_{n,k}-B_{n,k}=A_{n,k}(1-R_{n,k}).
\end{align}
The ratio has the exact product
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:R-product}
 R_{n,k}=\frac{B_{n,k}}{A_{n,k}}
 =\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{1+j/(2\ell)}{1+(j+1)/\ell}
 =\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\left(1-\frac{j+2}{2(\ell+j+1)}\right).
\end{equation}
All factors lie strictly between zero and one.

\begin{lemma}\label{fpf:lem:R}
For every $n>k\ge2$,
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:R-bound}
 0<R_{n,k}\le
 \exp\left(-\frac{(k-1)(k+4)}{4n}\right).
\end{equation}
If $k/\ell\to0$, then
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:logR}
 \log R_{n,k}=-\frac{(k-1)(k+4)}{4\ell}
                  +O\left(\frac{k^3}{\ell^2}\right).
\end{equation}
The last error is uniform when $k/\ell$ is sufficiently small.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Use $\log(1-u)\le-u$ in \eqref{fpf:eq:R-product}, the bound
$\ell+j+1\le n$, and
$\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}(j+2)=(k-1)(k+4)/2$.
For \eqref{fpf:eq:logR}, expand
$\log(1+j/(2\ell))-\log(1+(j+1)/\ell)$.
The first-order terms sum to the displayed expression; the sum of the
quadratic remainder bounds is $O(k^3/\ell^2)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Three uniform regimes]\label{fpf:thm:crossover}
The following assertions hold.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),leftmargin=2em]
\item If $2\le k=o(\sqrt n)$, then uniformly in this regime,
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:small-k-uniform}
 \frac{T(n,k)}{C_n}=p_k\left(1+O\left(\frac{k^2}{n}\right)\right).
\end{equation}
\item If $k/\sqrt n\to s\in(0,\infty)$, then
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:crossover}
 T(n,k)\sim\frac{4^n2^{-k}}{2\sqrt{\pi n}}
                  \left(1-e^{-s^2/4}\right).
\end{equation}
Equivalently,
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:crossover-ratio}
 \frac{T(n,k)/C_n}{p_k}\longrightarrow
 \frac{4(1-e^{-s^2/4})}{s^2}.
\end{equation}
The equivalents are uniform on compact subintervals of $0<s<\infty$.
\item If $k/\sqrt n\to\infty$ and $\ell=n-k\to\infty$, then for each fixed $J$,
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:large-k-uniform}
 T(n,k)=\frac{4^n2^{-k}}{2\sqrt{\pi\ell}}
 \left(\sum_{j=0}^J\frac{\gamma_j}{\ell^j}
                    +O_J(\ell^{-J-1})\right)(1-R_{n,k}),
\end{equation}
where $\gamma_0=1$, $\gamma_1=-9/8$, $\gamma_2=145/128$, and the
exponentially small ratio is bounded by \eqref{fpf:eq:R-bound}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The factor $A_{n,k}$ has the uniform Catalan expansion
\[
 A_{n,k}=\frac{4^n2^{-k}}{2\sqrt{\pi\ell}}
             \left(1-\frac9{8\ell}+O(\ell^{-2})\right).
\]
In the first regime, \eqref{fpf:eq:logR} and the expansion of $1-e^{-u}$ give
\[
 1-R_{n,k}=\frac{(k-1)(k+4)}{4\ell}
                \left(1+O(k^2/n)\right).
\]
Dividing by $C_n$ and using $\ell/n=1-k/n$ proves
\eqref{fpf:eq:small-k-uniform}. In the second regime $\ell/n\to1$ and
$R_{n,k}\to e^{-s^2/4}$ uniformly on the stated compact sets. This proves
\eqref{fpf:eq:crossover}, and comparison with $p_k\sim k^2/2^{k+3}$ proves
\eqref{fpf:eq:crossover-ratio}. In the third regime, the bound on $R_{n,k}$
tends to zero while the Catalan expansion at $\ell$ can be taken to any
fixed order. Retaining $1-R_{n,k}$ exactly yields the stated error without
confusing algebraic and exponential orders.
\end{proof}

\section{Two exponential sectors and the near-diagonal regime}

\subsection{Linear rays}
Suppose $\beta=(n-k)/n$ remains in a compact subset of $(0,1)$. Define
\begin{align}\label{fpf:eq:ray-exponents}
 h_0(\beta)&=(1+\beta)\log2,\notag\\
 h_1(\beta)&=(1+\beta)\log(1+\beta)-\beta\log\beta,\notag\\
 J(\beta)&=h_0(\beta)-h_1(\beta)>0.
\end{align}
The exact decomposition \eqref{fpf:eq:AB-split} gives two separately expandable
exponential sectors. If $\Cat(t)=e^{L_C(t)}$ denotes the formal Catalan
correction series, then
\begin{align}\label{fpf:eq:ray-A}
 A_{n,k}&\sim\frac{e^{nh_0(\beta)}}{2\sqrt{\pi\beta n}}
                         \Cat\left(\frac1{\beta n}\right),\\
 B_{n,k}&\sim\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{2\pi(1+\beta)n}}e^{nh_1(\beta)}
                         e^{L_\beta(1/n)},\label{fpf:eq:ray-B}
\end{align}
where
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:ray-log}
 L_\beta(t)=\sum_{r\ge1}
 \frac{B_{2r}\{(1+\beta)^{1-2r}-\beta^{1-2r}-1\}}
      {2r(2r-1)}t^{2r-1}.
\end{equation}
These follow directly from Stirling's formula, using
$B_{n,k}=\frac\beta{1+\beta}\binom{(1+\beta)n}{\beta n}$.
At every finite order the relative remainders are uniform on the stated
compact sets.

In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:R-ray}
 R_{n,k}\sim \frac{\sqrt2\,\beta}{\sqrt{1+\beta}}e^{-nJ(\beta)}.
\end{equation}
To see $J>0$, note that $J(1)=0$ and
$J'(\beta)=\log(2\beta/(1+\beta))<0$ for $0<\beta<1$.
Near the endpoint,
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:J-match}
 J(\beta)=\frac{(1-\beta)^2}{4}+O((1-\beta)^3),
\end{equation}
which explains the square-root crossover from the exponential-sector side.

\begin{remark}[What an exponentially small term does and does not mean]
Equations \eqref{fpf:eq:ray-A}--\eqref{fpf:eq:ray-B}, together with the exact
identity $T=A-B$, provide a two-sector asymptotic description. A finite
inverse-power approximation to $A$ has an error much larger than $B$.
Thus it would be incorrect to append the leading $B$ term to an arbitrarily
truncated $A$ series and claim the remainder is smaller than $B$. To
resolve the second sector numerically, retain $A$ exactly through its
binomial or gamma expression, or control its approximation to exponentially
small accuracy. Here both sectors are defined exactly before either is
expanded; no resurgence or optimal-truncation theorem is being assumed.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Fixed distance from the diagonal}
For an integer $\ell\ge1$ fixed and $n>\ell$, the exact formula specializes
to
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:fixed-deficit}
 \boxed{\displaystyle
 T(n,n-\ell)=2^{n-\ell-1}\binom{2\ell}{\ell-1}
                    -\binom{n+\ell-1}{\ell-1}.}
\end{equation}
The second term is a polynomial in $n$ of degree $\ell-1$. This is a literal
exponential-minus-polynomial expression, not just an asymptotic equivalent.
For example,
\[
 T(n,n-1)=2^{n-2}-1,\qquad
 T(n,n-2)=2^{n-1}-(n+1).
\]
The diagonal itself is $2^{n-1}$. The formulas are valid with their stated
ranges; coincidences with the first zero column at small $n$ are handled
without extra exceptions.

\section{Critical weighting: exact normalization and a beta law}

\subsection{An exact critical partition function}
For $y>0$ define a tilted measure by
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:tilt}
 \Prob_{n,y}(K_n=k)=\frac{T(n,k)y^k}{Z_n(y)}.
\end{equation}
The measure at $y=1$ is uniform on $\Av_n(123)$. The value $y=2$ exactly
balances the factor $2^{-k}$ visible in the asymptotics, so it requires a
separate analysis.

\begin{theorem}[Critical normalization and mean]\label{fpf:thm:critical-exact}
For $n\ge1$,
\begin{align}\label{fpf:eq:Z2}
 Z_n(2)&=(n+2)\binom{2n}{n}-4^n,\\
 \E_{n,2}\frac{K_n}{n}
 &=\frac23+\frac{4^n}{6Z_n(2)}.\label{fpf:eq:critical-mean}
\end{align}
In particular,
\begin{align}\label{fpf:eq:Z2-expansion}
 Z_n(2)=\frac{4^n\sqrt n}{\sqrt\pi}
 \left(1-\frac{\sqrt\pi}{\sqrt n}+\frac{15}{8n}
       -\frac{31}{128n^2}+\frac{21}{1024n^3}+O(n^{-4})\right),\\
 \E_{n,2}(K_n/n)=\frac23+\frac{\sqrt\pi}{6\sqrt n}+O(n^{-1}).
 \label{fpf:eq:mean-expansion}
\end{align}
The partition function has a full expansion obtained by continuing the
central-binomial Stirling series in \eqref{fpf:eq:Z2}.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
In terms of $z=\sqrt{1-4x}$, \eqref{fpf:eq:F-z} at $y=2$ reduces to
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:F2}
 F(x,2)=-1+\frac{3}{2z}-\frac1{z^2}+\frac1{2z^3}.
\end{equation}
Use
\[
 [x^n]z^{-1}=\binom{2n}{n},\qquad
 [x^n]z^{-2}=4^n,\qquad
 [x^n]z^{-3}=(2n+1)\binom{2n}{n}
\]
for $n\ge1$ to obtain \eqref{fpf:eq:Z2}.
Differentiating the exact bivariate function gives
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:F2-firstmoment}
 \left.y\frac{\partial F}{\partial y}\right|_{y=2}
 =-\frac1{2z}+\frac1{2z^2}-\frac1{2z^4}+\frac1{2z^5}.
\end{equation}
Since
$[x^n]z^{-4}=(n+1)4^n$ and
$[x^n]z^{-5}=\frac{(2n+1)(2n+3)}3\binom{2n}{n}$,
coefficient extraction gives
\[
 \sum_{k=1}^n k2^kT(n,k)
 =\frac{2n(n+2)}3\binom{2n}{n}-\frac n2\,4^n.
\]
Divide by $nZ_n(2)$ and rearrange to prove \eqref{fpf:eq:critical-mean}.
Finally, substitute
\[
 \binom{2n}{n}=\frac{4^n}{\sqrt{\pi n}}
 \left(1-\frac1{8n}+\frac1{128n^2}+\frac5{1024n^3}
                     +O(n^{-4})\right)
\]
in the exact expressions. The term $-4^n$ is responsible for the
half-power correction in \eqref{fpf:eq:Z2-expansion}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{An elementary positive-measure comparison}
To study the entire distribution, use the same binomial expressions in
\eqref{fpf:eq:AB-split} also for $k=1<n$, where $A_{n,1}=B_{n,1}$.
Extend the positive first term to the diagonal by
$A_{n,n}=2^{n-1}$, and put $B_{n,n}=0$. Set
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:b-critical}
 b_0=1,\qquad b_\ell=4^{-\ell}\binom{2\ell}{\ell-1}\quad(\ell\ge1).
\end{equation}
Writing $\ell=n-k$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:critical-positive}
 2^kA_{n,k}=\frac{4^n}{2}b_\ell,\qquad 0\le\ell\le n-1.
\end{equation}
These positive weights are close, after normalization, to the actual
critical weights $2^kT(n,k)$.

\begin{lemma}\label{fpf:lem:b-partial}
For $N\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:b-partial}
 \sum_{\ell=0}^N b_\ell
 =-1+\frac{(2N+1)(N+2)}{N+1}\,
                    \frac{\binom{2N}{N}}{4^N}
 =\frac{2\sqrt N}{\sqrt\pi}-1+O((N+1)^{-1/2}),
\end{equation}
where the last estimate is interpreted with a bounded error also at $N=0$.
Moreover,
\begin{align}\label{fpf:eq:AB-totals}
 \sum_{k=1}^n 2^kA_{n,k}
   &=(n+1)\binom{2n}{n}-\frac{4^n}{2},\notag\\
 \sum_{k=1}^n 2^kB_{n,k}
   &=\frac{4^n}{2}-\binom{2n}{n}.
\end{align}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The finite-sum identity in \eqref{fpf:eq:b-partial} is verified at $N=0$ and
then by subtracting its values at $N$ and $N-1$, using the ratio of
successive central binomial coefficients. Stirling's formula gives the
estimate. Multiply the identity with $N=n-1$ by $4^n/2$ and simplify to get
the first equation in \eqref{fpf:eq:AB-totals}. Subtract \eqref{fpf:eq:Z2} to get
the second.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Critical beta limit and error]\label{fpf:thm:beta}
Under $\Prob_{n,2}$,
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:beta-law}
 \frac{K_n}{n}\Rightarrow X,\qquad
 X\sim\Beta(1,\tfrac12),\qquad
 f_X(t)=\frac1{2\sqrt{1-t}}\quad(0<t<1).
\end{equation}
Writing $F_X(t)=1-\sqrt{1-t}$ on $[0,1]$, the distribution functions obey
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:beta-error}
 \sup_{0\le t\le1}
 \left|\Prob_{n,2}(K_n/n\le t)-F_X(t)\right|=O(n^{-1/2}).
\end{equation}
All moments converge; in particular the limiting mean is $2/3$ and the
limiting variance is $4/45$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $\widetilde\Prob_n$ be the measure on $k=1,\ldots,n$ proportional to
$2^kA_{n,k}$. If $A=\sum2^kA_{n,k}$ and $B=\sum2^kB_{n,k}$, then
$Z_n(2)=A-B$. Since all subtracted weights are nonnegative,
\[
 \sum_k\left|\frac{2^kT(n,k)}{A-B}-\frac{2^kA_{n,k}}A\right|
 \le\frac{2B}{A}=O(n^{-1/2}),
\]
by \eqref{fpf:eq:AB-totals}. Thus the two measures differ in total variation by
$O(n^{-1/2})$.

Under $\widetilde\Prob_n$, the deficit $\ell=n-k$ has weights $b_\ell$.
The exact partial sum \eqref{fpf:eq:b-partial} and its error imply, uniformly
for $0\le u\le1$,
\[
 \widetilde\Prob_n\{(n-K_n)/n\le u\}=\sqrt u+O(n^{-1/2}).
\]
Indeed the numerator is a partial sum through an index within one of $un$,
the denominator is the sum through $n-1$, and the estimates in
\eqref{fpf:eq:b-partial} give the claimed uniform error, including both
endpoints. Taking complements and adjusting a possible lattice atom
(which is also $O(n^{-1/2})$) gives the beta law and
\eqref{fpf:eq:beta-error}. Since $K_n/n\in[0,1]$, weak convergence implies
convergence of all moments. The beta density directly gives the stated
mean and variance.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[The first bulk correction]\label{fpf:prop:beta-correction}
Uniformly for $t$ in any compact subinterval of $(0,1)$,
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:beta-first-correction}
 \Prob_{n,2}(K_n/n\le t)
 =F_X(t)+\frac{\sqrt\pi}{\sqrt n}
                      \left(F_X(t)-\frac12\right)+O(n^{-1}).
\end{equation}
This expansion is not asserted uniformly through the endpoints.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Put $m=\lfloor nt\rfloor$. The positive cumulative weight is
$\frac{4^n}{2}\sum_{\ell=n-m}^{n-1}b_\ell$.
The constant $-1$ cancels in the difference of the two partial sums in
\eqref{fpf:eq:b-partial}. Since both endpoints are proportional to $n$,
\[
 \sum_{k\le m}2^kA_{n,k}
 =\frac{4^n\sqrt n}{\sqrt\pi}F_X(t)+O(4^nn^{-1/2}).
\]
For $k>m$, the ratio bound \eqref{fpf:eq:R-bound} makes the total $B$ weight
exponentially small compared with $4^n\sqrt n$. Hence
\[
 \sum_{k\le m}2^kB_{n,k}
 =\frac{4^n}{2}-\binom{2n}{n}+O(4^n e^{-c n})
\]
for some $c>0$ depending on the compact interval (a polynomial factor can
be absorbed by reducing $c$). The numerator is therefore
$\frac{4^n\sqrt n}{\sqrt\pi}F_X(t)-4^n/2+O(4^nn^{-1/2})$.
Divide by \eqref{fpf:eq:Z2-expansion} to obtain the result. The floor in $m$
changes the leading cumulative expression by only $O(n^{-1})$ after
normalization on the stated compact sets.
\end{proof}

\section{The full tilted phase diagram and its critical window}

\subsection{Below the transition}
\begin{theorem}[Subcritical law]\label{fpf:thm:subcritical}
For every fixed $0<y<2$,
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:subcritical}
 Z_n(y)=P(y)C_n(1+O_y(n^{-1})),\qquad
 \Prob_{n,y}(K_n=k)\longrightarrow \frac{p_ky^k}{P(y)}.
\end{equation}
The limiting law has an exponential moment, and $K_n/n\to0$ in probability.
The partition function has an all-orders inverse-power expansion at each
fixed subcritical $y$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The uniform generating-function estimate \eqref{fpf:eq:uniform-pgf} gives the
normalization. Combining it with the fixed-column probabilities gives the
pointwise limit, whose sum is one by definition of $P$. Choose a circle
$|u|=R<2$ with $R>y$. The same coefficient estimates give exponentially
weighted convergence after multiplying by $y^k$, so the family is tight
and has uniformly bounded moments. Expanding \eqref{fpf:eq:F-z} to further
odd powers of $z$ gives every fixed inverse-power order of the partition
function by the same transfer argument.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Above the transition}
For $y>2$ put
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:super-amplitude}
 q=\sqrt{1-2/y},\qquad
 \mathcal A(y)=\frac12\left(1+\frac1q-C\!\left(\frac1{2y}\right)\right)
              =\frac{q^2+1}{2q(q+1)}.
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Supercritical law]\label{fpf:thm:supercritical}
For every fixed $y>2$, there is $0<\eta_y<1$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:super-Z}
 Z_n(y)=\mathcal A(y)(2y)^n(1+O_y(\eta_y^n)).
\end{equation}
The deficit $D_n=n-K_n$ converges in distribution to a nonnegative
integer-valued random variable $D_y$ with
\begin{align}\label{fpf:eq:super-law}
 \Prob(D_y=0)&=\frac1{2\mathcal A(y)},\notag\\
 \Prob(D_y=\ell)&=\frac{\binom{2\ell}{\ell-1}}
                         {2\mathcal A(y)(2y)^\ell},\qquad \ell\ge1.
\end{align}
Its generating function is
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:super-pgf}
 \E u^{D_y}=\frac{H(u/(2y))}{H(1/(2y))},\qquad
 H(t)=1+(1-4t)^{-1/2}-C(t).
\end{equation}
In particular, $K_n/n\to1$ in probability.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The pole $\rho=1/(2y)$ of $1/(1-2xy)$ lies strictly inside the Catalan
radius $1/4$. The other denominator in \eqref{fpf:eq:main-gf} is nonzero at
$\rho$, since $\rho yC(\rho)=C(\rho)/2<1$. In fact it has no zero in a
slightly larger disk: the bound
$|xyC(x)|\le |x|yC(|x|)$ and continuity suffice.
The numerator of the simple pole is
\[
 \lim_{x\to\rho}(1-2yx)F(x,y)
 =\frac12+\frac{C(\rho)^3}{8y\{1-C(\rho)/2\}}
 =\mathcal A(y).
\]
Subtract $\mathcal A(y)/(1-2yx)$ from $F$. The remainder is analytic in a
disk of radius strictly larger than $\rho$, so Cauchy's coefficient bound
proves \eqref{fpf:eq:super-Z}.

For fixed $\ell\ge1$, multiply \eqref{fpf:eq:fixed-deficit} by $y^{n-\ell}$
and divide by \eqref{fpf:eq:super-Z}. The polynomial term tends to zero
relative to the exponential term, and the limit is
\eqref{fpf:eq:super-law}. The diagonal gives the mass at zero. Finally,
\[
 \sum_{\ell\ge1}\binom{2\ell}{\ell-1}t^\ell
 =(1-4t)^{-1/2}-C(t),
\]
so the limiting masses sum to
$H(1/(2y))/(2\mathcal A(y))=1$.
This proves tightness, the asserted law, and its generating function.
\end{proof}
The limiting deficit has an exponential tail since $1/(2y)<1/4$.
At fixed $y>2$, moments converge as well: for $u>1$ sufficiently close to
one, $y/u>2$ and
\[
 \E_{n,y}u^{D_n}=u^n\frac{Z_n(y/u)}{Z_n(y)}
 \longrightarrow\frac{\mathcal A(y/u)}{\mathcal A(y)}
 =\frac{H(u/(2y))}{H(1/(2y))}
\]
uniformly in a neighborhood of $u=1$.

\subsection{Free energy and a finite-size window}
\begin{corollary}\label{fpf:cor:freeenergy}
The limiting exponential rate is
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:freeenergy}
 f(y):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac1n\log Z_n(y)
 =\begin{cases}
 \log4,&0<y\le2,\\
 \log(2y),&y\ge2.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
As a function of $\log y$, its one-sided derivatives at $y=2$ are $0$ and
$1$. At exactly $y=2$, the finite-size mean fraction instead converges to
$2/3$, in accordance with the nondegenerate beta law.
\end{corollary}
This derivative jump is the precise sense in which the model has a
first-order transition. It is a description of the proved weighted
combinatorial model, not a claim about a physical system.

\begin{theorem}[Critical window]\label{fpf:thm:window}
Let $\lambda\in\mathbb R$ be fixed and $y_n=2e^{\lambda/n}$. Then
\begin{align}\label{fpf:eq:window-normalization}
 \frac{Z_n(y_n)}{Z_n(2)}&=M(\lambda)+O_\lambda(n^{-1/2}),\notag\\
 M(\lambda)&=\int_0^1\frac{e^{\lambda t}}{2\sqrt{1-t}}\,\dd t
             =e^\lambda\int_0^1e^{-\lambda u^2}\,\dd u.
\end{align}
Under $\Prob_{n,y_n}$, $K_n/n$ converges to the density
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:window-density}
 f_\lambda(t)=\frac{e^{\lambda t}}
                     {2M(\lambda)\sqrt{1-t}},\qquad 0<t<1.
\end{equation}
Both the normalization error and the distribution-function error are
$O(n^{-1/2})$ uniformly for $\lambda$ in a compact real interval.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The normalization ratio is exactly
$\E_{n,2}\exp(\lambda K_n/n)$. Apply
Theorem~\ref{fpf:thm:beta}; integration by parts bounds the difference of
expectations by the distribution-function error times the total variation
of $e^{\lambda t}$ on $[0,1]$. This variation is uniformly bounded for
$\lambda$ in a compact interval. The substitution $t=1-u^2$ gives the
second integral. For the tilted distribution functions, apply the same
argument to $e^{\lambda t}\ind_{t\le a}$ and divide by the normalization,
which remains uniformly bounded away from zero. This proves the stated
limit and rate.
\end{proof}
When $\lambda>0$ one may write
$M(\lambda)=e^\lambda\sqrt\pi\,\operatorname{erf}(\sqrt\lambda)/(2\sqrt\lambda)$;
the integral form is real and nonsingular for every real $\lambda$, with
$M(0)=1$.

\section{Asymptotic inversion and integer thresholds}\label{fpf:sec:inversion}

\subsection{A natural smooth continuation}
A discrete sequence does not have a unique real inverse without choosing
an interpolation. For fixed $k\ge2$ we use the gamma continuation of the
exact binomial formula:
\begin{align}\label{fpf:eq:gamma-continuation}
 T_k(x)
 &=\frac{2^{k-1}\Gamma(2x-2k+1)}{\Gamma(x-k)\Gamma(x-k+2)}\notag\\
 &\quad-\frac{\Gamma(2x-k)}{\Gamma(x-k)\Gamma(x+1)},\qquad x>k.
\end{align}
It equals $T(n,k)$ at all integers $n>k$. The product formula
\eqref{fpf:eq:R-product}, now with $\ell=x-k>0$, proves positivity because
each factor is less than one. Differentiated Stirling expansions show that
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:log-T}
 \log T_k(x)\sim ax-b\log x+\log A_k^*+
              \sum_{j\ge1}\ell_j(k)x^{-j},
\end{equation}
as an all-orders asymptotic expansion, where
\[
 a=\log4,\qquad b=\frac32,\qquad A_k^*=\frac{p_k}{\sqrt\pi},
 \qquad \ell_1=c_1,\quad \ell_2=c_2-\frac12c_1^2.
\]
Its derivative is $a-b/x+O(x^{-2})>0$ for all sufficiently large $x$.
Thus sufficiently large targets have a unique inverse on the eventual
increasing branch.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Lambert-$W$}{Lambert-W} reduction and corrections}
For a target $v\to\infty$, put
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:inverse-Y}
 Y=\log(v/A_k^*),\qquad
 N=-\frac ba\,W_{-1}\left(-\frac ab e^{-Y/b}\right).
\end{equation}
The real branch $W_{-1}$, not the principal branch, is required to make
$N\to\infty$. The defining identity for $W$ implies
$ aN-b\log N=Y$; standard branch conventions are recorded in
DLMF \S4.13 \cite{dlmfW}.

\begin{theorem}[Inverse expansion]\label{fpf:thm:inverse}
Let $x(v)$ be the eventual increasing inverse of $T_k$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:inverse-two}
 \boxed{\displaystyle
 x(v)=N-\frac{\ell_1}{aN}
       -\frac{\ell_2/a+b\ell_1/a^2}{N^2}+O_k(N^{-3}).}
\end{equation}
An expansion to every order is determined recursively by substituting
$\delta=\sum_{j\ge1}d_jN^{-j}$ in
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:inverse-rec}
 a\delta-b\log(1+\delta/N)
       +\sum_{j\ge1}\ell_j(N+\delta)^{-j}=0
\end{equation}
and setting each coefficient equal to zero. At order $N^{-j}$, the new
unknown $d_j$ has coefficient $a\ne0$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Write $x=N+\delta$ in \eqref{fpf:eq:log-T} and use the exact equation for $N$.
The remaining equation is \eqref{fpf:eq:inverse-rec}. At its first two orders,
\[
 ad_1+\ell_1=0,\qquad ad_2-bd_1+\ell_2=0,
\]
which gives \eqref{fpf:eq:inverse-two}. Inductively, after the first $J$
coefficients are chosen, the residual in the logarithmic equation is
$O(N^{-J-1})$. Its derivative in $\delta$ is $a+O(N^{-1})$, bounded away
from zero. The mean value theorem transfers the residual to an inverse
error of the same order. Differentiated Stirling remainder bounds justify
the derivatives used here \cite{dlmfgamma}.
\end{proof}

\begin{writenote}[Added 1 October 2026, batch~74: an instance of repository results]
Theorem~\ref{fpf:thm:inverse} is the balanced inverse theorem
\texttt{t2:thm:balanced-inverse} of the combinatorial-inverses volume
\cite{fpf-cti}, applied to the gamma continuation \eqref{fpf:eq:gamma-continuation}
(a gamma quotient of the kind covered by that volume's engine
\texttt{t2:thm:gamma}) with $a=\log4$, $\beta=-b=-\tfrac32$, $C=A_k^*$ and
$q_j=\ell_j(k)$. That theorem's
$d_1=-q_1/a$ and $d_2=\beta q_1/a^2-q_2/a$ are the two corrections in
\eqref{fpf:eq:inverse-two}; it also gives a nonrecursive formula for every
coefficient, and its branch rule ($W_{-1}$ for $\beta<0$) is the one used
here. The integer-threshold identity $Q_k(v)=\lceil x(v)\rceil$ and the
bracket \eqref{fpf:eq:inverse-bracket} below are parts (1) and (2) (exact
rounding and the separation condition) of the staircase theorem
\texttt{p0:thm:staircase} of the transseries-and-inversion volume
\cite{fpf-tai}. The tail inversion \eqref{fpf:eq:tail-U}--\eqref{fpf:eq:tail-inverse}
is a Lambert core of the same kind. No novelty is claimed for this section.
\end{writenote}
The Lambert quantity itself begins
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:inverse-log}
 N=\frac{Y+b\log Y-b\log a}{a}
                +O\left(\frac{\log Y}{Y}\right).
\end{equation}
Repeated substitution, or the standard expansion of $W_{-1}$, expands it
in powers of $Y^{-1}$ with polynomial coefficients in $\log Y$.
Keeping $N$ in Lambert form often gives the more accurate practical
approximation at a fixed number of displayed terms.

\subsection{The rounding issue}
Define the eventual integer threshold
\[
 Q_k(v)=\min\{n\ge k+1:T(n,k)\ge v\}.
\]
For sufficiently large $v$, all earlier finitely many values are below the
target and the continuation is increasing, so $Q_k(v)=\lceil x(v)\rceil$.
However, taking the ceiling of a \emph{truncated} inverse expansion is not
uniformly safe: the exact inverse may lie arbitrarily close to an integer.
If a rigorous remainder bound is $|x(v)-x_J(v)|\le\varepsilon_J$, the
valid conclusion is the bracket
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:inverse-bracket}
 \left\lceil x_J(v)-\varepsilon_J\right\rceil
 \le Q_k(v)\le
 \left\lceil x_J(v)+\varepsilon_J\right\rceil.
\end{equation}
An exact binomial evaluation resolves any remaining one- or two-integer
ambiguity. This report proves asymptotic remainder orders; it does not
claim that the floating-point demonstrations alone provide explicit
certified remainder constants or effective monotonicity cutoffs.

\subsection{Inverting the limiting tail}
The limiting discrete law also has a useful inverse asymptotic. Extend its
exact tail formula to large real $m$ and solve
\[
 \varepsilon=2^{-m-2}(m^2+5m+2),\qquad\varepsilon\downarrow0.
\]
Put $u=m+5/2$ and $a_2=\log2$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:tail-equation}
 \varepsilon=\sqrt2\left(u^2-\frac{17}4\right)e^{-a_2u}.
\end{equation}
The leading solution is
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:tail-U}
 U=-\frac2{a_2}W_{-1}\left(-\frac{a_2}{2}
                            \sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt2}}\right).
\end{equation}
Expanding the logarithm of \eqref{fpf:eq:tail-equation} about $u=U$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:tail-inverse}
 m=U-\frac52-\frac{17}{4a_2U^2}+O(U^{-3}).
\end{equation}
Indeed, the neglected factor contributes
$\log(1-17/(4U^2))=-17/(4U^2)+O(U^{-4})$ and the derivative of
$2\log u-a_2u$ is $-a_2+2/u$. This determines both the sign and the
coefficient of the correction. Further terms follow recursively. Integer
quantiles require the same rounding care as in
\eqref{fpf:eq:inverse-bracket}. This tail inversion concerns the limiting law;
for finite $n$, the exact cumulative formula \eqref{fpf:eq:exact-tail} is
available and should be preferred when feasible.

\subsection{Stable evaluation and complexity}
The two-binomial expression gives a direct exact evaluation with two
binomial coefficients and a power of two. With big integers it is safe
from cancellation, although bit complexity grows with the output length.
For floating-point evaluation when $k$ is small relative to $\sqrt n$,
the two leading binomials nearly cancel. The stable form is
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:stable-eval}
 \log T=\log A+\log\bigl(-\operatorname{expm1}(\log R)\bigr),
\end{equation}
using \texttt{log1p} for the factors in \eqref{fpf:eq:R-product}.
The verifier uses this form for the gamma continuation and inverse tests.

For a whole table, the suffix-state dynamic program has a straightforward
$O(N^3)$ arithmetic-operation implementation through size $N$, using suffix
sums to aggregate state transitions. This is an operation count, not a
bit-complexity bound; the integer sizes increase with $N$. For an individual
fixed column, the first-order recurrence is usually more convenient after
an initial value is known.

\section{Formalization boundary and further research}\label{fpf:sec:formal}

\subsection{A concrete path into ProveIt}
The project can be formalized in layers, with a small exact core rather
than an immediate attempt to formalize the entire analytic theory.
First define the restriction to the largest $k$ entries and prove its
monotonicity with respect to pattern containment. Next formalize deletion
and insertion of the minimum as inverse operations, and prove the active
suffix lemma. Finite counting then gives the safe-prefix distribution and
the first-failure convolution. These are the genuinely combinatorial
obligations; a formal manipulation of the conjectured generating function
alone would not establish that it counts the specified permutations.

The subsequent exact algebra is well delimited: Catalan powers, the
binomial telescoper, the entry and tail formulas, and the polynomial-factor
recurrence. Each can be checked without trusting floating-point arithmetic.
The critical partition function and its moment identities can then be
proved by formal-power-series coefficient extraction.

Finally, analytic modules can establish the Stirling expansion, the
uniform coefficient-transfer bound, and the probability limits. The
repository's generic asymptotic scales and differential-block framework
provide a natural home for the formal expansions and inverse recursion
\cite{proveit}. Their existence does not discharge the analytic remainder
obligations. The exact binomial split is especially valuable because it
lets a formal statement distinguish the two exponential sectors without
assuming an unproved summability or resurgent interpretation.

\subsection{Proposed research questions}
The following questions go beyond results established in this report.
They are proposed research directions, not assertions that every one is a
previously published open problem.

\paragraph{1. A family of first-failure statistics.}
Replace $132$ by $1(r+1)r\cdots2$ while retaining global $123$ avoidance.
At a $123$-active site the right-hand suffix decreases, so a new occurrence
is created precisely when at least $r$ entries remain to the right. Thus
safe sites become $j=0,\ldots,r-1$, subject to $j\le d$.
Determine the corresponding first-failure generating functions, explicit
entry formulas where possible, and the critical exponents and limiting
laws. The safe-state process is no longer the same binary reset process;
its analysis is the substantive new step.

\begin{writenote}[Added 5 October 2026, batch~98: answered in Part~II]
Part~II answers this question for every $r\ge2$. The safe-site rule stated
here is \eqref{fpf:lp:eq:safe-rule}; the safe-state process is solved by
the Chebyshev polynomials $\Delta_r$ (Lemmas~\ref{fpf:lp:lem:safe}
and~\ref{fpf:lp:lem:spectrum}; the safe counts are classical, by Chow and
West). The first-failure generating function is
Theorem~\ref{fpf:lp:thm:gf}, and the entry formula is the finite positive
sum \eqref{fpf:lp:eq:positive-formula}; a two-binomial closed form like
\eqref{fpf:eq:main-binomial} is not given for $r\ge3$. The critical weight
is $y_c=\sec^2(\pi/(r+2))$, and for every fixed $r\ge3$ Part~II proves the
subcritical law (Theorems~\ref{fpf:lp:thm:subcritical}
and~\ref{fpf:lp:thm:geometric}), a tight critical deficit with masses
$\asymp m^{-3/2}$, total-variation rate $n^{-1/2}$ and moment threshold
$\frac12$ (Theorems~\ref{fpf:lp:thm:critical} and~\ref{fpf:lp:thm:moments}),
the supercritical law (Theorem~\ref{fpf:lp:thm:supercritical}) and a
displaced window (Theorem~\ref{fpf:lp:thm:window}). Uniformity in $r$ and
joint limits $r=r(n)$ remain open (Part~II's questions~1 and~2).
\end{writenote}

\paragraph{2. Joint first-failure and suffix statistics.}
Retain the active suffix length at the first failing insertion and at the
final permutation. Derive a multivariate generating function and determine
whether the critical beta law admits a useful joint limit with either
suffix statistic. The convolution \eqref{fpf:eq:convolution} retains the
failing state $j$ and is a starting point, but the joint limit is not
proved here.

\begin{writenote}[Added 5 October 2026, batch~98]
Still open. Part~II's question~7 generalizes it to every $r$ and adds the
early-versus-late phase indicator.
\end{writenote}

\paragraph{3. A fully uniform higher-order theory.}
The leading square-root crossover and the bulk critical correction are
proved above. Develop higher-order expansions that remain uniform as
$k/\sqrt n$ tends to zero or infinity, and match them to the fixed-deficit
region. Similarly resolve the endpoint layers of the critical cumulative
law, where \eqref{fpf:eq:beta-first-correction} is not uniform. Explicit
remainder constants would make these expansions directly useful for
certified computation.

\paragraph{4. Complex zeros and finite-size scaling.}
Study the zeros of the polynomial $Z_n(y)$ near $y=2$ and globally.
The critical window suggests that the entire function
$M(\lambda)=e^\lambda\int_0^1e^{-\lambda u^2}\,\dd u$ should govern local
complex scaling. Establish the requisite complex-uniform convergence,
locate its zeros, and connect them to the accumulation of zeros of $Z_n$.
Real-window convergence alone does not supply this theorem.

\begin{writenote}[Added 5 October 2026, batch~98]
Still open at $r=2$ (Part~II's question~5 restates it). For the patterns
$1(r+1)r\cdots2$ with $r\ge3$, Part~II locates local sequences of zeros
through the complex-uniform window limit $2a_r+P_re^{-s}$
(Theorem~\ref{fpf:lp:thm:zeros}); that function and its window are
different from $M(\lambda)$ here.
\end{writenote}

\paragraph{5. The polynomial factors $H_k$.}
Determine the geometry and asymptotic distribution of the zeros of $H_k$
as $k\to\infty$, and seek structural coefficient bases or recurrences
that explain them. Positivity on $n>k$ and the leading coefficient are
proved here; real-rootedness or any broader zero-location claim is not
assumed. A useful outcome could be sharper rational bounds on column
ratios.

\paragraph{6. Effective inverse certificates.}
Make the inverse theorems effective by producing explicit monotonicity
cutoffs and remainder constants, then combine interval evaluation of
$W_{-1}$ and gamma functions with exact binomial checks. The goal is a
certified threshold algorithm, with a proof that a short interval contains
the answer, rather than an unqualified rounding of an asymptotic formula.

\paragraph{7. Direct bijections to familiar Catalan objects.}
Translate the first-failure parameter into a natural Dyck-path or ordered-tree
statistic, preferably preserving the two low-column interpretations.
The insertion tree already supplies an encoding, but a transparent
object-level bijection may explain the two-binomial cancellation and the
critical beta law without generating functions.

\begin{writenote}[Added 5 October 2026, batch~98]
Part~II's question~8 asks the same for every $r$.
\end{writenote}

\paragraph{8. Kernel-checked enumeration and reusable analytic interfaces.}
Implement the exact layer in Lean and use it as a test case for a reusable
interface between finite combinatorial enumeration, rational generating
functions over the Catalan extension, and asymptotic statements with
explicit remainders. The distinction between exact algebraic identities,
formal transseries, and analytic estimates should remain visible in the
theorem signatures and audit output.

\begin{writenote}[Added 5 October 2026, batch~98]
Part~II's question~10 extends this to the positive formula for every $r$
and to certified sampling; nothing in either Part is formalized.
\end{writenote}

\subsection{Conclusion}
The posted conjecture for A273821 is accessible through the first insertion
that creates a forbidden pattern. That combinatorial event leads to a
positive Catalan convolution, a telescoping binomial formula, and a complete
fixed-column asymptotic expansion. It also reveals behavior invisible in
the original growth-rate comment: a square-root crossover, a precise
separation of exponential sectors, and a weighted transition from bounded
first-failure time to bounded final deficit, with a beta law exactly at
criticality. The exact formulas, rather than numerical extrapolation,
support every asserted asymptotic regime.

\section{The question and the contribution}
\label{fpf:lp:sec:question}

An occurrence of a pattern in a permutation means an order-isomorphic
subsequence; the positions need not be consecutive. For \(r\ge2\), put
\[
 \tau_r=1(r+1)r\cdots2.
\]
For \(\pi\in\Av_n(123)\), let \(K_r(\pi)\) be the largest \(k\) such
that the subsequence consisting of the \(k\) largest values of \(\pi\),
in their original order, avoids \(\tau_r\). Thus \(K_r=n\) when the
whole permutation avoids both patterns. If \(K_r=k<n\), the next smaller
value is the first one whose inclusion creates \(\tau_r\).

Write
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:counts}
 T_r(n,k)=\#\{\pi\in\Av_n(123):K_r(\pi)=k\},\qquad
 Z_{n,r}(y)=\sum_{k=1}^n T_r(n,k)y^k.
\end{equation}
For real \(y>0\), the associated probability measure assigns weight
\(y^{K_r(\pi)}/Z_{n,r}(y)\) to \(\pi\). We denote its prefix and deficit
by \(K_{n,r}^{(y)}\) and \(M_{n,r}^{(y)}=n-K_{n,r}^{(y)}\).
The word ``prefix'' refers to the successive restriction to largest
values, not to consecutive positions of the original permutation.

At \(r=2\), \(T_r\) is David Callan's triangle A273821
\cite{oeis273821}. Its OEIS page still marks a generating function
conjectural in the retrieved record (retrieved by the manuscript on
4~October 2026, local date). Part~I of this report, however, already
proves that formula and studies its critical beta law. Its
Section~\ref{fpf:sec:formal}, first proposed research question,
specifically asks for the extension to \(1(r+1)r\cdots2\), including exact
enumeration and critical laws. That is the question answered here. The
inspected repository snapshot is \lprepo. The source is in
\begin{center}\small
\nolinkurl{SetTheory/Cardinals/docs/reports/enumerative-combinatorics/}\par
\nolinkurl{a273821-first-pattern-failure/article.tex}.
\end{center}

\subsection{What is prior, and what is proved here}

The unrefined enumeration of permutations avoiding both patterns is
classical. Reverse-complement fixes \(123\) and changes \(\tau_r\) to
\(r(r-1)\cdots1(r+1)\). Chow--West \cite{fpf-lp-cw}, Theorem 3.1, give the
corresponding Chebyshev quotient and growth constant; Krattenthaler
\cite{fpf-lp-kratt}, Theorem 9, supplies a Dyck-path formulation, while his
Theorem 8 marks occurrences rather than first failure. Related stopping
statistics in Catalan permutations also predate this work
\cite{fpf-lp-firstascent}. We rederive the needed safe-state formulas

\section{Exact first-failure enumeration}
\label{fpf:lp:sec:enumeration}

Put
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:C-D}
 C(x)=\frac{2}{1+\sqrt{1-4x}},\qquad
 \Delta_r(u)=\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor(r+1)/2\rfloor}
             (-u)^j\binom{r+1-j}{j}.
\end{equation}
The square root is the germ equal to one at the origin. In particular,
\(C(0)=1\), \(C=1+xC^2\), and
\[
 \Delta_0=1,\quad \Delta_1=1-u,\quad \Delta_r=\Delta_{r-1}-u\Delta_{r-2}.
\]

\begin{theorem}[The whole first-failure family]\label{fpf:lp:thm:gf}
For every integer \(r\ge2\), as an identity of formal power series,
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:main-gf}
 \boxed{\displaystyle
 F_r(x,y):=\sum_{n\ge1}\sum_{k=1}^n T_r(n,k)x^ny^k
 =\frac{u\Delta_{r-2}(u)}{\Delta_r(u)}
  +\frac{x u^r C(x)^{r+1}}{\Delta_r(u)(1-uC(x))},\qquad u=xy.}
\end{equation}
Let \(e_h=[u^h]\Delta_r(u)^{-1}\), with \(e_h=0\) for \(h<0\), and set
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:ballot}
 B(m,p)=[x^m]C(x)^p
 =\frac{p}{2m+p}\binom{2m+p}{m}\qquad(m\ge0,\ p\ge1).
\end{equation}
Then, for \(n>k\ge r\),
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:positive-formula}
 T_r(n,k)=\sum_{j=r}^k e_{k-j}B(n-k-1,j+1).
\end{equation}
Off the diagonal, \(T_r(n,k)=0\) when \(k<r\). On the diagonal,
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:safe-total}
 \sum_{n\ge1}T_r(n,n)u^n=\frac{u\Delta_{r-2}(u)}{\Delta_r(u)},\qquad
 1+\sum_{n\ge1}T_r(n,n)u^n=\frac{\Delta_{r-1}(u)}{\Delta_r(u)}.
\end{equation}
All terms in \eqref{fpf:lp:eq:positive-formula} are nonnegative. Every row
sums to the Catalan number \(C_n=(n+1)^{-1}\binom{2n}{n}\).
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Insertion of successive minima}

Deleting the minimum of a nonempty permutation and standardizing gives
its unique parent. Reverse this operation, starting from the one-entry
permutation. Let \(d\) be the length of the terminal decreasing run of
consecutive positions. An insertion site is indexed by the number \(j\)
of old entries to its right.

\begin{lemma}[Active sites and descendants]\label{fpf:lp:lem:active}
In a \(123\)-avoiding permutation of state \(d\), insertion of a new
minimum preserves \(123\) avoidance precisely at \(0\le j\le d\).
Appending it, \(j=0\), gives state \(d+1\); the other sites give state
\(j\). The generating function for descendants obtained by any number
of further allowed insertions is \(C(x)^{d+1}\).
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Any new increasing triple must start at the inserted minimum. Such a
triple exists exactly when the suffix to its right contains an increasing
pair. That suffix must therefore decrease, proving the active-site rule.
If the minimum is not appended, the decreasing suffix on its right is
separated from the preceding entries by an ascent, proving the state rule.

For the descendant series \(D_d\), this gives
\[
 D_d=1+x\sum_{j=1}^{d+1}D_j.
\]
The solution with all constant terms one is unique coefficient by
coefficient: the coefficient of degree \(m\) uses only degree \(m-1\)
at finitely many states. The proposed solution satisfies it because
\[
 x\sum_{j=1}^{d+1}C^{j+1}
 =(C-1)(1+C+\cdots+C^d)=C^{d+1}-1.
\]
Starting from state one, the number of size-\(n\) permutations is
\([x^{n-1}]C^2=C_n\).
\end{proof}

\begin{writenote}[Added 5 October 2026, batch~98: a second route]
Lemma~\ref{fpf:lp:lem:active} is Part~I's Lemma~\ref{fpf:lem:active}
together with Lemma~\ref{fpf:lem:descendants}, proved in the same way; it
does not involve \(r\). The manuscript names the descendant series
\(H_d\); it is Part~I's \(D_d\).
\end{writenote}

If the current permutation avoids \(\tau_r\), a new occurrence has to
start at the new minimum. At an active site the right suffix decreases,
so the insertion creates \(\tau_r\) exactly when \(j\ge r\). Safe
insertions thus have the state rule
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:safe-rule}
 d\longrightarrow d+1,\ 1,2,\ldots,\min(d,r-1).
\end{equation}
An occurrence, once created, cannot disappear when smaller entries are
inserted. This monotonicity makes a decomposition at the first failing
insertion exact.

\subsection{The safe suffix distribution}

Let \(a_{k,d}\) count safe size-\(k\) permutations of suffix state \(d\),
and \(A_d(u)=\sum_{k\ge1}a_{k,d}u^k\).

\begin{lemma}[Safe states and their tails]\label{fpf:lp:lem:safe}
For \(1\le d\le r-1\),
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:safe-states}
 A_d(u)=\frac{u^d\Delta_{r-d}(u)}{\Delta_r(u)},
\end{equation}
and, for \(d\ge r-1\),
\[
 A_d(u)=\frac{u^d(1-u)}{\Delta_r(u)}.
\]
The two expressions agree at \(d=r-1\). In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:safe-tail}
 \sum_{d\ge1}A_d(u)=\frac{u\Delta_{r-2}(u)}{\Delta_r(u)},\qquad
 \sum_{d\ge j}A_d(u)=\frac{u^j}{\Delta_r(u)}\quad(j\ge r).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
With \(A_0=0\), the state equations from \eqref{fpf:lp:eq:safe-rule} are
\[
 A_d=\mathbf1_{d=1}u+uA_{d-1}+u\sum_{e\ge d}A_e
       \quad(1\le d\le r-1),\qquad
 A_d=uA_{d-1}\quad(d\ge r).
\]
They determine every coefficient uniquely; in size \(k\) only states
\(d\le k\) occur. For the proposed expressions, summation gives
\[
 \sum_{e\ge d}A_e
 =\frac{u^d\Delta_{r-d-1}(u)}{\Delta_r(u)}\quad(1\le d\le r-1).
\]
This identity follows by telescoping the recurrence for \(\Delta\), with
the geometric tail at \(d=r-1\). For \(d\ge2\), subtraction of the two
successive formulas for \(A_d\) then gives the required equation. At
\(d=1\), it is the identity \(\Delta_{r-1}=\Delta_r+u\Delta_{r-2}\).
Summing the geometric tail proves the second assertion of
\eqref{fpf:lp:eq:safe-tail}.
\end{proof}

\begin{writenote}[Added 5 October 2026, batch~98: the case \(r=2\)]
At \(r=2\), \(\Delta_2=1-2u\) and every state is covered by the second
formula: \(A_d(u)=u^d(1-u)/(1-2u)\), whose coefficients are
\(a_{d,d}=1\) and \(a_{k,d}=2^{k-d-1}\) for \(k>d\); the tail is
\(u^j/(1-2u)\). This is Part~I's Lemma~\ref{fpf:lem:safe-count} with
\eqref{fpf:eq:safe-tail}, obtained from the state equations instead of the
position of the last reset.
\end{writenote}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{fpf:lp:thm:gf}]
The diagonal is the safe class and contributes the first term of
\eqref{fpf:lp:eq:main-gf}. Suppose the first failure occurs after \(k\)
safe entries, at a site with \(j\ge r\) entries to the right. The safe
prefix must have suffix state at least \(j\); its generating function
is \(u^j/\Delta_r(u)\). The failing insertion contributes \(x\), not
\(xy\), because it does not increase \(K_r\). By
Lemma~\ref{fpf:lp:lem:active}, subsequent insertions contribute
\(C(x)^{j+1}\). Summing gives
\[
 \frac{x}{\Delta_r(u)}\sum_{j\ge r}u^jC(x)^{j+1}
 =\frac{x u^rC(x)^{r+1}}{\Delta_r(u)(1-uC(x))}.
\]
Coefficient extraction proves \eqref{fpf:lp:eq:positive-formula}; the
ballot identity \eqref{fpf:lp:eq:ballot} follows from Lagrange inversion of
\(C=1+xC^2\). Positivity of \(e_h\) also follows from the positive-root
factorization in Lemma~\ref{fpf:lp:lem:spectrum} below. The insertion
decomposition partitions all \(123\)-avoiding permutations, giving the row
sums.
\end{proof}

\begin{writenote}[Added 5 October 2026, batch~98: a second route]
At \(r=2\), \(e_h=2^h\) and \(B(m,j+1)=b(m,j)\) of \eqref{fpf:eq:ballot},

\eqref{fpf:eq:convolution}, the diagonal \eqref{fpf:lp:eq:safe-total} is

\end{writenote}

\subsection{Specializations and an exact recurrence}

For \(r=2\), \(\Delta_2=1-2u\), and \eqref{fpf:lp:eq:main-gf} becomes
exactly the repository's proved A273821 formula \eqref{fpf:eq:main-gf}. For
\(r=3\),
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:r3-gf}
 F_3(x,y)=\frac{u(1-u)}{1-3u+u^2}
 +\frac{x u^3C(x)^4}{(1-3u+u^2)(1-uC(x))}.
\end{equation}
Its diagonal is \(T_3(n,n)=\operatorname{Fib}(2n-1)\), with the usual
Fibonacci convention \(\operatorname{Fib}(1)=\operatorname{Fib}(2)=1\). This
diagonal identity is classical.

\begin{writenote}[Added 5 October 2026, batch~98: a proof of the diagonal identity]
The manuscript cites no source for it; it follows from
\eqref{fpf:lp:eq:r3-gf}. Since \(\operatorname{Fib}(m+2)=
3\operatorname{Fib}(m)-\operatorname{Fib}(m-2)\), the sequence
\(\psi_n=\operatorname{Fib}(2n-1)\) satisfies
\(\psi_n=3\psi_{n-1}-\psi_{n-2}\) for \(n\ge3\), with \(\psi_1=1\),
\(\psi_2=2\). Hence \((1-3u+u^2)\sum_{n\ge1}\psi_nu^n=u-u^2\), which is the
diagonal term \(u(1-u)/(1-3u+u^2)\) of \eqref{fpf:lp:eq:r3-gf}. (Checked by
series expansion through \(n=40\) in this write.)
\end{writenote}

Let \(\delta_j=(-1)^j\binom{r+1-j}{j}\). Multiplication by \(\Delta_r\)
gives, off the diagonal and for \(n>k\ge r\),
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:recurrence}
 T_r(n,k)=-\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor(r+1)/2\rfloor}
 \delta_jT_r(n-j,k-j)+B(n-k-1,k+1).
\end{equation}
Terms outside the defined triangle are zero. The diagonal is supplied
by \eqref{fpf:lp:eq:safe-total}. This recurrence fills all rows through size
\(N\) in \(O(rN^2)\) arithmetic operations, apart from the bit cost of
the integer arithmetic and ballot coefficients.

For the first possible failing column,
\[
 T_r(n,r)=B(n-r-1,r+1)\qquad(n>r).
\]
More generally, every fixed column \(k\ge r\) has growth constant four:
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:fixed-column}
 T_r(n,k)\sim\frac{4^n}{\sqrt\pi\,n^{3/2}}
 \sum_{j=r}^k e_{k-j}\frac{(j+1)2^j}{4^{k+1}}.
\end{equation}
This follows term by term from the finite positive formula and
Stirling's formula for \eqref{fpf:lp:eq:ballot}. Thus the fixed-column part
of the repository question has a positive explicit answer for every \(r\).

\begin{writenote}[Added 5 October 2026, batch~98: the case \(r=2\)]

\section{The spectral data and the subcritical law}
\label{fpf:lp:sec:subcritical}

\begin{lemma}[Classical Chebyshev spectrum]\label{fpf:lp:lem:spectrum}
The polynomial \(\Delta_r\) satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:chebyshev}
 \Delta_r(u)=u^{(r+1)/2}U_{r+1}\!\left(\frac1{2\sqrt u}\right),
\end{equation}
understood as a polynomial identity. Its simple roots are
\[
 \rho_{r,j}=\frac1{4\cos^2(j\pi/(r+2))},\qquad
 1\le j\le h_r:=\lfloor(r+1)/2\rfloor.
\]
They are positive and increasing. In particular, \(\rho_r=\rho_{r,1}\)
is the unique root of smallest modulus and
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:d-r}
 \vartheta_r:=-\rho_r\Delta_r'(\rho_r)>0.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The right side of \eqref{fpf:lp:eq:chebyshev} has the same recurrence and
first two values as \(\Delta_r\). The usual formula
\(U_j(\cos\theta)=\sin((j+1)\theta)/\sin\theta\) gives the roots,
with the opposite cosine roots paired by squaring. Consequently
\(\Delta_r(u)=\prod_{j=1}^{h_r}(1-u/\rho_{r,j})\).
The derivative at the first root is negative. This also proves the
nonnegativity of the coefficients of \(1/\Delta_r\).
\end{proof}

Define, for \(0<y<y_c\),
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:L}
 L_r(y)=
 \frac{2^{r-2}(y/4)^r\bigl((r+1)-ry/2\bigr)}
 {\Delta_r(y/4)(1-y/2)^2}.
\end{equation}
In particular \(L_r(1)=1\), as follows either from Catalan row sums or
from \(\Delta_r(1/4)=(r+2)/2^{r+1}\).

\begin{theorem}[Subcritical prefix distribution]\label{fpf:lp:thm:subcritical}
For fixed \(r\ge2\) and \(0<y<y_c\),
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:sub-z}
 Z_{n,r}(y)=\frac{4^n}{\sqrt\pi\,n^{3/2}}
               \bigl(L_r(y)+O(n^{-1})\bigr).
\end{equation}
The random variable \(K_{n,r}^{(y)}\) converges to a discrete variable
\(K_r^{(y)}\) with probability generating function
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:sub-pgf}
 \E t^{K_r^{(y)}}=\frac{L_r(yt)}{L_r(y)}.
\end{equation}
For some \(R>1\),
\[
 \sum_{k\ge0}R^k\left|
 \Prob(K_{n,r}^{(y)}=k)-\Prob(K_r^{(y)}=k)\right|=O(n^{-1}).
\]
The constants may depend on \(r\) and \(y\). In particular, all fixed
moments converge.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let \(z=\sqrt{1-4x}\). In \eqref{fpf:lp:eq:main-gf}, \(u=xy=y(1-z^2)/4\)
has no term linear in \(z\), while \(C(x)=2-2z+O(z^2)\). Direct
expansion gives the coefficient of \(z\) as \(-2L_r(y)\). Both
denominators stay away from zero at \(x=1/4\) for \(|y|<y_c\), because
\(y_c\le2\). The expression is analytic in a dented neighborhood of
that point, uniformly when \(y\) stays in a smaller disk. Expansion
through the next odd power and the coefficient formula for
\((1-4x)^{1/2}\) prove \eqref{fpf:lp:eq:sub-z}. The remaining integer
powers are analytic locally and the next singular contribution has order
\(4^nn^{-5/2}\). This is the elementary square-root case of the transfer
principle \cite{fpf-lp-fo}.

Apply the same expansion at \(yt\) on a fixed circle \(|t|=R_1>1\)
with \(yR_1<y_c\), and divide by \eqref{fpf:lp:eq:sub-z}. The resulting
probability generating functions differ uniformly by \(O(n^{-1})\).
Cauchy's coefficient estimate gives an error \(O(R_1^{-k}n^{-1})\).
Summing after multiplication by any fixed \(1<R<R_1\) proves the
assertion, including that the limiting coefficients form a probability
distribution.
\end{proof}

\subsection{A positive independent-sum representation}

Our convention is that \(G\sim\Geom(q)\), for \(0\le q<1\), means
\(\Prob(G=m)=(1-q)q^m\) for \(m\ge0\).

\begin{theorem}[Geometric decomposition]\label{fpf:lp:thm:geometric}
Put
\[
 q_j=y\cos^2\!\frac{j\pi}{r+2}\quad(1\le j\le h_r),\qquad
 q_0=y/2,\qquad \beta_y=\frac1{1+r(1-q_0)}.
\]
For \(0<y<y_c\), all \(q_j,q_0<1\), and
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:independent}
 K_r^{(y)}\ \overset{d}=\
 r+\sum_{j=1}^{h_r}G_j+G+I G',
\end{equation}
where all variables are independent,
\(G_j\sim\Geom(q_j)\), \(G,G'\sim\Geom(q_0)\), and
\(I\sim\Ber(\beta_y)\).
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Factor \(\Delta_r(yt/4)\) using Lemma~\ref{fpf:lp:lem:spectrum}. After
extracting its normalized geometric factors and \(t^r\), the remaining
factor of \(L_r(yt)/L_r(y)\) is
\[
 \frac{1-q_0}{1-q_0t}
 \left(1-\beta_y+\beta_y\frac{1-q_0}{1-q_0t}\right).
\]
This is the generating function of \(G+I G'\), proving the claim.
\end{proof}

At \(y=1\), write \(K_r^\infty=K_r^{(1)}\). The distribution of
\(V_r=G+IG'\) becomes especially simple:
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:V}
 \Prob(V_r=m)=\frac{r+m+1}{(r+2)2^{m+1}},\qquad m\ge0.
\end{equation}
Logarithmic differentiation of \(\Delta_r\), or the independent sum, gives
\begin{align}
 \E K_r^\infty
  &=\frac{r(r+1)}6+r+1+\frac2{r+2},\label{fpf:lp:eq:mean-K}\\
 \Var K_r^\infty
  &=\frac{r(r+1)(r+3)(r+4)}{90}
       +2+\frac6{r+2}-\frac4{(r+2)^2}.\label{fpf:lp:eq:var-K}
\end{align}
For example, \(\E K_3^\infty=32/5\). The identities needed for these
computations are
\[
 \Delta_r(1/4)=\frac{r+2}{2^{r+1}},\qquad
 -\frac{\Delta_r'(1/4)}{4\Delta_r(1/4)}=\frac{r(r+1)}6,
\]
and the corresponding second logarithmic derivative. They follow by
expanding the two-root formula for \(\Delta_r\) in
Section~\ref{fpf:lp:sec:growing}.

For fixed \(r\ge3\), as \(y\uparrow y_c\), only \(q_1=y/y_c\)
approaches one. All the other independent summands stay tight. Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:sub-edge-exp}

\end{equation}
For \(r=2\), all three geometric contributions become large and

\section{An iterated limit for long forbidden patterns}
\label{fpf:lp:sec:growing}

The geometric decomposition makes it possible to let the pattern length
grow \emph{after} the permutation-size limit has been taken. It also
gives a quantitative comparison rather than only a Laplace-transform
calculation.

\begin{theorem}[Long-pattern law with an explicit rate]\label{fpf:lp:thm:growing}
Let \(E_j\), \(j\ge1\), be independent exponentials of rate one and put
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:W}
 \mathcal W=\sum_{j\ge1}\frac{E_j}{\pi^2j^2}.
\end{equation}
The sum is finite almost surely and in \(L^1\). With \(d_{\mathrm W}\)
denoting the Wasserstein distance for the cost \(|x-y|\),
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:W1}
 d_{\mathrm W}\!\left(\Law\!\left(\frac{K_r^\infty}{(r+2)^2}\right),
 \Law(\mathcal W)\right)
 \le\frac{9r^2+28r+32}{6(r+2)^3}.
\end{equation}
In particular, \(K_r^\infty/r^2\Rightarrow \mathcal W\), and
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:laplace-W}
 \E e^{-s\mathcal W}=\frac{\sqrt{s}}{\sinh\sqrt{s}}\quad(s\ge0),\qquad
 \E \mathcal W=\frac16,\qquad\Var \mathcal W=\frac1{90}.
\end{equation}
The quotient at \(s=0\) is interpreted by continuity.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let \(N=r+2\), \(h=h_r\), and
\(\gamma_j=-2\log\cos(\pi j/N)\). Couple the geometricals in
\eqref{fpf:lp:eq:independent}, at \(y=1\), by
\[
 G_j=\left\lfloor E_j/\gamma_j\right\rfloor.
\]
Indeed \(\Prob(G_j\ge m)=e^{-m\gamma_j}
=\cos^{2m}(\pi j/N)\). Since
\(-2\log\cos\theta\ge\theta^2\) on \([0,\pi/2)\),
\[
 0\le S_r:=\frac1{N^2}\sum_{j=1}^hG_j\le \mathcal W.
\]
The trigonometric sum implicit in \eqref{fpf:lp:eq:mean-K} gives
\[
 \E(\mathcal W-S_r)=\frac16-\frac{r(r+1)}{6N^2}
 =\frac{3r+4}{6N^2}.
\]
Choose \(V_r\) independently, as in \eqref{fpf:lp:eq:V}; then
\(\E V_r=1+2/N\). The triangle inequality in this coupling yields
\[
 \E\left|\frac{r+V_r}{N^2}+S_r-\mathcal W\right|
 \le\frac{r+1+2/N}{N^2}+\frac{3r+4}{6N^2},
\]
which simplifies to \eqref{fpf:lp:eq:W1}. The series for \(\mathcal W\) has
finite expectation \(\sum_j(\pi^2j^2)^{-1}=1/6\), hence is finite almost
surely. Independence and monotone convergence give the product of its
Laplace transforms. Euler's product for \(\sinh\), recorded in
\cite{fpf-lp-dlmf}, reduces that product to \eqref{fpf:lp:eq:laplace-W}.
Summing the variances gives \(1/90\). Finally \(N^2/r^2\to1\).
\end{proof}

For a direct algebraic check, put \(\nu=\sqrt{1-y}\). The recurrence for
\(\Delta_r\) gives
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:D-two-roots}
 \Delta_r(y/4)=
 \frac{(1+\nu)^{r+2}-(1-\nu)^{r+2}}{2^{r+2}\nu}.
\end{equation}
At \(y=e^{-s/r^2}\), \(r\nu\to\sqrt{s}\); substitution into
\eqref{fpf:lp:eq:L} again gives \(L_r(e^{-s/r^2})\to\sqrt{s}/\sinh\sqrt{s}\).
This is an independent consistency check of the scale and constants.

The order of limits matters. The theorem concerns
\(\lim_{r\to\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}\), with \(r\) fixed in the
inner limit. No assertion is made here for arbitrary choices \(r=r(n)\).
The spectral gap used later shrinks with \(r\), so such a joint theorem
requires additional estimates.

\section{A uniform estimate in prefix size and deficit}
\label{fpf:lp:sec:kernel}

From this point through Section~\ref{fpf:lp:sec:zeros}, fix \(r\ge3\). We
often omit \(r\) from \(\rho_r,\vartheta_r\) and from constants. The strict
inequality \(\rho<1/2\) is essential.

Define a series with nonnegative coefficients by
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:residue-series}
 \mathcal P_r(t)=\frac1{\vartheta_r}\left[
 \rho_r\Delta_{r-2}(\rho_r)
 +\frac{(t/4)\rho_r^r C(t/4)^{r+1}}{1-\rho_r C(t/4)}\right]
 =\sum_{m\ge0}p_{r,m}t^m.
\end{equation}
It has finite positive total mass
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:P}
 P_r=\mathcal P_r(1)=\frac1{\vartheta_r}\left[
 \rho_r\Delta_{r-2}(\rho_r)
 +\frac{2^{r-1}\rho_r^r}{1-2\rho_r}\right].
\end{equation}
Set
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:a}
 a_r=\frac{2^{r-2}\rho_r^r\bigl((r+1)-2r\rho_r\bigr)}
 {\vartheta_r\sqrt\pi(1-2\rho_r)^2}>0.
\end{equation}
These are unnormalized coefficients: the probability law used below
will have probabilities \(p_{r,m}/P_r\).

\begin{lemma}[The residue tail]\label{fpf:lp:lem:tail}
As \(m\to\infty\),
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:p-tail}
 p_{r,m}=a_r m^{-3/2}+O_r(m^{-5/2}).
\end{equation}
Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:tail-sum}
 \sum_{m>n}p_{r,m}=2a_r n^{-1/2}+O_r(n^{-3/2}).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
With \(z=\sqrt{1-t}\), the expansion
\(C(t/4)=2-2z+2z^2+O(z^3)\) gives
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:P-local}
 \mathcal P_r(t)=P_r-2\sqrt\pi\,a_r\sqrt{1-t}+O(1-t).
\end{equation}
For clarity, the linear coefficient before division by \(\vartheta_r\) is
minus
\[
 \frac{2^{r-1}\rho_r^r}{1-2\rho_r}
 \left(r+1+\frac{2\rho_r}{1-2\rho_r}\right).
\]
There is a convergent expansion in powers of \(z\) near zero. The next
noninteger power of \(1-t\) is \((1-t)^{3/2}\). On \(|t|\le1\),
\(\rho_r|C(t/4)|\le2\rho_r<1\), so the denominator does not vanish;
the same holds in a sufficiently small dented extension near \(t=1\).
There are no other singularities on the unit circle. Applying the
square-root coefficient formula, including its next-order error, yields
\eqref{fpf:lp:eq:p-tail}. Finally
\(\sum_{m>n}m^{-3/2}=2n^{-1/2}+O(n^{-3/2})\), by integral comparison
with the one-step endpoint error; the sum of the remainder is
\(O(n^{-3/2})\).
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Uniform two-index residue approximation]\label{fpf:lp:thm:kernel}
There exist constants \(\Theta_r<\infty\) and \(0<\eta_r<1\) such that
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:kernel}
 \boxed{\displaystyle
 \left|\rho_r^k4^{-m}T_r(k+m,k)-p_{r,m}\right|
 \le \Theta_r\eta_r^k(m+1)^{-3/2}\qquad(k,m\ge0).}
\end{equation}
Here \(T_r(0,0)=0\), and all entries with \(k=0\) are zero.
The constants depend on \(r\), but neither index is restricted.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Mark prefix and deficit separately:
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:separate-gf}
 \mathcal G_r(u,t):=\sum_{k,m\ge0}T_r(k+m,k)u^kt^m
 =\frac{u\Delta_{r-2}(u)+t u^rC(t)^{r+1}/(1-uC(t))}{\Delta_r(u)}.
\end{equation}
Choose
\(\rho_r<R<\min(\rho_{r,2},1/2)\), where a missing second root is
omitted. On \(|u|=R\), \(\Delta_r(u)\) is bounded away from zero.
For each fixed \(m\), let \(g_m(u)=[t^m]\mathcal G_r(u,t/4)\).
The only pole of \(g_m\) between a small circle about zero and
\(|u|=R\) is the simple pole at \(u=\rho_r\). Its principal part is
\(p_{r,m}/(1-u/\rho_r)\). Thus Cauchy's formula and the residue theorem
give
\[
 4^{-m}T_r(k+m,k)-p_{r,m}\rho_r^{-k}
 =\frac1{2\pi\ii}\int_{|u|=R}g_m(u)u^{-k-1}\,\dd u.
\]

It remains to bound \(g_m\) uniformly in \(m\). For integers \(p,m\ge1\),
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:ballot-bound}
 [t^m]C(t/4)^p
 =4^{-m}\frac{p}{2m+p}\binom{2m+p}{m}
 \le\frac{p2^{p-1}}{\sqrt\pi\,m^{3/2}}.
\end{equation}
Indeed \(\binom{2m+p}{m}\le2^p\binom{2m}{m}\), since each successive
ratio \((2m+j)/(m+j)\le2\), and
\(\binom{2m}{m}\le4^m/\sqrt{\pi m}\).
Expand \((1-uC)^{-1}\) geometrically in the numerator of
\eqref{fpf:lp:eq:separate-gf}. Bound \eqref{fpf:lp:eq:ballot-bound} reduces
the resulting majorant to a constant times
\(\sum_{j\ge0}(r+j+1)(2R)^j\), which converges. The leading factor
\(t/4\) only shifts the coefficient index; the cases \(m=0,1\) have
finite bounds and can be absorbed into the constant. Therefore
\[
 \sup_{|u|=R}|g_m(u)|\le \Theta_r'(m+1)^{-3/2}.
\]
Multiply the contour estimate by \(\rho_r^k\), and take
\(\eta_r=\rho_r/R<1\).
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Why this estimate is stronger than a pointwise limit]
\label{fpf:lp:rem:pointwise}
For each fixed deficit, a simple residue calculation already predicts
\(p_{r,m}\). It does not by itself control deficits growing with \(n\),
moments dominated by rare large deficits, or a moving weight. The summable
factor \((m+1)^{-3/2}\) in \eqref{fpf:lp:eq:kernel} supplies exactly those
missing controls. The possibility of choosing \(R<1/2\) is the analytic
distinction between \(r\ge3\) and \(r=2\).
\end{remark}

\begin{writenote}[Added 5 October 2026, batch~98: the series at \(r=2\), and why the estimate fails there]

\section{Critical condensation and the full moment threshold}
\label{fpf:lp:sec:critical}

\begin{theorem}[Critical partition function and deficit law]
\label{fpf:lp:thm:critical}
At \(y=y_c\),
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:critical-Z}
 Z_{n,r}(y_c)=4^n\left(P_r-\frac{2a_r}{\sqrt n}
                                  +O_r(n^{-3/2})\right).
\end{equation}
The deficit \(M_{n,r}^{(y_c)}\) converges to a variable \(M_r\) with
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:critical-law}
 \Prob(M_r=m)=\frac{p_{r,m}}{P_r},\qquad
 \E t^{M_r}=\frac{\mathcal P_r(t)}{P_r}.
\end{equation}
With total variation defined as one half the sum of absolute mass
differences, the convergence rate has the sharp leading term
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:critical-TV}
 \TV\bigl(\Law(M_{n,r}^{(y_c)}),\Law(M_r)\bigr)
 =\frac{2a_r}{P_r\sqrt n}+O_r(n^{-3/2}).
\end{equation}
Moreover,
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:prob-tail}
 \Prob(M_r=m)\sim\frac{a_r}{P_r}m^{-3/2},\qquad
 \Prob(M_r\ge m)\sim\frac{2a_r}{P_r}m^{-1/2}.
\end{equation}
In particular, \(K_{n,r}^{(y_c)}/n\to1\) in probability.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Write \(\varpi_{k,m}=\rho_r^k4^{-m}T_r(k+m,k)\). At \(y_c=4\rho_r\),
\[
 4^{-n}Z_{n,r}(y_c)=\sum_{m=0}^n\varpi_{n-m,m}.
\]
For any fixed \(0<\eta<1\), splitting at \(k=n/2\) shows
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:error-sum}
 \sum_{k=0}^n\eta^k(n-k+1)^{-3/2}=O(n^{-3/2}).
\end{equation}
Thus Theorem~\ref{fpf:lp:thm:kernel} replaces the sum by
\(\sum_{m\le n}p_{r,m}\) with error \(O(n^{-3/2})\). Equation
\eqref{fpf:lp:eq:tail-sum} proves \eqref{fpf:lp:eq:critical-Z}.

Let \(Q_n\) be the law with probabilities
\(p_{r,m}/\sum_{j\le n}p_{r,j}\), for \(0\le m\le n\).
The sum of absolute errors in the unnormalized weights is
\(O(n^{-3/2})\), so normalization gives
\(\TV(\Law(M_{n,r}^{(y_c)}),Q_n)=O(n^{-3/2})\).
The total variation between a probability law and its conditioning to
\(m\le n\) is exactly its omitted tail mass. Hence
\[
 \TV(Q_n,\Law(M_r))=\frac1{P_r}\sum_{m>n}p_{r,m}.
\]
The triangle inequality, in both directions, now proves
\eqref{fpf:lp:eq:critical-TV}. The tail statements follow from
Lemma~\ref{fpf:lp:lem:tail}. Tightness of the deficits implies
\(M_{n,r}^{(y_c)}/n\to0\).
\end{proof}

The limiting deficit has infinite mean. This is compatible with tightness:
most probability mass remains near small deficits, while rare large
deficits dominate the expectation. The following theorem quantifies
this distinction for every positive moment.

\begin{theorem}[Critical moment threshold]\label{fpf:lp:thm:moments}
For real \(b>0\), as \(n\to\infty\),
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:all-moments}
 \E\bigl(M_{n,r}^{(y_c)}\bigr)^b=
 \begin{cases}
  \E M_r^b+o(1),&0<b<1/2,\\[2pt]
  (a_r/P_r)\log n+O_r(1),&b=1/2,\\[2pt]
  \displaystyle\frac{a_r}{P_r(b-1/2)}\,n^{b-1/2}(1+o(1)),&b>1/2.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:first-two-moments}
 \E M_{n,r}^{(y_c)}\sim\frac{2a_r}{P_r}\sqrt n,
 \qquad
 \Var(M_{n,r}^{(y_c)})\sim\frac{2a_r}{3P_r}n^{3/2}.
\end{equation}
For every positive integer \(h\), both ordinary and falling-factorial
moments have leading term
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:factorial-moments}
 \E\bigl(M_{n,r}^{(y_c)}\bigr)^h
 \sim\E\fall{M_{n,r}^{(y_c)}}{h}
 \sim\frac{2a_r}{P_r(2h-1)}n^{h-1/2}.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
After multiplying \eqref{fpf:lp:eq:kernel} by \(m^b\) and summing along
\(k+m=n\), the error is \(O(n^{b-3/2})\): for \(k\le n/2\) the
factor \(m^{b}(m+1)^{-3/2}\) is \(O(n^{b-3/2})\), and for
\(k>n/2\) exponential decay absorbs any fixed power of \(n\).
Thus the unnormalized moment is governed by
\(\sum_{m\le n}m^bp_{r,m}\). Insert \eqref{fpf:lp:eq:p-tail}. The leading
power sum converges for \(b<1/2\), is logarithmic at \(b=1/2\), and
has equivalent \(n^{b-1/2}/(b-1/2)\) for \(b>1/2\). The next-order
tail gives a smaller contribution in every case. Division by
\eqref{fpf:lp:eq:critical-Z} proves \eqref{fpf:lp:eq:all-moments}.
For the variance, the squared mean is \(O(n)\), lower order than
\(n^{3/2}\). A falling factorial is a monic polynomial whose other
terms have smaller degree, proving \eqref{fpf:lp:eq:factorial-moments}.
\end{proof}

\section{Supercritical weights and the phase diagram}
\label{fpf:lp:sec:supercritical}

\begin{theorem}[Supercritical deficit as an exponential tilt]
\label{fpf:lp:thm:supercritical}
For fixed \(y>y_c\), let \(v=y_c/y<1\). Then, for some \(0<\xi<1\),
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:super-Z}
 Z_{n,r}(y)=\left(\frac y{\rho_r}\right)^n
                   \bigl(\mathcal P_r(v)+O_r(\xi^n)\bigr).
\end{equation}
The limiting deficit law is
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:super-law}
 \Prob(M_r^{(y)}=m)=\frac{p_{r,m}v^m}{\mathcal P_r(v)},\qquad
 \E t^{M_r^{(y)}}=\frac{\mathcal P_r(vt)}{\mathcal P_r(v)}.
\end{equation}
Convergence in total variation is exponentially fast for each fixed
\(y>y_c\). The constants and \(\xi\) may depend on \(y\).
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Normalize \eqref{fpf:lp:eq:counts} by \((y/\rho_r)^n\) and put \(m=n-k\).
The sum becomes \(\sum_{m\le n}v^m\varpi_{n-m,m}\). The uniform estimate
replaces this by \(\sum_{m\le n}v^mp_{r,m}\), with absolute error
bounded by a constant times
\(\sum_{m\le n}v^m\eta_r^{n-m}(m+1)^{-3/2}\).
This is \(O(\xi^n)\) for any fixed
\(\max(v,\eta_r)<\xi<1\). The omitted series tail has the same type
of bound. Applying the estimate to absolute coefficient differences
before normalization proves the total-variation assertion.
\end{proof}

The free energy, meaning the exponential growth rate of the weighted
count, is therefore
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:free-energy}
 f_r(y):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac1n\log Z_{n,r}(y)
 =\begin{cases}\log4,&0<y\le y_c,\\
                 \log(y/\rho_r),&y>y_c.
   \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Its one-sided derivative with respect to \(\log y\) jumps from zero
to one. In that precise sense the transition is first order.
The critical endpoint belongs to the late-failure phase in the law of
\(K/n\), despite the critical deficit's heavy tail.

For comparison, at \(r=2\) both \(\Delta_2(xy)\) and \(1-xyC(x)\)
vanish at \((x,y)=(1/4,2)\). Directly from \eqref{fpf:lp:eq:main-gf}, with
\(z=\sqrt{1-4x}\),
\[
 F_2(x,2)=\frac1{2z^3}-\frac1{z^2}+\frac3{2z}-1.
\]
Thus
\(Z_{n,2}(2)=(n+2)\binom{2n}{n}-4^n\), the prior identity
\eqref{fpf:eq:Z2} of Part~I. Its leading prefactor is \(\sqrt n\), rather
than the constant prefactor in \eqref{fpf:lp:eq:critical-Z}. The extra
vanishing factor explains why the \(r=2\) beta law cannot be extended
unchanged to longer patterns.

\section{A displaced coexistence window}
\label{fpf:lp:sec:window}

The critical limiting law describes \(y=y_c\). It does not locate the
finite-size region in which early and late failures both have substantial
probability. We first prove a general endpoint-sum lemma, then apply
the uniform estimate.

\subsection{Competition between a summable tail and a geometric weight}

\begin{lemma}[Endpoint competition]\label{fpf:lp:lem:competition}
Let \(p_m\ge0\), \(P=\sum_{m\ge0}p_m\in(0,\infty)\), and suppose
\(p_m\sim a m^{-\alpha}\), with \(a>0\) and \(\alpha>1\). For
complex \(s\), set
\[
 w_n(s)=(\alpha-1)\log n+\log\log n+s,
 \qquad
 S_n(s)=\sum_{m=0}^np_m
             \exp\!\left[-w_n(s)\frac{n-m}{n}\right].
\]
Then, locally uniformly for \(s\in\mathbb C\),
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:competition}
 n^{\alpha-1}\log n\,S_n(s)
 \longrightarrow Pe^{-s}+\frac a{\alpha-1}.
\end{equation}
The part with \(m\le n/2\) contributes \(Pe^{-s}\); the other part
contributes \(a/(\alpha-1)\).
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
All estimates below are uniform when \(s\) lies in a fixed compact
set. Write \(w=w_n(s)\) and \(L=(\alpha-1)\log n\).
For \(m\le n/2\), factor out \(e^{-w}\). Up to \(m\le n/L^2\),
\(e^{wm/n}=1+o(1)\) uniformly, and the partial sum of \(p_m\) tends
to \(P\). On the remaining interval,
\[
 \sum_{n/L^2<m\le n/2}p_m|e^{wm/n}|
 \le e^{\Re w/2}\,O((n/L^2)^{1-\alpha})=o(1).
\]
The last estimate follows because its power of \(n\) is
\(- (\alpha-1)/2<0\), whereas its other factors are powers of
\(\log n\). This proves that the late endpoint contributes
\(e^{-w}(P+o(1))\).

For \(m>n/2\), put \(k=n-m\). Uniformly on
\(0\le k\le n/\sqrt L\),
\(p_{n-k}=a n^{-\alpha}(1+o(1))\). The remaining weighted sum is
negligible: on \(n/\sqrt L<k<n/2\), the coefficients are
\(O(n^{-\alpha})\), and the sum of exponential weights is at most
\(O(n e^{-c\sqrt L})\), for a fixed \(c>0\).
For the principal range the geometric sum gives
\[
 \sum_{k\ge0}e^{-wk/n}
 =\frac1{1-e^{-w/n}}\sim\frac n w.
\]
Absolute sums obey the same bounds, so the uniform coefficient error
does not depend on cancellation when \(s\) is complex. Hence the
early endpoint contributes \(a n^{1-\alpha}/w\,(1+o(1))\).
Now \(e^{-w}=n^{1-\alpha}(\log n)^{-1}e^{-s}\), and
\(w/\log n\to\alpha-1\), proving \eqref{fpf:lp:eq:competition}.
\end{proof}

For real \(s\), the same proof gives two conditional laws for the
probability weights defining \(S_n\). Conditional on \(m\le n/2\),
the variable \(m\) converges in total variation to \(p_m/P\).
Conditional on \(m>n/2\), the variable \(k=n-m\) is asymptotically
geometric with success probability \(1-e^{-w/n}\), in total variation, and
\((w/n)k\Rightarrow\Exp(1)\). To see the variation assertions,
use the same two cutoffs: on the principal range the relative weights
are uniform approximations, while the absolute mass of the complements
tends to zero. This also shows that the conclusions remain valid under
an error whose total absolute mass is negligible relative to \(S_n\).

\subsection{The window and its two conditional limits}

For \(n\ge3\), define
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:window-param}
 b_n=\frac12\log n+\log\log n,\qquad
 y_n(s)=y_c\exp\!\left[-\frac{b_n+s}{n}\right].
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[The logarithmically displaced window]\label{fpf:lp:thm:window}
For every fixed \(r\ge3\), locally uniformly for complex \(s\),
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:window-Z}
 \boxed{\displaystyle
 \sqrt n\log n\,4^{-n}Z_{n,r}(y_n(s))
 \longrightarrow 2a_r+P_r e^{-s}.}
\end{equation}
For real \(s\), under the probability measure with weight \(y_n(s)^K\),
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:mixture}
 \frac{K_{n,r}^{(y_n(s))}}n
 \Rightarrow (1-\omega_r(s))\delta_0+\omega_r(s)\delta_1,
 \qquad
 \omega_r(s)=\frac{P_re^{-s}}{2a_r+P_re^{-s}}.
\end{equation}
Writing \(\epsilon_n=(b_n+s)/n\), which is positive for all sufficiently
large \(n\) for each fixed real \(s\), the early phase satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:early}
 \TV\!\left(
 \Law(K_{n,r}^{(y_n(s))}\mid K_{n,r}^{(y_n(s))}\le n/2),
 \Geom(e^{-\epsilon_n})\right)\longrightarrow0,
\end{equation}
and hence \(\epsilon_n K\Rightarrow\Exp(1)\) under that conditioning.
The late phase satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:late}
 \TV\!\left(
 \Law(n-K_{n,r}^{(y_n(s))}\mid K_{n,r}^{(y_n(s))}>n/2),
 \Law(M_r)\right)\longrightarrow0.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
By \eqref{fpf:lp:eq:kernel}, uniformly for \(s\) in a fixed compact subset
of \(\mathbb C\),
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:window-convolution}
 4^{-n}Z_{n,r}(y_n(s))
 =\sum_{m=0}^np_{r,m}e^{-(b_n+s)(n-m)/n}+O_r(n^{-3/2}).
\end{equation}
Indeed \(\Re(b_n+s)>0\) for all sufficiently large \(n\), so the
absolute exponential weight is at most one; the error is bounded by
\eqref{fpf:lp:eq:error-sum}. Apply Lemma~\ref{fpf:lp:lem:competition} with
\(\alpha=3/2\), \(a=a_r\), and \(P=P_r\). The scaled error is
\(O((\log n)/n)\), which tends to zero. This proves the asserted
complex-uniform limit, not only its real restriction.

For real \(s\), the two endpoint contributions in the lemma are positive.
Their normalized limits give the two weights in \eqref{fpf:lp:eq:mixture}.
The coefficient errors have total absolute mass \(O(n^{-3/2})\),
negligible compared with the mass of either endpoint,
which has order \(n^{-1/2}/\log n\). Thus the lemma's conditional
variation estimates pass to the actual permutation distribution.
The possible boundary term at \(K=n/2\) has negligible mass, so its
assignment to the early phase does not affect the conditional limits.
In the early phase, \(K/n\to0\) because \(n\epsilon_n\to\infty\);
in the late phase, the deficit is tight. This proves all assertions.
\end{proof}

The center can be normalized to eliminate the model-dependent constants.
Put
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:centered-window}
 s_r^*=\log\frac{P_r}{2a_r},\qquad
 \widetilde y_n(s)=y_c\exp\!\left[-\frac{b_n+s_r^*+s}{n}\right].
\end{equation}
Then the late-phase weight is exactly the limiting logistic expression
\(1/(1+e^s)\), and the limit in \eqref{fpf:lp:eq:window-Z}, divided by
\(2a_r\), is \(1+e^{-s}\).

The displacement is essential. If instead \(y_n=y_c e^{-c/n}\)
for a fixed real \(c\), dominated convergence in the convolution gives
\(4^{-n}Z_{n,r}(y_n)\to P_re^{-c}\), and the deficit still converges
to \(M_r\). Such a window sees only the late phase.

The reason for the extra \(\log\log n\) is explicit. Late failures
have mass about \(P_re^{-w}\), while early failures have mass about
\(a_r n^{-1/2}/w\). Equating them gives
\(w e^{-w}=(a_r/P_r)n^{-1/2}\). The large solution is expressible
using the negative real branch of Lambert \(W\); expanding that equation
gives precisely \(b_n+s_r^*+o(1)\). This is a way to understand the
center, not an exact finite-size formula for the weight giving a
half-and-half split.

\begin{writenote}[Added 5 October 2026, batch~98: relation to Part~I's window]
Part~I's Theorem~\ref{fpf:thm:window} is the \(r=2\) analogue: there the
window \(y=2e^{\lambda/n}\) has order \(1/n\) and is not displaced, and the

\end{writenote}

\section{Complex zeros near the displaced window}
\label{fpf:lp:sec:zeros}

The coefficients of \(Z_{n,r}(y)\) are nonnegative, so it has no positive
real zeros. The complex version of Theorem~\ref{fpf:lp:thm:window}
nevertheless locates zeros approaching the positive critical point from off
the real axis.

\begin{theorem}[Local complex zero asymptotics]\label{fpf:lp:thm:zeros}
Fix \(r\ge3\) and an integer \(j\). For all sufficiently large \(n\),
there is a zero \(z_{n,j}\) of \(Z_{n,r}\) satisfying
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:zeros}
 z_{n,j}=y_c\exp\!\left[-\frac{
 \tfrac12\log n+\log\log n+\log(P_r/(2a_r))
 +(2j+1)\pi\ii+o(1)}{n}\right].
\end{equation}
More precisely, in the \(s\)-coordinate of \eqref{fpf:lp:eq:window-param},
there is exactly one zero, counted with multiplicity, in each sufficiently
small fixed disk about
\(s_r^*+(2j+1)\pi\ii\), for all large \(n\).
On any compact region whose boundary contains no zero of
\(2a_r+P_re^{-s}\), the total number of zeros eventually equals that
of the limiting function.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The entire functions
\(\varphi_n(s)=\sqrt n\log n\,4^{-n}Z_{n,r}(y_n(s))\)
converge locally uniformly to \(\varphi(s)=2a_r+P_re^{-s}\).
The zeros of \(\varphi\) are precisely the displayed \(s\)-values, and are
simple because \(\varphi'(s)=2a_r\) there. On the boundary of a
sufficiently small disk about one of them, \(|\varphi|\) has positive
minimum. For large \(n\), \(|\varphi_n-\varphi|<|\varphi|\) on the
boundary, so Rouch\'e's theorem gives one zero inside. Shrinking the disk
proves convergence of its location. The exponential map defining
\(y_n(s)\) is locally one-to-one and has nonzero derivative, so
multiplicity is preserved and the image is a zero of \(Z_{n,r}\). The same
boundary argument proves the compact-region assertion.
\end{proof}

In particular, the pair corresponding to imaginary coordinates
\(\pm\pi\) has imaginary distance asymptotic to \(\pi y_c/n\) from
the real axis, while its real displacement from \(y_c\) is of order
\((\log n)/n\). The theorem concerns these local sequences of zeros.
It does not assert that they are globally the closest zeros, describe
all zeros of \(Z_{n,r}\), or settle the original \(r=2\) zero problem.

\subsection{A concrete route to proof-assistant formalization}

The first formalization target should be the combinatorial meaning of
the coefficients. Define pattern occurrence using order-preserving
maps of finite position sets, and define restriction to the largest
\(k\) values. Prove that inserting a new minimum and deleting it are
inverse operations after standardization. Then establish the active-site
lemma and show that a first \(\tau_r\) occurrence is created exactly
at sites \(j\ge r\). These steps connect the count to permutations;
a formal identity between rational generating functions alone would
not make that connection.

The next layer is algebraic: safe-state recurrences, the tail
\(u^j/\Delta_r(u)\), the Catalan descendant series, and the coefficient
formula. It can be checked with formal power series and finite sums.
The Chebyshev factorization and the geometric distribution give further
isolated targets, including exact moments and the coupling inequality.

The analytic layer should state \eqref{fpf:lp:eq:kernel} with explicit
quantifiers over both indices and over constants depending on \(r\).
From that interface, the critical laws and the endpoint lemma use
ordinary estimates for positive series. The complex-zero result adds
local uniform convergence and Rouch\'e's theorem. Nothing in this
article is claimed to have been formalized in Lean or Rocq, and the
repository's other formal developments do not change that status.

\section{Further questions and research}
\label{fpf:lp:sec:future}

The following questions are specific continuations of the results proved
here. They are proposed research directions; except where a source is
named, they are not represented as established open problems from the
literature.

\begin{writenote}[Added 5 October 2026, batch~98: how this list is kept]
Questions~1--10 are manuscript~07's section~12, printed in full. By the
repository's rule for unproved claims, questions~11 and~12 add the two
assertions of the manuscript that this Part does not prove (each with its
source, its sketch and what is missing). Where a question coincides with
one of Part~I's (Section~\ref{fpf:sec:formal}), both are kept and each
carries a note. No claim of the manuscript was found to be false.
\end{writenote}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,label=\textbf{\arabic*.}]
\item \textbf{A joint large-permutation, long-pattern theorem.}
Determine conditions on \(r=r(n)\) under which
\(K_{n,r}^{(1)}/r^2\Rightarrow \mathcal W\). The natural starting range is
\(r^2=o(n)\), but the current fixed-\(r\) error constants are not
uniform there. When \(r\) is of order \(\sqrt n\), the finite ceiling
\(K\le n\) interacts with the limit law and should create a new
crossover. No particular joint limiting formula is asserted here.

\item \textbf{Uniformity as the critical weight approaches one.}
Since \(y_c=\sec^2(\pi/(r+2))=1+\pi^2/(r+2)^2+O(r^{-4})\),
the weighted transition moves toward the unweighted model as the pattern
grows. Develop a theory in which this displacement competes with the
\((\log n)/n\) displacement of Theorem~\ref{fpf:lp:thm:window}. This
requires keeping the spectral gap and the tail constants uniform in \(r\).

\item \textbf{Higher-order coexistence asymptotics.}
Expand the endpoint convolution beyond its limit, including terms in
\((\log\log n)/\log n\), \(1/\log n\), and \(n^{-1/2}\).
The sizeable discrepancies in Table~\ref{fpf:lp:tab:numerical} make this
practically useful. A worthwhile endpoint is a rigorously controlled
approximation to the actual finite-size median transition weight.

\begin{writenote}[Added 5 October 2026, batch~98: the centre, what is proved and what is not]
The manuscript explains the centre of the window by the equation
\(we^{-w}=(a_r/P_r)n^{-1/2}\), whose large (Lambert-\(W_{-1}\)) solution
expands to \(b_n+s_r^*+o(1)\) (Section~\ref{fpf:lp:sec:window}), and calls
this ``not an exact finite-size formula''. Its leading order is in fact a
consequence of Theorem~\ref{fpf:lp:thm:window}. For fixed \(n\), the tilted
laws of \(K\) form an exponential family in \(\log y\), so
\(\Prob_{y}(K>n/2)\) is continuous and strictly increasing in \(y\), from
\(0\) to \(1\); let \(y_n^{\mathrm{med}}\) be the weight where it equals
\(\frac12\), and write it as \(\widetilde y_n(\sigma_n)\) in the centred
coordinate \eqref{fpf:lp:eq:centered-window}. The proof of
Theorem~\ref{fpf:lp:thm:window} shows that
\(\Prob_{\widetilde y_n(s)}(K>n/2)\to1/(1+e^s)\) for each real \(s\). For
\(\varepsilon>0\) the limits at \(s=\mp\varepsilon\) lie on either side of
\(\frac12\), so by monotonicity \(|\sigma_n|<\varepsilon\) for all large
\(n\): \(\sigma_n\to0\), that is,
\(y_n^{\mathrm{med}}=y_c\exp[-(b_n+s_r^*+o(1))/n]\). What remains open is
the rate of \(\sigma_n\to0\), which is this question; at \(r=3\) the
finite-size late probability at \(s=0\) of the uncentred window is still
\(0.343\) at \(n=4000\) against the limit \(0.234\)
(Table~\ref{fpf:lp:tab:numerical}).
\end{writenote}

\item \textbf{Effective constants in the uniform estimate.}
The proof permits a concrete contour radius \(R\) and explicit
majorants. Optimize these choices to produce usable numerical values
of \(\Theta_r\) and \(\eta_r\), then propagate them through the critical
and coexistence estimates. This would turn the present asymptotic laws
into certified finite-input error bounds.

\item \textbf{The original \(r=2\) complex-zero problem.}
The source report asks about zeros near \(y=2\). The present zero
theorem applies to \(r\ge3\); \(r=2\) has a different limiting
function because of the additional vanishing factor. Prove its
complex-uniform scaling limit and analyze the resulting entire
function's zeros, preserving that distinction.

\begin{writenote}[Added 5 October 2026, batch~98]
This is Part~I's question~4 (Section~\ref{fpf:sec:formal}), which stays
open; Part~I proposes \(M(\lambda)\) of \eqref{fpf:eq:window-normalization}
as the governing function. At \(r\ge3\) the corresponding function is
\(2a_r+P_re^{-s}\) (Theorem~\ref{fpf:lp:thm:zeros}).
\end{writenote}

\item \textbf{Global zeros for the longer-pattern family.}
Determine whether the local pair in Theorem~\ref{fpf:lp:thm:zeros} is
eventually closest to the positive real axis in an appropriate region.
Describe the other accumulation curves, and prove a zero-counting measure
limit. The local Rouch\'e argument gives no such global information.

\item \textbf{A direct path interpretation of first failure.}
Chow--West and Krattenthaler identify the safe counts with bounded-height
Dyck paths. Find a bijection carrying \(K_r\) to a natural path stopping
statistic, not merely preserving the total number of safe objects.
It may explain the geometric spectrum and the law \(\mathcal W\) at the
level of individual combinatorial objects.

\begin{writenote}[Added 5 October 2026, batch~98]

\end{writenote}

\item \textbf{Other forbidden-pattern families.}
Find classes whose safe transfer matrix has a dominant eigenvalue
strictly below the unrestricted Catalan growth constant, and whose
continuation law has a summable power tail. Establish hypotheses under
which a uniform analogue of \eqref{fpf:lp:eq:kernel} holds. Then
Lemma~\ref{fpf:lp:lem:competition} predicts the displacement
\((\alpha-1)\log n+\log\log n\) in the exponent for a tail
\(m^{-\alpha}\). The key difficulty is deriving the uniform kernel,
not applying the endpoint lemma afterward.

\item \textbf{Kernel-checked enumeration and certified sampling.}
Formalize the positive coefficient formula and use it for exact sampling
conditioned on \(K=k\). The independent geometric representation already

\section{A short exact-moment calculation}
\label{fpf:lp:app:moments}

Formula \eqref{fpf:lp:eq:D-two-roots} expands as the polynomial identity
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:lp:eq:D-derivative}
 \Delta_r(u)=2^{-r-1}\sum_{j\ge0}\binom{r+2}{2j+1}(1-4u)^j,
 \qquad
 \Delta_r^{(m)}(1/4)=2^{-r-1}(-4)^m m!\binom{r+2}{2m+1}.
\end{equation}
Terms with out-of-range binomial coefficients are zero. It follows that
\[
 \frac{\Delta_r'(1/4)}{\Delta_r(1/4)}=-\frac{2r(r+1)}3,
 \qquad
 \frac{\Delta_r''(1/4)}{\Delta_r(1/4)}
 =\frac4{15}r(r+1)(r-1)(r-2).
\]
These two identities determine the mean and variance of
\(\sum_jG_j\) by differentiating
\(\Delta_r(1/4)/\Delta_r(t/4)\) at \(t=1\). Adding \(r+V_r\), where
\[
 \E V_r=1+\frac2{r+2},\qquad
 \Var V_r=2+\frac6{r+2}-\frac4{(r+2)^2},
\]
gives \eqref{fpf:lp:eq:mean-K}--\eqref{fpf:lp:eq:var-K}. This derivation
also justifies the finite trigonometric sum used in the Wasserstein proof
without importing a separate summation theorem.

\subsection{The OEIS triangle recurrence}
With the boundary conventions stated in Section~\ref{fpf:sec:question}, the proved generating
function also verifies the recurrence displayed in the OEIS entry:
\begin{align}\label{fpf:eq:oeis-rec}
 T(n,k)={}&3T(n-1,k-1)-2T(n-2,k-2)\notag\\
 &+T(n,k+1)-2T(n-1,k)+\ind_{k=2}C_{n-2},
 \qquad n\ge3,\ 2\le k\le n.
\end{align}
Together with $T(1,1)=1$, $T(2,2)=2$, and the zero boundaries, it computes
rows from right to left. It is a consequence of the counting proof, not
an assumption used to derive it. One verification is to insert the
binomial expression and use Pascal's identity; another is to multiply
\eqref{fpf:eq:main-gf} by the recurrence kernel and compare coefficients.

\subsection{A third fixed-column correction}
The coefficient generator \eqref{fpf:eq:all-engine} gives
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:c3}
 c_3(k)=-\frac{
 \begin{gathered}
 2k^7-38k^6+130k^5+270k^4-3657k^3\\[-2pt]
 {}+26093k^2-34725k+13860
 \end{gathered}}
 {3072(k+4)}.
\end{equation}
This coefficient is independently regenerated in the symbolic check, rather
than merely inserted as a numerical fit. For higher orders, the power sums
in \eqref{fpf:eq:ab} and the exponential recurrence
\eqref{fpf:eq:exp-rec} are preferable to increasingly large printed polynomials.

\subsection{The second critical raw moment}
A second application of $y\partial_y$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{fpf:eq:second-gf}
 \left.(y\partial_y)^2F\right|_{y=2}
 =\frac1{2z}-\frac3{2z^2}+\frac2{z^3}-\frac3{2z^4}
            +\frac1{2z^5}-\frac1{z^6}+\frac1{z^7}.
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\begin{align}\label{fpf:eq:critical-second}
 \sum_{k=1}^n k^2 2^kT(n,k)
 ={}&\frac{2(4n^3+23n^2+63n+30)}{15}\binom{2n}{n}\notag\\
 &-\frac{(n+2)(n+4)}2\,4^n.
\end{align}
Together with \eqref{fpf:eq:Z2} and \eqref{fpf:eq:critical-mean}, this provides an
exact finite-$n$ variance and another independent check on the limiting
variance $4/45$ after scaling by $n^2$.
