\begin{abstract}
For a partition $\lambda=(1^{M_1}2^{M_2}\cdots)$ of $n$, consider the set of positive values taken by the multiplicities $M_j$. Part~I counts this set: $a(n)=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}D(\lambda)$ is OEIS A373271, and it proves $a(n)/p(n)=(6n)^{1/4}-\tfrac{23}{16}+Kn^{-1/4}+O(n^{-1/2})$ with an explicit $K$, the constant $-\tfrac{23}{16}$ needing an exact telescoping tail $-\tfrac12$ that a local expansion misses; it derives two relative corrections for $a(n)$, eventual strict increase and a two-ceiling inverse whose width before rounding tends to zero. Part~II sums the set: $a_n=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}W(\lambda)$ is OEIS A373273, and it proves an expansion of $a_n/p(n)$ through the five scales $\sqrt n\log n$, $\sqrt n$, $n^{1/4}$, $\log n$, $1$ with error $O(n^{-1/4})$, the leading term being $\sqrt{6n}\log n/(4\pi)$; there the square of a nonlocal correction and a finite-difference tail enter the constant, and zeta derivatives cancel. Both Parts rest on one exact identity, $\sum_na_nq^n=P(q)\sum_{m\ge1}w(m)(1-Q_m(q))$ with $w=1$ and $w(m)=m$, on a whole-circle bound for positive products that forbid one multiplicity, and on local saddle transfer. Part~II answers the case $w(m)=m$ of Part~I's question on weighted occupied values. Part~III, a continuation manuscript added on 7~October 2026, studies $D_\alpha=\sum_mm^\alpha\mathbf 1_{\{N_m>0\}}$ for every $\alpha\ge0$, so that $D_0=D$ and $D_1=W$: Gaussian fluctuations with an explicit covariance kernel for $\alpha<\tfrac12$, in particular $\Var_nD\sim v_0n^{1/4}$ with $v_0=6^{1/4}(\sqrt2-\tfrac34-\tfrac{3\sqrt2}8\log(1+\sqrt2))=0.3080002444\ldots$ and a central limit theorem, which answers Part~I's variance question; a factor $\log n$ in the variance at $\alpha=\tfrac12$, with a Gaussian field in a logarithmic window of exponents; non-Gaussian series of exponentials above $\tfrac12$, among them a Gumbel law for $W$ with $\Var_nW\sim n$; mutual independence of the three regimes; and the leading means of every $D_\alpha$. Mean expansions of the precision of Parts~I and~II remain open for other weights. The write adds the OEIS entries, the b-file of A373271 that Part~I could not open (all 200 terms agree), a comparison of the inverses with the repository's transseries volume, and, for Part~III, recomputations of its constants and tables and of the geometric-law means against Parts~I and~II. The report is unrefereed, and nothing in it is formalized.
\end{abstract}

\emph{One identity, two weights.} Write $N_m(\lambda)$ for the number of part
sizes of $\lambda$ with multiplicity exactly~$m$. Part~I studies
$D=\sum_{m\ge1}\mathbf 1_{\{N_m>0\}}$ and Part~II studies
$W=\sum_{m\ge1}m\,\mathbf 1_{\{N_m>0\}}$. Both start from the same exact
identity,
\[
\sum_{n\ge0}a_nq^n=P(q)\sum_{m\ge1}w(m)\bigl(1-Q_m(q)\bigr),\qquad
Q_m(q)=\prod_{j\ge1}\bigl(1-(1-q^j)q^{mj}\bigr),
\]
with $w\equiv1$ in Proposition~\ref{dmv:cnt:prop:gf} and $w(m)=m$
in~\eqref{dmv:sum:eq:gf}, and both control the coefficient circle through
the positive products $P_m=PQ_m$ that forbid one multiplicity. The OEIS
records the link: A373271 comments that summing the distinct multiplicities
instead gives A373273 (Remark~\ref{dmv:cnt:rem:oeis}).

\emph{Why one report, and how the Parts relate.} The two manuscripts treat
one family of statistics by one method. Report~198's package was adapted
from Report~197's (its README: the infrastructure ``adapted from the
preceding Report197 reproducibility package''), and Report~198 answers, for
the weight $w(m)=m$, the question on weighted occupied values that Part~I
leaves open (Section~\ref{dmv:cnt:sec:sources}, Question~4); the general
weight $m^\alpha$ stays open (Part~II, Question~2), and dated notes in both
question lists say so. \textbf{Neither Part uses a theorem of the other.}
Report~198's README states that ``No A373271 theorem or coefficient is used
as a mathematical premise'', and the write confirmed that Part~II cites
nothing of Part~I. The constants differ, so neither result is a special
case of the other.

\emph{Part~III, added 7~October 2026.} A third manuscript, \emph{Fluctuations
of Distinct Multiplicities in Random Integer Partitions} (7~October 2026,
archive \file{ProveIt_Research_2026-10-07.zip}), is printed as Part~III
(Sections~\ref{dmv:fl:sec:front}--\ref{dmv:fl:sec:verification}, labels
\file{dmv:fl:}). It was written from Part~I alone, as the continuation of
Part~I's Questions~2 and~4, and studies
$D_\alpha=\sum_{m\ge1}m^\alpha\mathbf 1_{\{N_m>0\}}$ for every $\alpha\ge0$,
so that $D_0=D$ of Part~I and $D_1=W$ of Part~II. It proves their
fluctuation laws (Gaussian below $\alpha=\tfrac12$, a logarithmic variance
factor at $\tfrac12$ with a Gaussian window field, non-Gaussian series of
exponentials above, mutually independent), the variance and central limit
theorem for $D$, the Gumbel law of $W$, and the leading means of every
$D_\alpha$. It thereby answers Part~I's Question~2 and Part~II's
Question~4, and, for the weights $m^\alpha$ as to fluctuations and leading
means, Part~I's Question~4 and part of Part~II's Question~2; the sentence
above saying that
$m^\alpha$ stays open, and the earlier notes in both question lists, were
written before Part~III arrived and are kept, with dated notes after them.
Its means at $\alpha=0,1$ re-derive, more weakly, terms of Parts~I and~II
(Section~\ref{dmv:fl:sec:answers}). Its provenance, checks, non-claims and
reading conventions are in its Section~\ref{dmv:fl:sec:front}; it is
numbered with the shift $k\mapsto k+25$ (Section~\ref{dmv:fl:sec:provenance}).

\begin{writenote}[What is not claimed, collected from both Parts]
\emph{Part~I.} The result is finite-order: no all-orders expansion, no
explicit numerical remainder constants or onset, no variance or fluctuation
theorem, no central limit theorem, no exact single-ceiling inverse (the two
ceilings can differ by one; no values of $C_1$, $Y_0$), no interpolation of
$a(n)$ assumed and no error asserted for piecewise-linear interpolation, and
no absolute novelty, complete-library coverage, global absence or historical
priority: the leading mean is ``naturally suggested by the Poisson
transition'' of Ralaivaosaona and the transfer mechanism is classical (Grabner,
Knopfmacher and Wagner); Corteel, Pittel, Savage and Wilf was read only at
the abstract level and Lugo's thesis only at targeted passages, so overlap
with them is not excluded. The b-file comparison was not made by the source.
Finite checks support the implementation, not the analytic remainders.
\emph{Part~II.} No explicit numerical onset, certified error constant,
all-orders expansion, distributional theorem, single exact ceiling for every
$Y$, numerical $X,M,n_0$, exponentially small final coefficient remainder,
new general saddle-transfer theorem or absolute novelty; the comparisons with
Archibald et al., Fill--Janson--Ward, Corteel--Pittel--Savage--Wilf and Lugo
distinguish the objects and are not exclusion certificates; the numerical
residuals prove no next coefficient; the diagnostics and the inverse root
calculation are not interval-certified. \emph{The write adds:} its checks are
floating, finite or hand computations; Remarks~\ref{dmv:cnt:rem:transseries}
and~\ref{dmv:sum:rem:transseries} claim no novelty for any inversion; the
merge claims no result relating the two statistics beyond the shared
identity.
\end{writenote}

\part{Distinct positive multiplicity values in integer partitions: matched asymptotics and a shrinking inverse for OEIS A373271}\label{dmv:cnt:part}
\partsource{Bundle Report~197, archive \file{Report197_TeX_and_Reproducible_Code.zip}, delivered as \file{Report197.tex} (18 pages), the base of the merge. Delivered title block ``Distinct positive multiplicity values in integer partitions / Matched asymptotics and a shrinking inverse for OEIS A373271 / Report 197 / 4 October 2026''. Printed as delivered, with \textbf{[write]} notes and remarks; section, statement and equation numbers are those of the delivery, and its labels carry the prefix \file{dmv:cnt:}. Its citation key \file{oeis} is \file{oeisA373271} here. This Part is self-contained.}
\begin{partabstract}
For a partition $\lambda$, let $D(\lambda)$ be the number of different positive integers that occur as multiplicities of its part sizes. If $a(n)=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}D(\lambda)$ and $p(n)$ is the partition number, we prove
\[
\frac{a(n)}{p(n)}=(6n)^{1/4}-\frac{23}{16}
+\frac{\pi}{6^{1/4}}\left(\frac{12365}{82944}+\frac{15}{8\pi^2}\right)n^{-1/4}
+O(n^{-1/2}).
\]
The constant correction requires matching the entire multiplicity range: an exact telescoping tail contributes $-1/2$, which a purely local expansion misses. A zero-free sector argument, explicit summed remainder bounds, and centered Euler--Maclaurin summation give the radial expansion. A uniform whole-circle bound for positive generating functions that forbid one multiplicity then permits coefficient extraction; the saddle monomials are used only locally. We derive two relative quarter-power corrections for the total sequence, eventual strict increase, and an integer inverse bracket whose width before rounding tends to zero. The accompanying code recomputes exact terms through $2000$ and checks independent models at smaller cutoffs. The result is finite-order; no explicit numerical remainder constants or onset are supplied. No all-orders expansion, fluctuation theorem, or absolute novelty claim is asserted.
\end{partabstract}

\section{The statistic and the main results}\label{dmv:cnt:sec:main}
Write a partition of $n$ as $\lambda=(1^{M_1}2^{M_2}\cdots)$, so that $M_j\ge0$ and $\sum_jjM_j=n$. Define
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:def}
D(\lambda)=\#\{m\ge1:\ M_j=m\text{ for some }j\ge1\},\qquad
 a(n)=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}D(\lambda).
\end{equation}
The empty partition has $D=0$, hence $a(0)=0$. This is OEIS A373271 \cite{oeisA373271}. For example, $\lambda=(5,5,3,3,3,1)$ has positive multiplicities $2,3,1$, so $D=3$; the partition $(5,5,3,3,1)$ has positive multiplicities $2,2,1$, so $D=2$. Repeated multiplicity values are counted once. Zero multiplicity is never counted.

Thus $D$ is neither the number of occupied part sizes, nor the maximum multiplicity, nor an indicator that all nonzero multiplicities are different. If $\lambda$ is uniform among the $p(n)$ ordinary partitions, then $a(n)/p(n)=\E_nD$.

Throughout the report, put
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:constants}
 A=\frac{\pi^2}{6},\qquad d=\frac{12365}{82944},\qquad
 \nu=n-\frac1{24},\qquad \tau=\sqrt{\frac A\nu}.
\end{equation}
The symbol $O$ has its usual asymptotic meaning, with a fixed implied constant once a sector or a derivative order has been fixed. In complex estimates, $O(t^\alpha)$ always means a bound in modulus by a constant times $|t|^\alpha$.

\begin{theorem}[Three-term mean]\label{dmv:cnt:thm:mean}
As $n\to\infty$,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:meantau}
\frac{a(n)}{p(n)}=\sqrt\pi\,\tau^{-1/2}-\frac{23}{16}
 +\sqrt\pi\left(d+\frac5{16A}\right)\tau^{1/2}+O(\tau).
\end{equation}
Equivalently,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:meann}
\frac{a(n)}{p(n)}=(6n)^{1/4}-\frac{23}{16}+K n^{-1/4}+O(n^{-1/2}),
\quad
K=\frac\pi{6^{1/4}}\left(\frac{12365}{82944}+\frac{15}{8\pi^2}\right).
\end{equation}
Numerically, $K=0.68058214956683591885\ldots$.
\end{theorem}

The quarter-power scale is consistent with the classical transition for the number of part sizes with one prescribed multiplicity \cite{ralaivaosaona}. The new work of the proof presented here is the complete whole-range matching and justified coefficient extraction for this occupied-value statistic; no historical priority claim is attached to that description.

\begin{theorem}[Total sequence]\label{dmv:cnt:thm:total}
Let
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:totalconstants}
 x=\sqrt{n-\tfrac1{24}},\qquad B=2\sqrt A,\qquad
 C=\frac{6^{1/4}}{4\sqrt3},\qquad
 b_1=-\frac{23}{16\,6^{1/4}},\qquad
 b_2=\sqrt A\,d-\frac3{16\sqrt A}.
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:totalx}
a(n)=C\ee^{Bx}x^{-3/2}
\left(1+b_1x^{-1/2}+b_2x^{-1}+O(x^{-3/2})\right).
\end{equation}
In unshifted variables this is
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:totaln}
a(n)=C n^{-3/4}\ee^{B\sqrt n}
\left(1+b_1n^{-1/4}+\left(b_2-\frac B{48}\right)n^{-1/2}
 +O(n^{-3/4})\right).
\end{equation}
The sequence $a(n)$ is eventually strictly increasing.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Shrinking integer inverse]\label{dmv:cnt:thm:inverse}
Set $\ell_1=b_1$ and $\ell_2=b_2-b_1^2/2$. For large positive $Y$, define
\begin{align}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:inverse}
x_0(Y)&=-\frac3{2B}W_{-1}\left(-\frac{2B}{3}(C/Y)^{2/3}\right),\\
Z(Y)&=x_0^2-\frac{2\ell_1}{B}x_0^{1/2}-\frac{2\ell_2}{B}+\frac1{24}.
\nonumber
\end{align}
Here $W_{-1}$ is the real branch of the Lambert function with values at most $-1$. There are constants $C_1>0$ and $Y_0$ such that the actual integer threshold
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:threshold}
N(Y)=\min\{n\ge0:a(n)\ge Y\}
\end{equation}
satisfies, for $Y\ge Y_0$,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:ceiling}
\left\lceil Z(Y)-C_1x_0^{-1/2}\right\rceil
\ \le N(Y)\le\
\left\lceil Z(Y)+C_1x_0^{-1/2}\right\rceil.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

The width $2C_1x_0^{-1/2}$ before rounding tends to zero. The two ceilings can nevertheless differ by one; this theorem is not an eventual exact single-ceiling formula. No explicit numerical values for $C_1$ or $Y_0$ are supplied. The proof does not assume an interpolation of $a(n)$, nor assert an error estimate for piecewise-linear interpolation.

\section{An exact occupancy generating function}\label{dmv:cnt:sec:exact}
Let
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:P}
P(q)=\prod_{j\ge1}(1-q^j)^{-1}=\sum_{n\ge0}p(n)q^n,\qquad |q|<1.
\end{equation}
For each positive integer $m$, define
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:Q}
Q_m(q)=\prod_{j\ge1}\bigl(1-(1-q^j)q^{mj}\bigr),\qquad
P_m(q)=P(q)Q_m(q).
\end{equation}
The product
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:forbidden}
P_m(q)=\prod_{j\ge1}\left(\frac1{1-q^j}-q^{mj}\right)
      =\prod_{j\ge1}\sum_{\substack{k\ge0\\k\ne m}}q^{jk}
\end{equation}
counts the partitions in which no part size has multiplicity $m$. In particular, its coefficients are nonnegative and no greater than the corresponding coefficients of $P$.

\begin{proposition}[Exact identity]\label{dmv:cnt:prop:gf}
As a formal power series and for $|q|<1$,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:gf}
\sum_{n\ge0}a(n)q^n=P(q)\sum_{m\ge1}(1-Q_m(q)).
\end{equation}
For each fixed $n$, the finite generating function
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:Fn}
F_n(q)=\sum_{m=1}^n\bigl(P(q)-P_m(q)\bigr)
\end{equation}
satisfies $[q^n]F_n(q)=a(n)$ exactly.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For each partition, write $D=\sum_{m\ge1}{\bf1}_{\{\exists j:M_j=m\}}$ and sum over partitions. The summand $P-P_m$ starts in degree $m$, so coefficientwise only finitely many $m$ contribute, proving both formal claims. For fixed $\rho=|q|<1$,
\[
\sum_{j,m\ge1}|(1-q^j)q^{mj}|
\le2\sum_{j\ge1}\frac{\rho^j}{1-\rho^j}<\infty.
\]
The associated products and sums therefore converge absolutely and locally uniformly. For example, $|1-Q_m|\le\exp(\sum_j|(1-q^j)q^{mj}|)-1$, and the right side is summable in $m$.
\end{proof}

Put $q=\ee^{-t}$ and abbreviate
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:g}
g(t)=\sum_{m\ge1}\left(1-Q_m(\ee^{-t})\right).
\end{equation}
At real $t>0$, independent geometric random variables $M_j$ with
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:boltzmann}
\Prob_t\{M_j=m\}=(1-\ee^{-tj})\ee^{-tmj},\qquad m\ge0,
\end{equation}
give the usual Boltzmann distribution on partitions. Accordingly $g(t)=\E_tD$ and $Q_m(\ee^{-t})$ is the probability that the value $m$ is absent. This probabilistic interpretation motivates the analytic products, but complex $t$ will be handled by explicit estimates.

\section{Uniform sector estimates and the small-multiplicity range}\label{dmv:cnt:sec:sector}
Fix a sufficiently small closed sector $|\arg t|\le\theta_0$, initially with $\theta_0\le\pi/12$, and let $t\to0$ within it. Write
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:sectornotation}
r=|t|,\quad h=\sqrt t\text{ on the principal branch},\quad
p_{jm}=(1-\ee^{-tj})\ee^{-tmj},\quad
S_{k,m}=\sum_{j\ge1}p_{jm}^k,\quad \mu_m=S_{1,m}.
\end{equation}
All constants below are independent of $m$ and $t$; dependence on the fixed sector and fixed $k$ is allowed.

\subsection{A zero-free logarithm}
For $z$ in the sector,
\[
1-\ee^{-z}=z\int_0^1\ee^{-uz}\,du,
\qquad |1-\ee^{-z}|\le|z|.
\]
Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:zero-free}
|(1-\ee^{-z})\ee^{-mz}|
\le |z|\ee^{-m\Rea z}\le\frac1{em\cos\theta_0}<1.
\end{equation}
This supplies one uniform disk of radius strictly less than $1$ for all $p_{jm}$. The power-series logarithm $-\log(1-p)$ is therefore analytic and single-valued here, agrees with the principal logarithm, and encounters no zero of $1-p$.

For every fixed integer $k\ge1$,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:Sbound}
|S_{k,m}|\le \sum_{j\ge1}(rj)^k\ee^{-kmj\Rea t}
\le\frac{C_k}{r m^{k+1}},\qquad
\sup_j|p_{jm}|\le \frac Cm.
\end{equation}
To see that the exponential-sum bound holds over the entire range of $m$, note that $a^{k+1}\sum_{j\ge1}j^k\ee^{-aj}$ is bounded for $a>0$. At zero this follows from an integral comparison; at infinity it decays exponentially.

\subsection{The matched first moment}
Use the notation
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:b}
\mathcal B(z)=\frac1{\ee^z-1},\qquad b(z)=\mathcal B(z)-\frac1z,\qquad
A_m=\frac1{tm(m+1)},\qquad \delta_m=\mu_m-A_m.
\end{equation}
The different symbols $\mathcal B$ and $B=2\sqrt A$ distinguish the Bose factor from the exponential-growth constant. Geometric summation gives
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:mu}
\mu_m=\mathcal B(tm)-\mathcal B(t(m+1)),\qquad
\delta_m=-t\int_0^1b'(t(m+u))\,du.
\end{equation}
The removable singularity of $b$ at zero has expansion
\[
b(z)=-\frac12+\frac z{12}+O(z^3).
\]
For each fixed $\ell\ge1$, analyticity on a bounded sector and $b(z)=-z^{-1}+O(\ee^{-\Rea z})$ at infinity show that
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:bderivative}
|b^{(\ell)}(z)|\le\frac{C_\ell}{1+|z|^{\ell+1}}.
\end{equation}
It follows that
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:delta}
|\delta_m|\le\frac{Cr}{1+(rm)^2},\qquad
\delta_m=-\frac t{12}+O(r^3m^2)\quad(rm\le1).
\end{equation}
In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:deltal1}
\sum_{m\ge1}|\delta_m|=O(1),\qquad
\sum_{m\ge1}|\delta_m|^2=O(r).
\end{equation}
Most importantly, exact telescoping before expansion gives
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:deltatel}
\sum_{m\ge1}\delta_m=\mathcal B(t)-\frac1t=-\frac12+O(r).
\end{equation}
The global $O(1)$ mass in this identity cannot be recovered by summing only the pointwise approximation $\delta_m\approx-t/12$ near $m\asymp r^{-1/2}$.

\subsection{Why fixed small multiplicities are harmless}
Let
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:L}
L_m=-\log Q_m(\ee^{-t})=\sum_{j\ge1}f_m(jt),\qquad
f_m(z)=-\log\bigl(1-(1-\ee^{-z})\ee^{-mz}\bigr).
\end{equation}
Here $-\log Q_m$ denotes the sum of the factor logarithms; it need not be the principal logarithm of the full product. For fixed $m$, $f_m$ is analytic on the sector, is $O(z)$ at zero, and decays exponentially at infinity. Its derivatives of every fixed order are integrable along each ray, uniformly in the closed angular interval. Euler--Maclaurin along a ray, using $f_m(0)=0$, yields
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:smallm}
L_m=\frac1t\int_{\arg z=\arg t}f_m(z)\,dz+O(r)=\frac{I_m}{t}+O(r),
\quad I_m=\int_0^\infty f_m(y)\,dy>0.
\end{equation}
For the contour deformation, the small circular arc contributes $O(\epsilon^2)$ because $f_m(z)=O(z)$, and the large arc tends to zero exponentially. No positivity off the real axis is needed. Since $\Rea(1/t)\ge\cos\theta_0/r$, for each fixed finite range of $m$ there is $c>0$ with $\Rea L_m\ge c/r$ for small $r$. Thus $Q_m=\ee^{-L_m}$ is exponentially small uniformly on that range. The same is true of $\ee^{-A_m}$ times any fixed power of $r^{-1}$. This will justify removing finitely many small $m$ in the algebraic expansions below.

\section{A summed logarithmic expansion with controlled remainder}\label{dmv:cnt:sec:sum}
The following elementary estimate records the effective scale of multiplicities:
\begin{lemma}[Weighted power sums]\label{dmv:cnt:lem:powersum}
For $k>1$ and $c>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:powerweight}
\sum_{m\ge1}m^{-k}\exp\left(-\frac{c}{rm^2}\right)
=O\bigl(r^{(k-1)/2}\bigr).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The integral has the asserted order after $m=u/\sqrt r$. The summand is unimodal on the positive real axis, so its sum is bounded by its integral plus a constant times its maximum. The maximum is $O(r^{k/2})$, smaller than the displayed bound for $r\le1$.
\end{proof}

Choose a sufficiently large fixed $M$. For $m\ge M$, \eqref{dmv:cnt:eq:Sbound} and the convergent logarithm give
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:logexpand}
L_m=\mu_m+\frac12S_{2,m}+\frac13S_{3,m}+R_{4,m},\qquad
|R_{4,m}|\le\frac{C}{rm^5}.
\end{equation}
Indeed, the tail of the logarithm is bounded by $C\sum_j|p_{jm}|^4$, and \eqref{dmv:cnt:eq:Sbound} applies also to the sum of absolute powers. The whole correction $H_m=L_m-\mu_m$ obeys $|H_m|\le C/(rm^3)$. On the other hand $\Rea A_m\ge c/(rm^2)$. Enlarging $M$ makes $|H_m|$ a fixed small fraction of $\Rea A_m$.

The first-moment error $\delta_m$ need not be relatively small compared with $A_m$ when $m\gg r^{-1}$, but its global absolute bound $|\delta_m|\le Cr$ suffices. Every exponential remainder is consequently dominated by a fixed multiple of $\exp(-c'/(rm^2))$ times its polynomial estimate.

\begin{proposition}[Whole-range matched identity]\label{dmv:cnt:prop:matched}
Uniformly in the fixed sector,
\begin{align}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:matched}
g(t)={}&G(t)+\sum_m\delta_m+\sum_m\delta_m(\ee^{-A_m}-1)
 +\frac12\sum_m\ee^{-A_m}S_{2,m}\nonumber\\
 &+\frac13\sum_m\ee^{-A_m}S_{3,m}
 -\frac18\sum_m\ee^{-A_m}S_{2,m}^2+O(r),\\
G(t)={}&\sum_{m\ge1}(1-\ee^{-A_m}).\nonumber
\end{align}
Every unqualified sum in this identity runs over all positive integers $m$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Expand $1-\ee^{-\mu_m-H_m}$ through $S_{3,m}$ and $S_{2,m}^2$. Terms from the logarithmic tail, the mixed quadratic term, higher quadratic products and the cubic exponential remainder are bounded, after summing, by a constant times
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:sumremainder}
\sum_m\ee^{-c/(rm^2)}
\left(\frac1{rm^5}+\frac1{r^2m^7}+\frac1{r^3m^9}\right)=O(r),
\end{equation}
by Lemma~\ref{dmv:cnt:lem:powersum}. Expanding $\mu_m=A_m+\delta_m$ adds a second-order error $O(\sum|\delta_m|^2)=O(r)$. Its linear term is written in the exactly summable form
\[
\sum_m\ee^{-A_m}\delta_m
=\sum_m\delta_m+\sum_m\delta_m(\ee^{-A_m}-1).
\]
Replacing $\ee^{-\mu_m}$ by $\ee^{-A_m}$ in the retained nonlinear terms costs at most
\[
Cr\sum_m\ee^{-c/(rm^2)}\bigl(|S_{2,m}|+|S_{3,m}|+|S_{2,m}|^2\bigr)=O(r).
\]
All these estimates were established for $m\ge M$. On the finitely many omitted $m$, the original absence probabilities and each weighted approximation term are exponentially small by \eqref{dmv:cnt:eq:smallm}. This completes the genuinely summed expansion.
\end{proof}

\section{Evaluation of the matched sums}\label{dmv:cnt:sec:evaluation}
We use centered coordinates
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:center}
y=h(m+\tfrac12),\qquad
A_m=(y^2-h^2/4)^{-1}.
\end{equation}
They eliminate an apparent $h$ correction in the quadratic occupancy term and make the cancellation transparent.

\subsection{Euler--Maclaurin profiles and their errors}
The profiles needed below are $f_0(y)=1-\ee^{-1/y^2}$ and functions of the form $y^{-k}\ee^{-1/y^2}$ with $k>1$. Their derivatives of every fixed order are integrable along rays $|\arg y|\le\theta_0/2$. At zero, the exponential suppresses every negative power; $f_0(0)=1$ and every positive-order endpoint derivative vanishes. At infinity, rational decay gives the required integrability.

For completeness, Euler--Maclaurin with four derivatives bounds the unscaled sum remainder by
\[
C|h|^3\int_0^\infty |f^{(4)}(ue^{\ii\arg h})|\,du.
\]
All boundary corrections on the full midpoint grid vanish, because the odd positive-order endpoint derivatives vanish. Its integral is initially along the same ray as $h$. Rotating it to the positive axis is legitimate: the small arc is exponentially small for the weighted profiles and $O(\epsilon)$ for $f_0$, and the large arc tends to zero by rational decay. Therefore
\begin{align}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:EM}
\sum_{m\ge1}f_0(h(m+\tfrac12))
 &=h^{-1}\int_0^\infty f_0(y)\,dy-1+O(|h|^3),\\
\sum_{m\ge1}f(h(m+\tfrac12))
 &=h^{-1}\int_0^\infty f(y)\,dy+O(|h|^3)\nonumber
\end{align}
for each of the weighted profiles. The $-1$ in the first line is the omitted midpoint $h/2$, whose value is $1$ up to an exponentially small error. The omitted midpoint is exponentially small for the other profiles. Applying more derivatives would strengthen these summation errors, but is unnecessary here.

The elementary integrals are
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:profileintegrals}
\int_0^\infty(1-\ee^{-1/y^2})\,dy=\sqrt\pi,\qquad
\int_0^\infty y^{-k}\ee^{-1/y^2}\,dy
=\frac12\Gamma\left(\frac{k-1}{2}\right)\quad(k>1).
\end{equation}
The second follows from $u=y^{-2}$; the first follows from integration by parts.

\subsection{The main exponential sum}
For $m\ge M$, expansion of the rational function in \eqref{dmv:cnt:eq:center} gives
\[
A_m=y^{-2}+\frac{h^2}{4y^4}+O(|h|^4|y|^{-6}).
\]
The error in expanding $1-\ee^{-A_m}$ through the displayed correction is bounded by
\[
C\ee^{-c/|y|^2}|h|^4(|y|^{-6}+|y|^{-8}).
\]
After summing, Lemma~\ref{dmv:cnt:lem:powersum} bounds this by $O(|h|^3)$, hence by $O(r)$. The finitely many small $m$ are exponentially harmless as before. Equations~\eqref{dmv:cnt:eq:EM}--\eqref{dmv:cnt:eq:profileintegrals} yield
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:G}
G(t)=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{h}-1+\frac{\sqrt\pi}{16}h+O(r).
\end{equation}

\subsection{The weighted first-moment correction}
Uniformly in $m$,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:weightdelta}
|\ee^{-A_m}-1|\le C\min\left(1,\frac1{rm^2}\right).
\end{equation}
For $m\le r^{-1}$ the error in replacing $\delta_m$ by $-t/12$ is bounded by \eqref{dmv:cnt:eq:delta}. Splitting once more at $r^{-1/2}$, its weighted sum is at most
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:deltabelow}
Cr^3\sum_{m\le r^{-1}}m^2\min(1,(rm^2)^{-1})=O(r).
\end{equation}
For $m>r^{-1}$, $|\delta_m+t/12|\le Cr$ and the same weight gives $O(\sum_{m>r^{-1}}m^{-2})=O(r)$. Thus the replacement is valid over the whole range, and
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:weighted-delta}
\sum_m\delta_m(\ee^{-A_m}-1)=\frac t{12}G(t)+O(r)
=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{12}h+O(r).
\end{equation}
This weighted correction is separate from the unweighted $-1/2$ in \eqref{dmv:cnt:eq:deltatel}.

\subsection{The nonlinear occupancy terms}
Expanding $(1-\ee^{-tj})^k$ and summing in $j$ gives the exact finite-difference formula
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:finite-difference}
S_{k,m}=\sum_{i=0}^k(-1)^i\binom ki\mathcal B\bigl(t(km+i)\bigr).
\end{equation}
For $k=2,3$, separate the rational pole and regular part:
\begin{align}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:T2T3}
S_{2,m}&=T_{2,m}+E_{2,m},&
T_{2,m}&=\frac1{2tm(m+1)(2m+1)},\\
S_{3,m}&=T_{3,m}+E_{3,m},&
T_{3,m}&=\frac6{t(3m)(3m+1)(3m+2)(3m+3)}.\nonumber
\end{align}
The regular finite differences can be represented as integrals of $t^2b''$ and $-t^3b'''$ over unit cubes. Hence \eqref{dmv:cnt:eq:bderivative} implies
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:Ebounds}
|E_{2,m}|\le\frac{Cr^2}{1+(rm)^3},\qquad
|E_{3,m}|\le\frac{Cr^3}{1+(rm)^4}.
\end{equation}
In particular $\sum|E_{2,m}|=O(r)$ and $\sum|E_{3,m}|=O(r^2)$. The $E_2$ error in the square term also causes no difficulty: its cross term is bounded by
$O(r^2)\sum_m\ee^{-c/(rm^2)}|T_{2,m}|=O(r^2)$, and $\sum|E_{2,m}|^2=O(r^3)$.

In the centered coordinate $y=h(m+1/2)$,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:center-T}
T_{2,m}=\frac{h}{4y^3}+O(|h|^3|y|^{-5}),\qquad
T_{3,m}=\frac{2h^2}{27y^4}+O(|h|^4|y|^{-6}).
\end{equation}
These estimates hold for $m\ge M$; the corresponding weighted remainders sum to the required order by Lemma~\ref{dmv:cnt:lem:powersum}. There is no $h^2$ term in $T_2$ and no term of order $h$ in $A_m-y^{-2}$. Therefore there can be no $h$ correction after summing $\ee^{-A_m}S_{2,m}/2$; explicitly,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:S2result}
\frac12\sum_m\ee^{-A_m}S_{2,m}
=\frac18\int_0^\infty y^{-3}\ee^{-1/y^2}\,dy+O(r)
=\frac1{16}+O(r).
\end{equation}
Similarly,
\begin{align}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:S3square}
\frac13\sum_m\ee^{-A_m}S_{3,m}
&=\frac{2h}{81}\int_0^\infty y^{-4}\ee^{-1/y^2}\,dy+O(r)
=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{162}h+O(r),\\
-\frac18\sum_m\ee^{-A_m}S_{2,m}^2
&=-\frac h{128}\int_0^\infty y^{-6}\ee^{-1/y^2}\,dy+O(r)
=-\frac{3\sqrt\pi}{1024}h+O(r).\nonumber
\end{align}

\subsection{The sector expansion and its essential tail}
Combining Proposition~\ref{dmv:cnt:prop:matched} with \eqref{dmv:cnt:eq:deltatel}, \eqref{dmv:cnt:eq:G}, \eqref{dmv:cnt:eq:weighted-delta}, \eqref{dmv:cnt:eq:S2result} and \eqref{dmv:cnt:eq:S3square} proves the following.
\begin{theorem}[Uniform radial expansion]\label{dmv:cnt:thm:radial}
In a fixed sufficiently small closed sector about the positive real axis,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:radial}
g(t)=\sqrt\pi\,t^{-1/2}-\frac{23}{16}+\sqrt\pi\,d\,t^{1/2}+O(|t|),
\qquad d=\frac{12365}{82944}.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
The bookkeeping, with each coefficient of $h$ divided by $\sqrt\pi$, is
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.2}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}\toprule
Contribution & Constant & Coefficient of $\sqrt\pi h$\\\midrule
$G(t)$ & $-1$ & $1/16$\\
$\sum\delta_m$ & $-1/2$ & $0$\\
$\sum\delta_m(\ee^{-A_m}-1)$ & $0$ & $1/12$\\
$\frac12\sum\ee^{-A_m}S_{2,m}$ & $1/16$ & $0$\\
$\frac13\sum\ee^{-A_m}S_{3,m}$ & $0$ & $1/162$\\
$-\frac18\sum\ee^{-A_m}S_{2,m}^2$ & $0$ & $-3/1024$\\\midrule
Total & $-23/16$ & $12365/82944$\\\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
In the third column, the entry $c$ means a contribution $c\sqrt\pi h$.
A calculation confined to $m\asymp t^{-1/2}$ and omitting \eqref{dmv:cnt:eq:deltatel} would give $-15/16$. That value is incorrect: the missing $-1/2$ is a whole-range tail effect. This is why pointwise occupancy limits are not enough for the constant term.

\section{Whole-circle localization from forbidden-value products}\label{dmv:cnt:sec:localization}
A radial expansion alone does not prove a coefficient asymptotic. We now establish a full-torus bound without assuming a global growth hypothesis for $g$.

Take $q=\ee^{-\tau+\ii\theta}$ with $|\theta|\le\pi$, where $\tau$ is defined in \eqref{dmv:cnt:eq:constants}. A factor in \eqref{dmv:cnt:eq:forbidden} is
\[
Z_m(z)=\sum_{\substack{k\ge0\\k\ne m}}z^k.
\]
For $j\in[\tau^{-1},2\tau^{-1}]$, put $\rho_j=\ee^{-\tau j}\in[\ee^{-2},\ee^{-1}]$. The normalized coefficients of $Z_m(\rho_j z)/Z_m(\rho_j)$ form a probability distribution. Every fixed permitted value $k$ has a probability bounded below uniformly in $m,j$, because $Z_m(\rho_j)\le(1-\rho_j)^{-1}$.

If $m\ne1$, the permitted values $0$ and $1$ form an adjacent pair. If $m=1$, use $2$ and $3$. A characteristic function $\phi(\alpha)=\sum_k\pi_k\ee^{\ii k\alpha}$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:characteristic}
|\phi(\alpha)|^2
=1-4\sum_{k<\ell}\pi_k\pi_\ell\sin^2\bigl((k-\ell)\alpha/2\bigr).
\end{equation}
Keeping the appropriate adjacent pair and using $\sqrt{1-u}\le\ee^{-u/2}$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:factor-damping}
\frac{|Z_m(\ee^{-\tau j+\ii j\theta})|}{Z_m(\ee^{-\tau j})}
\le\exp\bigl(-c\sin^2(j\theta/2)\bigr),
\end{equation}
uniformly in $m$. The unrestricted geometric factors satisfy the same estimate, using their values $0,1$.

\begin{lemma}[Consecutive-block damping]\label{dmv:cnt:lem:block}
For all sufficiently small $\tau>0$ and $|\theta|\le\pi$,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:block}
\sum_{j=\lceil1/\tau\rceil}^{\lfloor2/\tau\rfloor}\sin^2(j\theta/2)
\ge\frac c\tau\min\left(1,(\theta/\tau)^2\right).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
When $|\theta|\le a\tau$ for a fixed small $a>0$, the small-angle lower bound for sine and $\sum j^2\asymp\tau^{-3}$ give $c\theta^2\tau^{-3}$. On $a\tau\le|\theta|\le C\tau$, set $u=\theta/\tau$. The Riemann sum, multiplied by $\tau$, converges uniformly on this compact $u$ range to $\int_1^2\sin^2(uy/2)\,dy$, which has a strictly positive minimum there. Finally, for $C\tau\le|\theta|\le\pi$,
\[
\left|\sum_{j=J}^K\cos(j\theta)\right|
\le\frac1{|\sin(\theta/2)|}\le\frac\pi{|\theta|}.
\]
Since $\sin^2(j\theta/2)=(1-\cos(j\theta))/2$, a sufficiently large fixed $C$ makes the block-length term dominate this geometric-sum error. These three ranges cover the torus.
\end{proof}

All normalized factors outside the block have modulus at most $1$. Also $P_m(\ee^{-\tau})\le P(\ee^{-\tau})$. From \eqref{dmv:cnt:eq:Fn}, \eqref{dmv:cnt:eq:factor-damping} and Lemma~\ref{dmv:cnt:lem:block},
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:global}
|F_n(\ee^{-\tau+\ii\theta})|
\le2nP(\ee^{-\tau})
\exp\left[-\frac c\tau\min\left(1,(\theta/\tau)^2\right)\right].
\end{equation}
The same damping estimate holds for $P$, without the factor $2n$.

Let
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:central}
L=\log(1/\tau),\qquad \theta_*=\tau^{3/2}L.
\end{equation}
On the complementary arc $\theta_*\le|\theta|\le\pi$, the exponential factor in \eqref{dmv:cnt:eq:global} is at most $\ee^{-cL^2}$. Since the classical partition saddle has scale
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:partition-scale}
p(n)\asymp \ee^{n\tau}P(\ee^{-\tau})\tau^{3/2},
\end{equation}
and $n=O(\tau^{-2})$, the outer Cauchy integral for $F_n$ is $p(n)$ times a quantity smaller than every fixed power of $\tau$. Equation~\eqref{dmv:cnt:eq:partition-scale} also follows directly from the local saddle calculation in the next section combined with this same bound for $P$.

On the central arc $|\theta|\le\theta_*$, put $t=\tau-\ii\theta$. Then $t$ lies in the fixed sector of Theorem~\ref{dmv:cnt:thm:radial} for small $\tau$. For $m>n$,
\[
\sum_j|p_{jm}|\le2\sum_j\ee^{-\tau mj}=O(\ee^{-\tau m}),
\qquad |1-Q_m|=O(\ee^{-\tau m}).
\]
The sum over $m>n$ is $O(\tau^{-1}\ee^{-\tau n})=O(\ee^{-c\tau n})$ after reducing $c>0$. As $\tau n\asymp\tau^{-1}$, replacing the finite $F_n$ by $P(\ee^{-t})g(t)$ only on this arc causes an exponentially small relative coefficient error, even after the harmless polynomial factors from integration. The finite truncation is exact globally; the infinite extension is justified locally. No mild global bound on $g$ has been presumed.

\section{Local saddle transfer and the mean}\label{dmv:cnt:sec:transfer}
The classical modular expansion of the partition product is
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:modular}
P(\ee^{-t})=\left(\frac{t}{2\pi}\right)^{1/2}
\exp\left(\frac At-\frac t{24}\right)
\left(1+O(\ee^{-c/\tau})\right)
\end{equation}
on the shrinking central arc. It follows, for example, from the Dedekind eta transformation and the absolutely convergent transformed product. The same formula gives
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:central-gauss}
|P(\ee^{-t})|\le CP(\ee^{-\tau})\exp(-c\theta^2/\tau^3)
\quad (|\theta|\le\theta_*).
\end{equation}
Indeed, $\Rea(A/t)-A/\tau=-A\theta^2/(\tau(\tau^2+\theta^2))$ and $\theta/\tau=o(1)$. Thus a uniform $O(|t|)$ error in $g$ contributes $O(\tau)p(n)$ after absolute integration.

\subsection{A local monomial lemma}
For any fixed real $s$, define the local integral
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:Js}
J_s=\frac{\ee^{n\tau}}{2\pi}
\int_{-\theta_*}^{\theta_*}\ee^{-\ii n\theta}
P(\ee^{-\tau+\ii\theta})(\tau-\ii\theta)^s\,d\theta.
\end{equation}
It is important that $J_s$ is defined by a local contour integral. We do not regard $(-\log q)^s$ as a globally analytic generating function at $q=0$.

\begin{lemma}[Normalized monomial transfer]\label{dmv:cnt:lem:transfer}
For each fixed real $s$,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:transfer}
\frac{J_s}{p(n)}=\tau^s\left(1-\frac{s(s+3)}{4A}\tau+O(\tau^2)\right).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Set $\lambda=A/\tau=\nu\tau$ and change orientation if necessary so that $t=\tau(1+\ii v/\sqrt\lambda)$. The new interval is symmetric with $|v|\le\sqrt A L$. The amplitude exponent in \eqref{dmv:cnt:eq:modular} is $\beta=s+1/2$. Uniform Taylor expansion gives
\begin{align}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:saddleexp}
\nu t+A/t&=2\lambda-v^2+\frac{\ii v^3}{\sqrt\lambda}
+\frac{v^4}{\lambda}+\cdots,\\
(1+\ii v/\sqrt\lambda)^\beta
&=1+\frac{\ii\beta v}{\sqrt\lambda}
-\frac{\beta(\beta-1)v^2}{2\lambda}+\cdots.\nonumber
\end{align}
Odd terms have zero integral on the symmetric interval. Expanding through degree $\lambda^{-3/2}$, the remaining integrated error is $O(\lambda^{-2})$: on the logarithmic interval, analytic Taylor remainders are bounded by $\lambda^{-2}$ times fixed polynomials in $v$, multiplied by $\ee^{-c v^2}$ after slightly weakening the Gaussian. These polynomials have finite Gaussian moments. Extending the moments to the real line costs $O(\ee^{-cL^2})$.

Under the normalized density $\ee^{-v^2}/\sqrt\pi$, the moments needed at order $\lambda^{-1}$ are $\E v^2=1/2$, $\E v^4=3/4$, and $\E v^6=15/8$. The first correction is therefore
\begin{align}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:c-beta}
c(\beta)&=\frac34-\frac{15}{16}-\frac{3\beta}{4}
-\frac{\beta(\beta-1)}4\\
&=-\frac{4(\beta+1)^2-1}{16}.\nonumber
\end{align}
For $s=0$, $\beta=1/2$ and $c(1/2)=-1/2$. The already proved outer bound for $P$ shows that $J_0/p(n)=1+O(\tau^R)$ for every fixed $R$. Dividing the two saddle expansions and observing
\[
c(s+1/2)-c(1/2)=-\frac{s(s+3)}4
\]
gives \eqref{dmv:cnt:eq:transfer}, since $\lambda^{-1}=\tau/A$.
\end{proof}

This is the first-order form of the classical Bessel-ratio transfer mechanism; see Grabner, Knopfmacher and Wagner \cite{gkw}. Their global hypothesis is not imported without verification. Section~\ref{dmv:cnt:sec:localization} supplies the necessary independent outer-arc argument for the present statistic.

\subsection{Completion of the mean proof}
Apply Theorem~\ref{dmv:cnt:thm:radial} only in the central integral. Section~\ref{dmv:cnt:sec:localization} treats the complementary integral and the extension of the finite $m$ sum. Equation~\eqref{dmv:cnt:eq:central-gauss} transfers the radial $O(|t|)$ remainder to $O(\tau)p(n)$. Thus
\[
a(n)=\sqrt\pi J_{-1/2}-\frac{23}{16}J_0+\sqrt\pi dJ_{1/2}+O(\tau p(n)).
\]
In Lemma~\ref{dmv:cnt:lem:transfer}, $s=-1/2$ supplies the extra $5\tau/(16A)$; the correction for $s=1/2$ is already below the retained order. This proves \eqref{dmv:cnt:eq:meantau}. Since
\[
\sqrt\pi\tau^{-1/2}=6^{1/4}(n-1/24)^{1/4},
\]
replacing $n-1/24$ by $n$ changes the leading term by $O(n^{-3/4})$, smaller than the claimed remainder. The corresponding changes in later terms are even smaller. This proves Theorem~\ref{dmv:cnt:thm:mean}.

\section{Total asymptotics and eventual strict increase}\label{dmv:cnt:sec:total}
The same $s=0$ saddle calculation, including its leading normalization, gives
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:partition-asymp}
p(n)=\frac{\ee^{Bx}}{4\sqrt3\,x^2}
\left(1-\frac1{Bx}+O(x^{-2})\right),\qquad x=\sqrt{n-1/24}.
\end{equation}
For clarity, the leading saddle factor before substituting $\tau=\sqrt A/x$ is
\[
\frac1{2\pi}\left(\frac\tau{2\pi}\right)^{1/2}
\frac{\tau}{\sqrt\lambda}\ee^{2\lambda}\sqrt\pi
=\frac{\tau^2}{2\pi\sqrt{2A}}\ee^{2A/\tau},
\]
which equals $\ee^{Bx}/(4\sqrt3 x^2)$. Also $-1/(2\lambda)=-1/(Bx)$.

Inserting $\tau=\sqrt A/x$ into \eqref{dmv:cnt:eq:meantau}, multiplying by \eqref{dmv:cnt:eq:partition-asymp}, and factoring the leading term yields \eqref{dmv:cnt:eq:totalx}. The coefficient arithmetic is
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:b2check}
b_2=\sqrt A\left(d+\frac5{16A}\right)-\frac1{2\sqrt A}
=\sqrt A\,d-\frac3{16\sqrt A}
=\frac\pi{\sqrt6}\left(d-\frac9{8\pi^2}\right).
\end{equation}
The relative mean error is $O(x^{-3/2})$, so all omitted cross terms fall within that error. Expanding $x=\sqrt n-1/(48\sqrt n)+O(n^{-3/2})$ gives \eqref{dmv:cnt:eq:totaln}; the exponential contributes $-B/48$ to the $n^{-1/2}$ coefficient.

To prove eventual strict increase without differentiating an unknown remainder, put
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:Phi}
\Phi(x)=Bx-\frac32\log x+\log C+\ell_1x^{-1/2}+\ell_2x^{-1},
\quad \ell_1=b_1,\quad\ell_2=b_2-b_1^2/2.
\end{equation}
Taking logarithms in \eqref{dmv:cnt:eq:totalx}, there are $M>0$ and $n_0$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:logerror}
\log a(n)=\Phi(x_n)+R_n,\qquad
x_n=\sqrt{n-1/24},\qquad |R_n|\le Mx_n^{-3/2}
\quad(n\ge n_0).
\end{equation}
Since $x_{n+1}-x_n=1/(2x_n)+O(x_n^{-3})$,
\[
\Phi(x_{n+1})-\Phi(x_n)=\frac B{2x_n}+O(x_n^{-2}).
\]
The two independent remainder bounds give $|R_{n+1}-R_n|=O(x_n^{-3/2})=o(x_n^{-1})$. The adjacent logarithmic increment is therefore positive for all sufficiently large $n$. This proves the last statement of Theorem~\ref{dmv:cnt:thm:total}.

\section{A rigorous two-ceiling inverse}\label{dmv:cnt:sec:inverse}
We prove Theorem~\ref{dmv:cnt:thm:inverse} using monotone logarithmic envelopes, so no unspecified smooth interpolation enters the argument.

For large $Y$, the leading logarithmic equation
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:x0eq}
Bx_0-\frac32\log x_0+\log C=\log Y
\end{equation}
has a unique large solution. Rearranging $x_0\ee^{-2Bx_0/3}=(C/Y)^{2/3}$ gives exactly the $W_{-1}$ expression in \eqref{dmv:cnt:eq:inverse}. The argument tends to $0$ from below, so the branch is real and $x_0\to\infty$.

With $M$ as in \eqref{dmv:cnt:eq:logerror}, define
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:envelopes}
H_+(x)=\Phi(x)+Mx^{-3/2},\qquad
H_-(x)=\Phi(x)-Mx^{-3/2}.
\end{equation}
For all sufficiently large $x$, both derivatives exceed $B/2$. Let $x_+$ and $x_-$ be their unique large roots at height $\log Y$:
\[
H_+(x_+)=\log Y=H_-(x_-),\qquad x_+\le x_-.
\]
Consider
\[
\widehat x=x_0-\frac{\ell_1}{B}x_0^{-1/2}-\frac{\ell_2}{B}x_0^{-1}.
\]
Substitution using \eqref{dmv:cnt:eq:x0eq} gives $H_\pm(\widehat x)-\log Y=O(x_0^{-3/2})$. For example, the logarithmic change is $O((\widehat x-x_0)/x_0)=O(x_0^{-3/2})$, while the leading correction terms have already been canceled. The derivative lower bound and the mean-value theorem prove
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:rootexpand}
x_\pm=x_0-\frac{\ell_1}{B}x_0^{-1/2}-\frac{\ell_2}{B}x_0^{-1}
+O(x_0^{-3/2}).
\end{equation}
Squaring gives
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:squareroots}
x_\pm^2+\frac1{24}
=x_0^2-\frac{2\ell_1}{B}x_0^{1/2}-\frac{2\ell_2}{B}+\frac1{24}
+O(x_0^{-1/2})=Z(Y)+O(x_0^{-1/2}).
\end{equation}
The square of the correction is $O(x_0^{-1})$, which is smaller than the stated error.

Choose $Y$ larger than all exceptional early values $a(n)$ for which \eqref{dmv:cnt:eq:logerror} is unavailable. If an integer $n<x_+^2+1/24$, then
\[
\log a(n)\le H_+(x_n)<\log Y.
\]
If $n\ge x_-^2+1/24$, then
\[
\log a(n)\ge H_-(x_n)\ge\log Y.
\]
It follows, including equality cases at integral endpoints, that
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:root-ceilings}
\left\lceil x_+^2+\frac1{24}\right\rceil
\le N(Y)\le
\left\lceil x_-^2+\frac1{24}\right\rceil.
\end{equation}
A single fixed $C_1$ dominating the errors in \eqref{dmv:cnt:eq:squareroots} yields \eqref{dmv:cnt:eq:ceiling}. This is a genuine shrinking error bound for the threshold location before integer rounding. At values of $Y$ for which $Z(Y)$ is very near an integer, the theorem alone need not decide between the two adjacent possibilities.

\subsection{An exact signed-product algorithm}
For a cutoff $N$, work in $\mathbb Z[q]/(q^{N+1})$. Compute $p(0),\ldots,p(N)$ by ordinary partition dynamic programming. For each $1\le m\le N$, form
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:exact-truncate}
Q_m(q)\equiv\prod_{j=1}^{\lfloor N/m\rfloor}
(1-q^{mj}+q^{(m+1)j})\pmod{q^{N+1}}.
\end{equation}
Every factor is multiplied in descending coefficient order, so the in-place operation uses only coefficients from before that factor. Form
\[
R(q)=\sum_{m=1}^N(1-Q_m(q))\pmod{q^{N+1}},
\qquad a(n)=\sum_{k=1}^np(n-k)[q^k]R(q).
\]
All arithmetic is exact integer arithmetic. No coefficient of the asymptotic expansion is used in this calculation. The package recomputes every $a(n)$ and $p(n)$ for $0\le n\le2000$ and compares them with the included table.

\subsection{Independent finite models}
An independent positive dynamic program computes $[q^n]P_m$ directly from the permitted multiplicities. If $c_j(n)$ counts choices for part sizes at most $j$ avoiding $m$, then
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:cnt:eq:positiveDP}
c_j(n)=\sum_{\substack{k\ge0,\ jk\le n\\k\ne m}}c_{j-1}(n-jk).
\end{equation}
The unrestricted inner sum is implemented by the usual unbounded-knapsack recurrence, followed by subtraction of the single forbidden contribution from the previous row. This neither constructs $Q_m$ nor uses its convolution with $P$. Summing $p(n)-[q^n]P_m$ for $1\le m\le n$ agrees with every exact term through $n=400$.

\begin{partabstract}
For an integer partition, sum each positive multiplicity value once, however many part sizes have that multiplicity. Let $a_n$ be the total of this statistic over the partitions of $n$, and let $p(n)$ be the partition number. We prove an expansion of $a_n/p(n)$ through its five scales $\sqrt n\log n$, $\sqrt n$, $n^{1/4}$, $\log n$, and $1$, with error $O(n^{-1/4})$. A weighted matched decomposition is essential: the square of a nonlocal correction and a finite-difference tail both contribute to the constant. Mellin continuation evaluates the logarithmic leading terms; convergent regularized integrals produce constants whose zeta derivatives cancel. A positive-product estimate localizes the full coefficient integral on the entire circle, and local analytic differentiation of saddle monomials transfers logarithmic amplitudes. An explicit smooth template gives eventual strict increase and two ceiling bounds for the first crossing of a large threshold, with pre-rounding uncertainty $O((\log Y)^{-1/2}/\log\log Y)$. Exact computations through $2500$, independent positive dynamic programming through $450$, and partition enumeration through $43$ accompany the proof. These finite checks do not certify asymptotic error constants. No explicit numerical onset, all-orders expansion, distributional theorem, or absolute novelty assertion is supplied.
\end{partabstract}

\section{The statistic and the results}\label{dmv:sum:sec:results}
Write $\lambda=(1^{M_1}2^{M_2}\cdots)$ for a partition of $n$, where $\sum_j jM_j=n$. Define
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:def}
 W(\lambda)=\sum_{\substack{m\ge1:\ M_j=m\text{ for some }j}}m,
 \qquad a_n=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}W(\lambda),\qquad a_0=0.
\end{equation}
This is OEIS A373273 \cite{oeisA373273}. For example, $(5,5,3,3,3,1)$ has multiplicity values $2,3,1$, so $W=6$, whereas $(5,5,3,3,1)$ has values $2,2,1$, so $W=3$. Zero is excluded, and repeated positive values are summed only once. Thus $W$ is different from the number of distinct multiplicities and from the total number of parts. Under the uniform measure on partitions of $n$, $\E_nW=a_n/p(n)$.

Use the constants and scaled variables
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:notation}
 A=\frac{\pi^2}{6},\quad N=n-\frac1{24},\quad
 \tau=\sqrt{\frac A N},\quad \ell=\log\frac1\tau,
 \quad c=\frac32-\frac\gamma2,\quad K=\frac{9607}{20736}-\frac\gamma{24},
\end{equation}
where $\gamma$ is Euler's constant. All asymptotic statements concern $n\to\infty$ or $t\to0$ in a specified fixed sector. Complex error terms are bounds in modulus. Implied constants exist but are not assigned numerical values here.

\begin{theorem}[Five-scale mean]\label{dmv:sum:thm:mean}
With \eqref{dmv:sum:eq:notation},
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:mean}
\begin{split}
\frac{a_n}{p(n)}={}&\frac{\ell/2+c}{\tau}-\frac{7\sqrt\pi}{16\sqrt\tau}
 +\left(\frac1{24}+\frac1{4A}\right)\ell\\
 &+K+\frac{7/8-\gamma/4}{A}+O(\sqrt\tau).
\end{split}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, putting $k=\sqrt A$ and $d=1/24+1/(4A)$,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:mean-n}
\begin{split}
\frac{a_n}{p(n)}={}&\frac{\sqrt n}{4k}\log n
 +\frac{\sqrt n}{k}\left(c-\frac12\log k\right)
 -\frac{7\sqrt\pi}{16\sqrt k}n^{1/4}+\frac d2\log n\\
 &+K+\frac{7/8-\gamma/4}{A}-d\log k+O(n^{-1/4}).
\end{split}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
Replacing $N$ by $n$ in \eqref{dmv:sum:eq:mean-n} changes the first term by $O(n^{-1/2}\log n)$, which is smaller than the stated error. In particular the leading mean is $\sqrt{6n}\log n/(4\pi)$.

\begin{theorem}[Absolute expansion]\label{dmv:sum:thm:absolute}
Let $C_N=\ee^{2A/\tau}/(4\sqrt3 N)$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:absolute}
 a_n=C_N\left[\frac{\ell/2+c}{\tau}-\frac{7\sqrt\pi}{16\sqrt\tau}
                 +\frac\ell{24}+K+\frac1{8A}+O(\sqrt\tau)\right].
\end{equation}
The sequence $(a_n)$ is eventually strictly increasing.
\end{theorem}

The entire constant in \eqref{dmv:sum:eq:absolute}, including $1/(8A)$, is retained for the inverse error rate stated below. To state the inverse precisely, let
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:phi}
\begin{split}
\Phi(x)=\frac{\ee^{2kx}}{4\sqrt3 x^2}\bigg[
 &\frac{x}{k}\left(\frac12\log\frac{x}{k}+c\right)
 -\frac{7\sqrt\pi}{16\sqrt k}\sqrt x\\
 &+\frac1{24}\log\frac{x}{k}+K+\frac1{8A}\bigg].
\end{split}
\end{equation}
This is positive and strictly increasing for sufficiently large real $x$.

\begin{theorem}[Shrinking first-crossing bracket]\label{dmv:sum:thm:inverse}
For large $Y>0$, let $x_Y$ be the unique large solution of $\Phi(x_Y)=Y$, and put $v_Y=x_Y^2+1/24$. There exist constants $X>1$, $M>0$, and an integer $n_0$ such that the following construction is valid. Set
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:H}
 H_\pm(x)=\log\Phi(x)\pm M\frac{x^{-3/2}}{\log x}\qquad(x\ge X).
\end{equation}
Both functions are strictly increasing, and for all sufficiently large $Y$ the roots
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:roots}
 H_+(x_-(Y))=\log Y,\qquad H_-(x_+(Y))=\log Y
\end{equation}
exist beyond $\sqrt{n_0-1/24}$. Then, for $\nu(Y)=\min\{n\ge0:a_n\ge Y\}$,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:ceiling}
 \left\lceil x_-(Y)^2+\frac1{24}\right\rceil\le\nu(Y)
 \le\left\lceil x_+(Y)^2+\frac1{24}\right\rceil,
\end{equation}
where each unrounded endpoint is
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:width}
 v_Y+O\!\left(\frac{(\log Y)^{-1/2}}{\log\log Y}\right).
\end{equation}
The two ceilings are eventually equal or adjacent. The statement does not specify numerical $X,M,n_0$, and does not assert a single exact ceiling for every $Y$.
\end{theorem}

\section{Exact generating functions and finite models}\label{dmv:sum:sec:model}
Let $P(q)=\prod_{j\ge1}(1-q^j)^{-1}=\sum_{n\ge0}p(n)q^n$. Under the Boltzmann partition measure at real $q\in(0,1)$, the $M_j$ are independent with
\begin{equation}
 \Prob_q(M_j=m)=(1-q^j)q^{mj}.
\end{equation}
For $q=\ee^{-t}$ define
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:Q}
 p_{jm}=(1-\ee^{-tj})\ee^{-tmj},\qquad
 Q_m(q)=\prod_{j\ge1}(1-(1-q^j)q^{mj}).
\end{equation}
Thus $Q_m$ is the probability that multiplicity $m$ is absent. Linearity of expectation, applied to $W=\sum_{m\ge1}m\,1_{\{M_j=m\text{ for some }j\}}$, gives
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:gf}
 \sum_{n\ge0}a_nq^n=P(q)w(t),\qquad w(t)=\sum_{m\ge1}m(1-Q_m(\ee^{-t})).
\end{equation}
The identity also holds formally: the $m$th summand starts in degree $m$. On a compact subset of $\Rea t>0$, $\sum_{m,j}m|p_{jm}|<\infty$, and
\[
 |1-Q_m|\le\left(\sum_j|p_{jm}|\right)\exp\left(\sum_j|p_{jm}|\right),
\]
which proves local absolute convergence and analyticity.

The product
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:avoid}
 P_m(q)=P(q)Q_m(q)=\prod_{j\ge1}\left(\frac1{1-q^j}-q^{mj}\right)
\end{equation}
has nonnegative coefficients: it counts partitions avoiding the multiplicity $m$. Hence
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:finite}
 F_n(q)=\sum_{m=1}^n m(P(q)-P_m(q)),\qquad [q^n]F_n(q)=a_n.
\end{equation}
This positive interpretation later controls the full coefficient circle without imposing a global hypothesis on $w$.

For a cutoff $T$, an exact integer algorithm first computes $p(0),\ldots,p(T)$, then for each $1\le m\le T$ multiplies the finite factors
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:polynomial}
 Q_m(q)\equiv\prod_{1\le j\le T/m}(1-q^{mj}+q^{(m+1)j})\pmod{q^{T+1}}.
\end{equation}
Terms of degree greater than $T$ are discarded. Form the polynomial $w=\sum_{m=1}^T m(1-Q_m)$ and convolve with $p$. Descending coefficient updates, or separate old and new arrays, avoid accidentally reusing a factor. The construction uses integer arithmetic only. Its straightforward upper cost is $O(T^2\log T)$ arithmetic operations for the products plus $O(T^2)$ for the final convolution; this counts integer operations, not bit complexity.

A genuinely different check constructs each $P_m$ by positive allowed-occupancy dynamic programming: for part size $j$, convolve with $\sum_{r\ge0,\ r\ne m}q^{rj}$, truncated at the cutoff. This never computes $Q_m$ or a signed collision correction. A third check enumerates descending partitions and sums the set of positive adjacent gaps, including the last part minus zero. Under Ferrers conjugation these gaps are the positive multiplicities of the conjugate partition; summing over all partitions therefore gives the same total. The accompanying computations compare these models in Section~\ref{dmv:sum:sec:computation}.

\section{Uniform estimates in a zero-free sector}\label{dmv:sum:sec:sector}
Fix the closed sector $|\arg t|\le\theta_0=\pi/12$, and write $r=|t|$, $h=\sqrt t$. All powers and $\log t$ use their principal branches in this sector. Define
\begin{align}\label{dmv:sum:eq:basic}
 B(z)&=(\ee^z-1)^{-1},& b(z)&=B(z)-z^{-1},\\
 A_m&=\frac1{tm(m+1)},&
 \mu_m&=B(tm)-B((m+1)t),& \delta_m&=\mu_m-A_m,\nonumber\\
 S_{k,m}&=\sum_{j\ge1}p_{jm}^k.&&\nonumber
\end{align}
Regularity at zero and exponential decay of $B$ at sector infinity give
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:bder}
 b'(0)=\frac1{12},\quad b''(z)=O(z)\ (z\to0),\qquad
 |b^{(k)}(z)|\le\frac{C_k}{1+|z|^{k+1}}\quad(k\ge1).
\end{equation}
In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:delta}
\begin{split}
 \delta_m&=-t\int_0^1b'(t(m+u))\,du,\qquad
 |\delta_m|\le\frac{Cr}{1+(rm)^2},\\
 \delta_m&=-tb'(tm)+O\!\left(\frac{r^2}{1+(rm)^3}\right).
\end{split}
\end{equation}
For $z$ in the sector, integration of $\ee^{-vz}$ over $0\le v\le1$ yields
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:zerofree}
 |(1-\ee^{-z})\ee^{-mz}|\le |z|\ee^{-m\Rea z}
 \le\frac1{em\cos\theta_0}<1.
\end{equation}
Thus each factor has a logarithm given by its convergent power series. The sum of these factor logarithms defines an analytic logarithm of $Q_m$. It need not coincide with the principal logarithm of the whole product; no such identification is used.

For fixed $k\ge1$, the bound $|p_{jm}|\le rj\ee^{-mj\Rea t}$ and boundedness of $a^{k+1}\sum_{j\ge1}j^k\ee^{-aj}$ imply
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:Sbound}
 |S_{k,m}|\le\frac{C_k}{rm^{k+1}},\qquad \sup_j|p_{jm}|\le C/m.
\end{equation}
We repeatedly use, for $k>1$ and fixed $c_1>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:sumest}
 \sum_{m\ge1}m^{-k}\ee^{-c_1/(rm^2)}=O(r^{(k-1)/2}).
\end{equation}
An integral comparison after $m=x/\sqrt r$ proves this; the mesh-end pieces satisfy the same bound.

For each of finitely many small $m$, put
\[
 f_m(z)=-\log(1-(1-\ee^{-z})\ee^{-mz}).
\]
Its chosen logarithm is analytic by \eqref{dmv:sum:eq:zerofree}. The function vanishes at zero, decays at infinity, and has integrable derivatives on the sector rays. Ray Euler--Maclaurin summation, followed by rotation to the positive ray, gives
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:smallm}
 -\log Q_m=I_m/t+O(r),\qquad I_m=\int_0^\infty f_m(y)\,dy>0.
\end{equation}
Hence $Q_m$ and the approximants $\ee^{-A_m}$ times fixed inverse powers of $r$ are exponentially small for fixed $m$. This disposes of the range where a nonlinear exponential expansion alone is not uniformly small.

\section{A convergent weighted matched decomposition}\label{dmv:sum:sec:match}
Define the seven separately convergent sums
\begin{align}\label{dmv:sum:eq:pieces}
 L(t)&=\sum_{m\ge1}B(tm),&
 C_0(t)&=\sum_{m\ge1}m(1-\ee^{-A_m}-A_m),\nonumber\\
 D(t)&=\sum_{m\ge1}m\delta_m(\ee^{-A_m}-1),&
 E(t)&=-\frac12\sum_{m\ge1}m\ee^{-A_m}\delta_m^2,\\
 H_2(t)&=\frac12\sum_{m\ge1}m\ee^{-A_m}S_{2,m},&
 H_3(t)&=\frac13\sum_{m\ge1}m\ee^{-A_m}S_{3,m},\nonumber\\
 H_{22}(t)&=-\frac18\sum_{m\ge1}m\ee^{-A_m}S_{2,m}^2.&&\nonumber
\end{align}
The individual sum $\sum m\delta_m$ diverges and is never separated from its convergent combinations.

\begin{lemma}[Weighted matching]\label{dmv:sum:lem:matching}
Uniformly in the fixed sector,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:matched}
 w=L+C_0+D+E+H_2+H_3+H_{22}+O(\sqrt r).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The absolutely convergent telescoping identity $\sum_m m\mu_m=\sum_m B(tm)=L$ makes $L+C_0$ the convergent sum $\sum_m m(1-\ee^{-A_m}+\delta_m)$. Adding $D$ changes the linear delta contribution to its exponentially damped version.

For $m$ above a fixed sufficiently large $M$, logarithmic expansion gives
\[
 -\log Q_m=A_m+\delta_m+\tfrac12S_{2,m}+\tfrac13S_{3,m}
                  +O((rm^5)^{-1}).
\]
The nonlinear logarithmic part is $O((rm^3)^{-1})$, at most a small fixed fraction of $\Rea A_m$ for large $M$. The delta part is $O(r)$ absolutely; it need not be relatively small compared with $A_m$ in the far tail. Thus integral Taylor remainders of the exponential are dominated by a polynomial prefactor times $\ee^{-c_1/(rm^2)}$. Retain the linear delta, $S_2/2$, and $S_3/3$ terms, and the negative half-squares of delta and $S_2/2$. They give exactly \eqref{dmv:sum:eq:pieces}.

Here is the weighted accounting for all omitted terms:
\begin{align*}
 \text{logarithmic tail: }&
 Cr^{-1}\sum_m m^{-4}\ee^{-c_1/(rm^2)}=O(\sqrt r),\\
 \text{quadratic nonlinear cross terms: }&
 Cr^{-2}\sum_m m^{-6}\ee^{-c_1/(rm^2)}=O(\sqrt r),\\
 \text{cubic nonlinear terms: }&
 Cr^{-3}\sum_m m^{-8}\ee^{-c_1/(rm^2)}=O(\sqrt r),\\
 \text{delta--}S_2\text{ term: }&
 Cr\cdot r^{-1}\sum_m m^{-2}\ee^{-c_1/(rm^2)}=O(\sqrt r).
\end{align*}
Extra delta factors or replacement of $S_2$ by $S_3$ only improve these estimates. The pure delta-cubic remainder is bounded by
\[
 C\sum_m m|\delta_m|^3\le Cr^3\sum_m\frac{m}{(1+(rm)^2)^3}=O(r).
\]
Higher terms are bounded by these same expressions through the Taylor integral remainder and the damping just established. Finally \eqref{dmv:sum:eq:smallm} handles the finite range $m<M$ exponentially. All bounds refer to convergent combinations, not a rearrangement of $\sum m\delta_m$.
\end{proof}

\section{Mellin evaluation of the leading terms}\label{dmv:sum:sec:mellin}
The classical Mellin kernel of $L$ is $\Gamma(s)\zeta(s)^2$. Its double pole at $s=1$ and simple pole at zero give
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:L}
 L(t)=\frac{\log(1/t)+\gamma}{t}+\frac14+O(r).
\end{equation}
For instance, moving to $\Rea s=-3/2$ crosses also the residue $-t/144$, while the new line is $O(r^{3/2})$. Gamma decay dominates the polynomial vertical growth of zeta uniformly in our sector.

For $C_0$, the Mellin transform of $1-\ee^{-x}-x$ is $-\Gamma(s)$ on $-2<\Rea s<-1$. Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:Cmellin}
 C_0(t)=\frac1{2\pi\ii}\int_{(a)}[-\Gamma(-u)]Z(u)t^{-u}\,du,
 \quad Z(u)=\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{m}{[m(m+1)]^u},\quad 1<a<2.
\end{equation}
Absolute convergence justifies the initial interchange. We spell out the continuation needed for moving this contour, because formal expansion of $Z$ at zero would give a wrong constant.

Separate $m=1$ and expand $(1+1/m)^{-u}$ to $J$ terms:
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:Zcont}
 Z(u)=2^{-u}+\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}\frac{(-1)^j(u)_j}{j!}
                [\zeta(2u+j-1)-1]+R_J(u).
\end{equation}
The Taylor remainder is obtained from
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:Zrem}
\begin{split}
 &(1+x)^{-u}-\sum_{j<J}\frac{(-1)^j(u)_j}{j!}x^j\\
 &\hspace{1em}=\frac{(-1)^J(u)_Jx^J}{(J-1)!}
            \int_0^1(1-v)^{J-1}(1+vx)^{-u-J}\,dv.
\end{split}
\end{equation}
Here $x=1/m$ is positive real. Its modulus has no exponential dependence on $\Ima u$. Hence $R_J$ converges normally for $\Rea u>(2-J)/2$ and has polynomial vertical growth on closed substrips. Taking $J=4$ permits the line $\Rea u=-3/4$. Gamma decay, even after multiplication by $t^{-u}$, is $\ee^{-(\pi/2-\theta_0)|\Ima u|}$ up to polynomial factors; the shifted integral is $O(r^{3/4})$.

At $u=1$ the finite part is
\[
 Z(u)=\frac1{2(u-1)}+\gamma-1+O(u-1).
\]
Indeed subtracting $\zeta(2u-1)$ leaves at $u=1$ the convergent sum $\sum_m(1/(m+1)-1/m)=-1$. Combining this with
$-\Gamma(-u)=-1/(u-1)+1-\gamma+O(u-1)$ gives the contribution
\[
 t^{-1}\left[\tfrac12\log t+\tfrac32(1-\gamma)\right].
\]
At $u=1/2$, the residue of $Z$ is $-1/4$, producing $-\sqrt\pi/(2\sqrt t)$. At zero,
\[
 Z(0)=\zeta(-1)+\frac14=\frac16.
\]
The $1/4$ is the removable value of $(u)_2\zeta(2u+1)/2$; it cannot be dropped by substituting $u=0$ termwise into the original divergent series. The pole of $-\Gamma(-u)$ thus contributes $1/6$. Finally at $u=-1/2$ the residue of $Z$ is $1/32$, producing $-\sqrt\pi\sqrt t/32$, inside our retained error. We have proved
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:C0}
 C_0(t)=\frac{-\tfrac12\log(1/t)+\tfrac32(1-\gamma)}{t}
            -\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2\sqrt t}+\frac16+O(\sqrt r).
\end{equation}

\section{Nonlocal matching and convergent regularization}\label{dmv:sum:sec:nonlocal}
\subsection{The linear delta correction}
In $D$, replace $\delta_m$ by $-tb'(tm)$. The error is $O(r\log(1/r))$, by \eqref{dmv:sum:eq:delta} and
\[
 |1-\ee^{-A_m}|\le C\min(1,(rm^2)^{-1}),
\]
splitting the sum at $r^{-1/2}$ and $r^{-1}$. The replacement of $A_m$ by $(tm^2)^{-1}$ next has error bounded by
\[
 C\sum_m m^{-2}\ee^{-c_1/(rm^2)}=O(\sqrt r).
\]
Set $f_t(y)=yb'(y)(1-\ee^{-t/y^2})$. We obtain
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:Dint}
 D(t)=\sum_{m\ge1}f_t(tm)+O(\sqrt r)
     =\frac1t\int_0^\infty yb'(y)(1-\ee^{-t/y^2})\,dy+O(\sqrt r).
\end{equation}
The integral initially runs along the $t$ ray. Its rotation to the positive axis is valid: $b'$ has no intervening poles, $\arg(t/y^2)$ stays in $[-\theta_0,\theta_0]$, and the small and large arcs vanish.

Here is a uniform mesh-error justification. The profile $f_t$ vanishes at both endpoints and $\int|f_t''(y)|\,|dy|=O(1)$ along the sector ray. Split at $|y|=\sqrt r$ and $1$. Transition derivatives contain $r|y|^{-3}\ee^{-cr/|y|^2}$ and $r^2|y|^{-5}\ee^{-cr/|y|^2}$, each with bounded integral. On $\sqrt r\le|y|\le1$, the worst term is $O(r|y|^{-3})$, again with bounded integral. At infinity it is $O(r|y|^{-5})$; the remaining terms use $b''(y)=O(y)$ at zero. Second-order Euler--Maclaurin therefore bounds the mesh error by $O(r)$.

Let $b_0=1/12$. On the principal sector branch, define the exponential integral by
\[
 E_1(t)=-\gamma-\log t-\sum_{j\ge1}\frac{(-t)^j}{j\,j!}.
\]
For positive $t$, this equals $\int_t^\infty \ee^{-u}\,du/u$. The singular part is explicitly integrable:
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:E1}
 \frac1t\int_0^1y(1-\ee^{-t/y^2})\,dy
 =\frac12\left(\frac{1-\ee^{-t}}t+E_1(t)\right)
 =\frac12[\log(1/t)+1-\gamma]+O(r).
\end{equation}
After subtracting $b_0$ on $(0,1)$, replace the kernel $y(1-\ee^{-t/y^2})/t$ by $1/y$. The error is $O(r\log(1/r))$: its difference is bounded by $C\min(|y|^{-1},r|y|^{-3})$, while $b'(y)-b_0=O(y^2)$ at zero. The remaining convergent integral is
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:RD}
 R_D=\int_0^1\frac{b'(y)-1/12}{y}\,dy+\int_1^\infty\frac{b'(y)}y\,dy
       =-\zeta'(-1)-\frac\gamma{12}.
\end{equation}
To justify this value without integrating divergent pieces, set $F(y)=\ee^y/(\ee^y-1)^2$. The transform of $-F$ is $-\Gamma(s)\zeta(s-1)$ initially on $\Rea s>2$. Its singular-subtraction continuation is
\[
 \int_0^1y^{s-1}b'(y)\,dy-\int_1^\infty y^{s-1}F(y)\,dy-\frac1{s-2}.
\]
For $0<\Rea s<2$, the last term equals $\int_1^\infty y^{s-3}\,dy$, so this is the convergent Mellin transform of $b'$. The expansion
\[
 -\Gamma(s)\zeta(s-1)=\frac1{12s}-\zeta'(-1)-\frac\gamma{12}+O(s)
\]
then gives \eqref{dmv:sum:eq:RD} as its finite part at zero. Consequently,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:D}
 D(t)=\frac1{24}\log(1/t)+\frac1{24}-\frac\gamma8-\zeta'(-1)+O(\sqrt r).
\end{equation}

\subsection{The delta square cannot be discarded}
Replacing $\delta_m$ by $-tb'(tm)$ in $E$ costs $O(r)$. Replacing $\ee^{-A_m}$ by $1$ costs $O(r\log(1/r))$, bounded by
\[
 Cr^2\sum_m\frac{m}{(1+(rm)^2)^2}\min(1,(rm^2)^{-1}).
\]
A ray Riemann sum and rotation yield
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:Eint}
 E(t)=-\frac12\int_0^\infty yb'(y)^2\,dy+O(r\log(1/r)).
\end{equation}
This integral is absolutely convergent, since $b'$ is bounded at zero and $O(y^{-2})$ at infinity. Its Mellin identity is
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:bsquare}
 \int_0^\infty y^{s-1}b'(y)^2\,dy
 =\frac{\Gamma(s)}6[\zeta(s-3)-\zeta(s-1)]-2\Gamma(s-2)\zeta(s-3),
\end{equation}
for $0<\Rea s<4$, with removable values interpreted by continuation. For a convergent proof let $G=F^2-2F/y^2$. The expansion
\[
 F(y)^2=\sum_{j\ge1}\frac{j^3-j}{6}\ee^{-jy}
\]
proves that the right side is the transform of $G$ for $\Rea s>4$. At zero $G=-y^{-4}+O(1)$; the $y^{-2}$ terms cancel. Its continuation is
\[
 \int_0^1y^{s-1}(G+y^{-4})\,dy+\int_1^\infty y^{s-1}G\,dy-\frac1{s-4}.
\]
For $0<\Rea s<4$ this is exactly the convergent transform of $G+y^{-4}=b'^2$, proving \eqref{dmv:sum:eq:bsquare}. At $s=2+\epsilon$, the two right-hand terms respectively equal
\[
 -\frac1{6\epsilon}-\frac{13}{72}+O(\epsilon),\qquad
 \frac1{6\epsilon}-2\zeta'(-1)-\frac\gamma6+O(\epsilon).
\]
Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:E}
 \int_0^\infty yb'(y)^2\,dy=-\frac{13}{72}-2\zeta'(-1)-\frac\gamma6,
 \qquad
 E(t)=\frac{13}{144}+\zeta'(-1)+\frac\gamma{12}+O(r\log(1/r)).
\end{equation}
The $\zeta'(-1)$ terms cancel between $D$ and $E$, and their combined gamma contribution is $-\gamma/24$. This constant-order square term is particular to the weighted calculation.

\section{Collision terms and the far-tail constant}\label{dmv:sum:sec:collisions}
\subsection{The quadratic collision term}
Finite differences give the exact decomposition
\begin{align}\label{dmv:sum:eq:S2}
 S_{2,m}&=T_{2,m}+E_{2,m},&
 T_{2,m}&=\frac1{2tm(m+1)(2m+1)},\nonumber\\
 E_{2,m}&=b(2tm)-2b((2m+1)t)+b((2m+2)t)\\
 &=t^2b''(2tm)+O\!\left(\frac{r^3}{1+(rm)^4}\right).\nonumber
\end{align}
Its weighted remainder sums to $O(r)$. Replacing $\ee^{-A_m}$ by $1$ in its leading finite-difference term also costs $O(r)$, using $b''(y)=O(y)$ near zero and $O(y^{-3})$ at infinity, with the usual splits at $r^{-1/2}$ and $r^{-1}$. Thus
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:tail16}
 \frac12\sum_m m\ee^{-A_m}E_{2,m}
 =\frac12\int_0^\infty yb''(2y)\,dy+O(r)=-\frac1{16}+O(r).
\end{equation}
Indeed this integral is $\frac18\int_0^\infty ub''(u)\,du=-\frac18[b(\infty)-b(0)]=-1/16$. The rational approximation $T_{2,m}$ alone misses this constant.

For its rational part use the centered coordinate $z=h(m+1/2)$. Exactly,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:center}
 \frac12mT_{2,m}\ee^{-A_m}
 =\ee^{-1/(z^2-h^2/4)}\left[\frac1{8(z^2-h^2/4)}-
                       \frac h{16z(z^2-h^2/4)}\right].
\end{equation}
For the required summed precision this is
\[
 \ee^{-1/z^2}\left(\frac1{8z^2}-\frac h{16z^3}\right)
 +O\!\left(|h|^2\ee^{-c_1/|z|^2}(|z|^{-4}+|z|^{-6})\right).
\]
One first separates finitely many small $m$ as in \eqref{dmv:sum:eq:smallm}; elsewhere Taylor remainders preserve the damping. The last expression sums to $O(|h|)$. The profiles and all derivatives vanish at zero and have integrable tails. Ray Euler--Maclaurin gives their integral divided by $h$; the half-step displacement has no constant endpoint correction. Since
\[
 \int_0^\infty z^{-2}\ee^{-1/z^2}\,dz=\frac{\sqrt\pi}2,
 \qquad \int_0^\infty z^{-3}\ee^{-1/z^2}\,dz=\frac12,
\]
the rational part is $\sqrt\pi/(16\sqrt t)-1/32+O(\sqrt r)$. Together with \eqref{dmv:sum:eq:tail16},
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:H2}
 H_2(t)=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{16\sqrt t}-\frac3{32}+O(\sqrt r).
\end{equation}

\subsection{The remaining local constants}
The third finite difference similarly yields
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:S3}
 S_{3,m}=\frac6{t(3m)(3m+1)(3m+2)(3m+3)}
           +O\!\left(\frac{r^3}{1+(rm)^4}\right).
\end{equation}
The weighted error sums to $O(r)$. With $x=hm$, the leading rational profiles are
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:profiles}
 \frac m3S_{3,m}=\frac{2h}{81x^3}+O(|h|^2|x|^{-4}),\qquad
 -\frac m8T_{2,m}^2=-\frac h{128x^5}+O(|h|^2|x|^{-6}),
\end{equation}
where the first expansion refers to the rational part after its already bounded finite-difference error. In $H_{22}$ the replacement of $S_2$ by $T_2$ has weighted error $O(r^2\log(1/r))$: use
\[
 |E_{2,m}|\le Cr^2\min(rm,(rm)^{-3})
\]
with comparable bounds near $rm=1$, then split at $r^{-1}$ and use exponential damping below it. This is smaller than the required error.

Applying the same damped Euler--Maclaurin argument to \eqref{dmv:sum:eq:profiles} gives
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:H3H22}
 H_3(t)=\frac1{81}+O(\sqrt r),\qquad
 H_{22}(t)=-\frac1{256}+O(\sqrt r).
\end{equation}
All profile integrals follow from
\[
 \int_0^\infty x^{-j}\ee^{-1/x^2}\,dx=\frac12\Gamma\left(\frac{j-1}2\right),\qquad j>1.
\]
Ray rotation is harmless since $|\arg h|\le\pi/24$, where the zero-end exponential decays.

\begin{proposition}[Sector expansion]\label{dmv:sum:prop:radial}
Uniformly for $|\arg t|\le\pi/12$ as $t\to0$,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:radial}
 w(t)=\frac{\tfrac12\log(1/t)+c}{t}-\frac{7\sqrt\pi}{16\sqrt t}
                     +\frac1{24}\log(1/t)+K+O(\sqrt{|t|}).
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Combine \eqref{dmv:sum:eq:matched}, \eqref{dmv:sum:eq:L}, \eqref{dmv:sum:eq:C0}, \eqref{dmv:sum:eq:D}, \eqref{dmv:sum:eq:E}, \eqref{dmv:sum:eq:H2}, and \eqref{dmv:sum:eq:H3H22}. The rational part of the constant is exactly
\[
 \frac14+\frac16+\frac{19}{144}-\frac3{32}+\frac1{81}-\frac1{256}
       =\frac{9607}{20736}.
\]
The zeta derivatives cancel, and the surviving gamma term is $-\gamma/24$.
\end{proof}

\section{Localization on the whole coefficient circle}\label{dmv:sum:sec:circle}
A sector expansion alone does not justify a coefficient asymptotic. We localize the actual finite function $F_n$ in \eqref{dmv:sum:eq:finite}. Put $\rho=\ee^{-\tau}$ and $q=\rho\ee^{\ii\theta}$. For part sizes $j$ with $1\le\tau j\le2$, the normalized $j$th factor of $P_m$ is the characteristic function of a geometric variable with its value $m$ excluded.

When $m\ne1$, its probabilities at $0$ and $1$ have a uniform positive lower bound. When $m=1$, use instead the adjacent values $2$ and $3$. The normalization is at most $(1-\ee^{-1})^{-1}$, so the bounds are uniform in both $m$ and $j$. Squaring a characteristic function and retaining the contribution of this adjacent pair proves
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:atom}
 |Z_{m,j}(\theta)|\le\exp[-c_2\sin^2(j\theta/2)].
\end{equation}
The same estimate holds for unrestricted geometric factors in $P$.

For the consecutive block of these $j$, we have
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:block}
 \sum_{j=\lceil1/\tau\rceil}^{\lfloor2/\tau\rfloor}\sin^2(j\theta/2)
 \ge\frac{c_3}\tau\min(1,(\theta/\tau)^2),\qquad |\theta|\le\pi.
\end{equation}
For small $|\theta|/\tau$ use the small-angle estimate. On a fixed compact range of $|\theta|/\tau$ away from zero, the limiting integral over $[1,2]$ is strictly positive. For $|\theta|\ge C\tau$, the geometric-series bound gives $\sum\cos(j\theta)=O(1/|\sin(\theta/2)|)$, smaller than half the block length for large enough $C$. These ranges cover the whole circle, including rational angles.

Since $P_m(\rho)\le P(\rho)$ and $\sum_{m\le n}m=n(n+1)/2$, we conclude that
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:global}
 |F_n(\rho\ee^{\ii\theta})|\le n(n+1)P(\rho)
       \exp\left[-\frac{c_4}\tau\min(1,(\theta/\tau)^2)\right].
\end{equation}
Take the central arc $|\theta|\le\tau^{3/2}\log(1/\tau)$. Outside it, the integral is smaller than every fixed power of $\tau$ relative to the partition saddle scale, since the factor $n(n+1)=O(\tau^{-4})$ is only polynomial. The same unrestricted bound and the positive leading saddle term below show
\[
 p(n)\asymp\ee^{n\tau}P(\ee^{-\tau})\tau^{3/2}.
\]
On the central arc $t=\tau-\ii\theta$ lies in our fixed sector. Extending the $m$ sum from $m\le n$ to all $m$ costs exponentially little: for $m>n$, $\sum_j|p_{jm}|\le C\ee^{-\tau m}$, whence
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:mextend}
 \sum_{m>n}m|1-Q_m|\le C(n/\tau+\tau^{-2})\ee^{-\tau n}.
\end{equation}
Here $\tau n\sim A/\tau$. We therefore use \eqref{dmv:sum:eq:radial} only on the central arc and control its complement directly by \eqref{dmv:sum:eq:global}.

\section{Saddle transfer and analytic parameter differentiation}\label{dmv:sum:sec:transfer}
The modular transformation of the partition product gives, on the shrinking central arc,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:modular}
 P(\ee^{-t})=\sqrt{\frac t{2\pi}}\exp(A/t-t/24)
                        (1+O(\ee^{-c_5/\tau})).
\end{equation}
This classical partition saddle method, including logarithmic amplitudes, is substantial prior work; see Grabner, Knopfmacher, and Wagner \cite{gkw}. We give the needed local calculation explicitly rather than assuming their global multiplier hypothesis for $w$.

Put $\eta=\tau^{3/2}\log(1/\tau)$ and define the normalized central coefficient integral
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:Jsdef}
 J_s=\frac{\ee^{n\tau}}{2\pi}\int_{-\eta}^{\eta}
 P(\ee^{-\tau+\ii\theta})(\tau-\ii\theta)^s\ee^{-\ii n\theta}\,d\theta.
\end{equation}
Here $t=\tau-\ii\theta$ and $t^s$ uses the sector logarithm. This integral is analytic in complex $s$ on every fixed compact set. Write
\[
 t=\tau(1+\ii\sqrt{\tau/A}\,u),\qquad a=s+\tfrac12.
\]
The sign of $u$ is immaterial. Since $N=A/\tau^2$,
\[
 Nt+A/t=2A/\tau-u^2+\ii\sqrt{\tau/A}\,u^3+(\tau/A)u^4+\cdots.
\]
Combining this expansion with $(1+\ii\sqrt{\tau/A}\,u)^a$, the first relative integrated correction is
\begin{align}\label{dmv:sum:eq:gaussian}
 \frac\tau A\left[-\frac{a(a-1)}2\langle u^2\rangle
 +(1-a)\langle u^4\rangle-\frac12\langle u^6\rangle\right]
 =-\frac{(s+1)(s+2)}{4A}\tau,
\end{align}
where the Gaussian moments are $1/2,3/4,15/8$. To control the remainder, expand through the odd third order; its integral vanishes on the symmetric arc. The fourth-order remainder is bounded by a fixed polynomial times $\ee^{-c u^2}$ and has integrated size $O(\tau^2)$, uniformly on a slightly larger compact $s$ set. Gaussian tails past logarithmic width are smaller than every power. The leading normalization evaluates to $C_N$, so
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:Jsabs}
 J_s=C_N\tau^s\left[1-\frac{(s+1)(s+2)}{4A}\tau+O(\tau^2)\right].
\end{equation}
At $s=0$, with the excluded partition arcs already controlled,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:partition}
 p(n)=C_N[1-\tau/(2A)+O(\tau^2)].
\end{equation}
Dividing gives the local monomial transfer
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:Jsmean}
 \frac{J_s}{p(n)}=\tau^s\left[1-\frac{s(s+3)}{4A}\tau+O(\tau^2)\right].
\end{equation}
No global definition of a multiplier $t(q)^s$ is needed.

Analyticity and uniformity on the larger compact set permit Cauchy differentiation of the normalized remainder in $s$. Since $t^s\log(1/t)=-\partial_st^s$, we obtain
\begin{align}\label{dmv:sum:eq:logtransfer}
 J_{-1}/p(n)&=\tau^{-1}+1/(2A)+O(\tau),\nonumber\\
 -\left.\partial_s(J_s/p(n))\right|_{s=-1}
 &=\tau^{-1}\ell+\ell/(2A)+1/(4A)+O(\tau\ell).
\end{align}
Differentiating $\tau^s$ accounts for the extra logarithm. The first transfer correction of $t^{-1/2}$ is $O(\sqrt\tau)$; the omitted corrections for the remaining logarithm and constant are $O(\tau\ell)$.

The error in \eqref{dmv:sum:eq:radial} needs no differentiated bound. On the central arc, $|t|\asymp\tau$ and
\[
 |P(\ee^{-t})\ee^{nt}|\le CP(\ee^{-\tau})\ee^{n\tau}
                                  \ee^{-c\theta^2/\tau^3}.
\]
An absolute Gaussian integral therefore transfers its $O(\sqrt{|t|})$ bound to $O(\sqrt\tau)p(n)$. Combining this fact, \eqref{dmv:sum:eq:radial}, and \eqref{dmv:sum:eq:logtransfer} proves Theorem~\ref{dmv:sum:thm:mean}.

Finally multiplication by \eqref{dmv:sum:eq:partition} cancels the canonical $\ell/(4A)$ and $c/(2A)$ contributions, leaving the constant $1/(8A)$ in \eqref{dmv:sum:eq:absolute}. The dropped relative $O(\tau^2)$ partition factor contributes $O(\tau\ell)$ inside that bracket, smaller than $O(\sqrt\tau)$. This proves the absolute expansion. The logarithmic-width localization yields superpolynomially small omitted arcs; no exponentially small final coefficient remainder is asserted.

\section{Monotonicity and inversion with integer endpoints}\label{dmv:sum:sec:inverse}
Put $x_n=\sqrt{n-1/24}$ and $B=2k$. Equation~\eqref{dmv:sum:eq:absolute} says
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:relative}
 a_n=\Phi(x_n)\left[1+O\!\left(\frac{x_n^{-3/2}}{\log x_n}\right)\right],
 \qquad (\log\Phi)'(x)=B+O(1/x).
\end{equation}
The logarithm has the same error, because the relative error tends to zero. The template gap between successive integers is
\[
 \log\Phi(x_{n+1})-\log\Phi(x_n)=\frac B{2x_n}+O(x_n^{-2}).
\]
The sum of the two pointwise error bounds is $O(x_n^{-3/2}/\log x_n)=o(x_n^{-1})$. Thus $a_{n+1}>a_n$ eventually. This does not differentiate an arbitrary error sequence.

Choose $X>1$, $n_0$ with $x_{n_0}\ge X$, and $M>0$ large enough that
\[
 H_-(x_n)\le\log a_n\le H_+(x_n)\qquad(n\ge n_0)
\]
holds for \eqref{dmv:sum:eq:H}. Increasing $X,n_0$ if necessary makes both $H_\pm$ strictly increasing: their derivatives equal $B+O(1/x)$, since the envelope derivative is $O(x^{-5/2}/\log x)$. Choose $Y$ large enough that both roots in \eqref{dmv:sum:eq:roots} lie beyond $x_{n_0}$ and that
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:prefix}
 Y>\max\{a_n:0\le n<n_0\}.
\end{equation}
All finite early crossings are now excluded. Monotonicity gives $x_-(Y)\le x_Y\le x_+(Y)$, and the mean-value theorem gives
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:rooterror}
 x_\pm(Y)-x_Y=O\!\left(\frac{x_Y^{-3/2}}{\log x_Y}\right),\quad
 x_\pm(Y)^2+\frac1{24}=v_Y+O\!\left(\frac{x_Y^{-1/2}}{\log x_Y}\right).
\end{equation}

For completeness check the ceiling endpoints directly. An integer below $\lceil x_-^2+1/24\rceil$ is strictly below $x_-^2+1/24$, even when the latter is an integer. Its upper envelope is strictly below $\log Y$, so it cannot cross the threshold. At the right ceiling, the integer is at least $x_+^2+1/24$; its lower envelope is at least $\log Y$, including equality. Together with \eqref{dmv:sum:eq:prefix}, this proves \eqref{dmv:sum:eq:ceiling} for the first crossing, not just some later crossing.

The leading template is
\[
 \Phi(x)\sim C\ee^{Bx}\frac{\log x}{x},\qquad C=\frac1{8\sqrt3 k}.
\]
Thus $x_Y\sim(\log Y)/B$, and \eqref{dmv:sum:eq:rooterror} proves \eqref{dmv:sum:eq:width}. The unrounded interval tends to zero, so its endpoint ceilings are eventually equal or adjacent. Near integer boundaries it need not select one universal ceiling. An asymptotic initializer for solving the explicit template equation is
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:sum:eq:initializer}
 Bx_Y=\log Y+\log\log Y-\log\log\log Y+\log(4\sqrt3)+o(1).
\end{equation}
It follows by taking logarithms of $C\ee^{Bx}\log x/x$; $BC=1/(4\sqrt3)$. The finite initializer by itself does not establish the shrinking horizontal error. That statement uses the full equation \eqref{dmv:sum:eq:phi} and the envelopes.

\section{Relation to prior work and the scope of the claims}\label{dmv:sum:sec:sources}

Ralaivaosaona \cite{ralaivaosaona} studies $N_m$, the number of part sizes with a prescribed multiplicity $m$, including a fixed-$m$ central limit theorem and a Poisson transition when $m$ has order $n^{1/4}$. Weighting makes a local-only tail treatment insufficient.

Grabner, Knopfmacher, and Wagner \cite{gkw} provide a general asymptotic scheme for partition statistics, including Bessel and logarithmic-amplitude transfer. Their Section 1.5 concerns prescribed multiplicities; their general transfer theorem is substantial prior for the method. We claim no new general saddle-transfer theorem. Section~\ref{dmv:sum:sec:circle} supplies a separate whole-circle bound for the present weighted positive-product sum, instead of assuming their global multiplier condition.

The inspected material of Archibald et al.\ \cite{archibald} concerns multiplicities and sums associated with largest parts. Fill, Janson, and Ward \cite{fill} count partitions whose nonzero part multiplicities are pairwise different, a different restriction from averaging \eqref{dmv:sum:eq:def} over all ordinary partitions. These comparisons distinguish the objects; they are not exhaustive theorem-by-theorem exclusion certificates. Neither limited check excludes overlap with every theorem in those works.

Targeted sequence, phrase, and related-statistic searches, and bounded checks of available report and artifact records, located no exact earlier instance of this expansion. \section{Further questions}\label{dmv:sum:sec:further}
The finite-order proof leaves several concrete questions open.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item \textbf{The next matched order.} Can the $O(\sqrt t)$ sector remainder be expanded with a uniform smaller error? Additional collision terms and the nonlocal delta corrections must be retained together; possible logarithmic resonances require a fresh convergence analysis. The numerical residuals are not a proof of a next coefficient or an arbitrary-order scheme.
\item \textbf{Other multiplicity weights.} For $\sum_m m^\alpha1_{\{N_m>0\}}$, how do the leading scale and the matching constants change as $\alpha$ passes the logarithmic transition represented here by $\alpha=1$? This asks for a new family of estimates, rather than extrapolating the present coefficients.
\item \textbf{Explicit inverse certification.} Can the sector, localization, and saddle bounds be made numerical with a practical onset, so that $M$ and the finite-prefix threshold in Section~\ref{dmv:sum:sec:inverse} are computable and useful? The current floating checks do not answer this.
\item \textbf{Weighted fluctuations.} What are the variance and limiting law of $W$ under the uniform partition measure? Marginal results for one prescribed multiplicity do not alone settle the dependence in this weighted occupied-value sum.
\item \textbf{A fuller source comparison.} A complete reading of the unavailable or partially inspected related sources and their successors is needed for a stronger assessment of priority, independently of the correctness of this proof.
\end{enumerate}

\part{Fluctuations of distinct multiplicities in random integer partitions: exact variance, Gaussian and non-Gaussian limits, and a logarithmic transition window}\label{dmv:fl:part}
\partsource{A continuation manuscript of 7~October 2026, archive \file{ProveIt_Research_2026-10-07.zip}, delivered as \file{partitions/article.tex} with ten section files (35 pages). Delivered title block ``PROVEIT RESEARCH CONTINUATION / Fluctuations of Distinct Multiplicities in Random Integer Partitions / Exact variance, Gaussian and non-Gaussian limits, and a logarithmic transition window / 7 October 2026 / Research draft prepared with ChatGPT''. Printed as delivered after Section~\ref{dmv:fl:sec:front}, which the write added, with \textbf{[write]} notes; the manuscript's Section~$k$ is Section~$k+25$ here, its statements move with their sections, its equation~($k.m$) is equation~($k{+}25.m$), and its Figures~1 and~2 keep their numbers. Its labels carry the prefix \file{dmv:fl:}, and its citation key \file{oeis} is \file{oeisA373271} and \file{oeisA373273} here. This Part is self-contained: it uses no theorem of Parts~I and~II as a premise.}
\begin{partabstract}
Let $M_j$ be the multiplicity of the part size $j$ in a uniform partition
of $n$, and let $D_\alpha=\sum_{m\ge1}m^\alpha
\ind_{\{M_j=m\text{ for some }j\}}$. We determine the fluctuation law
for every fixed $\alpha\ge0$. For $0\le\alpha<1/2$ the limit is Gaussian,
with an explicit positive covariance kernel and variance of order
$n^{\alpha/2+1/4}$. At $\alpha=1/2$ the variance acquires a factor
$\log n$. For $\alpha>1/2$ the centered limit is an explicit convergent
series of powers of independent exponential random variables and is
non-Gaussian. In particular, the number $D_0$ of distinct multiplicities
has variance $0.3080002444\ldots\,n^{1/4}$ and a central limit theorem,
whereas the sum $D_1$ of distinct multiplicities has a Gumbel limit and
variance asymptotic to $n$. We also determine a Gaussian covariance field
in the moving window $\alpha=1/2+\theta/\log(1/t)$,
$t\sim\pi/\sqrt{6n}$. The three limiting families are mutually independent.

The proof treats the negative dependence between occupied multiplicity
values explicitly. A localization theorem shows that the entire occupied
set is determined, with arbitrarily small polynomial failure probability,
by part sizes at most $O(t^{-1/2}\log^{3/2}(1/t))$. A separately proved
local limit theorem then transfers both laws and second moments from the
independent geometric model to uniform partitions. This answers the
variance and fluctuation question in the inspected ProveIt report on
OEIS A373271 and develops its proposed weighted extension. The present
proofs are written mathematical proofs, accompanied by exact finite
checks; they have not been formalized in a proof assistant.
\end{partabstract}

\section{The statistic and the main results}\label{dmv:fl:sec:results}

Write a partition as $\lambda=(1^{M_1}2^{M_2}\cdots)$, with
$\sum_{j\ge1}jM_j=n$. Its set of positive multiplicity values is
\[
 \mathcal M(\lambda)=\{m\ge1:M_j=m\text{ for at least one }j\ge1\}.
\]
For a real parameter $\alpha\ge0$, define
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:statistic}
 D_\alpha(\lambda)=\sum_{m\in\mathcal M(\lambda)}m^\alpha.
\end{equation}
The value $0$ is excluded, and a multiplicity appearing at several part
sizes is counted only once. For example,
$\lambda=(5,5,3,3,1)$ has $\mathcal M=\{1,2\}$, and hence
$D_0=2$, $D_1=3$, and $D_2=5$.

Let $\Pp_n$ denote the uniform law on the $p(n)$ ordinary partitions of
$n$; expectations under this law carry subscript $n$. Put
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:tau}
 \tau_n=\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{6n}},\qquad
 c_* =1-\frac\pi4,\qquad
 M_\alpha=\frac{\Gamma((1-\alpha)/2)}{1+\alpha}
 \quad(0\le\alpha<1).
\end{equation}
All limits below are as $n\to\infty$. In the proofs, $t=t_n$ is the exact
positive solution of
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:saddle}
 \sum_{j\ge1}\frac{j}{\ee^{tj}-1}=n.
\end{equation}
We prove $t_n=\tau_n+O(\tau_n^2)$, so either parameter can be used in
the fluctuation statements.

For $0\le\alpha<1/2$ define
\begin{align}\label{dmv:fl:eq:Va}
 V_\alpha={}&\int_0^\infty y^{2\alpha}
       (\ee^{-1/y^2}-\ee^{-2/y^2})\,dy\\
 &\quad-2\int_0^\infty\int_0^\infty
       \frac{y^\alpha z^\alpha\ee^{-1/y^2-1/z^2}}{(y+z)^3}\,dy\,dz.
 \nonumber
\end{align}
These integrals are finite and $V_\alpha>0$. A one-dimensional formula is
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:Vaoned}
 V_\alpha=\Gamma(\tfrac12-\alpha)
 \left\{\frac{2^{\alpha+1/2}-1}{2\alpha+1}
 -\int_0^1[r(1-r)]^{1-\alpha}
       [r^2+(1-r)^2]^{\alpha-1/2}\,dr\right\}.
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[The three fluctuation regimes]\label{dmv:fl:thm:phase}
For every fixed $\alpha\ge0$ the following statements hold.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textnormal{(\roman*)},leftmargin=*]
\item If $0\le\alpha<1/2$, then
\begin{align}\label{dmv:fl:eq:lowphase}
 \Var_n D_\alpha&\sim V_\alpha\tau_n^{-\alpha-1/2},\\
 \tau_n^{\alpha/2+1/4}(D_\alpha-\E_nD_\alpha)
       &\dto N(0,V_\alpha).
 \nonumber
\end{align}
The same normal limit holds when $\E_nD_\alpha$ is replaced by
$M_\alpha\tau_n^{-(\alpha+1)/2}$.
\item At $\alpha=1/2$,
\begin{align}\label{dmv:fl:eq:criticalphase}
 \Var_nD_{1/2}&\sim\frac{c_*}{2\tau_n}\log\frac1{\tau_n},\\
 \sqrt{\frac{\tau_n}{\log(1/\tau_n)}}
       (D_{1/2}-\E_nD_{1/2})&\dto N(0,c_*/2).
 \nonumber
\end{align}
Again the mean may be replaced by $M_{1/2}\tau_n^{-3/4}$.
\item If $\alpha>1/2$, let $E_1,E_2,\ldots$ be independent exponential
variables of mean $1$, and set
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:Zalpha}
 Z_\alpha=\sum_{j\ge1}\frac{E_j^\alpha-\Gamma(\alpha+1)}{j^\alpha}.
\end{equation}
The series converges in $L^2$, and
\begin{align}\label{dmv:fl:eq:highphase}
 \tau_n^\alpha(D_\alpha-\E_nD_\alpha)&\dto Z_\alpha,\\
 \tau_n^{2\alpha}\Var_nD_\alpha&\longrightarrow
 [\Gamma(2\alpha+1)-\Gamma(\alpha+1)^2]\zeta(2\alpha).
 \nonumber
\end{align}
The limit $Z_\alpha$ is non-Gaussian. For $\alpha>1$ there is also the
uncentered limit, with convergence of the first two moments,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:uncentered}
 \tau_n^\alpha D_\alpha\dto
 Y_\alpha:=\sum_{j\ge1}\frac{E_j^\alpha}{j^\alpha}.
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

In particular, the order of the standard deviation changes from
$n^{\alpha/4+1/8}$ below $1/2$, to $n^{1/4}\sqrt{\log n}$ at $1/2$,
to $n^{\alpha/2}$ above $1/2$. The change comes from two different
portions of the partition. The subcritical statistic aggregates occupied
values $m\asymp n^{1/4}$ across many part sizes. Above the threshold,
the few smallest part sizes, whose multiplicities are of order $\sqrt n$,
control the fluctuation. The critical case accumulates a comparable
contribution over logarithmically many intermediate scales.

\begin{corollary}[The unweighted open question]\label{dmv:fl:cor:unweighted}
Put
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:vzero}
 v_0=6^{1/4}\left(\sqrt2-\frac34
       -\frac{3\sqrt2}{8}\log(1+\sqrt2)\right)
       =0.308000244403717958\ldots.
\end{equation}
Then
\[
 \Var_nD_0\sim v_0 n^{1/4},\qquad
 \frac{D_0-(6n)^{1/4}}{\sqrt{v_0}\,n^{1/8}}\dto N(0,1).
\]
Thus the variance and central limit questions stated in the inspected
ProveIt report on A373271 are answered.
\end{corollary}

\begin{corollary}[An explicit Gumbel law]\label{dmv:fl:cor:gumbel}
Let $G$ have the standard Gumbel distribution
$\Pp(G\le x)=\exp(-\ee^{-x})$, and let $\gamma$ denote Euler's constant.
For the sum $D_1$ of distinct positive multiplicities,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:gumbel}
 \tau_nD_1-\frac12\log\frac1{\tau_n}
       -\frac{3-3\gamma}{2}\dto G.
\end{equation}
Furthermore,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:gumbelmean}
 \E_nD_1=\frac1{2\tau_n}\log\frac1{\tau_n}
       +\frac{3-\gamma}{2\tau_n}+o(\tau_n^{-1}),
 \qquad \Var_nD_1\sim n.
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

\begin{corollary}[A quadratic-weight example]\label{dmv:fl:cor:quadratic}
One has
\[
 \frac{D_2}{n}\dto\frac6{\pi^2}\sum_{j\ge1}\frac{E_j^2}{j^2},
 \qquad\frac{\E_nD_2}{n}\longrightarrow2,
 \qquad\frac{\Var_nD_2}{n^2}\longrightarrow8.
\]
In particular, the quadratic-weight statistic does not concentrate about
its mean at its natural scale.
\end{corollary}

\subsection{The moving transition window}
The abrupt distinction between fixed exponents has a precise window.
For $\theta\in\R$ put
\[
 H_n=\log(1/\tau_n),\qquad
 \alpha_n(\theta)=\frac12+\frac\theta{H_n}.
\]
These exponents are positive for all sufficiently large $n$.

\begin{theorem}[The logarithmic window]\label{dmv:fl:thm:window}
For any fixed finite collection of real parameters $\theta$, the variables
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:windowvars}
 X_n(\theta)=\sqrt{\frac{\tau_n}{H_n}}
       \left(D_{\alpha_n(\theta)}-\E_nD_{\alpha_n(\theta)}\right)
\end{equation}
converge jointly to centered Gaussian variables $\mathcal G(\theta)$ with
covariance
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:windowkernel}
 K_{\rm crit}(\theta,\phi)=
 c_*\frac{\ee^{\theta+\phi}-\ee^{(\theta+\phi)/2}}{\theta+\phi},
\end{equation}
interpreted as $c_*/2$ when $\theta+\phi=0$. Their second moments converge.
An equivalent realization of this covariance is
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:brownian}
 \mathcal G(\theta)=\sqrt{c_*}
       \int_0^{1/2}\ee^{\theta(1-u)}\,dB(u),
\end{equation}
where $B$ is standard Brownian motion. In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:Wtheta}
 \Var_nD_{\alpha_n(\theta)}\sim
 \frac{H_n}{\tau_n}W(\theta),\qquad
 W(\theta)=c_*\frac{\ee^{2\theta}-\ee^\theta}{2\theta},
 \quad W(0)=c_*/2.
\end{equation}
The centering in \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:windowvars} may be replaced by
$M_{\alpha_n(\theta)}\tau_n^{-(\alpha_n(\theta)+1)/2}$.
\end{theorem}

Only finite-dimensional convergence is asserted in this theorem. No
tightness statement in a space of functions of $\theta$ is used or
claimed. The proof is uniform when the finitely many parameters range
over a fixed compact interval. Theorem~\ref{dmv:fl:thm:independence} further
proves that the subcritical Gaussian vector, critical-window Gaussian
vector, and fixed-supercritical series vector are mutually independent
in their joint limit. In particular the limits for $D_0$, $D_{1/2}$,
and $D_1$ are mutually independent.

\subsection{Proof structure}
The main proof has four logically separate tasks. We first localize the
occupied multiplicity set and justify conditioning on total size. We
then establish a Gaussian covariance field on the scale $m\sqrt t$ by
finite-order cumulants. A collision estimate compares weighted occupied
values with additive powers of geometric variables. That comparison
gives the critical and supercritical laws. Finally, matched integral
estimates supply the explicit centerings. Every limiting claim used in
this chain is proved below; the earlier report's refined mean expansion
is contextual information rather than a required premise.

\subsection{Relation to earlier partition literature}
Ralaivaosaona's theorem concerns
$N_m=\#\{j:M_j=m\}$ for one prescribed multiplicity $m$. It gives a
Gaussian--Poisson--degenerate transition as $m$ crosses the scale
$n^{1/4}$ \cite{ralaivaosaona}. Our statistic sums the indicators
$\ind_{\{N_m>0\}}$ over all $m$, so a one-bin limit theorem does not
determine its variance: weak pairwise correlations accumulate over
quadratically many pairs. The explicit covariance term in
\eqref{dmv:fl:eq:Va} is a quantitative expression of that obstruction.

The independent geometric model and conditioning strategy belong to the
classical probabilistic theory of partitions, notably Fristedt's work
\cite{fristedt}. We prove the particular localization and local limit
estimates needed here, including the moment-transfer statement; we do not
assume that convergence of unconditioned characteristic functions alone
justifies conditioning on an event of probability tending to zero.

The Gumbel law for the number of summands is classical
\cite{erdoslehner}. The new point here is that deleting repeated
\emph{multiplicity values} changes the expectation of $D_1$ at leading
order but changes its centered fluctuation by $o(\sqrt n)$.
The resulting centering in \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:gumbel} therefore differs from the
classical number-of-summands centering.

Earlier work of Corteel, Pittel, Savage, and Wilf concerns prescribed
multiplicities and sampling a part size \cite{cpsw}. Its bibliographic
record and abstract were located, but the attempted author PDF returned
an access error. This limitation is recorded because a finite source
search cannot establish global historical priority. The precise claims
made here are complete proofs of the displayed results and progress
beyond the inspected repository statements. No assertion that every
potentially overlapping paper has been excluded is needed.

\section{The geometric model and conditioning on total size}\label{dmv:fl:sec:conditioning}

\subsection{Independent geometric coordinates}
For $t>0$ let $\Q_t$ be the law of independent random variables $M_j$ with
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:geometric}
 \Q_t(M_j=m)=(1-\ee^{-tj})\ee^{-tjm},\qquad m=0,1,2,\ldots.
\end{equation}
Write
\[
 N=\sum_{j\ge1}jM_j,\quad
 p_{jm}=(1-\ee^{-tj})\ee^{-tjm},\quad
 N_m=\sum_{j\ge1}\ind_{\{M_j=m\}},\quad I_m=\ind_{\{N_m>0\}}.
\]
Since $\sum_j\Q_t(M_j>0)<\infty$, only finitely many $M_j$ are positive
almost surely. For a partition $\lambda$ of size $n$,
\[
 \Q_t(\lambda)=\ee^{-tn}\prod_{j\ge1}(1-\ee^{-tj}).
\]
Consequently the conditional law given $N=n$ is exactly $\Pp_n$.

We use $\eps=\sqrt t$ throughout. The expected number of occurrences
of a multiplicity value has the exact telescoping form
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:lambda}
 \lambda_m=\sum_jp_{jm}
 =\frac1{\ee^{tm}-1}-\frac1{\ee^{t(m+1)}-1}
 =\int_{tm}^{t(m+1)}g(u)\,du,
 \quad g(u)=\frac{\ee^u}{(\ee^u-1)^2}.
\end{equation}
For a finite set $S$ of positive multiplicities, the absence probability is
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:absence}
 Q_S=\Q_t(N_m=0\text{ for every }m\in S)
 =\prod_{j\ge1}\left(1-\sum_{m\in S}p_{jm}\right).
\end{equation}
In particular $Q_m:=Q_{\{m\}}\le\ee^{-\lambda_m}$. For $m\ne\ell$,
each factor satisfies
$1-p_{jm}-p_{j\ell}\le(1-p_{jm})(1-p_{j\ell})$, whence
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:negativecov}
 \Cov_t(I_m,I_\ell)=Q_{m,\ell}-Q_mQ_\ell\le0.
\end{equation}
Only this explicitly proved pairwise fact is needed for the weighted
tail variances below.

\begin{lemma}[Elementary uniform bounds]\label{dmv:fl:lem:basicbounds}
For all $m\ge1$ and $0<t\le1$,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:lambdabounds}
 \lambda_m\le\frac1{tm(m+1)}\le\frac1{tm^2},\qquad
 \lambda_m\le C t\ee^{-tm}\quad(tm\ge1).
\end{equation}
If $tm\le1$, then
$\lambda_m\ge c/(tm(m+1))$. For any finite $J$,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:truncatedlambda}
 \lambda_m^{(J)}:=\sum_{j\le J}p_{jm}
 \le\min\left\{\frac{tJ(J+1)}2,\frac1{tm^2}\right\}.
\end{equation}
For each fixed $r>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:momentbound}
 \E_t M_j^r\le C_r(tj)^{-r}.
\end{equation}
The constants in the last bound may be chosen uniformly for $r$ in a
fixed compact subinterval of $(0,\infty)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The identity $g(u)=[2\sinh(u/2)]^{-2}$ gives $g(u)\le u^{-2}$;
on $(0,2]$ it also gives a positive constant times $u^{-2}$ as a lower
bound. If $tm\le1$ and $t\le1$, the integration interval lies in
$(0,2]$. On $[1,\infty)$, $g(u)\le C\ee^{-u}$. Integrating proves
\eqref{dmv:fl:eq:lambdabounds}. The estimate $p_{jm}\le tj$ proves the first
part of \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:truncatedlambda}. Finally, on a common probability
space we may set
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:expcoupling}
 M_j=\left\lfloor\frac{E_j}{tj}\right\rfloor,
 \qquad E_j\text{ independent }\operatorname{Exp}(1).
\end{equation}
This has exactly the tail probabilities in \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:geometric}, and
$M_j^r\le E_j^r/(tj)^r$ proves the moment bound.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The entire occupied set is determined by small part sizes}
Let $\mathcal M_J=\{M_j:j\le J,\ M_j>0\}$ and
$D_\alpha^{(J)}=\sum_{m\in\mathcal M_J}m^\alpha$.

\begin{lemma}[Localization of the occupied set]\label{dmv:fl:lem:localization}
For every fixed $A>0$ there is a constant $C_A$ such that, with
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:J}
 J=\left\lceil C_A t^{-1/2}\log^{3/2}(1/t)\right\rceil,
\end{equation}
one has
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:setlocalization}
 \Q_t(\mathcal M\ne\mathcal M_J)=O_A(t^A).
\end{equation}
On the complementary event, $D_\alpha=D_\alpha^{(J)}$ simultaneously for all
$\alpha\ge0$. The constant $C_A$ is allowed to depend on $A$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Write $H=\log(1/t)$ and choose constants $B,C>0$ below. Put
$m_0=\lfloor t^{-1/2}/\sqrt{BH}\rfloor$ and
$J=\lceil Ct^{-1/2}H^{3/2}\rceil$.
For all sufficiently small $t$, the indices
\[
 \frac1{2tm_0}\le j\le\frac1{tm_0}
\]
lie below $J$. On this interval,
$(1-\ee^{-tj})\ee^{-tjm_0}\ge c tj$, with an absolute $c>0$.
Summing gives $\lambda_{m_0}^{(J)}\ge c'/(tm_0^2)\ge c'BH$.
Since $p_{jm}$ decreases in $m$, this lower bound holds for every
$1\le m\le m_0$. A union bound therefore gives
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:lowmiss}
 \Q_t(\text{some }m\le m_0\text{ is absent from }\mathcal M_J)
 \le m_0\ee^{-c'BH}.
\end{equation}
For the large values, sum the geometric tail exactly:
\begin{align}\label{dmv:fl:eq:highlate}
 \Q_t(\exists j>J:M_j>m_0)
 &\le\sum_{j>J}\ee^{-tj(m_0+1)}\\
 &=\frac{\ee^{-t(J+1)(m_0+1)}}{1-\ee^{-t(m_0+1)}}
 \le C' t^{-1/2}\sqrt{BH}\,\ee^{-CH/\sqrt B}.
 \nonumber
\end{align}
First choose $B$ so $c'B>A+2$, and then choose $C$ so
$C/\sqrt B>A+2$. Both displayed bounds are $O(t^A)$.
Outside these two events, every value supplied by a row $j>J$ already
appears in a row $j\le J$. This proves equality of the occupied sets.
\end{proof}

This localization concerns the set of multiplicities, not the size of
the partition. The rows $j>J$ carry most of the variance of $N$ and
provide the reservoir that makes conditioning asymptotically harmless.

\subsection{A uniform local limit estimate}
Put
\[
 A_J=\sum_{j\le J}jM_j,\qquad B_J=\sum_{j>J}jM_j,
 \qquad N=A_J+B_J.
\]
For the next lemma it suffices that $J\ge1$ and $Jt\to0$.

\begin{lemma}[Reservoir local limit theorem]\label{dmv:fl:lem:llt}
Let $\mu=\E_tN$, $\sigma^2=\Var_tN$, and let $\mu_B,\sigma_B^2$
be the corresponding parameters of $B_J$. Then
\begin{align}\label{dmv:fl:eq:sizemoments}
 \mu&=\frac{\pi^2}{6t^2}+O(t^{-1}),&
 \sigma^2&\sim\frac{\pi^2}{3t^3},&
 \sigma_B^2&\sim\sigma^2,\\
 \Var_t A_J&=O(Jt^{-2}),&
 \E_t(A_J-\E_tA_J)^4&=O((J^2+J)t^{-4}).
 \nonumber
\end{align}
Writing $\varphi(x)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}\ee^{-x^2/2}$, uniformly in integer $k$,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:LLT}
 \sigma_B\Q_t(B_J=k)
 =\varphi\!\left(\frac{k-\mu_B}{\sigma_B}\right)+O(\sqrt t).
\end{equation}
The identical estimate holds for $N$ with its own mean and variance.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The mean estimate follows by a Riemann sum for $u/(\ee^u-1)$.
For the variance, use the integrable function
$u^2\ee^{-u}/(1-\ee^{-u})^2$ and
\[
 \int_0^\infty\frac{u^2\ee^{-u}}{(1-\ee^{-u})^2}\,du
 =\sum_{k\ge1}k\int_0^\infty u^2\ee^{-ku}\,du
 =2\zeta(2)=\frac{\pi^2}3.
\]
Each summand $jM_j$ has variance $O(t^{-2})$ and centered fourth
moment $O(t^{-4})$, by \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:momentbound}. Independence gives the
two bounds for $A_J$ and hence $\sigma^2-\sigma_B^2=O(Jt^{-2})$.
The summed absolute third centered moments of all $jM_j$ are
$O(t^{-4})$: for $tj\le1$ use $O(t^{-3})$ per row, and for $tj>1$
use $O(j^3\ee^{-tj})$ and a Riemann sum.

Here are the Fourier estimates needed for a local, rather than merely
distributional, limit. For $q_j=\ee^{-tj}$,
\[
 \left|\E_t\ee^{\ii\theta jM_j}\right|^2
 =\frac{(1-q_j)^2}{(1-q_j)^2+4q_j\sin^2(j\theta/2)}.
\]
For $j\in[1/t,2/t]\cap\N$ the parameter $q_j$ stays in a compact
subinterval of $(0,1)$. All these rows belong to $B_J$ eventually. Thus
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:fourierbound}
 |\E_t\ee^{\ii\theta B_J}|
 \le\exp\left[-c\min\{\theta^2/t^3,1/t\}\right],
 \qquad -\pi\le\theta\le\pi.
\end{equation}
For completeness, the needed trigonometric bound is
\[
 \sum_{1/t\le j\le2/t}\sin^2(j\theta/2)
 \ge c\min\{\theta^2/t^3,1/t\}.
\]
When $|\theta|\le c_0t$, apply the elementary lower bound for sine
near zero. When $|\theta|\ge C_0t$, use
$|\sum_{a\le j\le b}\ee^{\ii j\theta}|\le1/|\sin(\theta/2)|$
and $\sin^2(u/2)=(1-\cos u)/2$. On the remaining compact range
$c_0\le|\theta|/t\le C_0$, a Riemann sum for
$\int_1^2\sin^2(s\theta/(2t))\,ds$ is bounded below by a positive
constant. This proves \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:fourierbound} on the entire circle.

For the centered characteristic function at
$\theta=u/\sigma_B$, Taylor expansion of its logarithm gives
\[
 \log\E_t\ee^{\ii u(B_J-\mu_B)/\sigma_B}
 =-u^2/2+O(\sqrt t\,|u|^3).
\]
For an explicit uniform derivative bound, the geometric logarithm series is
\[
 \log\E_t\ee^{\ii\theta B_J}
 =\sum_{j>J}\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{\ee^{-tjk}}{k}
       (\ee^{\ii jk\theta}-1).
\]
Its third derivative in absolute value is at most
$\sum_{j>J}j^3q_j(1+q_j)/(1-q_j)^3=O(t^{-4})$, uniformly in real
$\theta$. On $|u|\le c_1t^{-1/2}$ choose $c_1$ small
enough that the remainder is at most a fixed small multiple of $u^2$.
The difference from $\ee^{-u^2/2}$ is bounded there by
$C\sqrt t\,|u|^3\ee^{-c_2u^2}$.
On the rest of the Fourier interval, \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:fourierbound} gives an
exponentially small integral, including the range $|\theta|\ge t$.
Consequently the $L^1$ distance of the rescaled characteristic function
from $\ee^{-u^2/2}$ is $O(\sqrt t)$. Fourier inversion proves
\eqref{dmv:fl:eq:LLT}, uniformly in $k$. The proof for $N$ is the same.
\end{proof}

The saddle equation \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:saddle} has a unique positive solution:
its left side is continuous and strictly decreasing from infinity to
zero. The mean estimate in \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:sizemoments} gives
$t_n=\tau_n+O(\tau_n^2)$ as promised.

\begin{proposition}[Likelihood ratio and moment transfer]\label{dmv:fl:prop:transfer}
Take $t=t_n$, and let $J$ satisfy \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:J}. The conditional law of
$(M_j)_{j\le J}$ given $N=n$ has likelihood ratio, relative to its
unconditional law,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:likelihood}
 R_t=\frac{\Q_t(B_J=n-A_J)}{\Q_t(N=n)}.
\end{equation}
Uniformly over all low-row configurations, $0\le R_t\le C$, and
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:Rrate}
 \|R_t-1\|_{L^2(\Q_t)}=O(Jt+\sqrt t)=o(1).
\end{equation}
Thus the total variation distance of the two low-row laws tends to zero.
If a sequence of low-row random variables $Y_t$ has a limiting law and
uniformly integrable squares under $\Q_t$, its first and second moments
and its law have the same limits after conditioning. For any square
integrable low-row statistic $T$,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:meanshift}
 |\E_nT-\E_tT|
 \le C(Jt+\sqrt t)\sqrt{\Var_tT}.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Independence proves \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:likelihood}. Apply Lemma~\ref{dmv:fl:lem:llt}
at the saddle $\mu=n$. With
$z=(A_J-\E_tA_J)/\sigma_B$, it gives
\[
 R_t=\frac{\sigma}{\sigma_B}
       \frac{\ee^{-z^2/2}+O(\sqrt t)}{1+O(\sqrt t)}.
\]
The uniform upper bound follows. Moreover
$\sigma/\sigma_B=1+O(Jt)$, and
\[
 \E_tz^4=O((J^2+J)t^2),\qquad
 |1-\ee^{-z^2/2}|\le\min\{z^2/2,1\}.
\]
These prove \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:Rrate}. Since $\E_tR_t=1$, the mean difference
equals $\E_t[(T-\E_tT)(R_t-1)]$, and Cauchy--Schwarz proves
\eqref{dmv:fl:eq:meanshift}. Finally, boundedness of $R_t$ and convergence to
$1$ in probability allow truncation of any uniformly integrable family
$Y_t^2$, which proves the moment-transfer assertion. Total variation
and the same truncation prove the law and first-moment assertions.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Removing the row cutoff after conditioning}
The support-localization event involves both low and high rows, so it
must not be transferred using a likelihood ratio for low rows alone.
Instead, at $t=t_n$, Lemma~\ref{dmv:fl:lem:llt} gives
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:eventtransfer}
 \Pp_n(\mathcal M\ne\mathcal M_J)
 \le\frac{\Q_t(\mathcal M\ne\mathcal M_J)}{\Q_t(N=n)}
 =O_A(t^{A-3/2}).
\end{equation}
For each fixed $\alpha\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:polynomialgrowth}
 0\le D_\alpha\le N^{\max(1,\alpha)}.
\end{equation}
Indeed $m^\alpha\le m$ for $0\le\alpha\le1$, whereas for
$\alpha\ge1$ the sum of the $\alpha$th powers is at most the $\alpha$th
power of the sum. Also $\E_tN^r=O_r(t^{-2r})$ for every fixed positive
$r$, by the geometric moment or cumulant bounds (integer moments
suffice, followed by H\"older).

Choosing $A$ sufficiently large for the finitely many exponents and
moments under consideration, \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:eventtransfer},
\eqref{dmv:fl:eq:polynomialgrowth}, and Cauchy--Schwarz show that
$D_\alpha-D_\alpha^{(J)}$ is negligible in $L^2$ at every scale used in
this article, under both $\Q_t$ and $\Pp_n$. The same holds for any
specified finite moment. This explains why the quantifier ``for every
$A$'' in Lemma~\ref{dmv:fl:lem:localization} matters.
\section{A Gaussian field of occupied multiplicities}\label{dmv:fl:sec:gaussian}

\subsection{The covariance on the occupancy scale}
On the scale $y=\eps m$, a bin has expected occupancy of order one.
Uniformly for $a\le\eps m\le b$, where $0<a<b<\infty$ are fixed,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:localones}
 \lambda_m=y^{-2}+O(\eps),\qquad
 Q_m=\ee^{-y^{-2}}(1+O(\eps)).
\end{equation}
Indeed, \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:lambda} and $g(u)=u^{-2}+O(1)$ prove the first
estimate. For the second, use $\max_jp_{jm}=O_a(\eps)$ and
$\sum_jp_{jm}^2=O_a(\eps)$ in the logarithm of \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:absence}.

For distinct $m,\ell$ in this range, put $z=\eps\ell$. The Riemann sum
with $x=\eps j$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:pairintensity}
 \sum_jp_{jm}p_{j\ell}
 =\eps\int_0^\infty x^2\ee^{-(y+z)x}\,dx+O(\eps^2)
 =\frac{2\eps}{(y+z)^3}+O(\eps^2).
\end{equation}
Here all derivatives of the exponentially decaying integrands are
uniformly integrable when $y,z$ stay in the compact interval. Expanding
the logarithm of the pair absence probability yields
\[
 \log\frac{Q_{m,\ell}}{Q_mQ_\ell}
 =-\sum_jp_{jm}p_{j\ell}+O(\eps^2),
\]
because the cubic error is at most a constant times
$\sum_jp_{jm}p_{j\ell}(p_{jm}+p_{j\ell})=O(\eps^2)$. Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:covlocal}
 \Cov_t(I_m,I_\ell)
 =-\frac{2\eps\ee^{-1/y^2-1/z^2}}{(y+z)^3}+O(\eps^2).
\end{equation}
Each correlation is small, but there are order $\eps^{-2}$ pairs.
Their total is of the same order as the sum of the diagonal variances.

For compactly supported, continuous real functions $f,g$ on
$(0,\infty)$ define
\begin{align}\label{dmv:fl:eq:kernel}
 \mathcal K(f,g)={}&\int_0^\infty f(y)g(y)
       (\ee^{-1/y^2}-\ee^{-2/y^2})\,dy\\
 &-2\int_0^\infty\int_0^\infty
       \frac{f(y)g(z)\ee^{-1/y^2-1/z^2}}{(y+z)^3}\,dy\,dz.
 \nonumber
\end{align}
The same formula applies to bounded piecewise continuous functions
supported inside a compact subinterval. With
$T_f=\sum_m f(\eps m)I_m$, the diagonal formula and
\eqref{dmv:fl:eq:covlocal} prove
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:compactcov}
 \eps\Cov_t(T_f,T_g)\longrightarrow\mathcal K(f,g).
\end{equation}
The error $O(\eps^2)$ per off-diagonal pair contributes only $O(\eps)$
after this normalization.

\subsection{A finite-order cumulant lemma}
The following argument treats repeated indices as well as distinct
ones. This distinction is necessary when summing ordinary cumulants.
Let $Z_m=1-I_m$ be the absence indicator.

\begin{lemma}[Connected cumulant bound]\label{dmv:fl:lem:cumulants}
Fix $r\ge1$ and $0<a<b<\infty$. If an ordered $r$-tuple of indices
in $[a/\eps,b/\eps]$ contains $d$ distinct indices, its joint cumulant
of absence indicators, and hence of presence indicators for $r\ge2$,
is $O_{a,b,r}(\eps^{d-1})$. Consequently, for uniformly bounded weights
$f_m$ supported in this interval,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:sumcumulants}
 \kappa_r\left(\sum_m f_m I_m\right)=O_{a,b,r,\sup|f_m|}(\eps^{-1}).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Label the $d$ distinct bins by $[d]$. In the compact range, for every
nonempty subset $B\subseteq[d]$ of size at most $r$,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:rowproducts}
 \sum_j\prod_{i\in B}p_{ji}=O_{a,r}(\eps^{|B|-1}).
\end{equation}
To prove this, set $x=\eps j$ and use
$p_{ji}\le\eps x\ee^{-ax}$, followed by an exponentially convergent
Riemann sum. In particular $\sum_{i=1}^dp_{ji}\le1/2$ for sufficiently
small $\eps$.

For nonempty $B\subseteq[d]$ take the Boolean differences
\[
 c_B=\sum_{S\subseteq B}(-1)^{|B|-|S|}\log Q_S,
 \qquad Q_\varnothing=1.
\]
In the absolutely convergent expansion of
$\log(1-\sum_{i\in S}p_{ji})$, this difference kills every monomial
that omits an index of $B$. The sum of the remaining monomials is
bounded by $C_d\prod_{i\in B}p_{ji}$: after removing one occurrence of
each index, the remaining series is dominated by a geometric series
with argument at most $1/2$. Thus
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:hyperweights}
 c_B=O(\eps^{|B|-1}).
\end{equation}
Put $a_i=Q_{\{i\}}$ and $w_B=\ee^{c_B}-1$ for $|B|\ge2$.
Boolean inversion gives
\[
 Q_S=\prod_{i\in S}a_i
     \prod_{\substack{B\subseteq S\\|B|\ge2}}(1+w_B).
\]
For complex $s_i$ near zero, set
$z_i=\ee^{s_i}-1$, $h_i=1+a_iz_i$, and $b_i=a_iz_i/h_i$.
Expanding the moment generating function exactly, with
$\mathcal E=\{B\subseteq[d]:|B|\ge2\}$, gives
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:finitecluster}
 \E_t\ee^{\sum_i s_iZ_i}
 =\prod_i h_i\;R(w,b),\qquad
 R(w,b)=\sum_{F\subseteq\mathcal E}
       \left(\prod_{B\in F}w_B\right)
       \left(\prod_{i\in\bigcup F}b_i\right).
\end{equation}
This is a finite polynomial identity before taking the logarithm.
The function $\log R$ is analytic in common small polydisks in $w,s$,
uniformly for $0\le a_i\le1$, because $R=1$ at $w=0$.

Consider a Taylor monomial of $\log R$ in the edge variables $w_B$.
If its edge multiset splits into vertex-disjoint components, its
coefficient is zero unless there is only one component: setting all
other edge variables to zero factorizes $R$ across those components,
and its logarithm becomes a sum. A derivative with positive order in
every $s_i$ also vanishes unless the union of the edges covers $[d]$.
Every surviving term therefore has a connected spanning hypergraph.
If edge $B$ occurs $k_B$ times, connectivity implies
\[
 \sum_B k_B(|B|-1)\ge d-1.
\]
By \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:hyperweights}, such a term is $O(\eps^{d-1})$.
To justify the bound for the entire analytic series, separate it at
total $w$-degree $d-1$. The tail and each fixed $s$ derivative of the
tail are $O(\eps^{d-1})$, since every $w_B=O(\eps)$. Below that degree
there are finitely many terms, to which the connected-spanning argument
applies individually. When $d=1$, every fixed cumulant of a Bernoulli
variable is bounded.

Differentiating with possibly repeated $s_i$ now proves the claimed
ordinary joint cumulant bound. There are $O_r(\eps^{-d})$ ordered
$r$-tuples with $d$ distinct indices. Summing over $d=1,\ldots,r$
proves \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:sumcumulants}; bounded weights do not affect its order.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Compact occupancy Gaussian field]\label{dmv:fl:thm:field}
For any fixed finite family of bounded piecewise continuous test
functions supported inside compact subintervals of $(0,\infty)$,
\[
 \sqrt\eps\left(\sum_mf(\eps m)I_m-
                     \E_t\sum_mf(\eps m)I_m\right)
\]
converges jointly under $\Q_t$ to a centered Gaussian vector with
covariance $\mathcal K$. Every fixed moment of each compact test
statistic converges. Under $\Pp_n$ the same finite-dimensional law and
second-moment conclusion hold, with $t=t_n$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
After centering, the first cumulant vanishes, the second converges by
\eqref{dmv:fl:eq:compactcov}, and for $r\ge3$ Lemma~\ref{dmv:fl:lem:cumulants} gives
$O(\eps^{r/2-1})\to0$. The finite moment--cumulant formulas yield
convergence of every fixed moment to the Gaussian moment. Bounded
second moments give tightness, and the Gaussian distribution is
determined by its moments. Apply this scalar argument to every linear
combination to obtain the joint assertion. The fourth-moment bound
gives uniform integrability of squares. Localization and
Proposition~\ref{dmv:fl:prop:transfer} give the conditioned assertion.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Strict positivity of the covariance}
The negative double integral in \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:kernel} cannot cancel the
whole diagonal. This can be seen without numerical computation.
Write $\lambda(y)=y^{-2}$ and $h(x,y)=x\ee^{-xy}$ for $x,y>0$.
Then
\[
 \int_0^\infty h(x,y)\,dy=1,\quad
 \int_0^\infty h(x,y)\,dx=\lambda(y),\quad
 \int_0^\infty h(x,y)h(x,z)\,dx=\frac2{(y+z)^3}.
\]
For each fixed $x$, apply Cauchy--Schwarz with probability density
$h(x,y)\,dy$. Integrating in $x$ gives
\[
 2\iint\frac{f(y)f(z)\ee^{-\lambda(y)-\lambda(z)}}{(y+z)^3}\,dy\,dz
 \le\int f(y)^2\lambda(y)\ee^{-2\lambda(y)}\,dy.
\]
Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:strictpositive}
 \mathcal K(f,f)\ge
 \int_0^\infty f(y)^2\ee^{-2\lambda(y)}
       [\ee^{\lambda(y)}-1-\lambda(y)]\,dy>0
\end{equation}
for every nonzero real test function modulo equality almost everywhere.
The same proof applies whenever the integrals converge, including
$f(y)=y^\alpha$, $0\le\alpha<1/2$.

\subsection{Removing the multiplicity cutoff}
For nonnegative weights $w_m$ and a set of bins $S$, the exact sign
\eqref{dmv:fl:eq:negativecov} implies
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:tailvariance}
 \Var_t\left(\sum_{m\in S}w_mI_m\right)
 \le\sum_{m\in S}w_m^2\min\{Q_m,1-Q_m\}.
\end{equation}
Fix $0\le\alpha<1/2$. On $m<a/\eps$, the lower bound for $\lambda_m$
in Lemma~\ref{dmv:fl:lem:basicbounds} gives
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:lowweightedtail}
 \eps^{2\alpha+1}\Var_t\sum_{m<a/\eps}m^\alpha I_m
 \le C_\alpha\int_0^{2a}y^{2\alpha}\ee^{-c/y^2}\,dy+o(1).
\end{equation}
A direct maximum bound also proves this for sufficiently small $a$;
the displayed Riemann-integral version makes its limit zero explicit.
For $m>b/\eps$, \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:lambdabounds} gives
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:highweightedtail}
 \eps^{2\alpha+1}\Var_t\sum_{m>b/\eps}m^\alpha I_m
 \le\eps^{2\alpha-1}\sum_{m>b/\eps}m^{2\alpha-2}
 \le C_\alpha b^{2\alpha-1}.
\end{equation}
The low and high tails have nonpositive covariance. Their centered sum
is consequently small in $L^2$, after multiplication by
$\eps^{\alpha+1/2}$, as first $t\to0$ and then $a\downarrow0$,
$b\uparrow\infty$.

Apply Theorem~\ref{dmv:fl:thm:field} to
$f(y)=y^\alpha\ind_{[a,b]}(y)$. The two tail bounds extend the Gaussian
law to all bins and extend the limiting variance by Cauchy--Schwarz.
The resulting constant is $\mathcal K(y^\alpha,y^\alpha)=V_\alpha$.
They also prove uniform integrability of the normalized squares:
the compact statistic has bounded fourth moments and the tails are
arbitrarily small in $L^2$. Proposition~\ref{dmv:fl:prop:transfer} and the
support-localization lemma therefore transfer the full Gaussian and
variance statements to uniform partitions.

This proves the centered subcritical assertion of
Theorem~\ref{dmv:fl:thm:phase}. It also proves joint Gaussian limits for any
finite list $0\le\alpha_i<1/2$, with covariance
$\mathcal K(y^{\alpha_i},y^{\alpha_j})$ under their respective
normalizations. Linear combinations are treated using the individual
$L^2$ tail bounds, so no sign assumption on those combinations is needed.

\subsection{Evaluation and the critical pole}
For $0\le\alpha<1/2$, integration by parts gives
\[
 \int_0^\infty y^{2\alpha}
    (\ee^{-1/y^2}-\ee^{-2/y^2})\,dy
 =\Gamma(\tfrac12-\alpha)
       \frac{2^{\alpha+1/2}-1}{2\alpha+1}.
\]
In the double integral of \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:Va}, substitute $y=sr$,
$z=s(1-r)$. The Jacobian is $s$, and integration in $s$ gives the
integral subtracted in \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:Vaoned}. This proves that formula.
At $\alpha=0$,
\[
 \int_0^1\frac{r(1-r)}{\sqrt{r^2+(1-r)^2}}\,dr
 =\frac{3\sqrt2}{8}\log(1+\sqrt2)-\frac14,
\]
by $u=2r-1$ and elementary antiderivatives. Hence
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:Vzero}
 V_0=\sqrt\pi\left(\sqrt2-\frac34
 -\frac{3\sqrt2}{8}\log(1+\sqrt2)\right)
 =0.348809403800813564\ldots.
\end{equation}
Using $\tau_n^{-1/2}=6^{1/4}n^{1/4}/\sqrt\pi$ gives
\eqref{dmv:fl:eq:vzero}.

As $\alpha\uparrow1/2$, the bracket in \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:Vaoned} tends to
\[
 \frac12-\int_0^1\sqrt{r(1-r)}\,dr
 =\frac12-\frac\pi8=\frac{c_*}{2}.
\]
Since $\Gamma(1/2-\alpha)\sim(1/2-\alpha)^{-1}$,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:variancepole}
 V_\alpha\sim\frac{c_*}{1-2\alpha}.
\end{equation}
The pole indicates the failure of a uniformly integrable high-bin
variance tail at the endpoint. Section~\ref{dmv:fl:sec:critical} resolves that
failure directly and yields the logarithmic coefficient $c_*/2$.
\section{Explicit centerings and the logarithmic mean}\label{dmv:fl:sec:means}

The centered limit theorems do not require a high-order expansion of
the mean. Nevertheless, obtaining a genuinely explicit limit law
requires controlling the mean to better than the fluctuation scale.
This section does so without importing the earlier report's refined
unweighted expansion.

\begin{proposition}[Weighted means]\label{dmv:fl:prop:weightedmeans}
For every fixed $0<\alpha<1$, under both the geometric law and the
uniform partition law at the exact saddle,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:twotermmean}
 \E D_\alpha=M_\alpha t^{-(\alpha+1)/2}
       +\Gamma(\alpha+1)\zeta(\alpha)t^{-\alpha}
       +o(t^{-\alpha}).
\end{equation}
At $\alpha=1$,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:onemean}
 \E D_1=\frac1{2t}\log\frac1t+\frac{3-\gamma}{2t}+o(t^{-1}).
\end{equation}
For $\alpha=0$ the geometric mean is
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:zeromean}
 \E_tD_0=\sqrt\pi\,t^{-1/2}+O(1),
\end{equation}
and the same bounded-error formula holds under $\Pp_n$ at $t=t_n$.
All these statements remain valid with $t_n$ replaced by $\tau_n$.
The estimates used for \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:twotermmean} are uniform for $\alpha$
in a compact subinterval of $(0,1)$.
\end{proposition}

\subsection{Reduction to one integral}
For $0<\alpha<2$, define
\[
 F_t(u)=u^\alpha(1-\ee^{-tg(u)}),\qquad
 J_\alpha(t)=\int_0^\infty F_t(u)\,du.
\]
We first prove the geometric estimate
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:meanintegral}
 \E_tD_\alpha=t^{-\alpha-1}J_\alpha(t)+O_\alpha(t^{-\alpha/2}).
\end{equation}
There are three approximations, and their errors must all be summed
over the full range of multiplicities.

First, the maximum of $(1-q)q^m$ on $0\le q\le1$ is at most
$1/(m+1)\le1/2$. Expanding $-\log(1-p)$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:poissonerror}
 0\le\ee^{-\lambda_m}-Q_m
 \le\ee^{-\lambda_m}\sum_jp_{jm}^2
 \le\ee^{-\lambda_m}\lambda_m/m.
\end{equation}
For $tm\le1$, Lemma~\ref{dmv:fl:lem:basicbounds} bounds the weighted sum by
\[
 Ct^{-1}\sum_m m^{\alpha-3}\ee^{-c/(tm^2)}=O(t^{-\alpha/2}).
\]
For $tm>1$, use $\lambda_m\le Ct\ee^{-tm}$ to obtain
$O(t^{1-\alpha})$, which is no larger when $\alpha<2$.

Second, $\lambda_m\le tg(tm)$, and
\[
 0\le tg(tm)-\lambda_m\le
 \begin{cases}
 C/(tm^3),&tm\le1,\\
 Ct^2\ee^{-tm},&tm>1.
 \end{cases}
\]
The mean value theorem for the exponential, with the lower bound for
$\lambda_m$, yields the same weighted error $O(t^{-\alpha/2})$.

Third, the elementary estimates for $g,g'$ imply
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:variation}
 \int_0^\infty|F_t'(u)|\,du=O_\alpha(t^{\alpha/2}).
\end{equation}
For the derivative of $u^\alpha$, split at $u=\sqrt t$ and use
$1-\ee^{-tg(u)}\le\min(1,Ct/u^2)$ on $(0,1]$. For the other term
use $Ct u^{\alpha-3}\ee^{-ct/u^2}$. Beyond $1$, exponential decay
gives $O(t)$. These estimates prove \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:variation} for
$0<\alpha<2$. The bounded-variation Riemann-sum inequality is
\[
 \left|t\sum_{m\ge1}F_t(tm)-\int_0^\infty F_t(u)\,du\right|
 \le t\int_0^\infty|F_t'(u)|\,du.
\]
Multiplication by $t^{-\alpha-1}$ gives the third error in
\eqref{dmv:fl:eq:meanintegral}. Each argument is uniform on compact exponent
intervals inside $(0,2)$.

\subsection{Two terms below the mean threshold}
For $0<\alpha<1$, integration by parts gives
\[
 \int_0^\infty u^\alpha(1-\ee^{-t/u^2})\,du
 =M_\alpha t^{(\alpha+1)/2}.
\]
Moreover, $0<g(u)<u^{-2}$ and
$u^\alpha(u^{-2}-g(u))$ is integrable at both endpoints.
Dominated convergence therefore gives
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:regularmean}
 J_\alpha(t)=M_\alpha t^{(\alpha+1)/2}
 +t\int_0^\infty u^\alpha(g(u)-u^{-2})\,du+o(t).
\end{equation}
The regularized Mellin integral equals
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:mellinconstant}
 \int_0^\infty u^\alpha(g(u)-u^{-2})\,du
 =\Gamma(\alpha+1)\zeta(\alpha).
\end{equation}
Here is a direct convergent-integral justification. Let
$H(u)=(\ee^u-1)^{-1}-u^{-1}$. Boundary terms vanish in integration
by parts, so the left side is
$\alpha\int_0^\infty u^{\alpha-1}H(u)\,du$.
The identity
\[
 H(u)-2H(2u)=\frac1{\ee^u+1}
\]
implies that this last Mellin integral, multiplied by
$1-2^{1-\alpha}$, is $\Gamma(\alpha)\eta(\alpha)$.
This follows by integrating the alternating geometric expansion of the
right side; the integrated remainder tends to zero for $\alpha>0$.
Using $\eta(\alpha)=(1-2^{1-\alpha})\zeta(\alpha)$ proves
\eqref{dmv:fl:eq:mellinconstant}. Substitution in \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:meanintegral}
proves \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:twotermmean} under $\Q_t$.

For $\alpha<1/2$, the second term in \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:twotermmean} is smaller
than the standard deviation because
$\alpha<\alpha/2+1/4$. At $\alpha=1/2$ it is smaller by a factor
$\sqrt{\log(1/t)}$. At $1/2<\alpha<1$ it is exactly on the limiting
fluctuation scale and must be retained when an explicit centering is
wanted.

\subsection{The mean at weight one}
At $\alpha=1$ the integral in \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:mellinconstant} is no longer
convergent at infinity. Split at $u=1$ instead. Dominated convergence
gives
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:Jone}
 J_1(t)=\int_0^1u(1-\ee^{-t/u^2})\,du+tC_{\rm reg}+o(t),
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align*}
 C_{\rm reg}
 &=\int_0^1u(g(u)-u^{-2})\,du+\int_1^\infty ug(u)\,du\\
 &=\lim_{\delta\downarrow0}
 \left\{\frac{\delta}{\ee^\delta-1}
       -\log(1-\ee^{-\delta})+\log\delta\right\}=1.
\end{align*}
The singular part can be evaluated exactly:
\begin{align*}
 \int_0^1u(1-\ee^{-t/u^2})\,du
 &=\frac{1-\ee^{-t}}2+\frac t2\int_t^\infty\frac{\ee^{-v}}v\,dv\\
 &=\frac t2\left(\log\frac1t+1-\gamma\right)+O(t^2).
\end{align*}
Equations \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:meanintegral} and \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:Jone} prove
\eqref{dmv:fl:eq:onemean} under the geometric law. The mean constant
$(3-\gamma)/2$ and the standard-Gumbel shift $(3-3\gamma)/2$ in
Corollary~\ref{dmv:fl:cor:gumbel} have different roles; confusing them would
shift the limiting distribution by $\gamma$.

\subsection{A bounded error for the unweighted mean}
Set $a_m=[tm(m+1)]^{-1}$. Then $\lambda_m\le a_m$, and the exact
telescoping identity gives
\[
 \sum_{m\ge1}(a_m-\lambda_m)
 =\frac1t-\frac1{\ee^t-1}=O(1).
\]
The exponential function is Lipschitz on the nonnegative half-line.
Equation~\eqref{dmv:fl:eq:poissonerror}, summed with $\alpha=0$, contributes
only $O(1)$ by the same small- and large-$tm$ split. Thus
\[
 \E_tD_0=\sum_{m\ge1}(1-\ee^{-a_m})+O(1).
\]
The inequalities $(m+1)^{-2}\le[m(m+1)]^{-1}\le m^{-2}$ sandwich this
sum between two shifted Riemann sums. Monotonicity gives
\[
 \sum_{m\ge1}(1-\ee^{-1/(tm^2)})
 =t^{-1/2}\int_0^\infty(1-\ee^{-1/y^2})\,dy+O(1)
 =\sqrt\pi\,t^{-1/2}+O(1),
\]
proving \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:zeromean}.

\subsection{Transfer of these centerings to fixed size}
The centered variance estimates proved in Sections~\ref{dmv:fl:sec:gaussian},
\ref{dmv:fl:sec:large}, and \ref{dmv:fl:sec:critical} do not use the mean asymptotics.
Apply \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:meanshift} to $D_\alpha^{(J)}$, and use localization
to replace it by $D_\alpha$. Since $Jt=O(\sqrt t\,H^{3/2})$,
the mean shift for fixed $0\le\alpha<1/2$ is
\[
 O(t^{1/4-\alpha/2}H^{3/2})=o(1).
\]
At $\alpha=1/2$, and uniformly in the moving window, it is $O(H^2)$.
For fixed $\alpha>1/2$ it is $O(t^{1/2-\alpha}H^{3/2})$.
Each error is $o(t^{-\alpha})$ for $0<\alpha\le1$, and is
$o(1)$ for $\alpha=0$. This proves all conditioned mean statements.
The same bounds and the compact-uniform integral estimates prove the
explicit centering claim in Theorem~\ref{dmv:fl:thm:window}.

Finally, replacing $t_n$ by $\tau_n$ changes the leading mean for
$\alpha<1$ by $O(t_n^{(1-\alpha)/2})$, and changes the weight-one mean
by $O(\log(1/t_n))$. These are smaller than the stated mean remainders
and every relevant fluctuation scale. This completes the proof of
Proposition~\ref{dmv:fl:prop:weightedmeans} and the centering assertions in
Theorem~\ref{dmv:fl:thm:phase}.

\section{Collision removal and non-Gaussian limits}\label{dmv:fl:sec:large}

\subsection{An Efron--Stein estimate for repeated values}
For $\alpha>0$ and a finite row set $j\le J$, write
\[
 S_{\alpha,J}=\sum_{j\le J}M_j^\alpha,\qquad
 C_{\alpha,J}=S_{\alpha,J}-D_\alpha^{(J)}
             =\sum_{m\ge1}m^\alpha(N_{m,J}-1)_+,
\]
where $N_{m,J}=\sum_{j\le J}\ind_{\{M_j=m\}}$.
Thus $C$ is the contribution discarded when repeated multiplicity
values are counted only once.

\begin{lemma}[Collision variance]\label{dmv:fl:lem:collisions}
For every $\alpha>0$ and finite $J$,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:collisionbound}
 \Var_t C_{\alpha,J}
 \le 2\sum_{m\ge1}m^{2\alpha}\lambda_m^{(J)}
                                  \min\{1,\lambda_m^{(J)}\}.
\end{equation}
For every fixed $\alpha>1/2$, uniformly in $J$,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:collisionrates}
 \Var_t C_{\alpha,J}\le
 \begin{cases}
 O_\alpha(t^{-\alpha-1/2}),&1/2<\alpha<3/2,\\
 O(t^{-2}\log(1/t)),&\alpha=3/2,\\
 O_\alpha(t^{1-2\alpha}),&\alpha>3/2.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
In every case this is $o(t^{-2\alpha})$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Replace row $M_j$ by an independent copy $M'_j$. Removing its old
positive value $m$ changes $C$ only if another row has value $m$;
in that case the change has magnitude $m^\alpha$. The corresponding
statement holds for insertion of the new value. The square of the
net change is bounded by twice the sum of the squared removal and
insertion changes. Efron--Stein, with its factor $1/2$, therefore gives
\[
 \Var_tC_{\alpha,J}
 \le2\sum_{j\le J}\sum_{m\ge1}m^{2\alpha}p_{jm}
       \Q_t(\text{another row has value }m).
\]
The last probability is at most $\min(1,\lambda_m^{(J)})$, proving
\eqref{dmv:fl:eq:collisionbound}. All variables have finite moments, so the
variance inequality applies directly; infinite $J$ may also be
obtained by $L^2$ approximation when needed.

To prove \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:collisionrates}, split at
$m=t^{-1/2}$ and $m=t^{-1}$. On the first range the sum is bounded by
\[
 Ct^{-1}\sum_{m\le t^{-1/2}}m^{2\alpha-2}
 =O_\alpha(t^{-\alpha-1/2}),
\]
since $2\alpha-2>-1$. On the middle range it is at most
$Ct^{-2}\sum m^{2\alpha-4}$. This gives the three cases in
\eqref{dmv:fl:eq:collisionrates}, with a logarithm when $2\alpha-4=-1$.
Beyond $t^{-1}$ use the sharper exponential bound in
\eqref{dmv:fl:eq:lambdabounds}; the contribution is
\[
 Ct^2\sum_{m>t^{-1}}m^{2\alpha}\ee^{-2tm}
 =O_\alpha(t^{1-2\alpha}).
\]
Comparison with $t^{-2\alpha}$ proves the final assertion.
\end{proof}

The estimate concerns a centered error. Its expectation need not be
small at the fluctuation scale. The explicit means in the preceding
section are what allow that distinction to be handled correctly.

\subsection{The limiting series}
For fixed $\alpha>1/2$, the exponential coupling
\eqref{dmv:fl:eq:expcoupling} implies, for every fixed $j$,
\[
 t^\alpha(M_j^\alpha-\E_tM_j^\alpha)
 \Ltwo j^{-\alpha}(E_j^\alpha-\Gamma(\alpha+1)).
\]
Moreover, independence and \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:momentbound} give
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:seriestail}
 \Var_t\left[t^\alpha\sum_{K<j\le J}
                         (M_j^\alpha-\E_tM_j^\alpha)\right]
 \le C_\alpha\sum_{j>K}j^{-2\alpha}\longrightarrow0
 \quad(K\to\infty).
\end{equation}
The same bound holds for the tail of \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:Zalpha}.
Taking first $t\to0$ and then $K\to\infty$ proves
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:additiveLtwo}
 t^\alpha(S_{\alpha,J}-\E_tS_{\alpha,J})\Ltwo Z_\alpha
\end{equation}
on the common coupling, for $J$ in \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:J}.

By Lemma~\ref{dmv:fl:lem:collisions},
\[
 t^\alpha(C_{\alpha,J}-\E_tC_{\alpha,J})\Ltwo0.
\]
Subtracting proves the law and variance limit for $D_\alpha^{(J)}$
under $\Q_t$. It also proves uniform integrability of the normalized
squares. Alternatively, the additive sums have uniformly bounded
fourth centered moments, by independence and
$\sum_jj^{-2\alpha}<\infty$, and the collision error tends to zero
in $L^2$. Proposition~\ref{dmv:fl:prop:transfer} transfers these statements
to the conditioned low-row law, including the small difference between
its own mean and the geometric mean. Section~\ref{dmv:fl:sec:conditioning}
removes the cutoff. This proves \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:highphase}.

For a finite list $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_r>1/2$ the same coupling
gives joint convergence to the series built from the \emph{same}
exponentials $E_j$. Their limiting covariance is
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:highcov}
 \Cov(Z_\alpha,Z_\beta)=
 [\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+1)-\Gamma(\alpha+1)\Gamma(\beta+1)]
       \zeta(\alpha+\beta).
\end{equation}
This is generally nonzero; the supercritical limits are not independent
of one another.

\subsection{Why these limits are not Gaussian}
Write
$Z_\alpha=E_1^\alpha-\Gamma(\alpha+1)+R_\alpha$, with an independent
almost surely finite remainder $R_\alpha$. Choose $M$ so that
$p:=\Pp(R_\alpha\ge-M)>0$. For all sufficiently large $x$,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:nonGaussiantail}
 \Pp(Z_\alpha>x)
 \ge p\exp\left[-(x+M+\Gamma(\alpha+1))^{1/\alpha}\right].
\end{equation}
Since $1/\alpha<2$, this lower bound decays more slowly than the
right tail of every nondegenerate Gaussian distribution. The variance
in \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:highphase} is positive, so a degenerate Gaussian is also
excluded. This proves the non-Gaussian assertion without a uniqueness
theorem for independent decompositions.

\subsection{Uncentered limits for \texorpdfstring{$\alpha>1$}{alpha greater than one}}
For $\alpha>1$, the additive sums converge uncentered in $L^2$:
the centered tails satisfy \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:seriestail} and their means are
bounded by $C_\alpha\sum_{j>K}j^{-\alpha}\to0$.
To show that collisions have negligible \emph{mean} in this range,
use
\[
 \E_t(N_{m,J}-1)_+
 \le\min\{\lambda_m^{(J)},(\lambda_m^{(J)})^2/2\}.
\]
The first bound is immediate; the second follows from
$(k-1)_+\le k(k-1)/2$ and independence of rows. Splitting as in
Lemma~\ref{dmv:fl:lem:collisions}, now with exponent $\alpha$ rather than
$2\alpha$, gives
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:collisionmean}
 \E_tC_{\alpha,J}\le
 \begin{cases}
 O_\alpha(t^{-(\alpha+1)/2}),&1<\alpha<3,\\
 O(t^{-2}\log(1/t)),&\alpha=3,\\
 O_\alpha(t^{1-\alpha}),&\alpha>3.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Each is $o(t^{-\alpha})$. Along with the variance estimate, this
shows $t^\alpha C_{\alpha,J}\to0$ in $L^2$.
The conditioned likelihood is bounded, so the same negligible
uncentered collision error transfers. The additive normalized squares
are uniformly integrable, and the cutoff is harmless. Thus
\eqref{dmv:fl:eq:uncentered} holds with convergence of its first two moments.
In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:highmean}
 \E_nD_\alpha\sim\Gamma(\alpha+1)\zeta(\alpha)\tau_n^{-\alpha}
 \qquad(\alpha>1).
\end{equation}
\section{The critical exponent and its logarithmic window}\label{dmv:fl:sec:critical}

The fixed-exponent arguments on either side of $1/2$ cease to be uniform
at the boundary. The Gaussian variance integral develops a pole, whereas
the variance of the exponential series diverges. A moving exponent
resolves the transition directly.

In this section set
\[
 H=\log(1/t),\qquad \alpha_t(\theta)=\frac12+\frac\theta H,
 \qquad L=C_AH^{3/2},\qquad J=\lceil t^{-1/2}L\rceil.
\]
For a fixed finite bound $T$, all exponents with $|\theta|\le T$ lie in
$[2/5,3/5]$ once $t$ is sufficiently small. The constant $C_A$ is chosen
as in Lemma~\ref{dmv:fl:lem:localization}, with $A$ large enough for all the
moment errors used below. No assertion is made that one fixed $C_A$
works for every error power $A$.

\subsection{A uniform bound for the collision fluctuation}
The global estimate in Lemma~\ref{dmv:fl:lem:collisions} is insufficient at
$\alpha=1/2$: it would retain the full logarithmic variance. The
additional cutoff bound in \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:truncatedlambda} is decisive.

\begin{lemma}[Critical collision error]\label{dmv:fl:lem:criticalcollision}
Uniformly for $|\theta|\le T$,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:criticalcollision}
 \Var_t C_{\alpha_t(\theta),J}
 =O_T\!\left(t^{-1}(1+\log L)\right)=o(H/t).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
First take $\alpha=1/2$ and put $M=t^{-1/2}$. In
\eqref{dmv:fl:eq:collisionbound}, split the sum at $M/L$, $M$, and $1/t$.
On the first range, $\lambda_m^{(J)}\le CL^2$, so its contribution is
\[
 O\!\left(L^2\sum_{m\le M/L}m\right)=O(t^{-1}).
\]
On the second range, use $\lambda_m^{(J)}\le1/(tm^2)$ and
$\min(1,\lambda_m^{(J)})\le1$ to obtain $O(t^{-1}\log L)$.
On the third range, use the squared intensity bound to obtain
\[
 O\!\left(t^{-2}\sum_{m>M}m^{-3}\right)=O(t^{-1}).
\]
Finally, the exponential range $m>1/t$ contributes
$O(t^2\sum m\ee^{-2tm})=O(1)$.

For $|\theta|\le T$ and $m\le1/t$, the moving weight satisfies
\[
 m^{2\alpha_t(\theta)}
 =m\exp\left(\frac{2\theta\log m}{H}\right)\le\ee^{2T}m.
\]
Thus all preceding bounds remain uniform. Beyond $1/t$, the bound is
\[
 C_Tt^2\sum_{m>1/t}m^{2\alpha_t(\theta)}\ee^{-2tm}
 =O_T(t^{1-2\alpha_t(\theta)})=O_T(1).
\]
Since $\log L=O(\log H)=o(H)$, the result follows.
\end{proof}

The expectation of $C_{\alpha,J}$ has not been discarded. Only its
centered contribution is negligible; this is why the limit theorem
first uses the exact expectation before applying the mean expansion.

\subsection{A triangular array of independent row contributions}
For a geometric variable $M_s=\lfloor E/s\rfloor$, with
$E\sim\operatorname{Exp}(1)$, concavity of $x^\alpha$ gives, for
$0<a\le\alpha\le b<1$ and $s\le1$,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:floorwindow}
 \left|(sM_s)^\alpha-E^\alpha\right|\le s^\alpha\le s^a.
\end{equation}
Exponential moments of fixed polynomial orders are uniformly bounded
on compact exponent intervals. Consequently, uniformly for
$\alpha,\beta\in[a,b]$,
\begin{align}\label{dmv:fl:eq:covwindowrow}
 \Cov(M_s^\alpha,M_s^\beta)
 &=s^{-\alpha-\beta}
       \left(c_{\alpha,\beta}+O(s^a)\right),\\
 c_{\alpha,\beta}
 &=\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+1)-\Gamma(\alpha+1)\Gamma(\beta+1),
 \nonumber
\end{align}
and
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:fourthrowwindow}
 \E|M_s^\alpha-\E M_s^\alpha|^4\le C_{a,b}s^{-4\alpha}.
\end{equation}
In particular $c_{1/2,1/2}=1-\pi/4=c_*$.

\begin{lemma}[Additive critical-window limit]\label{dmv:fl:lem:additivewindow}
For each fixed finite list of parameters, the centered variables
\[
 \sqrt{t/H}\,
 (S_{\alpha_t(\theta),J}-\E_tS_{\alpha_t(\theta),J})
\]
converge jointly to a centered Gaussian vector with covariance
$K_{\rm crit}$ in \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:windowkernel}. Their covariances converge,
and their fourth moments are uniformly bounded. The covariance estimates
are uniform on compact parameter sets and the conclusions remain valid
for parameter sequences tending to fixed values.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Put $s_0=\theta+\phi$ and $\delta=s_0/H$. Since $tJ\to0$,
independence and \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:covwindowrow} give
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:windowsum}
 \Cov_t(S_{\alpha_t(\theta),J},S_{\alpha_t(\phi),J})
 =(c_*+o(1))t^{-1-\delta}\sum_{j\le J}j^{-1-\delta}.
\end{equation}
The row-approximation error relative to the sum is
$O((tJ)^{2/5})=o(1)$, uniformly for bounded parameters. Integral
comparison, together with $\log J=H/2+\log L+o(1)$, gives
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:harmonicwindow}
 \frac1H\sum_{j\le J}j^{-1-s_0/H}
 \longrightarrow\int_0^{1/2}\ee^{-s_0u}\,du.
\end{equation}
For bounded $s_0$, the sum--integral error is $O(1/H)$ and the
endpoint error is $O_T(\log L/H)$. Since $t^{-\delta}=\ee^{s_0}$,
equations \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:windowsum}--\eqref{dmv:fl:eq:harmonicwindow} yield
\[
 \frac tH\Cov_t(S_{\alpha_t(\theta),J},S_{\alpha_t(\phi),J})
 \longrightarrow c_*\int_0^{1/2}\ee^{(\theta+\phi)(1-u)}\,du.
\]
This is exactly \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:windowkernel}.

For the central limit theorem, \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:fourthrowwindow} implies
\[
 \sum_{j\le J}
 \E_t|M_j^{\alpha_t(\theta)}-\E_tM_j^{\alpha_t(\theta)}|^4
 \le C_Tt^{-4\alpha_t(\theta)}
       \sum_{j\ge1}j^{-4\alpha_t(\theta)}=O_T(t^{-2}).
\]
Here $4\alpha_t(\theta)\ge8/5>1$, uniformly for small $t$, and
$t^{-4\alpha_t(\theta)}=t^{-2}\ee^{4\theta}$.
After division by $(H/t)^2$, the fourth-moment sum is $O_T(H^{-2})$.
The same estimate holds for every fixed linear combination, using
$|\sum_{i=1}^r z_i|^4\le r^3\sum_{i=1}^r|z_i|^4$.
If its limiting variance is positive, Lyapunov's theorem applies;
if it is zero, convergence to zero holds in $L^2$. The Cram\'er--Wold
criterion gives the joint Gaussian limit.

For independent centered row contributions $U_j$, the fourth-moment
identity
\[
 \E\left(\sum_j U_j\right)^4
 =\sum_j\E U_j^4+6\sum_{i<j}\E U_i^2\E U_j^2
\]
and the covariance bound show that every normalized fourth moment is
bounded. All estimates were compact-uniform, which also proves the
claim for converging parameter sequences.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Returning to occupied values and uniform partitions}
\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{dmv:fl:thm:window} and the critical case]
Under $\Q_t$ the identity
\[
 D_{\alpha,J}-\E_tD_{\alpha,J}
 =S_{\alpha,J}-\E_tS_{\alpha,J}
       -(C_{\alpha,J}-\E_tC_{\alpha,J})
\]
holds, where $D_{\alpha,J}$ here denotes $D_\alpha^{(J)}$.
Lemma~\ref{dmv:fl:lem:criticalcollision} makes the last term negligible in
$L^2$ after multiplication by $\sqrt{t/H}$. The additive lemma therefore
proves the joint geometric limits and the convergence of covariances.
Localization removes the row cutoff with negligible first and second
moment errors, as established in Section~\ref{dmv:fl:sec:conditioning}.

For the uniform law, first retain the cutoff. The normalized additive
terms have bounded fourth moments, so Proposition~\ref{dmv:fl:prop:transfer}
transfers their laws and first two moments. Its bounded-likelihood
consequence also transfers the centered collision errors, whose $L^2$
norms tend to zero. In particular the normalized conditional mean
differs from the normalized geometric mean by $o(1)$, so the centering
can be changed to the conditional expectation. The rare-event bound
\eqref{dmv:fl:eq:eventtransfer} then removes the cutoff under
conditioning as well. It is applied separately because the support
mismatch event depends on both the initial block and the reservoir.

These arguments use the exact saddle $t=t_n$. To recover the statement
with $\tau_n$, observe the exact identity
\[
 \frac12+\frac\theta{\log(1/\tau_n)}
 =\frac12+\frac{\theta_t}{\log(1/t_n)},\qquad
 \theta_t=\theta\frac{\log(1/t_n)}{\log(1/\tau_n)}\longrightarrow\theta.
\]
The compact-uniform estimates just proved apply to $\theta_t$, while
$t_n/\tau_n\to1$. Continuity of the covariance kernel gives the stated
normalization and exponent without requiring a derivative estimate for
$D_\alpha$. Proposition~\ref{dmv:fl:prop:weightedmeans} supplies the explicit
leading-mean centering, uniformly in this window.

Finally, the deterministic Brownian integrands in \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:brownian}
have covariance
$c_*\int_0^{1/2}\ee^{(\theta+\phi)(1-u)}\,du$ by the It\^o isometry.
This proves that representation for the finite-dimensional limits.
Taking $\theta=0$ gives \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:criticalphase}. No tightness result
for the prelimit processes is invoked.
\end{proof}

The variable $u=\log j/H$ in \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:harmonicwindow} identifies the
scales responsible for the critical fluctuation. Part sizes from order
one up to order $t^{-1/2}$ occupy an interval of length $1/2$ in this
logarithmic coordinate. Their independent contributions accumulate to
the Brownian integral. The additional factor $L$ needed to localize
the entire occupied set changes that interval only by $o(1)$.
\section{Explicit consequences and separation of fluctuation sources}\label{dmv:fl:sec:consequences}

\subsection{The Gumbel distribution at weight one}
The series $Z_1$ has a classical closed distribution. For real $u$,
independence and $L^2$ convergence give
\begin{align}\label{dmv:fl:eq:gumbelproduct}
 \E\ee^{\ii uZ_1}
 &=\prod_{j\ge1}\frac{\ee^{-\ii u/j}}{1-\ii u/j}
 =\ee^{-\ii\gamma u}\Gamma(1-\ii u).
\end{align}
The last equality is Euler's convergent product for the gamma function;
it follows as well by taking the limit in
\[
 \prod_{j=1}^k\frac{\ee^{-\ii u/j}}{1-\ii u/j}
 =\ee^{-\ii uH_k}
   \frac{\Gamma(k+1)\Gamma(1-\ii u)}{\Gamma(k+1-\ii u)},
 \qquad H_k-\log k\longrightarrow\gamma.
\]
The standard Gumbel density is
$\ee^{-x}\exp(-\ee^{-x})$. Substituting $v=\ee^{-x}$ in its Fourier
integral yields the characteristic function $\Gamma(1-\ii u)$.
Thus $Z_1$ has the law $G-\gamma$. Proposition~\ref{dmv:fl:prop:weightedmeans}
then gives the location in \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:gumbel} exactly.

Also, $\Var Z_1=\zeta(2)=\pi^2/6$, so
\[
 \Var_nD_1\sim\frac{\pi^2}{6}\tau_n^{-2}=n.
\]
This proves every assertion of Corollary~\ref{dmv:fl:cor:gumbel}, including
the variance, which does not follow merely from weak convergence.
The statistic $\sum_jM_j$, counting all parts, is much larger in mean
than $D_1$. The collision argument explains why removing repeated
multiplicity values nonetheless leaves the same centered limiting
random series.

\subsection{Explicit centerings above the Gaussian range}
For $1/2<\alpha<1$, the two-term mean formula gives the useful form
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:explicitintermediate}
 \tau_n^\alpha
 \left(D_\alpha-M_\alpha\tau_n^{-(\alpha+1)/2}\right)
 \dto Z_\alpha+\Gamma(\alpha+1)\zeta(\alpha).
\end{equation}
Thus no unknown expectation is needed to state a limiting distribution
in any regime: use the leading mean below and at $1/2$, the displayed
deterministic shift for $1/2<\alpha<1$, the Gumbel centering for
$\alpha=1$, and the uncentered limit for $\alpha>1$.

At $\alpha=2$, $\E E_j^2=2$ and
$\Var(E_j^2)=24-4=20$. Therefore
\[
 \E Y_2=2\zeta(2)=\frac{\pi^2}{3},\qquad
 \Var Y_2=20\zeta(4)=\frac{2\pi^4}{9}.
\]
Multiplying by $\tau_n^{-2}/n=6/\pi^2$ proves
Corollary~\ref{dmv:fl:cor:quadratic}. More generally, for $\alpha>1$,
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:relativevariance}
 \frac{\Var_nD_\alpha}{(\E_nD_\alpha)^2}
 \longrightarrow
 \frac{[\Gamma(2\alpha+1)-\Gamma(\alpha+1)^2]\zeta(2\alpha)}
      {\Gamma(\alpha+1)^2\zeta(\alpha)^2}>0.
\end{equation}
For every fixed $0\le\alpha\le1$, in contrast, the same ratio tends
to zero by the mean and variance formulas already proved. The
fluctuation transition at $1/2$ and the loss of relative concentration
above $1$ are therefore distinct thresholds.

\subsection{Mutual independence of the three fluctuation regimes}
\label{dmv:fl:subsec:mutual-independence}

The separation between the three regimes is stronger than a comparison
of variances. Their limiting vectors are mutually independent, although
the coordinates within each vector generally remain dependent.

\begin{theorem}[Mutual independence across row scales]
\label{dmv:fl:thm:independence}
Fix any finite lists of exponents and parameters
\[
 0\le\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_r<\tfrac12,\qquad
 \theta_1,\ldots,\theta_s\in\mathbb R,\qquad
 \beta_1,\ldots,\beta_v>\tfrac12.
\]
Take the normalized centered subcritical variables from
Theorem~\ref{dmv:fl:thm:phase}, the moving-window variables from
Theorem~\ref{dmv:fl:thm:window}, and the normalized centered fixed
supercritical variables from Theorem~\ref{dmv:fl:thm:phase}.
They converge jointly. Their three limiting vectors are mutually
independent:
\begin{enumerate}
\item the subcritical Gaussian vector with covariance
      $\mathcal K(y^{\alpha_i},y^{\alpha_j})$;
\item the critical-window Gaussian vector with covariance
      $K_{\rm crit}(\theta_i,\theta_j)$;
\item the vector $(Z_{\beta_1},\ldots,Z_{\beta_v})$ built from the same
      exponential sequence within that vector.
\end{enumerate}
The assertions hold under both $\Q_t$ and $\Pp_n$, with their respective
expectations and the normalizations already specified.
All cross-covariances between the three normalized vectors converge
to zero.

The same conclusion holds if the first vector consists instead of a
finite family of normalized bounded piecewise continuous compactly
supported occupancy tests on $(0,\infty)$. These are
finite-dimensional assertions; no convergence or tightness in a
function space is claimed.
\end{theorem}

We first prove a row-deletion estimate for bounded signed weights.
It is essential to center the deletion error: its expectation need
not be small.

For a finite row set $A\subseteq[J]$, write
\[
 I_m^A=\ind_{\{\exists j\in A:M_j=m\}},\qquad
 T_f^A=\sum_{m\ge1}f(\eps m)I_m^A.
\]
Here $\eps=\sqrt t$. For the next lemma, $f$ is any bounded real
function supported in a fixed compact interval $[a,b]\subset(0,\infty)$;
no continuity or sign assumption is required.

\begin{lemma}[Deleting the smallest growing block of rows]
\label{dmv:fl:lem:small-row-deletion}
Let $0<\eta\le1$, put $R=\lfloor\eta/\eps\rfloor$, and assume
$1\le R<J$. Set $A=[R]$ and $B=\{R+1,\ldots,J\}$.
There is a constant $C_f$, depending only on $a,b$ and $\sup|f|$,
such that
\begin{equation}
 \Var_t\!\left(T_f^{[J]}-T_f^B\right)
 \le C_f\eta^2\eps^{-1}.
 \label{dmv:fl:eq:small-row-deletion-variance}
\end{equation}
In particular, the centered normalized statistics satisfy
\begin{equation}
 \left\|
 \sqrt\eps\left[
 T_f^{[J]}-\E_tT_f^{[J]}
 -(T_f^B-\E_tT_f^B)
 \right]\right\|_2
 \le C_f^{1/2}\eta.
 \label{dmv:fl:eq:small-row-deletion-Ltwo}
\end{equation}
The constants are uniform in $J$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Put $f_m=f(\eps m)$ and
$\mathcal B_f=\{m\ge1:a\le\eps m\le b\}$.
The exact difference is
\[
 \Delta_f:=T_f^{[J]}-T_f^B
 =\sum_{m\in\mathcal B_f}f_m I_m^A(1-I_m^B).
\]
This identity remains valid for signed $f$.
There are $O_{a,b}(\eps^{-1})$ relevant bins, and
\begin{align}
 \lambda_m^A:=\sum_{j\le R}p_{jm}
 &\le \frac{tR(R+1)}2\le\eta^2,
 \label{dmv:fl:eq:deleted-intensity}\\
 \lambda_m^B:=\sum_{R<j\le J}p_{jm}
 &\le\lambda_m\le\frac1{tm^2}\le a^{-2}
       \qquad(m\in\mathcal B_f).
 \label{dmv:fl:eq:retained-intensity}
\end{align}
The bound in \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:deleted-intensity} uses $R\ge1$, so
$R+1\le2R$, and $\eps R\le\eta$.

Apply Efron--Stein to $\Delta_f$, resampling one independent row at
a time. Suppose first that $j\in A$. Resampling $M_j$ can change
$I_m^A$ only at its old and new values. The factor $1-I_m^B$ is
unchanged and is at most one. Thus the squared change is bounded
by twice $\sup|f|^2$ times the sum of the indicators that the old
and new values belong to $\mathcal B_f$. Summing the resulting
Efron--Stein contributions over $j\in A$ gives at most
\[
 C\sup|f|^2
   \sum_{j\le R}\sum_{m\in\mathcal B_f}p_{jm}
 =
 C\sup|f|^2\sum_{m\in\mathcal B_f}\lambda_m^A
 \le C_f\eta^2\eps^{-1}.
\]

Now suppose that $j\in B$. A changed factor $1-I_m^B$ matters only
if $I_m^A=1$. The old and new values of row $j$ are independent
of all rows in $A$. The analogous squared-change estimate
therefore gives a total contribution at most
\[
 C\sup|f|^2
 \sum_{j\in B}\sum_{m\in\mathcal B_f}
     p_{jm}\Q_t(I_m^A=1)
 \le
 C\sup|f|^2
 \sum_{m\in\mathcal B_f}\lambda_m^B\lambda_m^A
 \le C_f\eta^2\eps^{-1},
\]
by \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:deleted-intensity}--\eqref{dmv:fl:eq:retained-intensity}.
We have only enlarged the estimate by dropping any condition on
the other retained rows. Hence it is valid regardless of repeated
values among those rows.
Adding the two row-block contributions proves
\eqref{dmv:fl:eq:small-row-deletion-variance}. Centering and multiplying
by $\sqrt\eps$ proves \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:small-row-deletion-Ltwo}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{dmv:fl:thm:independence}]
We first work under $\Q_t$ with bounded piecewise continuous
compactly supported tests $f_1,\ldots,f_r$ in the first vector.
Use the same row cutoff
\[
 J=\lceil t^{-1/2}L\rceil,\qquad
 L=C_AH^{3/2},\qquad H=\log(1/t),
\]
in every occupied statistic. Choose $A$ large enough for the
localization errors in all the finitely many moments and exponents
under consideration. Fix an integer $K\ge1$ and a number
$0<\eta\le1$. Put $R=\lfloor\eta/\eps\rfloor$.
For all sufficiently small $t$,
\[
             K<R<J.
\]
Partition the retained rows into the three disjoint sets
\begin{equation}
 [K],\qquad \{K+1,\ldots,R\},\qquad\{R+1,\ldots,J\}.
 \label{dmv:fl:eq:three-row-blocks}
\end{equation}
The next approximations use these three sets, respectively, for
the supercritical, critical-window, and compact occupancy vectors.

\paragraph{The compact occupancy vector.}
Replace its coordinate
$\sqrt\eps(T_{f_i}^{[J]}-\E_tT_{f_i}^{[J]})$ by
\[
 \sqrt\eps\left(T_{f_i}^{\{R+1,\ldots,J\}}
                    -\E_tT_{f_i}^{\{R+1,\ldots,J\}}\right).
\]
Lemma~\ref{dmv:fl:lem:small-row-deletion} bounds each error in $L^2$ by
$C_{f_i}^{1/2}\eta$. This estimate does not require the deleted
statistic itself to have a limit for a fixed $\eta$.

\paragraph{The critical-window vector.}
For $\alpha_t(\theta)=1/2+\theta/H$, first replace the occupied
statistic by its centered additive approximation, as proved in
Section~\ref{dmv:fl:sec:critical}. Next retain only the middle rows:
\[
 \sqrt{\frac tH}
 \sum_{K<j\le R}
       \left(M_j^{\alpha_t(\theta)}
                        -\E_tM_j^{\alpha_t(\theta)}\right).
\]
For parameters in a fixed compact interval, the variance of the
discarded first $K$ centered rows is, after normalization,
\begin{equation}
 \frac tH
 \Var_t\sum_{j\le K}M_j^{\alpha_t(\theta)}
 \le
 \frac{C_T}{H}\sum_{j\le K}j^{-1-2\theta/H}
 =O_T((1+\log K)/H).
 \label{dmv:fl:eq:window-small-block}
\end{equation}
For the discarded rows above $R$, use
$j^{-2\theta/H}\le C_T$ for $j\le J$ and small $t$ to obtain
\begin{align}
 \frac tH
 \Var_t\sum_{R<j\le J}M_j^{\alpha_t(\theta)}
 &\le\frac{C_T}{H}\sum_{R<j\le J}j^{-1-2\theta/H}
 \nonumber\\
 &\le\frac{C_T}{H}\bigl(1+\log(J/R)\bigr)
 =O_T\!\left(\frac{1+\log(L/\eta)}H\right).
 \label{dmv:fl:eq:window-large-block}
\end{align}
Both quantities tend to zero for fixed $K,\eta$, since
$\log L=O(\log H)$. Together with the centered collision bound,
these estimates make the middle-row approximation asymptotically
exact in $L^2$.

\paragraph{The fixed supercritical vector.}
For each fixed $\beta>1/2$, retain the first $K$ centered
additive rows:
\[
       t^\beta\sum_{j\le K}
                 (M_j^\beta-\E_tM_j^\beta).
\]
The centered collision estimate and \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:seriestail} imply
\begin{equation}
 \limsup_{t\downarrow0}
 \left\|
 t^\beta(D_\beta^{(J)}-\E_tD_\beta^{(J)})
 -
 t^\beta\sum_{j\le K}(M_j^\beta-\E_tM_j^\beta)
 \right\|_2
 \le
 C_\beta\left(\sum_{j>K}j^{-2\beta}\right)^{1/2}.
 \label{dmv:fl:eq:high-first-block}
\end{equation}
This bound tends to zero as $K\to\infty$. The retained finite
sums converge jointly to the first $K$ terms of the corresponding
exponential series.

\paragraph{Independence and the order of limits.}
For every $t$ with $K<R<J$, the three approximating vectors just
defined are independent under $\Q_t$: they depend on the three
disjoint row sets in \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:three-row-blocks}.
The marginal limits of the original vectors are already known.
To identify their joint limit without assuming any additional
limit for the deleted compact statistic, compare characteristic
functions directly. For any fixed frequency vectors, the inequality
$|\ee^{\ii x}-\ee^{\ii y}|\le|x-y|$ and the preceding $L^2$ bounds
show that the difference between the original joint characteristic
function and the product of its original marginal characteristic
functions has limiting upper bound
\[
 C\eta+
 C\sum_{\ell=1}^v
      \left(\sum_{j>K}j^{-2\beta_\ell}\right)^{1/2}.
\]
One obtains this bound by replacing all three vectors by their
independent approximations, then replacing the marginal
characteristic functions back; each replacement has the same
type of bound.

First let $t\downarrow0$ with $K,\eta$ fixed. Then let
$\eta\downarrow0$ and $K\to\infty$. The displayed error vanishes.
The known marginal characteristic functions therefore multiply
in the joint limit. This proves mutual independence for compact
occupancy tests.

\paragraph{Removing the multiplicity cutoffs.}
For a subcritical exponent $0\le\alpha<1/2$, use the compact
test
\[
           f_{\alpha,a,b}(y)=y^\alpha\ind_{[a,b]}(y).
\]
The normalized power statistic has the exact form
\[
 \eps^{\alpha+1/2}(D_\alpha^{(J)}-\E_tD_\alpha^{(J)})
 =
 \sqrt\eps\sum_{m\ge1}
        (\eps m)^\alpha(I_m^{[J]}-\E_tI_m^{[J]}).
\]
The centered low- and high-multiplicity bounds in
Section~\ref{dmv:fl:sec:gaussian} show that replacing this by the compact
test statistic has arbitrarily small limiting $L^2$ error as
$a\downarrow0$ and $b\uparrow\infty$. The same statement with
row cutoff $J$ follows by support localization and its moment
bounds; no localization assertion after deleting rows is needed.
For a finite list of exponents, apply the individual bounds and
the triangle inequality to every linear combination.

More explicitly, fix the compact bin cutoffs first, carry out the
limit $t\downarrow0$, then $\eta\downarrow0$ and $K\to\infty$,
and finally remove the compact bin cutoffs. A second characteristic
function approximation gives the claimed product limit for the
subcritical power statistics.

\paragraph{Conditioning and centerings.}
All original truncated statistics in this proof are measurable
with respect to $(M_j)_{j\le J}$. Proposition~\ref{dmv:fl:prop:transfer}
therefore transfers their entire joint law by total variation.
The marginal normalized squares are uniformly integrable by the
already proved fluctuation theorems and their moment estimates.
Thus changing each geometric centering to its own conditional
centering changes the normalized variable by $o(1)$.
The support-localization event and its separately proved
conditional moment bounds then replace the original statistics
with cutoff $J$ by the full statistics.

In particular, the argument never assumes that
$\mathcal M=\mathcal M_J$ remains true after deleting rows.
Deletion is performed inside the common finite block $[J]$;
localization is used only for the original, undeleted statistics.
The exact-saddle to $\tau_n$ replacement, including the window
reparameterization, is the one already proved in
Section~\ref{dmv:fl:sec:critical}.

Finally, uniform integrability of the individual normalized
squares implies uniform integrability of every pairwise product.
For example, split the event $|XY|>M$ into $|X|>\sqrt M$ or
$|Y|>\sqrt M$ and apply Cauchy--Schwarz.
Joint convergence therefore transfers all pairwise moments.
The limiting cross-covariances between the independent centered
vectors are zero, as asserted.
\end{proof}

For example, the Gaussian limit for $D_0$, the critical Gaussian
limit for $D_{1/2}$, and the standard Gumbel limit for $D_1$ are
mutually independent after the stated deterministic centerings.
Within the supercritical vector, the covariance
\eqref{dmv:fl:eq:highcov} remains strictly positive. Within the two
Gaussian vectors, the covariance kernels $\mathcal K$ and
$K_{\rm crit}$ describe their dependence. The independence theorem
concerns the separation between these three families.

\subsection{Consequences for total sequences}
Let $A_\alpha(n)=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}D_\alpha(\lambda)$, and let
$B_\alpha(n)=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}D_\alpha(\lambda)^2$.
The exact relation to the uniform law is
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:totals}
 \frac{A_\alpha(n)}{p(n)}=\E_nD_\alpha,\qquad
 \frac{B_\alpha(n)}{p(n)}
       -\left(\frac{A_\alpha(n)}{p(n)}\right)^2
 =\Var_nD_\alpha.
\end{equation}
The unweighted total $A_0$ is OEIS A373271; $A_1$ is its linked
weight-one counterpart A373273 \cite{oeisA373271,oeisA373273}. Thus, for example,
\[
 p(n)B_0(n)-A_0(n)^2\sim v_0p(n)^2n^{1/4},\qquad
 p(n)B_1(n)-A_1(n)^2\sim p(n)^2n.
\]
These are sequence-level forms of the variance theorems, with the
centering cancellation kept explicit. The first equality in
\eqref{dmv:fl:eq:totals} also converts every weighted mean formula into a
total-sequence asymptotic without another analytic transfer theorem.

\subsection{Independent exact finite calculations}
The finite check uses two different descriptions of a partition.
The first directly enumerates every partition through $n=40$, computes
its multiplicity set, and accumulates the first and second raw moments
of $D_0,D_1,D_2$ and the mixed moment $D_0D_1$. The second computes
the same totals by generating functions that forbid one or two
multiplicity values. Its state and recurrence do not enumerate
partitions or reuse their occupied sets.

Let $P(q)=\prod_{j\ge1}(1-q^j)^{-1}$ and, for a finite set $S$ of
positive integers, define
\begin{equation}\label{dmv:fl:eq:forbiddenGF}
 P_S(q)=\prod_{j\ge1}
 \left(\frac1{1-q^j}-\sum_{m\in S}q^{jm}\right).
\end{equation}
Each factor has coefficients $0$ or $1$ and permits precisely the
multiplicities outside $S$. Therefore $[q^n]P_S(q)$ counts the
partitions of $n$ in which every value in $S$ is absent.
Inclusion--exclusion gives
\begin{align}\label{dmv:fl:eq:exactmoments}
 \sum_{n\ge0}A_\alpha(n)q^n
 &=\sum_{m\ge1}m^\alpha(P-P_{\{m\}}),\\
 \sum_{n\ge0}B_\alpha(n)q^n
 &=\sum_{m\ge1}m^{2\alpha}(P-P_{\{m\}})\nonumber\\
 &\quad+2\sum_{m<\ell}m^\alpha\ell^\alpha
 (P-P_{\{m\}}-P_{\{\ell\}}+P_{\{m,\ell\}}).
 \nonumber
\end{align}
These are formal power-series identities: to compute through degree
$n$, only $j,m,\ell\le n$ can contribute. The analogous mixed-moment
identity has off-diagonal coefficient
$m^\alpha\ell^\beta+m^\beta\ell^\alpha$ and diagonal coefficient
$m^{\alpha+\beta}$. Euler's pentagonal recurrence independently
recomputes $p(n)$.

\subsection{A proposed research programme}
The following questions are ordered by their connection to the
results proved here. They are open within this article; a literature
claim of longstanding global openness is not implied.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=8pt]
\item \textbf{Effective Gaussian approximation.}
Obtain a quantitative bound in Kolmogorov or Wasserstein distance for
the normalized $D_0$, with computable constants and an explicit onset.
The compact cumulant calculation suggests the natural small parameter
$\sqrt\eps=n^{-1/8}$ up to constants, but it does not by itself prove
a bound of that order. A successful argument must make the cumulant
order dependence explicit, control both multiplicity tails, and retain
the conditioning error. Quantitative normal approximation for the
negatively dependent occupation indicators may be more efficient than
an all-order expansion.

\item \textbf{The next variance term and critical finite-size correction.}
Determine whether
$\Var_nD_0-v_0n^{1/4}$ has a finite limit, or whether a logarithmic or
other intermediate correction occurs. Independently, determine the
coefficient after $c_*H/(2t)$ in $\Var D_{1/2}$. The present
$O(t^{-1}\log H)$ collision bound may be nonsharp and does not
identify such a coefficient. This problem has immediate numerical
value because the critical approximation is visibly slow. It requires
matching pair occupancies and collision covariances, not only improving
the first-moment expansion.

\item \textbf{Functional convergence of the critical window.}
Strengthen finite-dimensional convergence of $X_n(\theta)$ to weak
convergence in $C([-T,T])$, or in a topology that also controls
derivatives in $\theta$. The proposed limit is the smooth Gaussian
field in \eqref{dmv:fl:eq:brownian}. A route is to estimate increments and
derivatives, whose weights introduce factors $\log m/H$, and obtain
uniform control of the collision process. Pointwise collision variance
alone is insufficient to claim process tightness. This would permit
simultaneous confidence bands over a range of exponents.

\item \textbf{Regularly varying weights.}
Replace $m^\alpha$ by $m^\alpha L(m)$ for a specified slowly varying
function $L$. Below the threshold the relevant argument of $L$ should
be $t^{-1/2}$, whereas above it the smallest rows sample $t^{-1}$.
At the boundary, a test case is
$w(m)=\sqrt m\,(\log(m+1))^\beta$. The row calculation suggests a
variance scale $t^{-1}H^{2\beta+1}$ and coefficient
\[
 c_*\int_0^{1/2}(1-u)^{2\beta}\,du.
\]
This formula is a proposed extension, not a theorem of this paper.
The task is to specify regularity conditions that justify the
collision estimate, whole-range mean approximation, and conditioning
uniformly for these weights.

\item \textbf{Largest occupied values and a joint point-process theorem.}
Study the random point measure formed by $tM_j$ for positive
multiplicities, with repeated values removed. Fixed rows converge to
$E_j/j$, and the high-weight moment series suggests a common limiting
point measure away from zero. Prove a topology and tightness statement
that makes this precise, identify the largest occupied values, and
couple this limit with the Gaussian occupancy field. A moment theorem
for a few power weights does not automatically imply a point-process
theorem near its accumulation point at zero.

\item \textbf{Conditioning corrections and universality.}
The likelihood estimate proves that total-size conditioning does not
change the leading laws. Determine the first correction it contributes
to covariance and moderate deviations. Then treat specified restricted
partition models, such as colored partitions or a prescribed allowed
set of part sizes. The occupancy intensity and reservoir may change;
the threshold $1/2$ should be derived from their scaling instead of
assumed universal. Partitions into distinct parts require a different
observable because their positive multiplicity set is essentially
trivial.

\item \textbf{Formalization and exact second-moment computation.}
Formalize first the exact finite identities
\eqref{dmv:fl:eq:forbiddenGF}--\eqref{dmv:fl:eq:exactmoments}, the pairwise covariance
sign, and the finite-row resampling inequalities. These supply
independently reusable lemmas before the analytic limit theory is
formalized. On the computational side, develop a faster exact
algorithm for $B_0(n)$ and $B_1(n)$ so that variance asymptotics can be
tested well beyond the enumeration range. The quadratic family of
forbidden pairs is a concrete algorithmic obstruction.

\item \textbf{Complete the historical comparison.}
Inspect the full 1999 multiplicity paper and follow its citations,
including statistics of distinct gaps in conjugate Ferrers diagrams.
The conjugate partition has successive positive gaps equal to the
original multiplicities, so the present observable may appear under
that terminology. This bibliographic task changes the appropriate
novelty claim and attribution, even though it does not change the
proofs. A priority statement should be strengthened only after that
comparison has actually been completed.
\end{enumerate}

For immediate continuation, the strongest mathematical targets are an
effective error bound for $D_0$ and the critical variance correction.
They address a limitation exposed by the present proof and the retained
data. Functional and point-process results would then organize the
individual limit laws into a fuller description of the random
multiplicity set. The independent sensitivity companion supplies a
different research track: optimize the certified exponent, improve the
local recurrences, or replace the probabilistic label construction.
