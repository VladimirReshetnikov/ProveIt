\begin{abstract}
For $a_n=\sum_k k^n k!S(n,k)$, we prove the leading equivalent already recorded in OEIS and obtain a complete fixed-order expansion with explicit coefficients. An exact vertical contour gives a branch-safe representation and a simple, nonasymptotic exponential truncation bound. The same method yields compact-direction two-size asymptotics, Gaussian block-count fluctuations, and a controlled inverse with integer-threshold envelopes. An elementary appendix proves the posted mod-prime periodicity conjecture, in the sense that $p-1$ is a period, and extends it to prime powers. The standard saddle methodology and existing leading result are explicitly credited.
\end{abstract}

\subsection{Results and attribution}\label{a122:results-and-attribution}

The leading equivalent for A122399 was posted by Vaclav Kotesovec on OEIS on August 9, 2018; its growth constant was already identified in 2013. \textbf{Neither that equivalent nor its numerical constant is claimed as a new conjecture.} This manuscript proves it and develops explicit, reproducible higher coefficients, a globally valid contour with an explicit exponentially small truncation bound, a compact-direction extension, a block-count central limit theorem, and controlled inversion. These are applications and refinements of standard analytic saddle methods, not a new general method. In particular Khera--Lundberg--Melczer (2021, preprint 2019), Section 3 and the paragraph following its equation (15), give directional asymptotics and explain higher-order expansion for the nearby but different lonesum-matrix denominator. No global novelty claim is made.

\subsection{1. Exact combinatorics}\label{a122:exact-combinatorics}

Put

\[
 A_{m,n}=\sum_{k=0}^m k^n k!\,S(m,k),\qquad a_n=A_{n,n},\qquad
 b_{m,n}=\frac{A_{m,n}}{m!n!}.
\]

The convention at the origin is \(0^0=1\). For positive \(m,n\), a structure consists of an ordered partition of an \(m\)-element red set into \(k\) nonempty blocks, followed by a map of an \(n\)-element blue set to these blocks. Its bivariate exponential generating function, with \(u\) marking blocks, is

\[
 F(x,y;u)=\frac1{1-u e^y(e^x-1)}.
\]

Thus the diagonal is exactly A122399, not an approximation to it. When a structure is chosen uniformly, its block count satisfies \(\Pr(K_n=k)=k^n k!S(n,k)/a_n\).

\subsection{2. Exact contour and a nonasymptotic tail bound}\label{a122:exact-contour-and-a-nonasymptotic-tail-bound}

For \(\Re z>0\), use the single analytic branch

\[
 X(z)=\Log(1+e^{-z}),\qquad p(z)=\frac1{1+e^z}=-X'(z).
\]

Here \(1+e^{-z}\) stays in the open disk of radius one about 1, and \(X(z)\ne0\). The following identities hold for every pair of integers \(m,n\ge1\) and every \(c>0\):

\[
\begin{aligned}
 b_{m,n}&=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}
 \frac{p(z)}{X(z)^{m+1}z^{n+1}}\,dz\\
 &=\frac{n+1}{m}\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}
 X(z)^{-m}z^{-n-2}\,dz.
\end{aligned}\tag{2.1}
\]

Both integrals are absolutely convergent. Integer powers are used, so there is no unresolved power branch.

\subsubsection{Proof}\label{a122:proof}

For \(\Re h>0\) and \(n\ge1\), the absolutely and locally uniformly convergent Mittag--Leffler identity is

\[
 \frac1{n!}\sum_{k\ge1}k^n e^{-kh}
 =\sum_{j\in\mathbb Z}(h+2\pi i j)^{-n-1}.\tag{2.2}
\]

It follows, for example, by differentiating \(1/(e^h-1)\)'s symmetric partial-fraction expansion; the constant term disappears after one derivative. It also follows by Poisson summation for \(t^n e^{-ht}\mathbf1_{t\ge0}/n!\), whose Fourier coefficients are the terms on the right.

Change the \(x\)-coefficient contour to \(q=e^x-1\), with \(|q|=e^{-c}<1\); the inverse is \(x=\Log(1+q)\), analytic and one-to-one near the closed contour, and it winds once around zero. On this circle choose the continuous parametrization \(q=e^{-c+it}\), \(-\pi\le t\le\pi\), so \(h=c-it\). Insert (2.2) in the Cauchy coefficient integral. Uniform absolute convergence permits the sum--integral interchange. The shifted intervals in \(h+2\pi i j\) join to the whole vertical line; \(X\) and \(p\) are \(2\pi i\)-periodic. This gives the first identity in (2.1). Since \((X^{-m})'=m p X^{-m-1}\), integration by parts gives the second. Its boundary term tends to zero: on a fixed vertical line \(X\) is bounded away from zero, and \(z^{-n-1}\to0\). This also proves absolute convergence.

This argument does \textbf{not} treat an individual \((h(x)+2\pi i j)^{-n-1}\) as a globally analytic power series at \(x=0\). Doing so would ignore its logarithmic monodromy.

\subsubsection{Explicit truncation theorem}\label{a122:explicit-truncation-theorem}

Let \(x_c=X(c)>0\), and let \(R_T\) denote the sum of the parts of the \textbf{second} integral in (2.1) with \(|\Im z|\ge T>0\), including its prefactor. Then

\[
 |R_T|\le\frac{x_c^{-m}c^{-n}}{\pi m T}
 \left(\frac{c}{\sqrt{c^2+T^2}}\right)^n.\tag{2.3}
\]

Indeed, with \(w=X(c+it)\),

\[
 e^{-c}=|e^w-1|\le e^{|w|}-1,
 \quad\text{so}\quad |X(c+it)|\ge X(c).
\]

Consequently the modulus of the tail is bounded by \((n+1)x_c^{-m}/(\pi m)\int_T^\infty(c^2+t^2)^{-(n+2)/2}dt\). Set \(v=\sqrt{c^2+t^2}\); since \(v/\sqrt{v^2-c^2}\) decreases, that integral is at most \((c^2+T^2)^{-n/2}/((n+1)T)\), proving (2.3).

There is therefore an exact, absolutely convergent strip decomposition obtained by splitting the second contour into \((2j-1)\pi\le\Im z\le(2j+1)\pi\), \(j\in\mathbb Z\). The noncentral strips together have bound (2.3) with \(T=\pi\). They are not asserted to be canonical saddle/transseries sectors: their artificial endpoint contributions can cancel, and no individual secondary-saddle expansion is proved here. A fixed-order Poincaré expansion for the central strip has algebraic error, despite the exponentially accurate contour truncation.

\subsection{3. The unique saddle and all orders}\label{a122:the-unique-saddle-and-all-orders}

Fix \(\lambda=m/n>0\). There is a unique \(y=y(\lambda)>0\) satisfying

\[
 x=X(y)=\lambda y p(y),\qquad
 B=B(\lambda)=1+\lambda^{-1}-y(1-p(y)).\tag{3.1}
\]

Moreover \(B>1\), and \(x,y,B\) depend analytically on \(\lambda\). To prove uniqueness, \(R(y)=yp(y)/X(y)\) increases strictly from 0 to infinity. In fact \(p/X>1-p\), since \(p+(1-p)\log(1-p)>0\) for \(0<p<1\); differentiating \(\log(p/X)\) shows its derivative is positive. Thus \(R'>0\). At the root \(y(1-p)<1/\lambda\), proving \(B>1\). The implicit-function theorem supplies analyticity.

Define the analytic phase germ at \(s=0\)

\[
 f_\lambda(s)=-\log(1+s)-\lambda\log\frac{X(y(1+s))}{x}
 =\frac B2s^2+\sum_{r\ge3}f_rs^r.\tag{3.2}
\]

Here logarithms are local germs, and \(f_\lambda(0)=f'_\lambda(0)=0\). Globally along \(s=i\theta\), the exponential in the following formula means its integer-power expression, so no global choice of \(f\) is needed:

\[
 b_{m,n}=\frac{x^{-m}y^{-n}}{\lambda y}\left(1+\frac1n\right)
 \frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
 \frac{e^{n f_\lambda(i\theta)}}{(1+i\theta)^2}\,d\theta.\tag{3.3}
\]

The bound from Section 2 gives \(|e^{n f_\lambda(i\theta)}|\le(1+\theta^2)^{-n/2}\).

\subsubsection{Explicit coefficient formula}\label{a122:explicit-coefficient-formula}

Set \((-1)!!=1\). For \(j\ge0\), sum over nonnegative integers \(a,m_3,\ldots,m_{2j+2}\) subject to

\[
 a+\sum_{r=3}^{2j+2}(r-2)m_r=2j,
 \qquad M=a+\sum_{r=3}^{2j+2}r m_r.
\]

The integer \(M\) is necessarily even. Define

\[
 d_j=\sum
 (-1)^a(a+1)\prod_{r=3}^{2j+2}\frac{f_r^{m_r}}{m_r!}
 \frac{(-1)^{M/2}(M-1)!!}{B^{M/2}},
 \quad c_0=1,\quad c_j=d_j+d_{j-1}\ (j\ge1).\tag{3.4}
\]

The empty product gives \(d_0=1\). For every fixed integer \(J\ge0\),

\[
 \boxed{\displaystyle
 A_{m,n}=\frac{m!n!\,x^{-m}y^{-n}}{\lambda y\sqrt{2\pi n B}}
 \left(\sum_{j=0}^J\frac{c_j(\lambda)}{n^j}+O(n^{-J-1})\right).}\tag{3.5}
\]

The remainder is uniform when \(m/n\) ranges over any fixed compact subset of \((0,\infty)\). No growing-direction or boundary-uniform assertion is made.

\subsubsection{All-orders proof and remainder justification}\label{a122:all-orders-proof-and-remainder-justification}

Choose a small fixed \(\delta>0\) uniformly over the compact direction set. Outside \(|\theta|<\delta\), the modulus bound above makes the integral exponentially small; integrate the power tail exactly or use (2.3). Inside, analyticity, \(B>1\), and compactness give uniform Taylor remainder bounds and a uniform quadratic bound \(\Re f(i\theta)\le-b\theta^2\), for some \(b>0\). Set \(\theta=t/\sqrt n\). Expand the analytic amplitude \((1+s)^{-2}\) and \(\exp\{n(f(s)-Bs^2/2)\}\) through order \(n^{-J-1/2}\), keeping enough further Taylor terms to bound the remainder. On \(|t|\le n^\epsilon\), with \(\epsilon>0\) chosen sufficiently small for this fixed \(J\), Taylor's theorem bounds the remainder by \(n^{-J-1}\) times a fixed polynomial in \(|t|\) times a Gaussian with uniformly negative exponent. On \(n^\epsilon<|t|<\delta\sqrt n\), the uniform quadratic bound is superpolynomially small. Extend the polynomial--Gaussian integrals to the real line with the same accuracy. Odd terms integrate to zero. The even Gaussian moments are \((M-1)!!B^{-M/2}\), with \(i^M=(-1)^{M/2}\), giving (3.4). The prefactor \(1+1/n\) gives \(c_j=d_j+d_{j-1}\). This proves (3.5) with its uniform remainder.

This is the ordinary analytic one-dimensional saddle expansion; the exact contour is what supplies the particularly simple global tail control here.

\subsection{4. Diagonal constants and reproducible coefficients}\label{a122:diagonal-constants-and-reproducible-coefficients}

On the diagonal write \(x=x(1),y=y(1),B=2-y+x\), \(D=(xy)^{-1}\), and

\[
 C=\frac1{y\sqrt{2\pi B}}.
\]

Numerically,

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \(x=0.2763928914715160816083833582415360977661\)
\item
  \(y=1.144554440911905470205106411159417937429\)
\item
  \(B=1.131838450559610611403276947082118160337\)
\item
  \(D=3.161088653865428813830172202588132491726\)
\item
  \(C=0.3276282855698694814422864924105070307103\)
\end{itemize}

Thus

\[
\begin{aligned}
 a_n=\frac{C D^n(n!)^2}{\sqrt n}\Bigg[1
 &-\frac{0.0600744155748035800316371200380}{n}\\
 &+\frac{0.00759452281787645507680871266458}{n^2}\\
 &+\frac{0.00371322633571244961947936026057}{n^3}\\
 &-\frac{0.000362980958541489832391365216986}{n^4}
 +O(n^{-5})\Bigg].
\end{aligned}\tag{4.1}
\]

An exact expression for the first coefficient is

\[
 c_1=1-\frac3B+\frac{3f_4-6f_3}{B^2}-\frac{15f_3^2}{2B^3},\tag{4.2}
\]

where, setting \(a=y-x\), \(b=y-2x\),

\[
 f_3=\frac{a(b-3)}6,
\quad f_4=\frac12-\frac a2+\frac{a^2}8+\frac{ab}6
 -\frac{a(y^2-6xy+6x^2)}{24}.
\]

The numerical generator uses exact rational symbolic algebra for the phase, then 90-digit arithmetic; it does not fit these coefficients to sequence values. It prints coefficients through \(n^{-7}\), exact checks through \(n=800\), and scaled remainders. The recurrence \(T_{m,k}=k(T_{m-1,k}+T_{m-1,k-1})\), \(T_{0,0}=1\), generates \(T_{m,k}=k!S(m,k)\), so the numerical checks use independent exact integer values.

The strip-tail factor for \(T=\pi\) is \(\rho_\pi=y/\sqrt{y^2+\pi^2}<0.343\). Accordingly the exact central-contour integral differs from \(a_n/(n!)^2\) by at most \(D^n\rho_\pi^n/(\pi^2 n)\). This is a contour-evaluation statement, not an exponentially accurate bound for a fixed truncation of (4.1).

\subsection{5. Controlled inverse}\label{a122:controlled-inverse}

Define \(P_J(t)=\sum_{j=0}^J c_jt^j\). For every fixed \(J\), it is positive for all sufficiently small nonnegative \(t\). For sufficiently large real \(v\), put

\[
 H_J(v)=2\log\Gamma(v+1)+v\log D-\tfrac12\log v+\log C+\log P_J(1/v).
\]

Then

\[
 \log a_n=H_J(n)+O(n^{-J-1}),\qquad
 H'_J(v)=2\log v+\log D+O(v^{-1})>0.
\]

Consequently the large solution \(\nu_J\) of \(H_J(\nu_J)=\log a_n\) is unique and satisfies

\[
 \boxed{\nu_J=n+O(n^{-J-1}/\log n).}\tag{5.1}
\]

This follows from the mean-value theorem, using (3.5) and the derivative estimate. It is a controlled inverse along the sequence, without choosing an arbitrary exact interpolation of the integers.

For a practical Lambert-W initialization, put \(L=\log A\) and

\[
 w=W_0\left(\frac{\sqrt D}{2e}L\right),\qquad N_0=\frac{L}{2w}.
\]

If \(A=a_n\), then \(N_0=n+O(1)\), and

\[
 n=N_0-\frac{\tfrac12\log N_0+\log(2\pi C)}{2(w+1)}
 +O\left(\frac1{N_0\log N_0}\right).\tag{5.2}
\]

Indeed Stirling's expansion gives \(\log a_n=2n\log n+(\log D-2)n+\frac12\log n+\log(2\pi C)+O(1/n)\), while \(N_0\) exactly solves its first two terms. Taylor expansion around \(N_0\) gives (5.2).

inverse is \(\nu_J\) of (5.1).
\end{writenote}

For arbitrary algebraic inverse precision use Newton iteration

\[
 N_{r+1}=N_r-\frac{H_J(N_r)-L}{H'_J(N_r)},\tag{5.3}
\]

starting at \(N_0\). Uniformly in its \(O(1)\) starting neighborhood, \(H''_J/H'_J=O((n\log n)^{-1})\). Therefore its error relative to \(\nu_J\) after fixed \(r\) iterations is \(O((n\log n)^{-(2^r-1)})\). Taking \(2^r-1\ge J+1\) and combining with (5.1) gives the inverse error in (5.1).

For an arbitrary large target \(A\), let \(\nu=H_J^{-1}(\log A)\) and \(M(A)=\min\{n:a_n\ge A\}\). There is a constant \(E_J>0\) such that

\[
 \boxed{\left\lceil\nu-E_J\frac{\nu^{-J-1}}{\log\nu}\right\rceil
 \le M(A)\le
 \left\lceil\nu+E_J\frac{\nu^{-J-1}}{\log\nu}\right\rceil}\tag{5.4}
\]

for all sufficiently large \(A\). Indeed, the uniform sequence-value error \(|\log a_k-H_J(k)|\le C_Jk^{-J-1}\) and the lower bound on \(H'_J\) show that integers below the lower real endpoint have \(a_k<A\), and an integer at or above the upper endpoint has \(a_k\ge A\). Enlarge \(E_J\) to cover both comparisons. The sequence is strictly increasing from \(n=1\) onward: its old positive block-count summands weakly increase and additional positive structures occur.

The envelope (5.4) allows two neighboring candidates near an integer boundary, so rounding a real inverse alone is not guaranteed. Exact integer comparison settles that boundary. The constants and starting threshold in (5.4) are asymptotic existence constants, not a certified finite-input error bar unless separately bounded.

\subsection{6. Block-count fluctuations}\label{a122:block-count-fluctuations}

For \(u=e^s\) near 1, replace \(X\) by \(X_s(z)=\Log(1+e^{-s-z})\), and let \(y(s)\), \(x(s)=X_s(y(s))\) solve \(x(s)=y(s)p(s)\), where \(p(s)=1/(1+e^{s+y(s)})\). Set

\[
 B(s)=2-y(s)(1-p(s)),\quad D(s)=[x(s)y(s)]^{-1},
 \quad C(s)=\frac1{y(s)\sqrt{2\pi B(s)}}.
\]

The all-orders theorem extends analytically and uniformly to a sufficiently small complex disk in \(s\). To justify this rather than assuming it, use the analytic implicit-function theorem at the nondegenerate saddle. The contour can be locally deformed through \(y(s)\) inside \(\Re(s+z)>0\). The fixed outer contour portions retain a strict exponential gap by continuity from \(s=0\), and the far tails retain uniform power bounds. The local analytic saddle expansion is therefore uniform in complex \(s\). Cauchy estimates allow differentiation of its remainder.

It follows that

\[
 \mathbb E e^{sK_n}=\frac{C(s)}{C(0)}
 \left(\frac{D(s)}{D(0)}\right)^n[1+O(n^{-1})],\tag{6.1}
\]

uniformly near zero. This is the standard analytic quasi-powers situation. A direct expansion of the logarithm of (6.1) at \(s=it/\sqrt n\), followed by the continuity theorem, gives the Gaussian limit stated below.

Let \(\mu=1/y\) and \(\sigma^2=(B-1)/(B y^2)>0\). Then

\[
 \begin{aligned}\mathbb EK_n&=\mu n+\mu_0+O(n^{-1}),\\
 \operatorname{Var}(K_n)&=\sigma^2 n+O(1),\end{aligned}
 \qquad \frac{K_n-\mu n}{\sigma\sqrt n}\Longrightarrow N(0,1),\tag{6.2}
\]

where

\[
 \mu_0=\frac{B-1}{B y}-\frac{B-1-p}{2B^2},\qquad p=x/y.
\]

For verification, envelope differentiation gives \((\log D)'=1/y\). Implicit differentiation of the saddle equation gives \(y'=1/B-1\), hence \((\log D)''=(B-1)/(By^2)\). Further, \(B'=(B-1-p)/B\) and \((\log C)'=-y'/y-B'/(2B)\), yielding \(\mu_0\).

Numerically,

\[
\begin{aligned} \mu&=0.873702433239668330496568304721,\\
 \sigma^2&=0.0889169868271170325484054553784,\\
 \mu_0&=0.144565688577761026837729354227.\end{aligned}
\]

This gives a probabilistic interpretation of the OEIS growth parametrization's constant \(r=0.873702\ldots\): it is the asymptotic fraction of occupied ordered blocks. No local limit theorem or growing-marking-range result is claimed.

\begin{writenote}[Added 5 October 2026, batch~98]
Part~II proves the local limit theorem on the diagonal,
\(\Pr(K_n=k)=(\sigma\sqrt n)^{-1}\varphi((k-\mu n-\mu_0)/(\sigma\sqrt
n))+O(n^{-1})\) uniformly in \(k\), with \(\varphi\) the standard normal
density (Corollary~\ref{a122:pd:cor:diaglclt}),
from an exact independent-Bernoulli representation of \(n-K_n\) and a
uniform lattice Edgeworth expansion at exact cumulants
(Theorem~\ref{a122:pd:thm:edgeworth}); the constants \(\mu\), \(\sigma^2\),
\(\mu_0\) are those of (6.2). No growing-marking-range result and no
large-deviation estimate is proved there either.
\end{writenote}

\subsection{7. Elementary congruence appendix}\label{a122:elementary-congruence-appendix}

The current OEIS entry conjectures that, for a prime \(p\), the reduction of \(a_n\) for \(n\ge1\) is purely periodic with period \(p-1\). The following proves that \(p-1\) is a period; it does not assert that it is always the least period.

\textbf{Proposition.} For every prime \(p\) and integer \(r\ge1\),

\[
 a_{n+\varphi(p^r)}\equiv a_n\pmod{p^r}\qquad(n\ge r).\tag{7.1}
\]

In particular \(a_{n+p-1}\equiv a_n\pmod p\) for every \(n\ge1\).

\textbf{Proof.} Terms with \(k\ge pr\) vanish modulo \(p^r\), since \(v_p(k!)\ge\lfloor k/p\rfloor\ge r\). We may extend the sum up to \(pr-1\) even when this exceeds \(n\), since \(S(n,k)=0\) for \(k>n\). The exact inclusion-exclusion formula gives

\[
 a_n\equiv\sum_{k=0}^{pr-1}\sum_{j=0}^k
 (-1)^{k-j}\binom{k}{j}(kj)^n\pmod{p^r}.
\]

For \(n\ge r\), a term with \(p\mid kj\) is zero modulo \(p^r\). Each other base \(kj\) is a unit, so Euler's theorem leaves its power unchanged on replacing \(n\) by \(n+\varphi(p^r)\). This proves (7.1). The argument is elementary and independent of the asymptotic analysis.

\begin{writenote}[Added 2 October 2026, batch~77]
The OEIS conjecture is credited there to Peter Bala (31 May 2022), as the
delivered \nolinkurl{source_review.md} records. Neither (7.1) nor any other
statement of this report is formalized. The one nontrivial identity in its
proof, the surjection formula \(k!\,S(n,k)=\sum_{j\le k}(-1)^{k-j}\binom kj
j^n\), is formalized in Lean as

(\nolinkurl{Analysis/FabiusFunction/Lean/FabiusFunction/StirlingBasisChange.lean}),

\end{writenote}

\subsection{8. Questions left by this analysis}\label{a122:questions-left-by-this-analysis}

The results separate three different levels of control: an exact contour with an explicit exponential tail, a fixed-order algebraic saddle expansion, and an asymptotic inverse. Several natural extensions require additional arguments.

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Exponentially improved expansions.} Locate and classify the complex saddles of \(z\Log(1+e^{-z})\), determine which are reached by valid contour deformations, and obtain an optimally truncated expansion with a controlled secondary-saddle remainder. The exact strip decomposition alone does not answer this question.
\item
  \textbf{Directions approaching the boundary.} Determine uniform laws when \(m/n\to0\) or \(m/n\to\infty\), including the transition scales where the compact-direction expansion ceases to be uniform. These regimes may require different saddle coordinates and block-count normalizations.

  \begin{writenote}[Added 5 October 2026, batch~98: answered in part]
  For \(m/n\to0\), Part~II locates the transition at \(n=m(\log m+c)\),
  where the number of missing blocks tends to a Poisson law of parameter
  \(e^{-c}/2\) and \(A_{m,n}/(m!\,m^n)\to\exp(e^{-c}/2)\); it proves a
  complete expansion there (Theorem~\ref{a122:pd:thm:allorders}),
  explicit bounds for all \(m,n\) (Theorem~\ref{a122:pd:thm:finite}), and
  the regime of every path \(n(m)\) (Theorem~\ref{a122:pd:thm:trichotomy}).
  For \(m/n\to\infty\) it gives Gaussian local laws at exact moments
  (Theorem~\ref{a122:pd:thm:edgeworth}) but no count asymptotics. Open:
  count asymptotics for \(m/n\to\infty\), and a uniform count
  approximation joining the window, the growing Poisson region and (3.5)
  (Question~\ref{a122:pd:q:bridge}).
  \end{writenote}
\item
  \textbf{Effective finite-input inverse bounds.} Turn the implicit all-orders constants into computable numerical bounds and starting thresholds, combining analytic Taylor estimates with the explicit contour tail. This would make the ceiling envelope a certified finite-input bracket and could reduce the need for exact comparisons away from sequence-value boundaries.
\item
  \textbf{Sharper block-count laws.} Prove a local limit theorem, explicit Edgeworth terms, or a uniform large-deviation estimate for the block count. The analytic marking germ provides the central cumulants, but the required global Fourier or tilted-contour estimates are not supplied here.

  \begin{writenote}[Added 5 October 2026, batch~98: answered in part]
  The local limit theorem is proved in Part~II
  (Corollary~\ref{a122:pd:cor:diaglclt}), with error \(O(n^{-1})\), and
  Edgeworth terms to every fixed order are proved at the exact cumulants
  for every path with diverging variance
  (Theorem~\ref{a122:pd:thm:edgeworth}); the global Fourier estimate comes
  from the real-rootedness of the block polynomial, not from a contour.
  Open: the Edgeworth terms in fixed diagonal constants
  (Question~\ref{a122:pd:q:diagedge}) and any large-deviation estimate.
  \end{writenote}
\end{enumerate}

These are limitations and possible continuations of this analysis, not claims that the corresponding general techniques or all related results are absent from the literature.

\subsubsection{A guide to the principal formulas}
Let $K=K_{m,n}$ be the number of blocks in a uniformly chosen structure,
and let $D=m-K$ be its defect. Define
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:parameters}
 r=\frac nm,\qquad \lambda=\frac m2e^{-r},\qquad
 R_{m,n}=\frac{A_{m,n}}{m!m^n},\qquad
 \theta=\frac{m-1}{2}\left(1-\frac1m\right)^n.
\end{equation}
The parameter $\theta$ is an exact coefficient; $\lambda$ is the more
convenient asymptotic coordinate. They must not be confused.
For $n=m(\log m+c)+o(m)$,
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:mainlimit}
 D\Longrightarrow\Poi(e^{-c}/2),\qquad
 R_{m,n}\longrightarrow\exp(e^{-c}/2).
\end{equation}
The first correction, with the actual $r=n/m$ and $\lambda$ of
\eqref{a122:pd:eq:parameters}, is
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:mainfirst}
 \log R_{m,n}=\lambda-
 \frac{(r/2+1)\lambda+(r/2+5/6)\lambda^2}{m}
 +O\!\left(\frac{(1+r)^2}{m^2}\right).
\end{equation}
The use of the actual $n$ retains the effect of integer rounding.
The all-orders theorem below makes \eqref{a122:pd:eq:mainfirst} part of a
systematic power--logarithmic expansion.

\subsection{Exact combinatorics and a finite-defect coefficient identity}
\label{a122:pd:sec:exact}
A structure counted by $A_{m,n}$ consists of an ordered partition of a
red $m$-element set into $k$ nonempty blocks, together with an arbitrary
map from a blue $n$-element set to those blocks. There are $k!S(m,k)$
ordered partitions and $k^n$ maps. Thus
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:law}
 \Prob(K=k)=\frac{k^n k!S(m,k)}{A_{m,n}}.
\end{equation}
With $u$ marking blocks, the bivariate exponential generating function is
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:egf}
 \sum_{m,n\ge0} A_{m,n}(u)\frac{x^m}{m!}\frac{y^n}{n!}
 =\frac1{1-u e^y(e^x-1)}.
\end{equation}
Here the extension at $m=0$ is $A_{0,0}=1$, $A_{0,n}=0$ for $n>0$.
Indeed, one block contains a nonempty red set and an arbitrary blue set,
so it contributes $u(e^x-1)e^y$; an ordered sequence of blocks gives the
geometric series in \eqref{a122:pd:eq:egf}.

Write
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:Qdef}
 w_j=\frac{(m-j)!S(m,m-j)}{m!}\left(1-\frac jm\right)^n,
 \qquad
 \cQ_{m,n}(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}w_jz^j.
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:Qlaw}
 w_0=1,\qquad \cQ_{m,n}(1)=R_{m,n},\qquad
 \E z^D=\frac{\cQ_{m,n}(z)}{\cQ_{m,n}(1)}.
\end{equation}
The denominator $m!m^n$ counts the structures whose red partition is all
singletons. Thus $\Prob(D=0)=R_{m,n}^{-1}$.

\begin{lemma}[Exact defect identity]\label{a122:pd:lem:defect}
Set
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:h}
 \psi(t)=\frac{e^t-1}{t}=\sum_{a\ge0}\frac{t^a}{(a+1)!}.
\end{equation}
For $0\le j\le m-1$,
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:dj}
 d_j(m):=\frac{(m-j)!S(m,m-j)}{m!}
 =\coeff{t}{j}\psi(t)^{m-j}.
\end{equation}
For fixed $j$, $d_j(m)$ is a polynomial in $m$ of degree $j$, with leading
coefficient $1/(2^j j!)$. In addition,
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:domination}
 0\le w_j\le \frac{\lambda^j}{j!},\qquad
 1\le R_{m,n}\le e^\lambda.
\end{equation}
These identities and inequalities also hold when the exponent $n$ is
replaced by any real $t\ge0$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The exponential generating function for $S(m,k)$ gives
$k!S(m,k)/m!=\coeff{t}{m}(e^t-1)^k$.
Put $k=m-j$ and factor out $t^{m-j}$. This proves \eqref{a122:pd:eq:dj}.
Expanding the power of $\psi$ gives the finite profile formula
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:profile}
 d_j(m)=
 \sum_{\substack{b_1,b_2,\ldots\ge0\\\sum a b_a=j}}
 \fall{m-j}{|b|}\prod_{a\ge1}
 \frac1{b_a!\,((a+1)!)^{b_a}},\qquad |b|=\sum_{a\ge1}b_a.
\end{equation}
Only finitely many $b_a$ are nonzero. The term $b_1=j$ has degree $j$;
all other terms have lower degree. This proves the polynomial assertion.

Since $(a+1)!\ge2^a$, coefficientwise comparison of power series with
nonnegative coefficients gives
\[
 \psi(t)\preceq(1-t/2)^{-1},\qquad
 d_j(m)\le 2^{-j}\binom{m-1}{j}\le\frac{m^j}{2^j j!}.
\]
Also $(1-j/m)^n\le e^{-nj/m}$ for real $n\ge0$.
Combining the two estimates proves the first part of
\eqref{a122:pd:eq:domination}; summing proves the second.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The coefficientwise domination in \eqref{a122:pd:eq:domination} is useful for
summation of asymptotic series: it controls every defect, including those
for which a fixed-$j$ expansion is inappropriate. It does not by itself
imply a Poisson approximation, because the normalization is $R_{m,n}$,
not $e^\lambda$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Real zeros and an exact independent-Bernoulli representation}
\label{a122:pd:sec:roots}
Define the block polynomial
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:blockpoly}
 \cP_{m,n}(u)=\sum_{k=1}^m k^n k!S(m,k)u^k.
\end{equation}
This section assumes that $n$ is a nonnegative integer.
The real-exponent continuation used later for inversion will not be
assumed to preserve real-rootedness.

\begin{theorem}[Root location and interlacing]\label{a122:pd:thm:roots}
For $m\ge1$ and integer $n\ge0$, $\cP_{m,n}$ has a simple zero at zero.
Its other $m-1$ zeros are simple and lie in $(-1,0)$.
Consequently there exist distinct numbers
$\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_{m-1}\in(0,1)$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:factor}
 \cP_{m,n}(u)=m!m^n\,u\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}(u+\alpha_i),\qquad
 \cQ_{m,n}(z)=\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}(1+\alpha_i z).
\end{equation}
As $n$ increases by one, the nonzero zeros strictly interlace and move
towards zero in their ordered positions.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $F_m(u)=\cP_{m,0}(u)$. The Stirling recurrence gives
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:Frec}
 F_{m+1}(u)=u\big((1+u)F_m(u)\big)'.
\end{equation}
The induction starts with $F_1(u)=u$. If $F_m$ has its simple zeros in
$(-1,0]$, with zero as its rightmost zero, then $(1+u)F_m$ has $m+1$
simple zeros in $[-1,0]$. Rolle's theorem places one zero of its
derivative in each of the $m$ intervening open intervals. The derivative
has degree $m$, so these are all its zeros and all are simple.
Multiplication by $u$ adds one simple zero at zero. This proves the
claim for $n=0$.

For general integer $n$,
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:Euler}
 \cP_{m,n+1}(u)=u\cP_{m,n}'(u).
\end{equation}
Apply the same Rolle argument to the $m$ simple zeros of
$\cP_{m,n}$ in $(-1,0]$. The derivative has exactly one zero between
successive zeros. Multiplication by $u$ restores the zero at zero.
This also proves the asserted direction of interlacing.
The leading coefficient of $\cP_{m,n}$ is $m!m^n$, which yields the
first factorization in \eqref{a122:pd:eq:factor}. Reversing coefficients yields
the second.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Bernoulli factorization]\label{a122:pd:cor:bernoulli}
There are independent variables $\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_{m-1}$ with
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:pi}
 \xi_i\sim\Ber(p_i),\qquad p_i=\frac{\alpha_i}{1+\alpha_i}\in(0,1/2),
\end{equation}
such that
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:Bernlaw}
 D\ \stackrel{\mathrm{law}}=\ \sum_{i=1}^{m-1} \xi_i.
\end{equation}
Equivalently, $K$ is one plus a sum of $m-1$ independent Bernoulli
variables with success probabilities in $(1/2,1)$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Divide the second product in \eqref{a122:pd:eq:factor} by its value at $z=1$:
\[
 \E z^D=\prod_i\frac{1+\alpha_i z}{1+\alpha_i}
       =\prod_i(1-p_i+p_i z).
\]
This is the probability generating function of the stated independent
sum.
\end{proof}

The independent variables in \eqref{a122:pd:eq:Bernlaw} are a distributional
representation; they are not claimed to be directly visible independent
choices in the original ordered partition. Nevertheless, the
representation is exact and immediately gives concentration,
log-concavity, and limit theorems. For example, the coefficient sequence
of $\cQ$ is ultra-log-concave: dividing $w_j$ by
$\binom{m-1}{j}$ gives a log-concave sequence. This follows from Newton's
inequalities for the elementary symmetric functions of the positive
$\alpha_i$. The main use below is quantitative rather than qualitative.

\subsection{Two coefficients give global finite-input bounds}
\label{a122:pd:sec:finite}
Let
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:thetaDelta}
 \theta=w_1,\qquad \Delta=w_1^2-2w_2,
\end{equation}
where $w_2=0$ if $m=2$. The case $m=1$ is deterministic and will be
omitted in statements involving these parameters. Directly from the
partitions with one or two defects,
\begin{align}
 \theta&=\frac{m-1}{2}\left(1-\frac1m\right)^n,\label{a122:pd:eq:w1}\\
 w_2&=\frac{(m-2)(3m-5)}{24}\left(1-\frac2m\right)^n.
 \label{a122:pd:eq:w2}
\end{align}
For \eqref{a122:pd:eq:w2} at $m=2,n=0$, use $0^0=1$; the zero prefactor still
makes $w_2=0$.

\begin{theorem}[Finite enumeration and approximation bounds]\label{a122:pd:thm:finite}
For integers $m\ge2$, $n\ge0$, put $M=\E D$ and $V=\Var(D)$. Then
\begin{align}
 &0\le\Delta\le
 \theta^2\min\left\{1,
 \frac{n+(5m-7)/3}{(m-1)^2}\right\},\label{a122:pd:eq:Deltabound}\\
 &\theta-\frac\Delta2\le\log R_{m,n}\le\theta,\label{a122:pd:eq:logRbound}\\
 &\max\{\theta/2,\theta-\Delta\}\le M\le\theta,\label{a122:pd:eq:meanbound}\\
 &\max\{\theta/4,\theta-2\Delta\}\le V\le\theta,\label{a122:pd:eq:varbound}\\
 &\TV\bigl(\mathcal L(D),\Poi(\theta)\bigr)
 \le\min\{1,2\Delta\}.\label{a122:pd:eq:TVbound}
\end{align}
Here $\TV(\mu,\nu)=\tfrac12\sum_j|\mu\{j\}-\nu\{j\}|$.
All constants in these inequalities are explicit; no asymptotic
threshold is involved.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The factorization gives
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:powersums}
 \theta=\sum_i\alpha_i,\qquad
 \Delta=\sum_i\alpha_i^2.
\end{equation}
Thus $\Delta\ge0$, and
\eqref{a122:pd:eq:w1}--\eqref{a122:pd:eq:w2} give the exact identity
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:Deltaratio}
 \frac{\Delta}{\theta^2}
 =1-\left(1-\frac{5m-7}{3(m-1)^2}\right)
       \left(1-\frac1{(m-1)^2}\right)^n.
\end{equation}
The two quantities subtracted from one lie in $[0,1]$.
The elementary inequality $1-(1-a)(1-b)^n\le a+nb$ proves
\eqref{a122:pd:eq:Deltabound}; the upper bound by one is immediate.

For $x\ge0$, $x-x^2/2\le\log(1+x)\le x$.
Summing this inequality over $x=\alpha_i$ proves
\eqref{a122:pd:eq:logRbound}. Also
\[
 M=\sum_i\frac{\alpha_i}{1+\alpha_i},\qquad
 V=\sum_i\frac{\alpha_i}{(1+\alpha_i)^2}.
\]
The restrictions $0<\alpha_i<1$ imply $\theta/2\le M\le\theta$
and $\theta/4\le V\le\theta$.
Moreover,
\[
 \theta-M=\sum_i\frac{\alpha_i^2}{1+\alpha_i}\le\Delta,
\]
and
\[
 \theta-V=\sum_i
 \frac{\alpha_i^2(2+\alpha_i)}{(1+\alpha_i)^2}\le2\Delta.
\]
This proves the moment bounds.

For completeness, the required elementary Poisson approximation is
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:elementaryLC}
 \TV\left(\mathcal L\Bigl(\sum_i\Ber(p_i)\Bigr),
                  \Poi\Bigl(\sum_i p_i\Bigr)\right)
 \le\sum_i p_i^2.
\end{equation}
For one variable the distance is
$p(1-e^{-p})\le p^2$. Replace factors one at a time in the convolution
of their laws; convolution with a probability law cannot increase total
variation. This proves \eqref{a122:pd:eq:elementaryLC}, the usual elementary
Le Cam bound \cite{a122:pd:lecam}. Since $\sum_i p_i^2\le\Delta$ and
$0\le\theta-M\le\Delta$, couple a $\Poi(M)$ variable with an
independent $\Poi(\theta-M)$ increment. The resulting Poisson laws have
distance at most $\theta-M$. The triangle inequality gives
\eqref{a122:pd:eq:TVbound}.
\end{proof}

\subsubsection{Replacing the exact parameter by the asymptotic one}
For all $m\ge2$ and $n\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:thetalam}
 0\le\lambda-\theta\le
 \lambda\left(\frac1m+\frac{n}{2m(m-1)}\right).
\end{equation}
Indeed,
\[
 \frac\theta\lambda=(1-1/m)
 \exp\!\left[-n\sum_{a\ge2}\frac1{a m^a}\right],\qquad
 \sum_{a\ge2}\frac1{a m^a}\le\frac1{2m(m-1)}.
\]
Use $1-e^{-x}\le x$. It follows that
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:TVlambda}
 \TV\bigl(\mathcal L(D),\Poi(\lambda)\bigr)
 \le2\Delta+\lambda-\theta.
\end{equation}
In particular, if $n=O(m\log m)$,
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:growingwindow}
 \TV\bigl(\mathcal L(D),\Poi(\lambda)\bigr)
 =O\!\left(\frac{(\lambda+\lambda^2)(1+r)}m\right).
\end{equation}
This is useful even when $\lambda$ grows. If
$\lambda^2(1+r)/m\to0$, with $n=O(m\log m)$ and $\lambda$ bounded
below by a positive constant, then
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:growingcount}
 R_{m,n}=e^\lambda(1+o(1)),\qquad
 \TV\bigl(\mathcal L(D),\Poi(\lambda)\bigr)=o(1).
\end{equation}
The count statement follows from
$|\log R_{m,n}-\lambda|\le\lambda-\theta+\Delta/2$.
Thus there is an explicit overlap region
$1\ll\lambda\ll\sqrt{m/\log m}$ in which a growing Poisson
approximation and a Gaussian approximation are simultaneously valid.
No assertion that this sufficient window is sharp is made.

\begin{writenote}[Added 5 October 2026, batch~98: the count half of the window is sharp]
The write proves that the condition $\lambda^2(1+r)/m\to0$ is also
\emph{necessary} for the count statement in
\eqref{a122:pd:eq:growingcount}; the total-variation half is not
settled and stays open (\cref{a122:pd:q:twindow}).

\emph{Claim.} Let $m\to\infty$ and let $n=n(m)\ge0$ be integers with
$\lambda\ge\lambda_0$ for a fixed $\lambda_0>0$. Then
$R_{m,n}e^{-\lambda}\to1$ if and only if $\lambda^2(1+r)/m\to0$.

\emph{Proof.} The condition $\lambda\ge\lambda_0$ gives
$r\le\log(m/(2\lambda_0))$, so $n=O(m\log m)$ and sufficiency is
\eqref{a122:pd:eq:growingcount}. For necessity, $f(x)=x-x^2/2+x^3/3-\log(1+x)$
has $f(0)=0$ and $f'(x)=x^3/(1+x)\ge0$, so
$\log(1+x)\le x-x^2/2+x^3/3$ for $x\ge0$. Sum over the $\alpha_i\in(0,1)$ of
\eqref{a122:pd:eq:factor}, using \eqref{a122:pd:eq:powersums} and
$\sum_i\alpha_i^3\le\sum_i\alpha_i^2=\Delta$:
$\log R_{m,n}\le\theta-\Delta/2+\Delta/3=\theta-\Delta/6$. With
$\theta\le\lambda$ from \eqref{a122:pd:eq:thetalam},
\[
 \log R_{m,n}-\lambda\le-\Delta/6.
\]
Next bound $\Delta$ below by \eqref{a122:pd:eq:Deltaratio}. Put
$a_m=(5m-7)/(3(m-1)^2)$ and $b_m=1/(m-1)^2$, both in $[0,1]$ for $m\ge2$.
Then $(1-a_m)(1-b_m)^n\le e^{-(a_m+nb_m)}$, and concavity of $1-e^{-z}$
gives $1-e^{-z}\ge(1-e^{-1})\min\{1,z\}$ for $z\ge0$. Moreover
$a_m\ge1/m$, because $(5m-7)m-3(m-1)^2=(2m-3)(m+1)\ge0$, and
$nb_m\ge n/m^2=r/m$. Hence, for all $m\ge2$ and $n\ge0$,
\[
 \Delta\ge(1-e^{-1})\,\theta^2\min\Bigl\{1,\frac{1+r}{m}\Bigr\}.
\]
Finally \eqref{a122:pd:eq:thetalam} gives
$\theta/\lambda\ge1-1/m-r/(2(m-1))\to1$, since $r=O(\log m)$; so
$\theta\ge\lambda/2$ for all large $m$. If $R_{m,n}e^{-\lambda}\to1$, then
$\Delta\to0$, hence $\lambda^2\min\{1,(1+r)/m\}\to0$; as
$\lambda\ge\lambda_0$, the minimum tends to zero, so it eventually equals
$(1+r)/m$ and $\lambda^2(1+r)/m\to0$. \qed

In particular, for every path with $\lambda\ge\lambda_0$ (where
$r=\log(m/(2\lambda))\le\log m+O(1)$), the count statement holds exactly
when $\lambda=o(\sqrt{m/\log m})$, the upper end of the overlap region
above. (Dated note, 5~October 2026: the first version of this sentence
read ``along $r=\log m+O(1)$ with $\lambda\to\infty$'', a regime that
cannot occur, since $r=\log m+O(1)$ forces $\lambda=\Theta(1)$. An
independent adversarial check made by the intake after the write proved
the corrected statement in both directions, found the necessity proof,
the degree bound $\deg_\ell\tau_h\le h+1$ (attained for $h\le5$) and
$\tau_2$ valid, and tested them against exact counts; a careful reading,
not a formal verification.)
\end{writenote}

\subsection{An all-direction Gaussian--Poisson--degenerate trichotomy}
\label{a122:pd:sec:phase}
We now let $m\to\infty$ through integers and allow any nonnegative
integer function $n=n(m)$. The only parameter that matters for the
qualitative regime is the exact first coefficient $\theta$.

\begin{theorem}[Global trichotomy]\label{a122:pd:thm:trichotomy}
With $D=D_{m,n(m)}$, the following statements hold.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)},itemsep=3pt]
\item If $\theta\to0$, then $D\to0$ in total variation, with
$\Prob(D\ne0)\le\theta$, and $R_{m,n}\to1$.
\item If $\theta\to\theta_0\in(0,\infty)$, then
$D\to\Poi(\theta_0)$ in total variation and
$R_{m,n}\to e^{\theta_0}$.
\item If $\theta\to\infty$, then $V\to\infty$ and
$(D-M)/\sqrt V\Longrightarrow N(0,1)$. In fact, the uniform local
and all-order Edgeworth assertions of \cref{a122:pd:thm:edgeworth} hold.
\end{enumerate}
Equivalently, put $c_m=n/m-\log m$. If $c_m\to+\infty$ the first
case applies; if $c_m\to c\in\R$ the second case has
$\theta_0=e^{-c}/2$; if $c_m\to-\infty$ the third case applies.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For (i), Markov's inequality gives
$\Prob(D\ne0)\le M\le\theta$, while
$0\le\log R_{m,n}\le\theta$.
If $\theta$ tends to a finite nonzero limit, the exact expression
\eqref{a122:pd:eq:w1} forces
$n=m\log m+O(m)$: solve its logarithm for $n$, using
$-\log(1-1/m)=m^{-1}+O(m^{-2})$.
Consequently \eqref{a122:pd:eq:Deltabound} gives
$\Delta=O(\log m/m)\to0$. Equations
\eqref{a122:pd:eq:TVbound} and \eqref{a122:pd:eq:logRbound} prove (ii).
For (iii), $V\ge\theta/4\to\infty$; the independent-Bernoulli
local theorem proved in the next section gives the conclusion.

For the equivalent description, $\lambda=e^{-c_m}/2$.
If $c_m\to+\infty$, then $\theta\le\lambda\to0$.
In the finite-$c_m$ case, \eqref{a122:pd:eq:thetalam} gives
$\theta/\lambda\to1$.
If $c_m\to-\infty$, then eventually $n\le m\log m$;
again \eqref{a122:pd:eq:thetalam} gives $\theta/\lambda\to1$, and
$\lambda\to\infty$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Centering in the dense Gaussian region]
The Gaussian statement is centered at the exact mean $M$ and scaled by
the exact variance $V$. Replacing both by $\theta$ without a further
error estimate would be incorrect in general. For example, the ordinary
diagonal $n=m$ lies well inside the Gaussian region but not inside the
small-$\Delta$ window. The bounds in \cref{a122:pd:thm:finite} deliberately
separate these situations.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Local limit laws and all-order Edgeworth expansions}
\label{a122:pd:sec:local}
This section proves the local theorem needed above without assuming a
fixed direction or a separate saddle-point representation. Let
$\kappa_a$ denote the cumulants of $D$, so that
\[
 \kappa_1=M,\qquad \kappa_2=V,\qquad
 \kappa_3=\sum_i p_i(1-p_i)(1-2p_i).
\]
Write
\[
 \varphi(x)=\frac{e^{-x^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}},\qquad
 H_a(x)=(-1)^a e^{x^2/2}\frac{\mathrm d^a}{\mathrm dx^a}e^{-x^2/2}
\]
for the Gaussian density and the probabilists' Hermite polynomials.

\begin{theorem}[Uniform lattice Edgeworth expansion]\label{a122:pd:thm:edgeworth}
For every fixed integer $J\ge0$, there is a constant $C_J$ independent
of $m,n$ such that, as $V\to\infty$,
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:edgeworth}
 \Prob(D=k)=\frac{\varphi(x)}{\sqrt V}
 \sum_{\substack{b_3,\ldots,b_{J+2}\ge0\\
              \sum_{a=3}^{J+2}(a-2)b_a\le J}}
 H_{\sum a b_a}(x)
 \prod_{a=3}^{J+2}\frac1{b_a!}
 \left(\frac{\kappa_a}{a! V^{a/2}}\right)^{b_a}
 +O_J\!\left(V^{-(J+2)/2}\right),
\end{equation}
uniformly for all integers $k$, where $x=(k-M)/\sqrt V$.
The empty multi-index contributes $1$. In particular,
\begin{align}
 \sup_{k\in\mathbb Z}\left|
 \sqrt V\,\Prob(D=k)-\varphi\!\left(\frac{k-M}{\sqrt V}\right)
 \right|&=O(V^{-1/2}),\label{a122:pd:eq:lclt}\\
 \Prob(D=k)&=\frac{\varphi(x)}{\sqrt V}
 \left[1+\frac{\kappa_3}{6V^{3/2}}(x^3-3x)\right]
 +O(V^{-3/2}).\label{a122:pd:eq:firstedge}
\end{align}
The same result applies to $K=m-D$ by reflection.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
We record the two ingredients that make the constants uniform across
this entire triangular family.
First, for real $t$,
\[
 |1-p+pe^{it}|^2=1-4p(1-p)\sin^2(t/2).
\]
Using $1-x\le e^{-x}$ and multiplying the independent factors gives
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:chardamp}
 \left|\E e^{it(D-M)}\right|
 \le\exp\{-2V\sin^2(t/2)\},\qquad |t|\le\pi.
\end{equation}
There is no competing lattice peak in $(-\pi,\pi)$.

Second, on a fixed small complex disk about zero,
\[
 \log(1+p(e^z-1))-pz
\]
is analytic uniformly for $0\le p\le1/2$.
After division by $p(1-p)$ it has a removable limit at $p=0$ and is
uniformly bounded on a still smaller disk. Cauchy's estimate therefore
implies, for every fixed $a\ge2$,
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:cumbound}
 |\kappa_a|\le C_a V.
\end{equation}
It also bounds the Taylor remainder of the cumulant series by
$C_A V|z|^{A+1}$ through any fixed order $A$.

Use lattice Fourier inversion and put $t=s/\sqrt V$:
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:fourier}
 \sqrt V\,\Prob(D=k)=\frac1{2\pi}
 \int_{-\pi\sqrt V}^{\pi\sqrt V}e^{-isx}
 \E e^{is(D-M)/\sqrt V}\dd s.
\end{equation}
For $|s|\le V^\varepsilon$, with $\varepsilon>0$ sufficiently small
for the fixed order $J$, expand
\[
 \E e^{is(D-M)/\sqrt V}
 =e^{-s^2/2}
 \exp\!\left\{\sum_{a\ge3}\frac{\kappa_a(is)^a}{a!V^{a/2}}\right\}.
\]
By \eqref{a122:pd:eq:cumbound}, the term indexed by a multi-index
$(b_a)$ has order at most $V^{-\frac12\sum(a-2)b_a}$ times a
fixed polynomial in $|s|$.
Retain exactly the indices of total grade at most $J$.
Taylor's theorem, the uniform analytic remainder bound, and a slight
weakening of the Gaussian exponent bound the integrated discarded part
by $O_J(V^{-(J+1)/2})$.
More explicitly, the local remainder is bounded by that power of $V$
times a fixed polynomial in $|s|$ times $e^{-c s^2}$ for an absolute
$c>0$, after shrinking $\varepsilon$ if necessary.

For $V^\varepsilon<|s|\le\delta\sqrt V$, a fixed sufficiently
small $\delta$ and \eqref{a122:pd:eq:chardamp} give a bound
$e^{-c s^2}$; its integral is smaller than every inverse power of $V$.
For $\delta\sqrt V\le|s|\le\pi\sqrt V$,
\eqref{a122:pd:eq:chardamp} gives an exponentially small integral.
Thus the retained polynomial--Gaussian integrals may be extended to the
whole real line with the same error.

Finally,
\[
 \frac1{2\pi}\int_{\R}e^{-isx}e^{-s^2/2}(is)^a\dd s
 =H_a(x)\varphi(x).
\]
Termwise integration gives \eqref{a122:pd:eq:edgeworth}; division by $\sqrt V$
produces its stated error. All estimates before integration use
$|e^{-isx}|=1$, so the error is uniform in $k$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The error in \eqref{a122:pd:eq:edgeworth} is an absolute error. It is not a
relative large-deviation estimate arbitrarily far into the tails.
Also, using exact cumulants is important: the theorem gives a uniform
expansion for any diverging-variance path $n(m)$ without first requiring
asymptotic formulas for those cumulants.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The critical-window expansion to every fixed order}
\label{a122:pd:sec:allorders}
The limit theorem \eqref{a122:pd:eq:mainlimit} does not quantify the logarithmic
finite-size corrections. We now construct them, including a uniform
remainder that justifies summing over all defects.
Throughout this section $r=n/m\ge0$ and
$\lambda=(m/2)e^{-r}$, but $n$ may be a nonnegative real number.

\subsubsection{A finite rational coefficient algorithm}
Use the convergent germ
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:logh}
 \Lambda(t)=\log\psi(t)=\sum_{a\ge1}\gamma_a t^a
 =\frac t2+\frac{t^2}{24}-\frac{t^4}{2880}
  +\frac{t^6}{181440}-\cdots.
\end{equation}
Thus $\gamma_1=1/2$, $\gamma_{2a}=B_{2a}/((2a)(2a)!)$, and
$\gamma_{2a+1}=0$ for $a\ge1$. These identities may also be obtained
by formal division and logarithm of \eqref{a122:pd:eq:h}; no knowledge of a
Bernoulli-number convention is needed by the construction.

For indeterminates $v,j,r$, set
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:E_h}
 \mathcal E_h(v,j,r)=2^{h+1}\gamma_{h+1}v^{h+1}
        -j2^h\gamma_h v^h-\frac{rj^{h+1}}{h+1},\qquad h\ge1.
\end{equation}
Define polynomials $\mathcal B_h(v,j,r)$ by
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:Brec}
 \mathcal B_0=1,\qquad
 \mathcal B_h=\frac1h\sum_{a=1}^h a\,\mathcal E_a\mathcal B_{h-a}.
\end{equation}
Equivalently, $\mathcal B_h$ is the coefficient of $\varepsilon^h$ in
$\exp(\sum_{a\ge1}\mathcal E_a\varepsilon^a)$.
Let $\cT_j$ be the linear map acting on powers of $v$ by
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:Tmap}
 \cT_j(v^a)=\fall{j}{a},
\end{equation}
leaving coefficients in $j,r$ untouched, and define
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:p_h}
 p_h(j,r)=\cT_j \mathcal B_h(v,j,r).
\end{equation}
Finally, define the Poisson transform
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:c_h}
 c_h(\lambda,r)=e^{-\lambda}
 \sum_{j\ge0}\frac{\lambda^j}{j!}p_h(j,r).
\end{equation}
This is a polynomial, not an infinite object that must be evaluated by
summation: replace each monomial $j^a$ in $p_h$ by the Touchard polynomial
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:touchard}
 \mathsf T_a(\lambda)=\sum_{b=0}^a S(a,b)\lambda^b.
\end{equation}
One has
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:degrees}
 \deg_j p_h\le2h,\quad \deg_r p_h\le h,\qquad
 \deg_\lambda c_h\le2h,\quad \deg_r c_h\le h.
\end{equation}
All coefficients are rational, and $p_0=c_0=1$.

\begin{theorem}[Complete boundary expansion]\label{a122:pd:thm:allorders}
Fix $0<\lambda_-<\lambda_+<\infty$, $Z>0$, and an integer $J\ge0$.
Uniformly for real $n\ge0$ with $\lambda\in[\lambda_-,\lambda_+]$ and for complex
$z$ with $|z|\le Z$,
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:Qexpansion}
 \cQ_{m,n}(z)=e^{\lambda z}
 \left\{\sum_{h=0}^J\frac{c_h(\lambda z,r)}{m^h}
 +O_{\lambda_\pm,Z,J}\!\left(\frac{(1+r)^{J+1}}{m^{J+1}}\right)\right\}.
\end{equation}
In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:Rexpansion}
 A_{m,n}=m!m^n e^\lambda
 \left\{\sum_{h=0}^J\frac{c_h(\lambda,r)}{m^h}
 +O_{\lambda_\pm,J}\!\left(\frac{(1+r)^{J+1}}{m^{J+1}}\right)\right\}.
\end{equation}
Here $r=\log(m/(2\lambda))=\log m+O(1)$.
The expansion can be differentiated any fixed number of times with
respect to $z$, with uniform remainders on smaller fixed disks.
\end{theorem}

\subsubsection{Formal derivation of the coefficient algorithm}
Put $\varepsilon=1/m$ and replace $t$ by $2\varepsilon v$ in
\eqref{a122:pd:eq:dj}. Then
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:G_j}
 G_j(\varepsilon):=j!(2\varepsilon)^j d_j(1/\varepsilon)
 =j!\coeff{v}{j}
 \exp\{(\varepsilon^{-1}-j)\Lambda(2\varepsilon v)\}.
\end{equation}
The apparent singularity at $\varepsilon=0$ is removable. Expanding the
exponent gives
\[
 (\varepsilon^{-1}-j)\Lambda(2\varepsilon v)
 =v+\sum_{h\ge1}
 (2^{h+1}\gamma_{h+1}v^{h+1}-j2^h\gamma_h v^h)\varepsilon^h.
\]
The exponential tilt contributes
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:tilt}
 (1-j\varepsilon)^{r/\varepsilon}e^{rj}
 =\exp\!\left\{-r\sum_{h\ge1}
           \frac{j^{h+1}\varepsilon^h}{h+1}\right\}.
\end{equation}
For any nonnegative integer $j$,
$j!\coeff{v}{j}e^v v^a=\fall{j}{a}$.
Combining \eqref{a122:pd:eq:G_j} and \eqref{a122:pd:eq:tilt} yields
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:fixeddefectformal}
 w_j\sim\frac{\lambda^j}{j!}
 \sum_{h\ge0}\frac{p_h(j,r)}{m^h}.
\end{equation}
The degree bounds follow directly from \eqref{a122:pd:eq:E_h}:
a term of grade $h$ has degree at most $2h$ after applying $\cT_j$,
and at most $h$ factors of $r$.
Summing \eqref{a122:pd:eq:fixeddefectformal} formally gives \eqref{a122:pd:eq:c_h}.
The next subsection supplies the missing uniform justification.

\subsubsection{Uniform control of the defect expansion}
\begin{lemma}[A polynomial Cauchy bound]\label{a122:pd:lem:Cauchy}
There are absolute positive constants $c,C$ such that, for every integer
$j\ge0$ and real $r\ge0$, the function
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:Fjr}
 F_{j,r}(\varepsilon)=G_j(\varepsilon)
 \exp\!\left\{-r\sum_{h\ge1}
       \frac{j^{h+1}\varepsilon^h}{h+1}\right\}
\end{equation}
is analytic and bounded in modulus by $C$ on
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:radius}
 |\varepsilon|\le\rho_{j,r}:=
 \frac{c}{(1+r)(1+j)^2}.
\end{equation}
Its Taylor coefficient of order $h$ is $p_h(j,r)$.
For $0\le\varepsilon\le\rho_{j,r}/2$,
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:localrem}
 \left|F_{j,r}(\varepsilon)-\sum_{h=0}^J
 p_h(j,r)\varepsilon^h\right|
 \le C_J\big((1+r)(1+j)^2\varepsilon\big)^{J+1}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The coefficient statement follows from the formal derivation, so it
suffices to obtain the analytic bound. For $j=0$ the function is one.
For $j\ge1$, multiply \eqref{a122:pd:eq:profile} by
$j!(2\varepsilon)^j$ and put $m=\varepsilon^{-1}$.
A profile with $|b|=\sum b_a$ contributes
\[
 \frac{j!}{\prod b_a!}
 \prod_{a\ge1}\left(\frac{2^a}{(a+1)!}\right)^{b_a}
 \varepsilon^{j-|b|}
 \prod_{g=0}^{|b|-1}(1-(j+g)\varepsilon).
\]
The last product has modulus at most $\exp(2j^2|\varepsilon|)$.
After fixing $b_a$ for $a\ge2$, the value
$b_1=j-\sum_{a\ge2}a b_a$ is forced. Use
$j!/b_1!\le j^{\sum_{a\ge2}a b_a}$ and then enlarge the sum by
allowing all choices of $b_a$ for $a\ge2$. This gives
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:Gbound}
 |G_j(\varepsilon)|\le
 \exp\!\left\{2j^2|\varepsilon|+
 \sum_{a\ge2}\frac{2^a}{(a+1)!}
           j^a|\varepsilon|^{a-1}\right\}.
\end{equation}
When $j|\varepsilon|\le1/4$, the sum in this exponent is bounded
by a constant times $j^2|\varepsilon|$.
The series in the second exponential of \eqref{a122:pd:eq:Fjr} is analytic
for $|j\varepsilon|<1$, and its modulus is bounded by
$C r j^2|\varepsilon|$ on the same disk.
Choosing $c$ sufficiently small in \eqref{a122:pd:eq:radius} bounds both
exponents by absolute constants. This proves the uniform analytic bound.
Cauchy's coefficient estimate and summation of a geometric tail at
$|\varepsilon|/\rho_{j,r}\le1/2$ give \eqref{a122:pd:eq:localrem}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{a122:pd:thm:allorders}]
Because $\lambda\in[\lambda_-,\lambda_+]$, one has $r=\log m+O_{\lambda_\pm}(1)$.
Split the defect sum at $j_*=\lfloor m^{1/4}\rfloor$.
For $j\le j_*$, the condition in \cref{a122:pd:lem:Cauchy} holds for all
sufficiently large $m$, uniformly in the parameter range. Multiply
\eqref{a122:pd:eq:localrem} by $|\lambda z|^j/j!$ and sum. Since
\[
 \sum_{j\ge0}\frac{(\lambda_+Z)^j}{j!}(1+j)^{2J+2}<\infty,
\]
the total remainder is
$O_{\lambda_\pm,Z,J}((1+r)^{J+1}m^{-J-1})$.

For $j>j_*$, \eqref{a122:pd:eq:domination} bounds the original terms by
$(\lambda_+Z)^j/j!$. This tail is smaller than every fixed inverse power of $m$.
The tails of the approximating terms have the same property: by
\eqref{a122:pd:eq:degrees} they are bounded by a fixed power of $j$ and of
$1+r$ times $(\lambda_+Z)^j/j!$. Thus each truncated sum may be extended to
all $j\ge0$. Formula \eqref{a122:pd:eq:c_h} then proves
\eqref{a122:pd:eq:Qexpansion}. Factoring out $e^{\lambda z}$ does not change
the order of the uniform error, since its reciprocal is bounded on the
fixed disk. Setting $z=1$ proves \eqref{a122:pd:eq:Rexpansion}.
Finally, apply Cauchy's derivative estimate to a slightly larger disk
to obtain the differentiated statements.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Meaning of ``all orders'']\label{a122:pd:rem:allorders}
For every fixed $J$ there is a proved remainder of the indicated order.
The article does not assert convergence of the infinite series in $h$,
a uniform bound as $J\to\infty$, Borel summability, or an exponentially
improved remainder. After substituting
$r=\log m-\log(2\lambda)$, the result is a complete
power--logarithmic, or perturbative transseries, expansion. Exponentially
small sectors have not been identified here.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Explicit corrections and the geometry of rare defects}
\label{a122:pd:sec:corrections}
The first two defect polynomials are
\begin{align}
 p_1(j,r)&=-\frac{(3r+5)j^2+j}{6},\label{a122:pd:eq:p1}\\
 p_2(j,r)&=\frac{j}{72}\left[
 (9r^2+30r+25)j^3-(18r+18)j^2-j-6\right].\label{a122:pd:eq:p2}
\end{align}
It is shorter to print the logarithmic count coefficients. Define
$L_h(\lambda,r)$ by
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:Lrec}
 L_1=c_1,\qquad
 L_h=c_h-\frac1h\sum_{a=1}^{h-1}aL_a c_{h-a}\quad(h\ge2).
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:logexp}
 \log R_{m,n}=\lambda+\sum_{h=1}^J\frac{L_h(\lambda,r)}{m^h}
 +O\!\left(\frac{(1+r)^{J+1}}{m^{J+1}}\right).
\end{equation}
The first two are
\begin{align}
 L_1={}&-\left(\frac r2+1\right)\lambda
       -\left(\frac r2+\frac56\right)\lambda^2,\label{a122:pd:eq:L1}\\
 L_2={}&\left(\frac{r^2}{8}+\frac r6\right)\lambda
       +\left(\frac{3r^2}{4}+\frac{5r}{3}+\frac76\right)\lambda^2
       +\left(\frac{r^2}{2}+\frac{4r}{3}+1\right)\lambda^3.
       \label{a122:pd:eq:L2}
\end{align}
Thus $c_1=L_1$, $c_2=L_2+L_1^2/2$.
The third logarithmic coefficient is printed in \cref{a122:pd:app:third}; the
package supplies exact coefficients through the fourth order and an
algorithm for any prescribed finite order.

\subsubsection{The full defect law, not just the total count}
The complex-marked expansion yields, on any fixed disk and for large
$m$, a branch of the logarithm with
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:pgflog}
 \log\E z^D=\lambda(z-1)+\sum_{h=1}^J
 \frac{L_h(\lambda z,r)-L_h(\lambda,r)}{m^h}
 +O\!\left(\frac{(1+r)^{J+1}}{m^{J+1}}\right).
\end{equation}
To see that the logarithm is legitimate, \eqref{a122:pd:eq:Qexpansion} shows
uniformly that $\cQ_{m,n}(z)e^{-\lambda z}=1+o(1)$ on that disk.
In particular the polynomial is zero-free there for sufficiently large
$m$. The logarithm near one is then unambiguously continued across the
disk after factoring out $e^{\lambda z}$.

For every fixed defect $j$,
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:pmfcorrection}
 \Prob(D=j)=e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^j}{j!}
 \left[1+\frac{p_1(j,r)-c_1(\lambda,r)}m
 +O_j\!\left(\frac{(1+r)^2}{m^2}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
This is a coefficient statement, whereas \eqref{a122:pd:eq:pgflog} simultaneously
encodes factorial moments and cumulants.
Putting $z=e^s$ and differentiating at $s=0$ gives
\begin{align}
 M={}&\lambda-
 \frac{(r/2+1)\lambda+(r+5/3)\lambda^2}{m}
 +O\!\left(\frac{(1+r)^2}{m^2}\right),\label{a122:pd:eq:Mcorrection}\\
 V={}&\lambda-
 \frac{(r/2+1)\lambda+(2r+10/3)\lambda^2}{m}
 +O\!\left(\frac{(1+r)^2}{m^2}\right).\label{a122:pd:eq:Vcorrection}
\end{align}
In particular,
\[
 M-V=\frac{(r+5/3)\lambda^2}{m}
 +O\!\left(\frac{(1+r)^2}{m^2}\right)>0
\]
for large $m$ in a compact critical window. The exact inequality
$M>V$ already follows from the Bernoulli representation; the expansion
quantifies the first departure from Poisson variance.

\subsubsection{Why the limiting defects are pairs}
A red partition of defect $j$ whose blocks have size at most two has
exactly $j$ doubletons and $m-2j$ singletons. Its contribution to $d_j(m)$
is
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:pairterm}
 d_j^{\mathrm{pair}}(m)=\frac{\fall{m-j}{j}}{2^j j!},
\end{equation}
with the value zero when there are too few elements. For fixed $j$,
\[
 j!\left(\frac2m\right)^j d_j^{\mathrm{pair}}(m)
 =1-\frac{j(3j-1)}{2m}+O_j(m^{-2}).
\]
The corresponding full coefficient at $r=0$ is
$1-(5j^2+j)/(6m)+O_j(m^{-2})$ by \eqref{a122:pd:eq:p1}.
The difference is $2j(j-1)/(3m)$.

\begin{proposition}[First non-pair defect]\label{a122:pd:prop:triples}
Uniformly for $\lambda$ in a compact subset of $(0,\infty)$, the
probability that the red partition contains a block of size at least
three is
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:tripleprob}
 \Prob(\text{some red block has size at least }3)
 =\frac{2\lambda^2}{3m}
 +O\!\left(\frac{(1+r)^2}{m^2}\right).
\end{equation}
Consequently, with probability tending to one, every non-singleton red
block is a doubleton; their number converges to $\Poi(\lambda_0)$
when $\lambda\to\lambda_0\in(0,\infty)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Subtract the pair-only coefficients from the full coefficients, multiply
by the common tilt, and use the first-order difference just computed.
The same profile-product estimate used in \cref{a122:pd:lem:Cauchy} applies to
the pair-only term (it is simply a finite product). Thus the difference
can be summed uniformly over $j$, with a remainder of the order displayed
in \eqref{a122:pd:eq:tripleprob}. Its unnormalized leading sum is
\[
 \frac2{3m}\sum_{j\ge0}\frac{\lambda^j}{j!}j(j-1)
 =e^\lambda\frac{2\lambda^2}{3m}.
\]
Divide by $R_{m,n}=e^\lambda(1+O((1+r)/m))$.
The second statement follows because on the pair-only event the number
of doubletons equals $D$.
\end{proof}

This identifies the combinatorial origin of the factor $1/2$ in the
critical Poisson parameter: a single red collision is a pair, with
exponential generating-function weight $1/2!$. Triples first contribute
one order later.

\subsection{Inverting the transition: a prescribed singleton probability}
\label{a122:pd:sec:inverse}
A useful inverse problem is not to invert the already studied diagonal
count, but to ask how many blue labels are needed to make the all-singleton
red partition have a prescribed probability. This turns the boundary
expansion into an operational threshold formula.

For integer $m\ge2$ and real $t\ge0$, use the exact continuation
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:Rreal}
 R_m(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}d_j(m)(1-j/m)^t,
 \qquad q_m(t)=R_m(t)^{-1}.
\end{equation}
The term $j=0$ equals one and all the others are strictly decreasing,
so $q_m$ is continuous and strictly increasing, with limit one as
$t\to\infty$. For every fixed $q\in(0,1)$, a unique solution
$q_m(t_q)=q$ exists for all sufficiently large $m$.
Indeed, $R_m(0)\ge1+(m-1)/2\to\infty$.

\begin{theorem}[Inverse critical transseries]\label{a122:pd:thm:inverse}
Fix $q$ in a compact subinterval of $(0,1)$ and put
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:betaell}
 \beta=-\log q,\qquad \ell=\log\frac{m}{2\beta}.
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:inverse2}
 t_q=m\ell+\tau_0(\beta,\ell)+\frac{\tau_1(\beta,\ell)}m
 +O\!\left(\frac{(1+\ell)^3}{m^2}\right),
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align}
 \tau_0(\beta,\ell)={}&-\frac{1+\beta}{2}\ell-1-\frac{5\beta}{6},
 \label{a122:pd:eq:d0}\\
 \tau_1(\beta,\ell)={}&
 \beta^2\left(\frac{\ell^2}{8}+\frac\ell3+\frac38\right)
 +\beta\left(\frac{\ell^2}{4}+\frac\ell3+\frac5{12}\right)
 -\frac\ell{12}.\label{a122:pd:eq:d1}
\end{align}
More generally, for every fixed $J\ge1$ there are effectively
computable polynomials $\tau_0,\ldots,\tau_{J-1}\in\mathbb Q[\beta,\ell]$
such that
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:inverseall}
 t_q=m\ell+\sum_{h=0}^{J-1}\frac{\tau_h(\beta,\ell)}{m^h}
 +O_J\!\left(\frac{(1+\ell)^{J+1}}{m^J}\right).
\end{equation}
The error is uniform over the stated compact set of target probabilities.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The critical-window expansion holds for the real exponent $t$, by
\cref{a122:pd:thm:allorders}. It first locates the root at
$t=m\ell+O(\log m)$: moving $t/m$ by a fixed small constant on either
side of $\ell$ moves the limiting value of $\log R_m(t)$ to either
side of $\beta$, and the first correction then refines the displacement
to $O(\log m)$.

Put $\varepsilon=1/m$ and write
$r=t/m=\ell+\tau_0\varepsilon+\tau_1\varepsilon^2+\cdots$.
Then
$\lambda=\beta\exp[-(r-\ell)]$.
Insert these expressions into
\[
 \log R_m(t)=\lambda+\varepsilon L_1(\lambda,r)
                  +\varepsilon^2L_2(\lambda,r)+\cdots
\]
and set the result equal to $\beta$.
The coefficient of $\varepsilon$ gives
\[
 \tau_0=\frac{L_1(\beta,\ell)}\beta,
\]
which is \eqref{a122:pd:eq:d0}. The next coefficient gives
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:d1algorithm}
 \tau_1=\frac{\tau_0^2}{2}
 +\frac{\tau_0}{\beta}
  (\partial_rL_1-\beta\partial_\lambda L_1)(\beta,\ell)
 +\frac{L_2(\beta,\ell)}\beta.
\end{equation}
Simplification using \eqref{a122:pd:eq:L1}--\eqref{a122:pd:eq:L2} gives
\eqref{a122:pd:eq:d1}.

For the general construction, each $c_h(0,r)$ and $L_h(0,r)$ vanishes
for $h\ge1$, because $p_h(0,r)=0$.
Define polynomial coefficients $\mathcal M_h(\lambda,r)$ by
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:Mh}
 \log\left(1+\sum_{h\ge1}\frac{L_h(\lambda,r)}\lambda
                     \varepsilon^h\right)
 =\sum_{h\ge1}\mathcal M_h(\lambda,r)\varepsilon^h.
\end{equation}
After taking the logarithm of $\log R_m(t)=\beta$, the formal inverse
equation becomes
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:inversefixed}
 r=\ell+\sum_{h\ge1}
 \mathcal M_h\bigl(\beta e^{-(r-\ell)},r\bigr)\varepsilon^h.
\end{equation}
This is triangular in powers of $\varepsilon$: its coefficient of
order $h$ determines the next coefficient of $r$ using only earlier
ones. It uses only polynomial operations and finite exponential series,
so the resulting $\tau_h$ lie in $\mathbb Q[\beta,\ell]$.
The degree bounds inherited from \eqref{a122:pd:eq:degrees} show inductively
that $\tau_h$ has degree at most $h+1$ in $\ell$.

\begin{writenote}[Added 5 October 2026, batch~98: proof of the degree bound]
The manuscript only sketches this induction, and the error term
$(1+\ell)^{J+1}$ of \eqref{a122:pd:eq:inverseall} relies on it. A proof:
by \eqref{a122:pd:eq:degrees}, $\deg_rc_h\le h$; by induction on
\eqref{a122:pd:eq:Lrec}, $L_h$ is a sum of products
$c_{h_1}\cdots c_{h_k}$ with $h_1+\dots+h_k=h$, so $\deg_rL_h\le h$; each
$L_h/\lambda$ is a polynomial (as $L_h(0,r)=0$), and by
\eqref{a122:pd:eq:Mh} $\mathcal M_h$ is a sum of products of
$L_{h_1}/\lambda,\ldots,L_{h_k}/\lambda$ with $h_1+\dots+h_k=h$, so
$\deg_r\mathcal M_h\le h$. Let $\mathcal A$ be the set of formal series
$\sum_{k\ge1}P_k\varepsilon^k$ with $P_k\in\mathbb Q[\beta,\ell]$ and
$\deg_\ell P_k\le k$. It is closed under sums and products, it contains
$\varepsilon$ and $\varepsilon\ell$, and $e^{-i\omega}-1\in\mathcal A$
whenever $\omega\in\mathcal A$ and $i\ge0$. For $\omega\in\mathcal A$ let
$\Phi(\omega)=\sum_{h\ge1}\mathcal M_h(\beta e^{-\omega},\ell+\omega)\varepsilon^h$.
A monomial $\lambda^ir^k$ of $\mathcal M_h$, $k\le h$, contributes
$\beta^ie^{-i\omega}\,\varepsilon^{h-k}(\varepsilon\ell+\varepsilon\omega)^k$,
which lies in $\mathcal A$ (for $k=0$ use $h\ge1$); so
$\Phi(\mathcal A)\subseteq\mathcal A$. Every term of $\Phi(\omega)$ carries
a factor $\varepsilon$ beyond the orders of $\omega$ it involves, so the
coefficient of $\varepsilon^k$ in $\Phi(\omega)$ depends only on the
coefficients of $\omega$ of order below $k$. Hence the solution
$\omega=r-\ell$ of \eqref{a122:pd:eq:inversefixed} is the
$\varepsilon$-adic limit of $\omega_0=0$, $\omega_{k+1}=\Phi(\omega_k)$,
whose terms agree with $\omega$ through order $k$ and lie in
$\mathcal A$; so $\omega\in\mathcal A$. Since
$\omega=\sum_{h\ge0}\tau_h\varepsilon^{h+1}$, $\deg_\ell\tau_h\le h+1$.
\qed

A symbolic check of the write (formal inversion of the shipped $L_1,\dots,L_4$
of \nolinkurl{data/02-poisson-defects-coefficients.json}, SymPy~1.14.0)
reproduces \eqref{a122:pd:eq:d0}--\eqref{a122:pd:eq:d1} and finds
$\deg_\ell\tau_h=\deg_\beta\tau_h=h+1$ for $h=0,1,2,3$, with
\[
\begin{aligned}
 \tau_2=-\tfrac{1}{6480}\bigl[&(540\ell^3+1755\ell^2+2700\ell+1601)\beta^3
 +(1890\ell^3+3375\ell^2+4050\ell+1665)\beta^2\\
 &+(540\ell^3-1350\ell^2-270\ell-450)\beta+270\ell\bigr].
\end{aligned}
\]
At $q=e^{-1}$ it lowers the last column of \cref{a122:pd:tab:inverse} from

\end{writenote}

It remains to turn the formal calculation into a root estimate. A
truncation through $\tau_{J-1}$ has a residual in $\log R_m(t)-\beta$
of size $O_J((1+\ell)^{J+1}m^{-J-1})$, by the direct expansion and
Taylor's theorem on a fixed compact critical window. On this window,
\[
 -\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\log R_m(t)
 =\frac{\sum_j d_j(m)(1-j/m)^t[-\log(1-j/m)]}{R_m(t)}
 \ge\frac{w_1}{mR_m(t)}\ge\frac{c_q}{m}
\]
for a positive constant uniform over the target compact set.
Here $w_1$ is bounded away from zero and $R_m(t)$ stays bounded
on the critical window; at the root, $w_1\to\beta$.
The mean value theorem therefore multiplies the residual by at most
$O(m)$, proving \eqref{a122:pd:eq:inverseall} and \eqref{a122:pd:eq:inverse2}.
\end{proof}

\subsubsection{Integer thresholds and the rounding obstruction}
For the original counting problem, the relevant threshold is
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:intthreshold}
 N_q(m)=\min\{n\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}:\Prob(D_{m,n}=0)\ge q\}
       =\lceil t_q\rceil.
\end{equation}
The equality is exact by strict monotonicity of $q_m(t)$.
An asymptotic approximation to $t_q$ with error tending to zero does
not by itself justify taking its ceiling: the exact root may lie closer
to an integer than the error.
If a rigorously bounded error is $\eta$, the valid envelope is
\[
 \lceil \widetilde t_q-\eta\rceil\le N_q(m)
 \le\lceil\widetilde t_q+\eta\rceil.
\]
For sufficiently large $m$ the two sides differ by at most one if $\eta<1/2$.
\begin{writenote}[Added 5 October 2026, batch~98]
The qualifier ``for sufficiently large $m$'' is superfluous: the two
arguments differ by $2\eta<1$, and the ceilings of two reals less than
one apart differ by at most one, for every $m$. What needs large $m$, or
explicit constants (\cref{a122:pd:q:formal}), is a rigorous $\eta<1/2$.
\end{writenote}
The asymptotic constants in \cref{a122:pd:thm:inverse} have not been optimized
into a certified finite-input cutoff. For rational $q$, an exact
comparison of integers in \eqref{a122:pd:eq:law} decides any candidate boundary;
for a nonrational specified target, a certified representation or
interval evaluation of that target is additionally required.

\subsection{A new local conclusion on the original OEIS diagonal}
\label{a122:pd:sec:diagonal}
The boundary analysis concerns a two-parameter extension rather than a
new OEIS identification. The local-limit result also gives an immediate
conclusion for the original sequence itself.
The prior report \cite{a122:pd:repo} defines positive constants $x,y$ by
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:diagsaddle}
 x=\log(1+e^{-y}),\qquad x=\frac{y}{1+e^y},\qquad B=2-y+x,
\end{equation}
and proves
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:diagmoments}
 \E K_{n,n}=\mu n+\mu_0+O(n^{-1}),\qquad
 \Var(K_{n,n})=\sigma^2n+O(1),
\end{equation}
where
\[
 \mu=\frac1y=0.8737024332396683\ldots,\qquad
 \sigma^2=\frac{B-1}{By^2}=0.0889169868271170\ldots,
\]
and $\mu_0=0.1445656885777610\ldots$.
These numerical constants are quoted from the earlier manuscript and
are not new numerical discoveries here.

\begin{corollary}[Diagonal local limit theorem]\label{a122:pd:cor:diaglclt}
Let $K_n$ have law
\[
 \Prob(K_n=k)=\frac{k^n k!S(n,k)}{a_n}.
\]
Then, uniformly for all integers $k$,
\begin{equation}\label{a122:pd:eq:diaglclt}
 \Prob(K_n=k)=\frac1{\sigma\sqrt n}
 \varphi\!\left(\frac{k-\mu n-\mu_0}{\sigma\sqrt n}\right)
 +O(n^{-1}).
\end{equation}
All higher local corrections can be generated from exact cumulants by
\cref{a122:pd:thm:edgeworth}; combining that theorem with the analytic marked
expansion of \cite{a122:pd:repo} converts them to fixed diagonal constants.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
On the diagonal,
$\theta=(n-1)(1-1/n)^n/2\sim n/(2e)$, hence the variance diverges.
Apply \eqref{a122:pd:eq:lclt} to $K_n=n-D$ using its exact mean and variance.
Substitute \eqref{a122:pd:eq:diagmoments}; the change in the Gaussian density,
uniformly in its argument, is $O(n^{-3/2})$ from the variance replacement
and smaller from the stated mean replacement. This is absorbed by the
$O(n^{-1})$ local error.
\end{proof}

For clarity, the local theorem with exact moments does not depend on the
earlier saddle proof. Only the explicit replacement of the moments by
$\mu n+\mu_0$ and $\sigma^2n$ invokes \cite{a122:pd:repo}.
This separates the new finite-polynomial argument from inherited
asymptotic input.

\begin{writenote}[Added 5 October 2026, batch~98]
The proof establishes \eqref{a122:pd:eq:diaglclt} only. The corollary's
last sentence, that the higher local corrections can be converted to
fixed diagonal constants via Part~I's marked expansion (6.1), is a
one-line sketch: Part~I states (6.1) and (6.2) to first order only, and
the bookkeeping that turns exact cumulants into constants is not carried
out. It is recorded as open in \cref{a122:pd:q:diagedge}. This
corollary answers Part~I's Question~4 in part (local limit theorem and,
at exact cumulants, Edgeworth terms; no large deviations).
\end{writenote}

\begin{question}[The sharp Poisson window]\label{a122:pd:q:poisson}
Determine the widest regime in which the defect law is close in total
variation to a Poisson law with its exact mean. The bound
$2\Delta$ is simple and explicit but loses a possible large-mean
improvement. Can one give necessary and sufficient conditions in terms
of $m,n$, and obtain a sharp leading constant for the distance?
\end{question}
A plausible route is to combine sharper Bernoulli--Poisson estimates with
asymptotics of the root power sums $\sum_i\alpha_i^a$. This must be
kept separate from the stronger requirement
$R_{m,n}/e^\lambda\to1$, which concerns the count normalization.

\begin{question}[The connected-degree pattern]\label{a122:pd:q:degree}
The generated logarithmic coefficients satisfy
$\deg_\lambda L_h=h+1$ for $1\le h\le4$, although the general theorem
only proves the upper bound $2h$. Prove or refute
\[
 \deg_\lambda L_h\le h+1\qquad(h\ge1)
\]
and identify a connected combinatorial interpretation of their highest
powers of $\lambda$.
\end{question}
Such a result could greatly enlarge the useful growing-$\lambda$
window of the logarithmic expansion. Finite verification through order
four is not a proof of the pattern.

\begin{question}[A uniform bridge to the interior saddle]\label{a122:pd:q:bridge}
Construct a single count approximation with controlled relative error
that connects the present finite-defect regime, the growing-defect
Poisson region, and the fixed-positive-direction saddle expansion of
\cite{a122:pd:repo}. Determine the correct rescaled special function, if any,
when the Gaussian saddle approaches the endpoint.
\end{question}
The all-direction local theorem already controls the distribution at its
exact moments; what remains here is explicit asymptotic evaluation of
the count and of those moments across the whole transition.

\begin{question}[The zero distribution and real exponent continuation]\label{a122:pd:q:zeros}
For $n/m\to r_0\ge0$, find a limiting empirical measure of the
$\alpha_i$ and derive root-density formulas for the cumulants.
Separately, classify real $t\ge0$ for which
$\sum_k k^t k!S(m,k)u^k$ remains real-rooted.
\end{question}
The proof by repeated Euler differentiation applies only to integer
$t$. The inverse theorem does not assume an affirmative answer for real
$t$, so this issue is an extension rather than a hidden dependency.

\end{writenote}

\begin{question}[Beyond every algebraic order]\label{a122:pd:q:beyond}
Determine the large-order growth of $c_h$ and $L_h$, the possible optimal
truncation scale, and the exponentially small sectors associated with
noncentral complex singularities. Can the inverse expansion be made
exponentially accurate with a corresponding Stokes analysis?
\end{question}
Nothing in the present fixed-order Cauchy argument supplies those
conclusions. A solution should distinguish genuine complex-saddle
contributions from artificial contour endpoints.

\begin{question}[A formal and effective proof layer]\label{a122:pd:q:formal}
Formalize the exact Stirling recurrences, root interlacing, Bernoulli
factorization, and finite-input inequalities in Lean. Then replace the
existence constants in the boundary and inverse remainder theorems by
explicit computable bounds, sufficiently sharp to certify integer
thresholds directly.
\end{question}
The finite part requires only polynomial algebra, Rolle's theorem,
finite probability, and elementary inequalities. It is a natural first
formalization target before the all-order analytic estimates.

\begin{question}[{[write]} The total-variation half of the growing window]\label{a122:pd:q:twindow}
Source: the last sentence of \cref{a122:pd:sec:finite}, ``No assertion
that this sufficient window is sharp is made''. Let $\lambda\ge\lambda_0>0$
grow with $m$. Find necessary and sufficient conditions for
$\TV(\mathcal L(D),\Poi(\lambda))\to0$.
\end{question}
What is known: \eqref{a122:pd:eq:growingwindow} gives sufficiency of
$\lambda^2(1+r)/m\to0$, and the write's note in
\cref{a122:pd:sec:finite} shows that this condition is \emph{necessary}
for the count statement $R_{m,n}e^{-\lambda}\to1$. For the distance it is
probably not: a heuristic sketch, not proved here, combines the
Barbour--Hall bound \cite{a122:pd:barbourhall}
$\TV(\mathcal L(D),\Poi(M))\le\Delta/M$ (up to the
factor $1-e^{-M}$), which is of order $\lambda(1+r)/m$, with the distance
between $\Poi(M)$ and $\Poi(\lambda)$, of order
$|\lambda-M|/\sqrt\lambda\lesssim\lambda^{3/2}(1+r)/m$ by
\eqref{a122:pd:eq:thetalam} and $\theta-M\le\Delta$. That suggests that
$\lambda^{3/2}(1+r)/m\to0$ suffices, and perhaps is necessary, since the
mean mismatch is then larger than a standard deviation. Missing: a
checked statement of the Barbour--Hall bound and of the Poisson--Poisson
distance with constants, and a matching lower bound. This sharpens the
question of the window in Question~\ref{a122:pd:q:poisson}, which asks
about the exact-mean Poisson law.

\begin{question}[{[write]} Fixed-constant Edgeworth terms on the diagonal]\label{a122:pd:q:diagedge}
Source: the last sentence of \cref{a122:pd:cor:diaglclt}, which asserts
without proof that the higher local corrections of
\cref{a122:pd:thm:edgeworth} can be converted to fixed diagonal constants
by Part~I's marked expansion. Prove this, and compute the first
correction explicitly.
\end{question}
The source's sketch: Part~I extends its saddle expansion uniformly to a
small complex disk of the marking variable $s$ and allows its remainder
to be differentiated by Cauchy estimates (its Section~6). Carried to every
fixed order, this would give
$\kappa_a(K_n)=n(\log D)^{(a)}(0)+(\log C)^{(a)}(0)+O(n^{-1})$ and finer
expansions, with Part~I's $D(s)$ and $C(s)$; inserting them in
\eqref{a122:pd:eq:edgeworth} and re-expanding the Gaussian factor about
$(k-\mu n-\mu_0)/(\sigma\sqrt n)$ would express each correction through
fixed constants. Missing: Part~I states (6.1) and (6.2) only to first
order and computes no derivative of $\log D(s)$ beyond the second; the
all-order version in $s$ and the bookkeeping are not written. Without
them \eqref{a122:pd:eq:diaglclt} is what is proved.

\subsection{Conclusion}
The same array that has a fixed-direction saddle expansion also has a
simple endpoint geometry. The exact block polynomial exposes that
geometry: all of its nonzero zeros lie in $(-1,0)$, so missing blocks form
an independent-Bernoulli sum. Two coefficients control its Poisson
distance and count normalization, while the variance alone controls its
local Gaussian expansion. At $n=m(\log m+c)$, finitely many pair defects
survive; their Poisson parameter is $e^{-c}/2$.

The finite-defect identity then produces a complete, uniformly summable
power--logarithmic expansion. Its inversion gives the number of blue
labels needed for a prescribed all-singleton probability, including the
logarithmic correction and every further algebraic order. This extends
the inspected ProveIt report in directions it explicitly left outside
its scope, while retaining a clear boundary between proved fixed-order
results, numerical diagnostics, inherited diagonal constants, and
questions about sharp uniformity or beyond-all-orders behavior.
