## Exact coefficients and contour

For \(n\ge1\), the Mittag-Leffler series

\[
\frac1{n!}\sum_{k\ge1}k^n e^{-kh}
=\sum_{j\in\mathbb Z}(h+2\pi i j)^{-n-1}
\]

is absolutely and uniformly convergent on the compact segment \(h=c-it\), \(-\pi\le t\le\pi\). Its terms have a uniform summable \(O(|j|^{-n-1})\) majorant. Consequently the interchange with the finite coefficient contour is legitimate even at \(n=1\).

For \(|q|=e^{-c}<1\), the map \(x=\Log(1+q)\) is analytic and injective on a neighborhood of the closed disk, has its only zero at \(q=0\), and maps the circle to a contour winding once around zero. The transformed Jacobian is exactly

\[
\frac{q}{1+q}=p(h).
\]

The orientation reversal from \(h=c-it\) is canceled on rewriting the joined intervals upward. This yields the first identity (2.1) with the stated positive normalization. No term of the Mittag-Leffler sum has been incorrectly treated as single-valued at \(x=0\).

On \(\Re z=c>0\), the logarithm is the principal analytic branch on the disk centered at 1 of radius \(e^{-c}<1\). It has no zero there. Both \(p(z)\) and \(X(z)^{-1}\) are bounded on the entire line by periodicity and compactness. The first integral is therefore absolutely convergent for \(n\ge1\), and the integration-by-parts boundary term vanishes. Since

\[
(X^{-m})'=mpX^{-m-1},
\]

the second identity's factor \((n+1)/m\) is correct.

The global bound

\[
|X(c+it)|\ge\log(1+e^{-c})
\]

follows directly from \(|e^w-1|\le e^{|w|}-1\). In particular, it does not require a false assertion about the real part of the logarithm. With \(v_0=\sqrt{c^2+T^2}\), the tail estimate is

\[
\int_T^\infty(c^2+t^2)^{-(n+2)/2}\,dt
\le\frac{v_0^{-n}}{(n+1)T}.
\]

The resulting prefactor and power in (2.3) are exact. On the diagonal with \(T=\pi\), the claimed bound is precisely \(D^n\rho_\pi^n/(\pi^2n)\).

Seven independent finite-contour comparisons passed. They include \((m,n,c,T/\pi)=(1,1,0.3,6)\), \((2,1,1.7,4)\), \((2,3,0.8,3)\), \((3,2,1.4,3)\), the diagonal saddle at \(n=10\), an off-diagonal nonsaddle line, and complex marking \(s=0.02+0.03i\). The largest observed error divided by its analytic tail bound was below 0.09.

## Saddle and coefficient formula

Writing \(p=(1+e^y)^{-1}\) gives \(X=-\log(1-p)\). The inequality

\[
p+(1-p)\log(1-p)>0\qquad(0<p<1)
\]

implies \(p/X>1-p\). Thus \(p/X\), and hence \(yp/X\), increase strictly. The endpoint limits establish existence and uniqueness for every positive direction \(\lambda\). At the root,

\[
B=1+\lambda^{-1}-y(1-p)>1.
\]

The analytic implicit-function argument is nondegenerate. On any compact direction interval, the relevant saddles stay in a compact subset of the positive axis, and a uniform analytic neighborhood and uniform Gaussian bound exist.

The normalization in (3.3) was checked independently. In the coefficient sum, the index constraint gives

\[
M=2j+2\sum_{r\ge3}m_r,
\]

so every included Gaussian moment has even order. The signs, factorials, amplitude coefficients, and the final multiplication by \(1+1/n\) all agree with (3.4). The stated all-orders proof is a valid fixed-order analytic saddle argument; no uniformity in growing \(J\), in boundary directions, or in growing marking ranges follows from it or is claimed.

An independent coefficient identity is useful for checking the implementation. Set

\[
v=s\sqrt{\frac{2f_\lambda(s)}{Bs^2}},\qquad v=s+O(s^2).
\]

Lagrange inversion and the even Gaussian moments give

\[
d_j=\frac{(-1)^j(2j-1)!!}{B^j}
[s^{2j}](1+s)^{-2}
\left(\frac{2f_\lambda(s)}{Bs^2}\right)^{-j-1/2},
\qquad c_j=d_j+d_{j-1}.
\]

This single coefficient-extraction formula is independent of the producer's sparse expansion. It reproduces \(c_0,\ldots,c_4\) exactly as symbolic expressions in \(B,f_3,\ldots,f_{10}\), with no floating-point atoms. Direct composition using

\[
X(y(1+s))-x
=\log\left(1+\frac{x}{\lambda y}(e^{-ys}-1)\right)
\]

reproduces the diagonal phase polynomials through \(f_{10}\).

Independent 110-digit evaluation reproduces all eight coefficients \(c_0,\ldots,c_7\). The largest absolute discrepancy from the producer's 80-digit exported numbers is \(2.475\times10^{-80}\), consistent with rounding at the last exported digit.

Off-diagonal checks at directions \(1/2,3/4,1,5/4,2\) show the expected approach of scaled order-three residuals to \(c_4(\lambda)\).

## Complex marking and block-count fluctuations

The manuscript's compact-complex-marking paragraph is correct, though necessarily condensed. Here is an explicit justification of its global step.

Fix the original vertical line \(\Re z=c=y(0)\) and take a small closed disk \(|s|\le\eta\), with \(\eta<c\). On that line,

\[
|X_s(c+it)|\ge a_s:=\log(1+e^{-\Re s-c})>0.
\]

The minimum of \(a_s\) on the disk is positive. The analytic saddle \(y(s)\), \(x(s)\), and \(B(s)\) remain nonzero, with \(\Re B(s)>0\), after reducing the disk if necessary. Choose the analytic square-root branch that is positive at \(s=0\).

For the far tails, take \(T\) sufficiently large that

\[
\sup_{|s|\le\eta}
\frac{|x(s)y(s)|}{a_s\sqrt{c^2+T^2}}<q<1.
\]

The same tail integration then bounds the tails by an exponentially small multiple of the saddle exponential scale, uniformly on the disk. On the remaining compact outer portions, the strict gap at \(s=0\) persists by continuity when \(\eta\) is reduced.

The local contour can be moved to \(z=y(s)(1+i\theta)\), with short connecting arcs near the old endpoints. All of this stays in

\[
\Re z>0,\qquad\Re(s+z)>0,
\]

so it avoids \(z=0\), the logarithmic cut, and zeros of \(X_s\). The connectors retain the outer exponential gap. Along the local contour the quadratic term is \(-B(s)\theta^2/2\), with uniformly negative real part. This establishes the claimed complex-uniform saddle expansion and its analytic remainder. Cauchy estimates apply on a slightly smaller disk. There is no reliance on a real-parameter asymptotic being automatically differentiable.

The derivative identities were independently checked:

\[
y'=B^{-1}-1,\quad
(\log D)'=1/y,\quad
(\log D)''=\frac{B-1}{By^2},\quad
B'=\frac{B-1-p}{B}.
\]

These yield the manuscript's \(\mu\), \(\sigma^2\), and \(\mu_0\), including the mean constant's sign. The variance slope is positive because \(B>1\). The characteristic-function argument gives the claimed central limit theorem.

The complex expansions were separately checked at \(s=0.02\), \(0.02i\), and \(-0.02+0.02i\), at \(n=40,80\). Exact-integer moment computations at \(n=40,80,160,320\) show the expected approach to \(\mu_0\). For example, at \(n=320\), the exact mean minus \(\mu n\) is approximately 0.144539886889, approaching \(\mu_0=0.144565688578\ldots\), with an error of order \(1/n\).

## Inverse and integer threshold

For fixed \(J\), \(P_J(1/v)\) is eventually positive and analytic on the positive real axis. The logarithmic remainder is \(O(n^{-J-1})\), and

\[
H'_J(v)=2\log v+\log D+O(v^{-1})>0.
\]

The mean-value argument therefore gives the stated \(O(n^{-J-1}/\log n)\) inverse error. The Lambert-W normalization is correct: \(N_0\) solves exactly the two leading terms \(2N_0\log N_0+(\log D-2)N_0=L\). The displayed first correction accounts for the remaining \(\tfrac12\log N_0+\log(2\pi C)\); its residual is \(O(1/(N_0\log N_0))\).

The Newton recurrence also has the claimed rate. In the relevant \(O(1)\) neighborhood,

\[
|e_{r+1}|\le\frac{K}{n\log n}|e_r|^2,
\]

so \(e_r=O((n\log n)^{-(2^r-1)})\). The stated number of fixed iterations suffices for each fixed \(J\). Independent checks compare both a directly solved inverse root and the indicated Newton iterates.

The new arbitrary-target ceiling envelope (5.4) is valid. One chooses its constant strictly larger than the ratio of the sequence-value remainder bound to a local lower derivative bound. Integers below the lower real endpoint then have \(a_k<A\); the ceiling of the upper endpoint has \(a_k\ge A\). Strict sequence monotonicity excludes more distant candidates. For sufficiently large targets the envelope's real width is less than one, hence at most two neighboring integer candidates remain.

The warning about rounding is essential and correctly included. Likewise, (5.4) has existence constants and an asymptotic starting threshold; it is not a computable finite-input certificate without effective bounds on those constants.

## Prime-power congruence

The proof of (7.1) is sound. Terms with \(k\ge pr\) vanish modulo \(p^r\), because \(v_p(k!)\ge\lfloor k/p\rfloor\ge r\). Extending the remaining sum to \(pr-1\) uses the exact identity \(S(n,k)=0\) for \(k>n\). Inclusion-exclusion then produces the finite sum in bases \(kj\).

For \(n\ge r\), each nonunit base contributes zero modulo \(p^r\); every unit base has exponent period dividing \(\varphi(p^r)\). This proves a period, with no assertion that it is the least period.

Independent checks used full exact inclusion-exclusion values, with no modular recurrence or truncated sum, for 208 comparisons in 13 prime-power cases. The starting condition matters: \(a_1=1\) and \(a_3=211\) differ modulo 4, so the general prime-power assertion cannot be strengthened to \(n\ge1\).
