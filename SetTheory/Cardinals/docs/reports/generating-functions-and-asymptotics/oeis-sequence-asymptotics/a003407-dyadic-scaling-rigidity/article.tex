\begin{abstract}
Let $\theta(n)$ count permutations of $[n]$ having no three-term arithmetic
progression as a subsequence. A single exponential growth constant does not
exist, by a theorem of Boon Suan Ho. We determine a more informative asymptotic
object: a unique continuous function $P$, periodic in the binary logarithm,
with
\[
 \log\theta(n)=nP(\log_2 n)-Q(n),\qquad
 \log 2\le Q(n)\le\log 21.
\]
The construction is an application of established divide-and-conquer theory;
an elementary proof with explicit constants is supplied. The central further
result is an exact scale-rigidity theorem. Published enumerative data certify
that $P$ has fundamental period exactly one. Consequently, the positive real
scales $c$ for which
$\log\theta(\lfloor cn\rfloor)-c\log\theta(n)=o(n)$ are precisely the integral
powers of two. At every other scale, exponential gain and exponential loss
both occur on sets of positive lower natural density.
For the concrete split $3n=2n+n$, integer certificates give a factor
$(49/50)^n$ loss when $n=128\cdot2^t$, and a factor $(21/20)^n$ gain when
$n=172\cdot2^t$. We also describe the entire interval of subsequential growth
rates, all cutoff-dependent empirical limit laws, logarithmic averaging,
and polynomial and geometric sampling. A finite-data enclosure algorithm,
reproducible arithmetic certificates, an independent small-count verification,
and a research program complete the article.
\end{abstract}

\section{The question and the contribution}
\label{dsr:sec:intro}

\subsection{What should replace a nonexistent growth constant?}
Many counting problems are organized around an asymptotic expression of the
form $C\rho^n n^\alpha$. For three-term-progression-free permutations, even the
coarsest candidate $\rho=\lim\theta(n)^{1/n}$ fails to exist. Merely recording
this failure leaves several natural structural questions unanswered. Does the
sequence exhibit arbitrary exponential-scale oscillation, or a coherent
phase law? Which changes of scale preserve its growth? Can differently sized
blocks be combined at only subexponential cost? Does averaging eliminate the
oscillation?

This article answers these questions in a unified way. The right object is a
continuous growth profile on the circle $\R/\Z$, sampled at the phase
$\log_2 n$. Its period is not just some divisor of one: a short finite
certificate proves that its fundamental period is exactly one. This numerical
shape information has a qualitative consequence for \emph{every real scale},
not merely the finitely many sizes in the table.

Here is the central classification. In this display, $c>0$ is fixed and the
integer $\lfloor cn\rfloor$ is positive for all sufficiently large $n$:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\quad
 \log\theta(\lfloor cn\rfloor)-c\log\theta(n)=o(n)
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad c\in\{2^j:j\in\Z\}.
 \quad}
\label{dsr:eq:headline}
\end{equation}
Outside these scales, the left side has positive and negative excursions of
order $n$, each occurring on a set of positive lower natural density. Thus the
obstruction is substantially stronger than failure of a ratio limit.

\subsection{Relationship to existing work}
Davis, Entringer, Graham, and Simmons established the parity-splitting
construction for these permutations \cite{DEGS}. Sharma proved the uniform
upper splitting estimate used below \cite[Theorem 2.8]{Sharma}. Correll and
Randy W.~Ho developed dynamic programming and extended the count table
\cite{CorrellHo}; the OEIS table now supplies values through $200$, with later
terms attributed to Alois P.~Heinz \cite{OEIS}.

Boon Suan Ho proved in 2026 that $\theta(n)^{1/n}$ does not converge, by
separating limits along two dyadic subsequences \cite{Ho}. That theorem is
\emph{not} claimed as a result of this article. Separately, Hwang, Janson, and
Tsai give a general theory of continuous periodic fluctuations for
floor/ceiling divide-and-conquer recurrences \cite[Theorem 2.10]{HJT}. Our
profile construction is the equal-weight, bounded-toll specialization of that
theory. We give the proof because the explicit bounds and interpolation are
needed for the subsequent certificates, not because the underlying analytic
principle is new.

The additional contribution developed here consists of the exact period
certificate, the resulting classification \eqref{dsr:eq:headline}, exponential
obstructions to unequal-size gluing, and the complete sampling and empirical
laws for this particular counting sequence. These statements were not found
in the sources inspected for this article. This is a targeted literature
assessment, not a claim of exhaustive priority verification or of resolution
of a separately named published conjecture.

\section{Combinatorial input and the bounded toll}
\label{dsr:sec:input}

\begin{definition}
A permutation $\pi=(\pi_1,\ldots,\pi_n)$ of $[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}$ is
\emph{3AP-free} if there are no indices $i<j<k$ such that
$\pi_i+\pi_k=2\pi_j$. Let $\theta(n)$ be the number of these permutations, with
$\theta(0)=1$. Both increasing and decreasing arithmetic progressions are
forbidden; a progression need not occupy consecutive positions.
\end{definition}
For example, $\theta(1)=1$, $\theta(2)=2$, $\theta(3)=4$, and $\theta(4)=10$.
The distinction between consecutive positions and arbitrary subsequences is
essential to the count.

\begin{lemma}[Parity splitting]
For every integer $n\ge2$,
\begin{equation}
 \theta(n)\ge 2\theta(\lfloor n/2\rfloor)
                         \theta(\lceil n/2\rceil).
\label{dsr:eq:lower}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Choose a 3AP-free ordering of the even numbers and one of the odd numbers in
$[n]$, obtained by affine rescaling of smaller 3AP-free permutations.
Concatenate the two blocks, in either order. The two endpoints of any
integer three-term progression have the same parity. If its endpoints were
in one block and its midpoint in the other, the midpoint could not lie
between the endpoints in the concatenated order. If all three entries were
in one block, that block's avoidance property would be violated. Thus both
concatenations avoid three-term progressions. Their orders and their
restrictions to the two nonempty parity blocks determine the input pair, so
the construction counts exactly twice the number of input pairs.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Sharma's upper splitting bound]
For $n\ge3$,
\begin{equation}
 \theta(n)\le 21\theta(\lfloor n/2\rfloor)
                          \theta(\lceil n/2\rceil).
\label{dsr:eq:upper}
\end{equation}
The same inequality is valid at $n=2$ by direct evaluation.
\end{theorem}
This is \cite[Theorem 2.8]{Sharma}. The proof uses structural restrictions on
how fixed odd and even traces can interleave. We do not replace that
combinatorial theorem by a numerical fit or an unverified recurrence.

\begin{remark}[Formal status of the two inputs \wtag]
\label{dsr:rem:inputs-formal}
Inequality~\eqref{dsr:eq:upper} for $n\ge3$ is the Lean theorem
\lean{LeanProofs.Sharma2012.theorem_2_8_holds}; the case $n=2$ is the direct
evaluation $\theta(2)=2\le21$, which the Lean statement does not include.
Inequality~\eqref{dsr:eq:lower} for all $n\ge2$ is the pair of Lean theorems
\lean{even_count_recurrence_holds} ($n=2k$) and
\lean{odd_count_recurrence_holds} ($n=2k+1$) in the namespace
\lean{LeanProofs.DavisEntringerGrahamSimmons1977}, for $k\ge1$. The proof of
the parity-splitting lemma above is the manuscript's own, and is a second
route to a result already machine-checked. See
Section~\ref{dsr:sec:formal}.
\end{remark}

For the remainder of the paper set
\begin{equation}
 a_n=\log\theta(n),\qquad b_n=\frac{a_n}{n},\qquad
 L=\log2,\quad U=\log21,\quad D=U-L=\log(21/2),
\label{dsr:eq:notation}
\end{equation}
where unqualified logarithms are natural. Define the \emph{toll}
\begin{equation}
 r_n=a_n-a_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}-a_{\lceil n/2\rceil},\qquad n\ge2.
\label{dsr:eq:toll}
\end{equation}
Equations \eqref{dsr:eq:lower}--\eqref{dsr:eq:upper} say precisely that
\begin{equation}
 L\le r_n\le U. \label{dsr:eq:bounded-toll}
\end{equation}
This is an exact recurrence with a bounded, generally unknown toll; it is not
an assertion that the toll converges or has any simple closed form.

\begin{lemma}[Elementary global bounds]
For every $n\ge1$,
\begin{equation}
 (n-1)L\le a_n\le(n-1)U.
\label{dsr:eq:rough}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The assertion holds at $n=1$. If it holds at the two smaller arguments in
\eqref{dsr:eq:toll}, their sizes sum to $n$, and their lower bounds sum to
$(n-2)L$. Adding $r_n\ge L$ gives the lower bound at $n$; the upper bound is
identical with $U$ in place of $L$. Strong induction completes the proof.
\end{proof}

\section{A continuous growth profile with a bounded correction}
\label{dsr:sec:profile}

\subsection{The interpolation identity}
Let $A:[1,\infty)\to\R$ be the piecewise-affine interpolation of $a_n$:
\begin{equation}
 A(n+u)=(1-u)a_n+u a_{n+1},\qquad 0\le u\le1.
\end{equation}
For $x\ge2$, define
\begin{equation}
 R(x)=A(x)-2A(x/2).
\label{dsr:eq:R}
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}[No interpolation error]
$R$ is the piecewise-affine interpolation of $r_n$. In particular,
\begin{equation}
 L\le R(x)\le U,\qquad
 |R(x)-R(y)|\le\min\{D|x-y|,D\}\quad(x,y\ge2).
\label{dsr:eq:Rbounds}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
At even integers $n=2m$ we have $2A(n/2)=2a_m$; at odd integers $n=2m+1$ we
have $2A(n/2)=a_m+a_{m+1}$. Thus $R(n)=r_n$ in both cases. On each interval
$[n,n+1]$, both $A(x)$ and $A(x/2)$ are affine: a breakpoint of the latter
can only occur at an even integer. Consequently $R$ is affine there, with
endpoint values $r_n,r_{n+1}$. These values lie in $[L,U]$, so its slope has
absolute value at most $D$, and its entire range has diameter at most $D$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Construction and uniqueness}
\begin{theorem}[Bounded-error log-periodic law]
\label{dsr:thm:profile}
There is a unique continuous $1$-periodic function $P:\R\to\R$ such that
$a_n=nP(\log_2n)+O(1)$. More precisely, there is a continuous function
$Q:[1,\infty)\to[L,U]$ for which
\begin{equation}
 A(x)=xP(\log_2x)-Q(x),\qquad x\ge1.
\label{dsr:eq:profile-real}
\end{equation}
In particular,
\begin{equation}
 \frac{e^{nP(\log_2n)}}{21}\le\theta(n)
 \le\frac{e^{nP(\log_2n)}}{2},\qquad n\ge1.
\label{dsr:eq:count-envelope}
\end{equation}
Also $L\le P(t)\le U$ for every $t$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For $x\ge1$ and $k\ge0$ put $F_k(x)=2^{-k}A(2^kx)$. By
\eqref{dsr:eq:R},
\begin{equation}
 F_{k+1}(x)-F_k(x)=2^{-(k+1)}R(2^{k+1}x).
\end{equation}
The uniform bound on $R$ makes this a uniformly convergent telescoping series,
indeed uniformly on all $x\ge1$. Its limit is
\begin{equation}
 F(x)=A(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}2^{-j}R(2^jx).
\label{dsr:eq:F}
\end{equation}
Each summand is continuous, so $F$ is continuous. The tail estimate is
\begin{equation}
 L2^{-k}\le F(x)-F_k(x)\le U2^{-k}.
\label{dsr:eq:uniform-tail}
\end{equation}
Shifting the defining limit by one index gives $F(2x)=2F(x)$.

Define $P(t)=2^{-t}F(2^t)$ initially for $t\ge0$. The scaling identity gives
$P(t+1)=P(t)$ and matching endpoint values, so this defines a continuous
$1$-periodic function on all of $\R$. Taking
\begin{equation}
 Q(x)=F(x)-A(x)=\sum_{j\ge1}2^{-j}R(2^jx)
\label{dsr:eq:Q}
\end{equation}
gives $L\le Q(x)\le U$ and proves \eqref{dsr:eq:profile-real}. Exponentiation at
integer $x=n$ gives \eqref{dsr:eq:count-envelope}. Interpolating
\eqref{dsr:eq:rough} and taking the scaled limit gives $Lx\le F(x)\le Ux$,
which proves the asserted bounds on $P$.

For uniqueness, suppose a second continuous $1$-periodic function $P_1$
satisfies the same bounded-error assertion. Fix $t\in[0,1]$ and take
$n_k=\lfloor2^{k+t}\rfloor$. Then $\log_2n_k-k\to t$. Division of both
asymptotic formulas by $n_k$ and continuity imply
$P(t)=\lim b_{n_k}=P_1(t)$. Periodicity extends this equality to every real
argument.
\end{proof}

The theorem is the equal-weight bounded-toll case of the framework in
\cite{HJT}; the proof above makes the constants needed here explicit. The
bound on $Q$ is a genuine uniform multiplicative control on $\theta(n)$,
not just a relative $o(1)$ error in its logarithm. It does \emph{not} assert
that $Q(n)$ converges, so it should not be rewritten as an asymptotic
equivalence with a fixed prefactor.

\subsection{A quantitative modulus of continuity}
Write
\begin{equation}
 \Phi(s)=\frac{F(s)}s=P(\log_2s),\qquad 1\le s\le2.
\end{equation}
We call $s$ the binary mantissa. The endpoints are identified, since
$\Phi(1)=\Phi(2)$.

\begin{proposition}[Explicit log-Lipschitz control]
\label{dsr:prop:modulus}
For $s,t\in[1,2]$, $0<h=|s-t|\le1/2$, and
$J=\lceil\log_2(1/h)\rceil$,
\begin{equation}
 |\Phi(s)-\Phi(t)|
 \le h\bigl[L+U+D(J+1)\bigr].
\label{dsr:eq:modulus}
\end{equation}
Consequently $P$ is H\"older continuous of every exponent less than one, and
has a circle modulus $O(h\log(1/h))$ as $h\downarrow0$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
On $[1,2]$, $A(s)=L(s-1)$. In the series \eqref{dsr:eq:F}, each of the first $J$
terms changes by at most $Dh$ when $s$ is replaced by $t$, by the Lipschitz
bound in \eqref{dsr:eq:Rbounds}. The remaining terms change by at most
$D\sum_{j>J}2^{-j}=D2^{-J}\le Dh$. Hence
$|F(s)-F(t)|\le Lh+Dh(J+1)$. Finally,
\[
 \left|\frac{F(s)}s-\frac{F(t)}t\right|
 \le |F(s)-F(t)|+Uh,
\]
because $F(t)\le Ut$ and $s,t\ge1$. This proves \eqref{dsr:eq:modulus}.
Composition with $s=2^t$ transfers the estimate to each phase interval.
Across the identified endpoints, split the distance at the endpoint and use
$\Phi(1)=\Phi(2)$. The H\"older assertions follow from
$h\log(1/h)=O_\alpha(h^\alpha)$ for $0<\alpha<1$.
\end{proof}
We do not claim that this modulus is optimal for the particular sequence, or
that $P$ is differentiable, Lipschitz, or nowhere differentiable.

\section{The growth spectrum and finite-data reconstruction}
\label{dsr:sec:reconstruction}

\subsection{Every intermediate growth rate occurs}
Define
\begin{equation}
 p_- =\min_{t\in[0,1]}P(t),\qquad p_+=\max_{t\in[0,1]}P(t),\qquad
 \rho_-=e^{p_-},\quad\rho_+=e^{p_+}.
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Complete subsequential spectrum]
\label{dsr:thm:spectrum}
The sets of subsequential limits are
\begin{equation}
 \clust(b_n)=[p_-,p_+],\qquad
 \clust\bigl(\theta(n)^{1/n}\bigr)=[\rho_-,\rho_+].
\label{dsr:eq:spectrum}
\end{equation}
For each $s\in[1,2]$,
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\log\theta(\lfloor2^ks\rfloor)}{\lfloor2^ks\rfloor}
 \longrightarrow\Phi(s),
\label{dsr:eq:phase-ray}
\end{equation}
with error $O(k2^{-k})$. At an exact integer dyadic ray $n=m2^k$, the error
relative to $P(\log_2m)$ is at most $U/(m2^k)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The identity in Theorem~\ref{dsr:thm:profile} gives
$|b_n-P(\log_2n)|\le U/n$. Every sequence of phases has a convergent
subsequence on the circle, so every cluster value belongs to the range of
$P$. Conversely, $\log_2\lfloor2^ks\rfloor-k\to\log_2s$, proving
\eqref{dsr:eq:phase-ray}. The rounding error in the phase is $O(2^{-k})$;
Proposition~\ref{dsr:prop:modulus} turns it into $O(k2^{-k})$ in the value.
A continuous real function on the circle has a compact interval as its
range. Exponentiation gives the second statement of \eqref{dsr:eq:spectrum}.
On an exact dyadic ray there is no phase rounding, giving the stronger error
bound directly.
\end{proof}
Thus the failure of a single growth constant is organized by a continuum of
phases, rather than by two isolated exceptional subsequences. The exact-period
certificate in the next section will in particular show $p_-<p_+$.

\subsection{A certified enclosure from one dyadic-width band}
\begin{theorem}[Finite-band reconstruction]
\label{dsr:thm:band}
Suppose the counts $\theta(N),\ldots,\theta(2N)$ are known, where $N\ge1$ is an
integer. For every real $x\in[N,2N]$,
\begin{equation}
 \frac{A(x)+L}{x}\le P(\log_2x)\le\frac{A(x)+U}{x}.
\label{dsr:eq:band}
\end{equation}
The two envelopes are separated by at most $D/N$, and the interval of phases
covered has length one. Furthermore,
\begin{align}
 \min_{N\le n\le2N}\frac{a_n+L}{n}
 &\le p_-\le
 \min_{N\le n\le2N}\frac{a_n+U}{n}, \label{dsr:eq:min-band}\\
 \max_{N\le n\le2N}\frac{a_n+L}{n}
 &\le p_+\le
 \max_{N\le n\le2N}\frac{a_n+U}{n}. \label{dsr:eq:max-band}
\end{align}
Each displayed enclosure has width at most $D/N$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Equation \eqref{dsr:eq:band} is \eqref{dsr:eq:profile-real} together with
$Q\in[L,U]$. On a unit interval, either envelope has the form
$\alpha+\beta/x$, so its maximum and minimum occur at endpoints (or it is
constant). Thus extrema over the whole band can be taken at integers. Since
$\log_2(2N)-\log_2N=1$, the band traverses every phase, which proves
\eqref{dsr:eq:min-band}--\eqref{dsr:eq:max-band}. Pointwise separation is $D/x\le D/N$;
taking maxima or minima cannot increase this bound.
\end{proof}

The theorem gives a uniform approximation algorithm, not an efficient count
enumeration algorithm. In principle one can compute all needed finite counts
by exhaustive search and hence compute $P$ uniformly, and $p_\pm$ as real
numbers, to any prescribed accuracy. Achieving log-profile error $\varepsilon$
requires counts through $O(\varepsilon^{-1})$ by this bound, but obtaining those
counts may be very expensive.

\section{A finite certificate for the exact fundamental period}
\label{dsr:sec:period}
The previous sections prove that $P$ is $1$-periodic. A function with period
one could still have period $1/2$, $1/3$, or another smaller period. Excluding
this possibility is essential for the classification of \emph{real} scale
factors in \eqref{dsr:eq:headline}.

\begin{lemma}[Published-data phase gap]
\label{dsr:lem:phase-gap}
The following inequalities hold:
\begin{equation}
 e^{P(0)}>\frac{57}{25}=2.28,
 \qquad
 \sup_{1/4\le t\le1/2}e^{P(t)}<\frac94=2.25.
\label{dsr:eq:phase-gap}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
All arithmetic needed for the proof is contained in the finite certificate
\begin{align}
 2\theta(128)\,25^{128}&>57^{128}, \label{dsr:eq:gap-cert-one}\\
 21\theta(n)\,4^n&<9^n\qquad(152\le n\le182). \label{dsr:eq:gap-cert-band}
\end{align}
These 32 strict integer inequalities are verified by \code{verify.py} from
the published data \cite{OEIS}. First, $128$ has phase zero, so
Theorem~\ref{dsr:thm:band}, or \eqref{dsr:eq:profile-real} directly, gives
$e^{P(0)}\ge(2\theta(128))^{1/128}>57/25$.

Next, \eqref{dsr:eq:gap-cert-band} says $a_n+U<n\log(9/4)$ at every integer in
$[152,182]$. Linear interpolation therefore gives
$A(x)+U<x\log(9/4)$ throughout that interval. The real interval
\[
 [128\cdot2^{1/4},\,128\sqrt2]
\]
is contained in $[152,182]$: the lower inclusion follows from
$19^4<2\cdot16^4$, and the upper from $2\cdot128^2<182^2$.
For $t\in[1/4,1/2]$, put $x=128\cdot2^t$ and apply the upper envelope.
Periodicity gives $P(\log_2x)=P(7+t)=P(t)$, proving the second inequality.
The bound is uniform and strict because it holds on a compact interval.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Exact period group]
\label{dsr:thm:period}
The set of periods of $P$ is exactly $\Z$. In particular, its fundamental
positive period is one.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The phase gap makes $P$ nonconstant. An irrational period $\tau$, together
with period one, would make $P$ constant: the multiples of $\tau$ modulo one
are dense, and continuity propagates the value at any point along that dense
orbit. Thus any additional period must be rational.

If $p/q$ in lowest terms is a period, B\'ezout's identity and period one show
that $1/q$ is a period. Suppose $q\ge2$. There is an integer $j$ with
\begin{equation}
 \frac14\le\frac jq\le\frac12.
\end{equation}
For $q=2,3$ take $j=1$; for $q\ge4$ take $j=\lceil q/4\rceil$.
Period $1/q$ would imply $P(j/q)=P(0)$, contradicting
\eqref{dsr:eq:phase-gap}. Therefore $q=1$. Integer periods already follow from
the construction, completing the proof.
\end{proof}

The certificate proves substantially more than nonconstancy, and it does so
without locating a maximum or minimum. In particular, it does \emph{not}
prove that phase zero is a global maximum. It only proves that a whole
specified phase interval lies below its value, which is enough to rule out
every smaller period at once.

\section{Scaling rigidity and exponential gluing obstructions}
\label{dsr:sec:rigidity}

\subsection{Classification of all positive real scales}
For $c>0$ define, for sufficiently large integers $n$,
\begin{equation}
 T_c(n)=\log\theta(\lfloor cn\rfloor)-c\log\theta(n),
 \qquad
 \Delta_c(t)=P(t+\log_2c)-P(t).
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}[Uniform phase formula for a scale change]
\label{dsr:lem:scale-formula}
For each fixed $c>0$,
\begin{equation}
 T_c(n)=cn\Delta_c(\log_2n)+O_c(\log n).
\label{dsr:eq:scale-formula}
\end{equation}
If $c$ is an integer, the remainder can be replaced by $O_c(1)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Let $m=\lfloor cn\rfloor$. We have $m=cn+O(1)$ and
$\log_2m=\log_2n+\log_2c+O_c(1/n)$. By
Proposition~\ref{dsr:prop:modulus}, changing the phase by this amount changes $P$
by $O_c(\log n/n)$. Insert this into
$a_m=mP(\log_2m)-Q(m)$, subtract
$ca_n=cnP(\log_2n)-cQ(n)$, and use boundedness of $P$ and $Q$.
If $c$ is integral, there is no rounding and the remainder is exactly
$cQ(n)-Q(cn)$, hence bounded.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Real-scale rigidity and frequent gain/loss]
\label{dsr:thm:scales}
For $c>0$, $T_c(n)=o(n)$ if and only if $c=2^j$ for an integer $j$.
For every other $c$ there are $\eta_c>0$ and two sets $E_c^+,E_c^-\subseteq\N$
of positive lower natural density such that
\begin{align}
 \theta(\lfloor cn\rfloor)&\ge e^{\eta_cn}\theta(n)^c
                  &&(n\in E_c^+),\label{dsr:eq:scale-plus}\\
 \theta(\lfloor cn\rfloor)&\le e^{-\eta_cn}\theta(n)^c
                  &&(n\in E_c^-).\label{dsr:eq:scale-minus}
\end{align}
Finite initial exceptions may be removed from these sets.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
If $c=2^j$, periodicity makes $\Delta_c$ zero, and
\eqref{dsr:eq:scale-formula} proves $T_c=o(n)$. Conversely, if $T_c=o(n)$, apply
\eqref{dsr:eq:scale-formula} along $n_k=\lfloor2^{k+t}\rfloor$ for each fixed
$t$. Continuity yields $\Delta_c(t)=0$. Thus $\log_2c$ is a period of $P$,
and Theorem~\ref{dsr:thm:period} gives $\log_2c\in\Z$.

Now take an off-dyadic scale. The continuous periodic function $\Delta_c$ is
not identically zero and has integral zero on a period, by translation
invariance of the integral. It therefore has both positive and negative
values. Continuity supplies phase intervals on which $c\Delta_c$ is bounded
below by $2\eta_c$ or above by $-2\eta_c$, for some $\eta_c>0$.
Equation \eqref{dsr:eq:scale-formula} gives \eqref{dsr:eq:scale-plus} and
\eqref{dsr:eq:scale-minus} at all sufficiently large integers with those phases.

For completeness, such phase intervals have positive lower natural density.
Choose a closed mantissa interval $[u,v]\subset(1,2)$ of positive length
inside one of them. If $2^k\le N<2^{k+1}$, the complete dyadic blocks below
$2^k$ contain at least
\[
 (v-u)(2^k-1)-O(k)
\]
integers with mantissa in $[u,v]$. Division by $N<2^{k+1}$ gives lower density
at least $(v-u)/2>0$. Apply this to both sign intervals.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Integer arities]
\label{dsr:cor:integer-arity}
For an integer $m\ge2$, a uniform subexponential comparison
$\theta(mn)=\theta(n)^m\exp(o(n))$ holds exactly when $m$ is a power of two.
For $m=2^r$ the stronger bounds
\begin{equation}
 2^{m-1}\theta(n)^m\le\theta(mn)\le21^{m-1}\theta(n)^m
\label{dsr:eq:dyadic-amplification}
\end{equation}
hold for every $n\ge1$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The classification is Theorem~\ref{dsr:thm:scales}. Iterating the even recurrence
$r$ times gives factors with total exponent $1+2+\cdots+2^{r-1}=m-1$,
which proves \eqref{dsr:eq:dyadic-amplification}.
\end{proof}

There is an important quantifier here: the error $o(n)$ must hold as $n$
runs through \emph{all} sufficiently large integers. Particular off-dyadic
phases can still have zero scale difference. The theorem does not exclude
such accidental equalities of profile values.

\subsection{Explicit exponential failure at the split \texorpdfstring{$2n+n$}{2n+n}}
Define the unequal-block gluing ratio
\begin{equation}
 G(n)=\frac{\theta(3n)}{\theta(2n)\theta(n)}.
\end{equation}
The parity recurrence gives
$\log\theta(2n)-2\log\theta(n)\in[L,U]$, so
\begin{equation}
 \log G(n)=3n\Delta_3(\log_2n)+O(1).
\label{dsr:eq:Gphase}
\end{equation}
In particular, exponential loss and exponential gain for $G(n)$ each occur
on a set of positive lower natural density. The following stronger explicit
subsequences need only four count values.

\begin{theorem}[Certified $2{:}1$ gluing loss and gain]
\label{dsr:thm:explicit-gluing}
For every integer $t\ge0$,
\begin{align}
 G(128\cdot2^t)&<\left(\frac{49}{50}\right)^{128\cdot2^t},
 \label{dsr:eq:explicit-loss}\\
 G(172\cdot2^t)&>\left(\frac{21}{20}\right)^{172\cdot2^t}.
 \label{dsr:eq:explicit-gain}
\end{align}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Iteration of the even recurrence gives, for $s\ge0$,
\begin{equation}
 2^{2^s-1}\theta(k)^{2^s}\le\theta(k2^s)
 \le21^{2^s-1}\theta(k)^{2^s}.
\label{dsr:eq:iteration}
\end{equation}
Set
\begin{equation}
 R_- =\frac{(21\theta(192))^2}{(2\theta(128))^3},\qquad
 R_+ =\frac{(2\theta(129))^4}{(21\theta(172))^3}.
\end{equation}
The integer certificates checked by the accompanying program are
\begin{align}
 (21\theta(192))^2 50^{128}
  &<(2\theta(128))^3 49^{128},\label{dsr:eq:loss-cert}\\
 (2\theta(129))^4 20^{172}
  &>(21\theta(172))^3 21^{172}.\label{dsr:eq:gain-cert}
\end{align}
Thus $R_-<(49/50)^{128}$ and $R_+>(21/20)^{172}$.

For $n=128\cdot2^t$, write $3n=192\cdot2^{t+1}$. Using the upper bound in
\eqref{dsr:eq:iteration} in the numerator and the lower bounds in the denominator
of $G(n)$ gives
\begin{equation}
 G(n)\le\frac4{21}\,R_-^{2^t}
       <(49/50)^n.
\end{equation}
For $n=172\cdot2^t$, write $3n=129\cdot2^{t+2}$. Use the lower bound for the
numerator and upper bounds for both denominator factors instead. This gives
\begin{equation}
 G(n)\ge\frac{21^2}{2}\,R_+^{2^t}
       >(21/20)^n.
\end{equation}
The prefactors are respectively less than and greater than one, so no
asymptotic threshold is needed: both statements hold for every $t\ge0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[No subexponential-loss universal gluing]
There is no function $\varepsilon(N)\to0$ such that, for all sufficiently
large $u,v$,
\begin{equation}
 \theta(u+v)\ge e^{-\varepsilon(u+v)(u+v)}\theta(u)\theta(v).
\end{equation}
Nor does the analogous upper bound with $e^{\varepsilon(u+v)(u+v)}$ hold
uniformly for all large $u,v$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Take $u=2n,v=n$ along the loss or gain subsequence in
Theorem~\ref{dsr:thm:explicit-gluing}. The proposed error exponent is $o(n)$,
whereas the exhibited logarithmic discrepancy has magnitude bounded below
by a positive constant times $n$.
\end{proof}

The loss has a direct counting interpretation. Along the first subsequence,
any map from all pairs of 3AP-free permutations of sizes $2n$ and $n$ into
3AP-free permutations of size $3n$ must identify exponentially many pairs
somewhere, if it is defined on the entire domain. In particular, a universal
injective concatenation-type construction at this size ratio is impossible.
This does not rule out successful constructions on a restricted family or
methods that record additional output information.

\section{Averaging and complete empirical limit laws}
\label{dsr:sec:statistics}
The interval of cluster values describes which limits are possible. It does
not describe how often the values occur. Because the phase is logarithmic,
logarithmic and ordinary sampling answer this question differently.

\subsection{Logarithmic averaging has a unique limiting law}
Let $H_N=\sum_{n=1}^N1/n$ and define probability measures
\begin{equation}
 \lambda_N=\frac1{H_N}\sum_{n=1}^N\frac1n\delta_{b_n}.
\end{equation}
Here $\delta_x$ is the point mass at $x$, and convergence of probability
measures means convergence against every continuous test function on a
compact interval containing their supports.

\begin{theorem}[Logarithmic empirical law]
\label{dsr:thm:log-law}
For every continuous test function $f$,
\begin{equation}
 \lim_{N\to\infty}\frac1{H_N}\sum_{n\le N}\frac{f(b_n)}n
   =\int_0^1f(P(t))\dd t
   =\frac1{\log2}\int_1^2 f(\Phi(s))\frac{\dd s}{s}.
\label{dsr:eq:log-law}
\end{equation}
For Lipschitz $f$ the error is $O_f(1/\log N)$. The limiting measure has
support $[p_-,p_+]$. Replacing $b_n$ by $\theta(n)^{1/n}$ pushes this law
forward under the exponential map.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
First take $f$ Lipschitz. Replacing $b_n$ by $P(\log_2n)$ changes the
unnormalized weighted sum by at most
$\operatorname{Lip}(f)U\sum n^{-2}=O_f(1)$.
On $[n,n+1]$, the phase changes by $O(1/n)$, so the modulus of $P$ gives a
change $O(\log n/n)$ in its value. The difference between the resulting sum
and the integral is therefore bounded by a constant times
$\sum_{n\ge2}\log n/n^2$, together with a bounded initial contribution.
Consequently,
\begin{align}
 \sum_{n\le N}\frac{f(b_n)}n
 &=\int_1^N f(P(\log_2x))\frac{\dd x}{x}+O_f(1)\\
 &=(\log N)\int_0^1f(P(t))\dd t+O_f(1),
\end{align}
where the last step splits the logarithmic integral into complete periods
and one bounded remainder. Since $H_N=\log N+O(1)$, this proves the asserted
rate. Continuous test functions follow by uniform approximation by
piecewise-affine, hence Lipschitz, functions on the compact range.

Every neighborhood of a value in the image of a continuous $P$ has a
preimage containing a nonempty phase interval, of positive Lebesgue measure.
Thus the support is the entire image $[p_-,p_+]$. The substitution
$s=2^t$ proves the mantissa formula, and continuity of the exponential gives
the final assertion.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Ordinary cutoffs retain a phase}
Define the ordinary empirical measure
\begin{equation}
 \mu_N=\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^N\delta_{b_n}.
\end{equation}
For $x\in[1,2]$ define a probability measure $\nu_x$ by its test-function
integrals:
\begin{align}
 \nu_x(f)
 &=\frac1x\left(\int_1^2f(\Phi(s))\dd s
                     +\int_1^x f(\Phi(s))\dd s\right)\label{dsr:eq:nu}\\
 &=\frac2x\int_1^x f(\Phi(s))\dd s
      +\frac1x\int_x^2f(\Phi(s))\dd s.\nonumber
\end{align}
The weights integrate to one. At the endpoints $\nu_1=\nu_2$, as required
when the cutoff phase wraps around.

\begin{theorem}[All ordinary empirical limit laws]
\label{dsr:thm:ordinary}
For $N_k=\lfloor2^kx\rfloor$ with $x\in[1,2]$,
\begin{equation}
 \mu_{N_k}\Longrightarrow\nu_x.
\end{equation}
More generally, the complete set of weak subsequential limits of $\mu_N$ is
$\{\nu_x:1\le x\le2\}$. The map $x\mapsto\nu_x$ is weakly continuous and
all its measures have support $[p_-,p_+]$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For Lipschitz $f$, replacing $b_n$ by $P(\log_2n)$ changes its ordinary
average by $O_f(H_N/N)$, which tends to zero. Continuous $f$ again follow by
uniform approximation. Put $u_n=n/2^k$. Since $P$ is $1$-periodic,
$P(\log_2n)=P(\log_2u_n)$. Riemann sums on $[\varepsilon,x]$, followed by
$\varepsilon\downarrow0$, give
\begin{equation}
 \lim_{k\to\infty}\mu_{\lfloor2^kx\rfloor}(f)
   =\frac1x\int_0^x f(P(\log_2u))\dd u.
\label{dsr:eq:riemann-law}
\end{equation}
The integrand is bounded and continuous away from zero, so the omitted
interval has arbitrarily small contribution.

Let $C_f=\int_1^2f(\Phi(s))\dd s$. Splitting $(0,1]$ into dyadic intervals
and substituting $u=2^{-j}s$ shows
\begin{equation}
 \int_0^1f(P(\log_2u))\dd u
   =\sum_{j\ge1}2^{-j}C_f=C_f.
\end{equation}
Thus \eqref{dsr:eq:riemann-law} equals \eqref{dsr:eq:nu}.

The same Riemann-sum argument holds for a sequence of upper endpoints
$x_k\to x$, by boundedness of the integrand and the small length of the
interval between $x_k$ and $x$. Every sequence of cutoffs has a subsequence
whose mantissas $N/2^{\lfloor\log_2N\rfloor}$ converge in $[1,2]$.
This proves that no other weak limit is possible, while the fixed-mantissa
cutoffs realize every $\nu_x$. Formula \eqref{dsr:eq:nu} proves weak continuity.
Its mantissa density is at least $1/2$ throughout $[1,2]$, so the support is
the full image of $\Phi$, by the same neighborhood argument used for the
logarithmic law.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Ordinary averages do not wash out the oscillation]
\label{dsr:thm:mean-nonconvergence}
Neither of the ordinary Ces\`aro averages
\begin{equation}
 \frac1N\sum_{n\le N}\frac{\log\theta(n)}n,
 \qquad
 \frac1N\sum_{n\le N}\theta(n)^{1/n}
\end{equation}
converges. In particular, neither corresponding ordinary empirical measure
has a single weak limit.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For the first average its cutoff-dependent limit is
\begin{equation}
 M(x)=\frac{C+\int_1^x\Phi(s)\dd s}{x},\qquad
 C=\int_1^2\Phi(s)\dd s.
\end{equation}
Continuity of $\Phi$ makes $M$ continuously differentiable, with
\begin{equation}
 M'(x)=\frac{\Phi(x)-M(x)}x.
\label{dsr:eq:M-ode}
\end{equation}
If $M$ were constant, \eqref{dsr:eq:M-ode} would force $\Phi$ to be constant,
contradicting the phase gap. Thus two cutoff phases give distinct limiting
means. For the second average replace $\Phi$ throughout by $e^\Phi$, which
is likewise continuous and nonconstant. Nonconvergence of these continuous
test-function integrals precludes weak convergence of the empirical measures.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Full support is not the same as existence of a probability density. The
pushforward laws could in principle have atoms at values corresponding to
positive-measure level sets of $P$. Nothing in the arguments above proves
absolute continuity, absence of atoms, or a particular shape of a histogram.
\end{remark}

\section{Polynomial and geometric sampling}
\label{dsr:sec:sampling}

\subsection{Multiplicatively fine sampling preserves the spectrum}
\begin{proposition}
\label{dsr:prop:fine-sampling}
Let $n_k$ be a strictly increasing unbounded sequence of positive integers
with $n_{k+1}/n_k\to1$. Then
\begin{equation}
 \clust(b_{n_k})=[p_-,p_+],\qquad
 \clust\bigl(\theta(n_k)^{1/n_k}\bigr)=[\rho_-,\rho_+].
\end{equation}
In particular this holds along every eventually positive arithmetic
progression with positive common difference and every eventually positive,
nonconstant integer-valued
polynomial sampled at positive integers.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Fix $s\in[1,2]$ and let $n_{k(j)}$ be the first sample not below $2^js$.
Its predecessor is below $2^js$, so
\[
 1\le\frac{n_{k(j)}}{2^js}
 \le\frac{n_{k(j)}}{n_{k(j)-1}}\longrightarrow1.
\]
Consequently its phases converge to $\log_2s$ modulo one, and the profile
law gives limit $\Phi(s)$. These values fill the interval; no new values are
possible because the sampled sequence is a subsequence of the original.
A polynomial of positive degree and positive leading coefficient is
strictly increasing eventually and has successive ratios tending to one.
Arithmetic progressions are the degree-one case. Removing a finite initial
segment changes none of the assertions.
\end{proof}
This assertion concerns the cluster set, not the frequency distribution
under polynomial sampling. It already shows that the continuum of growth
rates is not confined to specially chosen powers of two.

\subsection{Geometric sampling is governed by rotation}
\begin{theorem}[Geometric sampling classification]
\label{dsr:thm:geometric}
Let $c>0$, $b>1$, $n_k=\lfloor cb^k\rfloor$, and put
$\alpha=\log_2b$, $\beta=\log_2c$. Ignore any initial zero terms.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
\item If $\alpha=p/q$ is rational in lowest terms, the cluster values of
$b_{n_k}$ are
\begin{equation}
 \{P(\beta+jp/q):0\le j<q\},
\end{equation}
and the empirical law is the average of the $q$ corresponding point masses.
Some of the displayed values may coincide.
\item If $\alpha$ is irrational, the cluster set is $[p_-,p_+]$, and
\begin{equation}
 \lim_{K\to\infty}\frac1K\sum_{k=1}^K f(b_{n_k})
       =\int_0^1f(P(t))\dd t
\end{equation}
for every continuous $f$.
\end{enumerate}
Both statements transfer to $\theta(n_k)^{1/n_k}$ by exponentiation.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Rounding gives
$\log_2n_k=\beta+k\alpha+O(b^{-k})$. The modulus of continuity and bounded
correction imply
\begin{equation}
 b_{n_k}=P(\beta+k\alpha)+O(kb^{-k}).
\end{equation}
In the rational case the phase sequence is periodic with period $q$, and
each residue class of $k$ has frequency $1/q$.

For irrational $\alpha$, each nonzero integer $h$ satisfies
\[
 \frac1K\sum_{k=1}^K e^{2\pi i h(\beta+k\alpha)}\longrightarrow0,
\]
by the finite geometric-sum formula, because
$e^{2\pi i h\alpha}\ne1$. Approximation of continuous periodic functions by
trigonometric polynomials gives uniform distribution of these phases.
Apply it to $f\circ P$. The orbit is dense, so continuity also gives the
full cluster interval. The vanishing rounding error changes neither the
cluster sets nor the limiting averages.
\end{proof}
For example, sampling $n=3^k$ produces the full growth interval and the
logarithmic limiting law, because $\log_2 3$ is irrational. In contrast,
sampling $n=2^k$ converges to the single phase-zero growth value. Exact
fundamental period one does not mean that different phases always have
different values: a continuous real periodic function cannot be injective
on its circle.

\section{Local ratios and the remaining monotonicity problem}
\label{dsr:sec:ratios}
The profile theorem should not be confused with a proof that $\theta(n)$
increases at every step. Ho explicitly distinguishes that open question from
the root-growth question \cite{Ho}. The following exact identity shows where
the difficulty resides.

\begin{proposition}[Binary descent for adjacent logarithmic ratios]
\label{dsr:prop:ratio}
Let $d_n=a_{n+1}-a_n$. Then $d_1=L$, and for $n\ge2$,
\begin{equation}
 d_n=d_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}+r_{n+1}-r_n.
\label{dsr:eq:d-rec}
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\begin{equation}
 L-D\lfloor\log_2n\rfloor\le d_n
       \le L+D\lfloor\log_2n\rfloor,
\label{dsr:eq:d-bound}
\end{equation}
and, with $\gamma=\log_2(21/2)$,
\begin{equation}
 2n^{-\gamma}\le\frac{\theta(n+1)}{\theta(n)}\le2n^\gamma.
\label{dsr:eq:ratio-bound}
\end{equation}
The elementary deletion bound improves the upper side to $n+1$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For $n=2m$, subtract the recurrences for $a_{2m+1}$ and $a_{2m}$; for
$n=2m+1$, subtract those for $a_{2m+2}$ and $a_{2m+1}$. Both subtractions
produce \eqref{dsr:eq:d-rec}. Iteration reaches index one after exactly
$\lfloor\log_2n\rfloor$ steps. Each toll difference is in $[-D,D]$, proving
\eqref{dsr:eq:d-bound}; exponentiation gives \eqref{dsr:eq:ratio-bound}.
For the deletion bound, remove the entry $n+1$ from a 3AP-free permutation
of $[n+1]$. The remaining ordering avoids all progressions in $[n]$. Together
with its original insertion position, this is an injective encoding into
at most $(n+1)\theta(n)$ possibilities.
\end{proof}

The lower bound in \eqref{dsr:eq:ratio-bound} is positive but falls below one; it
therefore does not solve monotonicity. Proving $d_n\ge0$ would require more
information about the correlated toll differences along binary descent than
the independent bounds $r_n\in[L,U]$ supply.

The nondegenerate growth interval also prevents eventual log-convexity or
eventual log-concavity. Either property would make adjacent logarithmic
ratios eventually monotone. The exponential bounds on $a_n$ would then
force their Ces\`aro means $a_n/n$ to converge, contradicting
Theorem~\ref{dsr:thm:spectrum}. This is a limitation of possible regularity
arguments, not a substitute for the unresolved monotonicity question.

\begin{remark}[Adjacent ratios have no limit \wtag]
\label{dsr:rem:ratio}
The same Ces\`aro argument shows that $\theta(n+1)/\theta(n)$ has no limit
in $[0,\infty]$. A finite positive limit $\lambda$ would give
$d_n\to\log\lambda$ and hence $b_n\to\log\lambda$, contradicting
Theorem~\ref{dsr:thm:spectrum} with $p_-<p_+$ (or Ho's theorem \cite{Ho});
the limits $0$ and $\infty$ would force $b_n\to-\infty$ and
$b_n\to+\infty$ respectively, contradicting
\eqref{dsr:eq:rough}. The special case that the ratio does not tend to $2$ is
machine-checked in \repo\ (Section~\ref{dsr:sec:formal}); the general
statement is not formalized. The upper side of
Proposition~\ref{dsr:prop:ratio} is also improved in Lean, to
$\theta(n+1)\le\lfloor(n+3)/2\rfloor\,\theta(n)$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Why the independent enumeration is correct}
For a fixed $n$, represent the set of values already placed in a prefix by
$S\subseteq[n]$. A candidate next value $y\notin S$ is allowed if, for every
$d\ge1$ with $y-d,y+d\in[n]$, either both endpoints belong to $S$ or neither
does. Equivalently,
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf1_S(y-d)=\mathbf1_S(y+d).
\label{dsr:eq:DP-test}
\end{equation}
Let $C(S)$ be the number of continuations generated by this rule, with
$C([n])=1$ and
\begin{equation}
 C(S)=\sum_{\substack{y\notin S\\y\ \mathrm{allowed}}}C(S\cup\{y\}).
\label{dsr:eq:DP}
\end{equation}
Then $C(\varnothing)=\theta(n)$.

To prove this, consider a complete permutation and a prospective middle
value $y$ of an arithmetic progression. At the moment $y$ is placed, that
progression appears as a subsequence if and only if exactly one of its two
endpoints has already appeared. Thus \eqref{dsr:eq:DP-test} excludes exactly the
forbidden full permutations. Every full permutation corresponds to exactly
one chain of subsets and choices, so \eqref{dsr:eq:DP} counts each permitted
permutation once. The condition depends only on the used set, which justifies
memoization; no unrecorded ordering information is needed for this rule.
This is an independently written implementation of the standard subset-state
idea, not a claim of a new enumeration algorithm \cite{CorrellHo}.

\subsection{Certificate design for a proof assistant}
A practical formalization can be layered rather than beginning with the
analytic limit.

First prove the finite permutation definition, the parity injection, and
soundness of \eqref{dsr:eq:DP}. Next expose Sharma's estimate as an explicit
imported theorem or an explicitly named hypothesis until its full
combinatorial proof has been formalized. Define $a_n$ and $r_n$, prove the
piecewise-affine interpolation lemma, and construct $F$ by uniform convergence
of the geometric tail. This supplies the periodic profile without any
numerical experiments.

The period and gluing proofs then reduce to a small finite certificate over
natural-number powers, plus the already proved interpolation envelope.
A verified certificate evaluator should distinguish ``the table entry is the
count'' from ``the inequality involving that entry is true.'' Finally,
formalize the density and integral arguments on compact intervals. This
separation prevents a conditional analytic theorem from being reported as an
unconditional theorem about the combinatorial sequence.

The present package contains no Lean file asserting these results. The route
just described is an implementation plan, not an executed formalization.

\wtag\ Part of the first layer already exists in \repo, and the second
layer's ``imported theorem'' is not needed there: the permutation
definition, the parity construction and both parity recurrences, the count
identification between the three papers' definitions, and Sharma's
Theorem~2.8 itself are proved in Lean (Section~\ref{dsr:sec:formal}). The

profile, and every later layer are not formalized. The existing finite-count

\section{Further research questions and topics}
\label{dsr:sec:questions}
The results narrow several questions without resolving all of the fine
structure. The following proposed program separates concrete next steps from
more speculative extensions.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\arabic*.},leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Extremizing phases and sharp constants.}
Determine the exact values or substantially narrower enclosures for
$p_\pm$, and characterize their maximizing and minimizing phase sets. The
certificate used here does not show that $P(0)$ is a global maximum, nor
that either extremum has a unique phase. Larger count bands immediately
produce better certified enclosures, while a structural argument could be
far more informative than additional enumeration.

\item \textbf{Regularity beyond the general bound.}
Is $P$ Lipschitz? Is it differentiable at typical phases, at any dyadic
phase, or on a set of full measure? Is the logarithmic loss in
\eqref{dsr:eq:modulus} actually necessary? These questions require information
about cancellations between toll increments; boundedness alone does not
settle them.

\item \textbf{The distributions of growth values.}
Determine whether the logarithmic pushforward law has atoms, a density, or
a singular component. Study the dependence of the ordinary laws $\nu_x$
on $x$, including when two distinct cutoff phases produce the same law.
The formula \eqref{dsr:eq:nu} is exact, but it does not determine the regularity
of the pushforward without further knowledge of level sets of $P$.

\item \textbf{The bounded multiplicative correction.}
Understand $Q(n)$ and the tolls $r_n$ along dyadic and other arithmetic
subsequences. A limiting correction would strengthen a phase-dependent
exponential envelope to an asymptotic equivalence on that subsequence.
Nothing proved here implies such convergence, and a nontrivial limiting
law for the corrections may be more realistic than a constant prefactor.

\item \textbf{Monotonicity and adjacent ratios.}
Decide whether $\theta(n+1)\ge\theta(n)$ always holds. The binary-descent
identity \eqref{dsr:eq:d-rec} offers a precise target: control a sum of
correlated toll differences, not merely each difference separately.
A combinatorial injection would resolve monotonicity but must accommodate
the unequal-size exponential obstructions proved in this paper.
\wtag\ This is Sharma's open problem~(1), recorded without proof in \repo\
as \lean{Sharma2012.open_problem_1}.

\item \textbf{Sharper interleaving bounds and effective computation.}
Can the uniform factor $21$ be reduced, perhaps beyond a finite threshold,
or replaced by a phase-sensitive bound? Either improvement would sharpen
all the enclosures automatically. Separately, develop an independently
checkable enumeration pipeline for larger count bands with explicit memory,
time, and certificate-size accounting.

\item \textbf{Machine-checked end-to-end certificates.}
Formalize the complete implication from a definition of $\theta$ and a
verified finite-count evaluator to the phase gap, exact period, and gluing
inequalities. The analytic construction is reusable for other balanced
recurrences. The main proof-engineering challenge is avoiding hidden large
count assumptions while keeping the certificate small.
\wtag\ In \repo\ the definition of $\theta$, both splitting recurrences
(including Sharma's upper one) and the values through $n=20$ are already
formalized (Section~\ref{dsr:sec:formal}). What remains is the verified
evaluator for the counts the certificates use ($100\le n\le200$) without a
native trust boundary, the analytic profile, and the certificate
implications themselves.

\item \textbf{Other avoidance classes and generating functions.}
Search for analogous bounded-toll recurrences in longer-progression
avoidance or other permutation classes. Ask whether the resulting profile
can have a smaller fundamental period or whether an exact scale group can
again be read from finite data. For $\sum\theta(n)z^n$, investigate what the
nonconstant exponential profile implies about analytic continuation or
algebraic/differential equations. No non-D-finiteness or natural-boundary
claim is proved here.

\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
A nonexistent growth constant need not signal unstructured behavior. For
3AP-free permutations, the balanced combinatorial recurrence yields a unique
continuous logarithmic phase profile with a bounded correction. Published
finite data do more than distinguish two limits: they certify the profile's
exact fundamental period. That finite certificate determines the entire
real scaling-symmetry group and forces exponential gain and loss at every
off-dyadic scale.

The explicit $2{:}1$ gluing inequalities show a concrete consequence for
combinatorial constructions, while the empirical laws explain which forms of
averaging preserve or erase the cutoff phase. These results leave the fine
regularity, precise extrema, bounded corrections, and monotonicity of the
counting sequence as well-defined further problems rather than concealing
them inside an unsupported asymptotic ansatz.

\textbf{Abstract of Part II.}
We establish non-P-recursiveness of the number $\tht(n)$ of
three-term-arithmetic-progression-free permutations, OEIS A003407, and of

positive integer sequences with rational generating functions can have
nonconvergent $n$th roots. We instead prove a finite-window rigidity lemma.
A nonzero leading Nilsson expansion has a fixed-length window whose maximum

sequence with subexponential forward increases must have an exponential
growth rate. For $\tht$, deleting the largest entry gives the required
one-sided local estimate, whereas Boon Suan Ho's 2026 theorem supplies

arithmetic sampling, positive polynomial observables, and multiplicative

\section{The question and the contribution}
\label{dsr:nh:sec:intro}
A permutation $\pi=(\pi_1,\ldots,\pi_n)$ of $[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}$ is
\emph{3AP-free} if no indices $i<j<k$ satisfy
\begin{equation}
\pi_i+\pi_k=2\pi_j.
\label{dsr:nh:eq:avoid}
\end{equation}
The positions need not be consecutive, and progressions in both numerical
directions are forbidden. Let $\tht(n)$ count these permutations, with
$\tht(0)=1$. The first values are
\[
1,1,2,4,10,20,48,104,282,496,1066,2460,6128,\ldots.
\]
This is OEIS A003407 \cite{OEIS}.

The distinction between understanding a sequence's growth and understanding
its exact generating function is particularly sharp here. Ho proved that
$\tht(n)^{1/n}$ has no limit \cite{Ho}. The recent \repo\ report
\emph{Dyadic Scaling Rigidity and Log-Periodic Growth of 3AP-Free
Permutations} develops a continuous periodic growth profile and its
consequences, but explicitly makes no non-D-finiteness or natural-boundary
claim \partref{Section~\ref{dsr:sec:questions}, item~8}. We resolve the former issue, not the latter.

\subsection{Precise statements}
\begin{definition}
A sequence $(u_n)$ is \emph{P-recursive over a field $K$} if there are
polynomials $p_0,\ldots,p_d\in K[n]$, not all zero, such that
\begin{equation}
\sum_{j=0}^{d}p_j(n)u_{n+j}=0
\label{dsr:nh:eq:prec}
\end{equation}
for every sufficiently large integer $n$. A formal power series is
\emph{D-finite over $K(z)$} if it satisfies a nonzero homogeneous linear
differential equation with coefficients in $K[z]$.
\end{definition}
Allowing an arbitrary starting index in \eqref{dsr:nh:eq:prec} is important:
non-P-recursiveness below excludes eventual recurrences, not merely
recurrences valid from $n=0$.

\begin{theorem}[Main result]
\label{dsr:nh:thm:main}
For every pair of integers $q\ge1$ and $r\ge0$, the sequence
\[
\bigl(\tht(qn+r)\bigr)_{n\ge0}
\]
is not P-recursive over $\C$. In particular, neither
\begin{equation}
F(z)=\sum_{n\ge0}\tht(n)z^n,
\qquad
E(z)=\sum_{n\ge0}\tht(n)\frac{z^n}{n!}
\label{dsr:nh:eq:generating}
\end{equation}
is D-finite over $\C(z)$.
\end{theorem}
The ordinary generating function has positive finite radius of convergence;
the exponential generating function is entire. Thus their non-D-finiteness
concerns two rather different analytic regimes.

\begin{theorem}[Robustness under approximation]
\label{dsr:nh:thm:robustintro}
Fix integers $q\ge1$, $r\ge0$ and a real number $d>0$.
There is no eventually positive, integer-valued P-recursive sequence $(v_n)$
for which
\begin{equation}
\log v_n=d\log\tht(qn+r)+o(n).
\label{dsr:nh:eq:proxy}
\end{equation}
The same conclusion holds for positive rational $v_n$ if the least common
denominator of $v_0,\ldots,v_n$ is at most $C^{n+1}$ for some fixed $C$.
\end{theorem}
The approximation condition is pointwise as $n\to\infty$, not an average
condition or a condition on selected subsequences. It rules out, for example,
a P-recursive positive integer surrogate within a polynomial factor of the
counts. It does not rule out approximations with unrestricted real
coefficients or uncontrolled rational denominators.

\begin{theorem}[Positive polynomial observables]
\label{dsr:nh:thm:polyintro}
Let $q\ge1$, $r,s\ge0$ be integers, and let
\[
\Phi\in\Z_{\ge0}[N,X_0,\ldots,X_s]
\]
be a polynomial that depends on at least one $X_j$. Then
\begin{equation}
v_n=\Phi\bigl(n,\tht(qn+r),\tht(qn+r+1),\ldots,\tht(qn+r+s)\bigr)
\label{dsr:nh:eq:observable}
\end{equation}
is not P-recursive over $\C$.
\end{theorem}
This includes powers of the counts, products of finitely many shifted
counts, and positive polynomially weighted finite sums. Nonnegative
coefficients are a genuine hypothesis: the argument does not control
cancellation in signed combinations.

\section{The enumerative inputs}

\subsection{A local estimate needing no structural classification}
\begin{lemma}[Deletion bound]
\label{dsr:nh:lem:deletion}
For every $n\ge0$,
\begin{equation}
\tht(n+1)\le(n+1)\tht(n).
\label{dsr:nh:eq:deletion}
\end{equation}
Consequently, for fixed $j\ge0$ and $n\ge1$,
\begin{equation}
\tht(n+j)\le (n+j)^j\tht(n).
\label{dsr:nh:eq:forwardwindow}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Delete the largest entry $n+1$ from a 3AP-free permutation of $[n+1]$.
The remaining permutation of $[n]$ is still 3AP-free, because deleting an
entry cannot create a previously absent subsequence. The original
permutation is determined by the remaining permutation and the position at
which $n+1$ was inserted. There are at most $n+1$ positions. This is an
injection into a set of cardinality $(n+1)\tht(n)$. Iterating the estimate
proves \eqref{dsr:nh:eq:forwardwindow}.
\end{proof}
No monotonicity assertion is used. In particular, we do not assume
$\tht(n+1)\ge\tht(n)$.

\wtag\ This is the deletion bound at the end of Part~I's
Proposition~\ref{dsr:prop:ratio}. \repo\ proves the stronger bound
$\tht(n+1)\le\lfloor(n+3)/2\rfloor\,\tht(n)$, $n\ge1$, in Lean as
\lean{insertion_count_upper_bound_holds}
(Subsection~\ref{dsr:nh:sec:formal}); the argument below needs only the
weaker one.

\subsection{The balanced splitting inequalities}
\begin{proposition}[Classical splitting estimates]
\label{dsr:nh:prop:split}
For every integer $n\ge2$,
\begin{equation}
2\tht(\lfloor n/2\rfloor)\tht(\lceil n/2\rceil)
\le \tht(n)
\le21\tht(\lfloor n/2\rfloor)\tht(\lceil n/2\rceil).
\label{dsr:nh:eq:split}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
The lower estimate is the parity construction of Davis, Entringer, Graham,
and Simmons \cite{DEGS}. For completeness, order the even entries and the
odd entries separately by affine copies of smaller 3AP-free permutations,
and concatenate the blocks in either order. The endpoints of an integer
three-term progression have the same parity. They either lie in a common
3AP-free block with their midpoint, or their midpoint lies in the other
block and cannot occur between them. The two block orders give disjoint
families, proving the factor $2$.

The upper estimate for $n\ge3$ is Sharma's Theorem~2.8 \cite{Sharma}; its
proof is imported. At $n=2$ it follows from $\tht(2)=2$. We do not infer
this upper estimate from a fit to finite data.

\wtag\ Proposition~\ref{dsr:nh:prop:split} is Part~I's
\eqref{dsr:eq:lower} and \eqref{dsr:eq:upper}. In \repo\ both halves are
Lean theorems: the upper one for $n\ge3$ is
\lean{LeanProofs.Sharma2012.theorem_2_8_holds}, the lower one is
\lean{even_count_recurrence_holds} and \lean{odd_count_recurrence_holds}
(Remark~\ref{dsr:rem:inputs-formal}). Nothing derived from them below is
formalized (Subsection~\ref{dsr:nh:sec:formal}).

Write
\begin{equation}
a_n=\log\tht(n),\quad L=\log2,\quad U=\log21,\quad D=U-L,
\label{dsr:nh:eq:constants}
\end{equation}
and, for $n\ge2$, define
\begin{equation}
\tau_n=a_n-a_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}-a_{\lceil n/2\rceil}.
\label{dsr:nh:eq:toll}
\end{equation}
Thus $L\le\tau_n\le U$.

\begin{lemma}[Exponential bounds]
\label{dsr:nh:lem:exponential}
For $n\ge1$,
\begin{equation}
2^{n-1}\le\tht(n)\le21^{n-1}.
\label{dsr:nh:eq:rough}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The logarithmic version holds at $n=1$. In a strong induction step, the
two smaller indices sum to $n$, so their lower bounds sum to $(n-2)L$.
Adding $\tau_n\ge L$ gives $(n-1)L$. The upper bound is identical with
$U$ in place of $L$.
\end{proof}
The much sharper published upper bound $\tht(n)\le2.7^n/21$ for $n\ge11$
\cite{Sharma} is unnecessary for our argument. The deliberately weak
bound \eqref{dsr:nh:eq:rough} already provides all the arithmetic growth control
needed below.

\subsection{A two-sided shift estimate for the stronger corollaries}
The main non-P-recursiveness theorem needs only
Lemma~\ref{dsr:nh:lem:deletion}. Positive polynomial observables will also use
a lower comparison for nearby counts.

\begin{lemma}[Binary descent for adjacent logarithms]
\label{dsr:nh:lem:ratios}
Put $\Delta_n=a_{n+1}-a_n$ for $n\ge1$, and
$\gamma=D/\log2=\log_2(21/2)$. Then
\begin{equation}
\abs{\Delta_n-\log2}\le D\lfloor\log_2 n\rfloor,
\qquad
2n^{-\gamma}\le\frac{\tht(n+1)}{\tht(n)}\le2n^{\gamma}.
\label{dsr:nh:eq:twosided}
\end{equation}
For each fixed integer $s\ge0$,
\begin{equation}
\max_{0\le j\le s}\abs{a_{n+j}-a_n}=O_s(\log(n+1)).
\label{dsr:nh:eq:shiftflat}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For $m\ge1$, subtracting two consecutive instances of
\eqref{dsr:nh:eq:toll} gives
\begin{align}
\Delta_{2m}&=\Delta_m+\tau_{2m+1}-\tau_{2m},\\
\Delta_{2m+1}&=\Delta_m+\tau_{2m+2}-\tau_{2m+1}.
\end{align}
Thus $\Delta_n=\Delta_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}+\varepsilon_n$ for $n\ge2$,
where $|\varepsilon_n|\le D$. Iterating reaches $\Delta_1=\log2$ after
$\lfloor\log_2 n\rfloor$ steps. Exponentiation and summation over a fixed
number of adjacent differences give the remaining statements.
\end{proof}
The same descent identity appears in the prior repository report
\partref{Proposition~\ref{dsr:prop:ratio}}; it is restated and reproved here as an input, not
claimed as a new result.

\section{The two distinct exponential scales}
\label{dsr:nh:sec:separation}
The only numerical facts used in the infinite nonconvergence argument are
the two published counts displayed below. The deduction from them is exact.

\begin{lemma}[Dyadic limits and their enclosures]
\label{dsr:nh:lem:dyadic}
For every $m\ge1$, the sequence
\[
\frac{a_{m2^t}}{m2^t}\quad(t\ge0)
\]
converges to a finite number $\ell_m$, and
\begin{equation}
\frac{\log(2\tht(m))}{m}\le\ell_m
\le\frac{\log(21\tht(m))}{m}.
\label{dsr:nh:eq:dyadicbounds}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
At even indices the recurrence is $a_{2k}=2a_k+\tau_{2k}$, so
\[
\frac{a_{m2^{t+1}}}{m2^{t+1}}
-\frac{a_{m2^t}}{m2^t}
=\frac{\tau_{m2^{t+1}}}{m2^{t+1}}.
\]
The increments form an absolutely summable series. Summing their lower and
upper bounds gives respectively $L/m$ and $U/m$, proving
\eqref{dsr:nh:eq:dyadicbounds}.
\end{proof}

The published values used by Ho \cite{Ho}, also in the Correll--Ho/OEIS
data \cite{CorrellHo,OEIS}, are
\begin{align}
T_{64}:=\tht(64)&=39911512393313043466768,\\
T_{75}:=\tht(75)&=30235147387260979648843264.
\label{dsr:nh:eq:largecounts}
\end{align}
They yield the exact strict comparison
\begin{equation}
(2T_{64})^{75}>(21T_{75})^{64}.
\label{dsr:nh:eq:exactsep}
\end{equation}
Equivalently,
\begin{equation}
\ell_{64}\ge\frac{\log(2T_{64})}{64}
>\frac{\log(21T_{75})}{75}\ge\ell_{75}.
\label{dsr:nh:eq:logsep}
\end{equation}
For a short rational certificate, the accompanying program verifies
\begin{align}
(2T_{64})1000^{64}&>2279^{64},\\
(21T_{75})500^{75}&<1139^{75}.
\end{align}
Hence $e^{\ell_{64}}>2.279$ and $e^{\ell_{75}}<2.278$. In particular,
\begin{equation}
\ell_{64}-\ell_{75}>
\log\frac{2279}{2278}>\frac1{2279}.
\label{dsr:nh:eq:rationalgap}
\end{equation}
The last strict inequality follows by integrating $1/t$ on
$[1,1+1/2278]$, or from $\log(1+x)>x/(1+x)$ for $x>0$.

\begin{theorem}[Ho's growth nonconvergence]
\label{dsr:nh:thm:ho}
The sequence $\tht(n)^{1/n}$ has no limit.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The subsequences $n=64\cdot2^t$ and $n=75\cdot2^t$ converge to the
distinct limits $e^{\ell_{64}}$ and $e^{\ell_{75}}$ by
Lemma~\ref{dsr:nh:lem:dyadic} and \eqref{dsr:nh:eq:logsep}.
\end{proof}
This is a reproduction of the published mechanism \cite{Ho}, not a new
claim of nonconvergence. The decimal separation between the two enclosure
endpoints is approximately $0.00109603287135$ on the logarithmic scale.
The exact inequalities, not that decimal, are the proof certificates.

Theorem~\ref{dsr:nh:thm:ho} directly from the published paper. These are two
presentations of the same dependency, not two independent verifications of
the large enumeration.
\end{remark}

\section{From a polynomial recurrence to a finite-window law}
\label{dsr:nh:sec:window}
This section contains the central inference. We separate the published
asymptotic input from the finite-dimensional argument that uses it.

\subsection{Descending complex recurrence coefficients}
\begin{lemma}[Rational descent]
\label{dsr:nh:lem:descent}
A rational-valued sequence that is P-recursive over $\C$ is P-recursive
over $\Q$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Fix the order $d$, a coefficient degree bound $e$, and a starting index
$N$ for a complex recurrence. Its coefficients form a nonzero vector
in $\C^{(d+1)(e+1)}$ annihilated by every rational row
\[
\bigl(n^k u_{n+j}\bigr)_{0\le j\le d,\;0\le k\le e},
\qquad n\ge N.
\]
The rational span of these rows has a basis consisting of finitely many
rows. Their matrix has the same rank over $\Q$ and $\C$, since rank is
characterized by nonzero minors. Its complex kernel is nonzero, so its
rational kernel is nonzero. A nonzero rational vector in that kernel
annihilates every original row, producing the required rational recurrence.
\end{proof}
This also makes clear why no restriction to rational recurrence
coefficients is hidden in Theorem~\ref{dsr:nh:thm:main}.

\subsection{The arithmetic asymptotic input}
For a rational sequence $(u_n)$, let $b_n$ be the least positive integer
such that $b_nu_0,\ldots,b_nu_n$ are all integers. We say that its
\emph{common denominators grow exponentially} if $b_n\le C^{n+1}$ for some
$C$. For integer sequences this holds with $b_n=1$.

\begin{theorem}[Published G-function input]
\label{dsr:nh:thm:ginput}
Suppose $(u_n)$ is rational-valued, not eventually zero, and P-recursive
over $\Q$, with $|u_n|\le C^{n+1}$ and exponentially growing common
denominators. Then its generating series is a G-function. Its coefficients
have a nonzero leading asymptotic layer of the form
\begin{equation}
u_n=R^n n^\alpha(\log n)^\beta
\left(\sum_{j=1}^{s}c_j\zeta_j^n+o(1)\right),
\label{dsr:nh:eq:leading}
\end{equation}
where $R>0$, $\alpha\in\Q$, $\beta\in\Z_{\ge0}$, the $\zeta_j$ are distinct
complex numbers of modulus $1$, and the $c_j$ are nonzero complex constants.
\end{theorem}
The G-function criterion and coefficient expansion are the relevant parts
of Garoufalidis's Theorem~3 and Proposition~2.5 \cite{Gar09}; the precise
nonzero leading-layer convention is explained in \cite{Gar11}. The underlying
arithmetic theorem makes the minimal differential operator regular singular
with rational exponents \cite{Andre}. These are the deep imported results.
We do not appeal to a conjectural geometric origin of G-functions.

To clarify the reduction to \eqref{dsr:nh:eq:leading}, start with the finite
power-logarithmic families at the dominant singularities, collect equal
frequencies, and choose the largest power of $n$ having a nonzero
coefficient, then the largest logarithmic power within it. The remaining
terms are $o(1)$ after the displayed normalization. Contributions from
singularities farther away are exponentially smaller. Regular singularity
is essential to this explanation: quasi-unipotent monodromy alone, without
control of singular growth, would not justify such a local form.

\subsection{Why cancellation cannot persist through a whole window}
\begin{lemma}[Finite-window noncancellation]
\label{dsr:nh:lem:window}
Suppose a complex sequence satisfies \eqref{dsr:nh:eq:leading}. There are constants
$c,C>0$ and $n_0$ such that, for $n\ge n_0$,
\begin{equation}
cR^n n^\alpha(\log n)^\beta
\le \max_{0\le k<s}|u_{n+k}|
\le CR^n n^\alpha(\log n)^\beta.
\label{dsr:nh:eq:window}
\end{equation}
In particular,
\begin{equation}
\lim_{n\to\infty}
\left(\max_{0\le k<s}|u_{n+k}|\right)^{1/n}=R.
\label{dsr:nh:eq:windowroot}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Write $H_n=R^n n^\alpha(\log n)^\beta$. For each fixed $k$,
\[
\frac{H_{n+k}}{H_n}=R^k(1+o(1)).
\]
Since there are only $s$ values of $k$, the errors are uniform for
$0\le k<s$. Define the $s\times s$ matrix
\[
V_{kj}=(R\zeta_j)^k,
\qquad 0\le k<s,\quad1\le j\le s,
\]
and the column vector $w_n=(c_j\zeta_j^n)_{j=1}^{s}$. Then
\begin{equation}
\left(\frac{u_{n+k}}{H_n}\right)_{k=0}^{s-1}=Vw_n+o(1)
\label{dsr:nh:eq:vector}
\end{equation}
in the maximum norm. The Vandermonde determinant is
\[
\det V=\prod_{1\le i<j\le s}(R\zeta_j-R\zeta_i)\ne0.
\]
Moreover, $\|w_n\|_\infty=c_*:=\max_j|c_j|>0$ for every $n$. Thus
\[
\|Vw_n\|_\infty\ge\frac{c_*}{\|V^{-1}\|_\infty}>0,
\qquad
\|Vw_n\|_\infty\le\|V\|_\infty c_*.
\]
After increasing $n_0$, the error in \eqref{dsr:nh:eq:vector} is less than half
of the lower constant. This proves \eqref{dsr:nh:eq:window}. Finally,
$H_n^{1/n}\to R$, because $\log n/n$ and $\log\log n/n$ tend to zero.
\end{proof}
The phases may be roots of unity, algebraic points with irrational
arguments, or arbitrary distinct unit complex numbers in this lemma.
No equidistribution or Diophantine lower bound for a single oscillating
sum is required.

\subsection{One-sided local growth is enough}
\begin{definition}
An eventually positive sequence has \emph{subexponential forward
increases} if
\begin{equation}
\logp\frac{u_{n+1}}{u_n}=o(n),
\qquad \logp x=\max\{0,\log x\}.
\label{dsr:nh:eq:local}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}
This is a one-sided condition. It permits downward jumps, and is weaker
than $\log(u_{n+1}/u_n)=o(n)$.

\begin{theorem}[Local-growth rigidity]
\label{dsr:nh:thm:rigidity}
Let $(u_n)$ be eventually positive, rational-valued, exponentially bounded,
and have exponentially growing common denominators. If it is P-recursive
over $\C$ and satisfies \eqref{dsr:nh:eq:local}, then
\begin{equation}
\lim_{n\to\infty}u_n^{1/n}=R
\label{dsr:nh:eq:rootlimit}
\end{equation}
exists in $(0,\infty)$. In particular, the assertion applies to positive
integer sequences with $u_{n+1}/u_n\le Cn^A$ for fixed $C,A>0$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Rational descent and Theorem~\ref{dsr:nh:thm:ginput} give a leading layer
\eqref{dsr:nh:eq:leading}. Set $W_n=\max_{0\le k<s}u_{n+k}$. If
$h_n=\logp(u_{n+1}/u_n)$, then for every $k<s$,
\[
\log u_{n+k}\le\log u_n+\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}h_{n+i}.
\]
The finite sum is $o(n)$, uniformly over these $k$. Since $u_n\le W_n$,
\[
0\le\log W_n-\log u_n\le o(n).
\]
Lemma~\ref{dsr:nh:lem:window} gives $\log W_n/n\to\log R$, so
$\log u_n/n\to\log R$ as well. Exponentiation proves
\eqref{dsr:nh:eq:rootlimit}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[A necessary jump phenomenon]
\label{dsr:nh:cor:jumps}
If an eventually positive, exponentially bounded, integer P-recursive
sequence has no $n$th-root limit, then there is $\varepsilon>0$ such that
\[
\frac{u_{n+1}}{u_n}>e^{\varepsilon n}
\]
for infinitely many $n$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Otherwise the nonnegative sequence in the left side of
\eqref{dsr:nh:eq:local}, divided by $n$, tends to zero, contradicting
Theorem~\ref{dsr:nh:thm:rigidity}.
\end{proof}
This is the appropriate obstruction: persistent exponential-scale
oscillation in an arithmetic P-recursive sequence requires exponentially
large upward transitions somewhere. The deletion estimate prevents them
for the permutation counts.

\section{Proof of non-P-recursiveness and all arithmetic sections}
\label{dsr:nh:sec:mainproof}
\subsection{The base sequence}
Suppose $\tht(n)$ were P-recursive over $\C$. It is positive and integral,
and Lemma~\ref{dsr:nh:lem:exponential} bounds it exponentially. Furthermore,
\eqref{dsr:nh:eq:deletion} implies
\[
\logp\frac{\tht(n+1)}{\tht(n)}\le\log(n+1)=o(n).
\]
Theorem~\ref{dsr:nh:thm:rigidity} would force $\tht(n)^{1/n}$ to converge,
contradicting Theorem~\ref{dsr:nh:thm:ho}. This proves the base case of
Theorem~\ref{dsr:nh:thm:main}.

\subsection{A bounded-gap interpolation principle}
The all-sections assertion is stronger than the base case: a
non-P-recursive sequence may have P-recursive sections. Here the local
estimate prevents that escape.

\begin{lemma}[A growth rate on one arithmetic section forces a global rate]
\label{dsr:nh:lem:sectionlimit}
Let $(u_m)$ be positive and exponentially bounded, with subexponential
forward increases. Fix integers $q\ge1$, $r\ge0$. If
\[
\lim_{n\to\infty}u_{qn+r}^{1/n}=B\in(0,\infty),
\]
then $u_m^{1/m}\to B^{1/q}$ as $m\to\infty$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For large $m$, choose $n$ with $x=qn+r\le m<y=q(n+1)+r$.
Let $h_k=\logp(u_{k+1}/u_k)=o(k)$. At most $q$ consecutive terms occur
in either of the following sums, so
\[
\log u_m\le\log u_x+\sum_{k=x}^{m-1}h_k
=\log u_x+o(m),
\]
and
\[
\log u_m\ge\log u_y-\sum_{k=m}^{y-1}h_k
=\log u_y-o(m).
\]
Now $n/m\to1/q$, and the two bounding logarithms, divided by $m$,
both tend to $(\log B)/q$. The conclusion follows by squeezing.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of the arithmetic-section part of Theorem~\ref{dsr:nh:thm:main}]
Fix $q,r$ and write $v_n=\tht(qn+r)$. This is a positive integer sequence
bounded above by $21^{qn+r}$. Repeated deletion yields
\[
\frac{v_{n+1}}{v_n}
\le\prod_{j=1}^{q}(qn+r+j)
\le(qn+r+q)^q.
\]
If $v_n$ were P-recursive, Theorem~\ref{dsr:nh:thm:rigidity} would give a finite
positive limit for $v_n^{1/n}$. Lemma~\ref{dsr:nh:lem:sectionlimit} would then give
a global $n$th-root limit for $\tht(n)$, contradicting
Theorem~\ref{dsr:nh:thm:ho}.
\end{proof}
Notice that neither the continuous phase profile nor the two-sided
estimate \eqref{dsr:nh:eq:twosided} is needed for this proof. The stronger
all-sections conclusion already follows from one-sided insertion control.

\subsection{Explicit separated limits within every section}
For later applications it is useful to see the two scales directly.
For large $t$, let
\[
n_t^{(m)}=\left\lfloor\frac{m2^t-r}{q}\right\rfloor,
\qquad m\in\{64,75\}.
\]
The number $qn_t^{(m)}+r$ differs from $m2^t$ by less than $q$.
By Lemma~\ref{dsr:nh:lem:ratios}, their logarithmic counts differ by $O_q(t+1)$.
Therefore
\begin{equation}
\frac{\log\tht(qn_t^{(m)}+r)}{n_t^{(m)}}\longrightarrow q\ell_m.
\label{dsr:nh:eq:sectionphases}
\end{equation}
The two limiting logarithmic rates are separated by more than
$q/2279$. This construction provides explicit infinite subsequences;
it does not rely on identifying the global extrema of the phase profile.

\section{Stable non-holonomicity}
\label{dsr:nh:sec:stable}
\subsection{Subexponential multiplicative perturbations}
\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{dsr:nh:thm:robustintro}]
Assume a sequence $v_n$ as in the statement exists, and put
$b_n=\log\tht(qn+r)$ and $e_n=\log v_n-db_n$. Then $e_n=o(n)$.
The exponential bound for $\tht$ shows $v_n\le C^{n+1}$ after increasing
$C$ to absorb a finite prefix. Repeated deletion gives
$b_{n+1}-b_n\le q\log(qn+r+q)$. Hence
\[
\log\frac{v_{n+1}}{v_n}
=d(b_{n+1}-b_n)+e_{n+1}-e_n
\le O(\log n)+|e_{n+1}|+|e_n|=o(n).
\]
Thus $v_n$ has subexponential forward increases. Its assumed integrality,
or its rationality and common-denominator bound, lets us apply
Theorem~\ref{dsr:nh:thm:rigidity}. Consequently $\log v_n/n$ converges.
But \eqref{dsr:nh:eq:proxy}, with $d>0$, then forces $b_n/n$ to converge,
contradicting Lemma~\ref{dsr:nh:lem:sectionlimit} and
Theorem~\ref{dsr:nh:thm:ho}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
\label{dsr:nh:cor:powers}
For every real $d>0$ and integers $q\ge1$, $r\ge0$, the integer sequence
\[
\left\lfloor\tht(qn+r)^d\right\rfloor
\]
is not P-recursive. In particular, every positive integral power of every
arithmetic section is non-P-recursive.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The lower bound \eqref{dsr:nh:eq:rough} makes $\tht(qn+r)^d$ exponentially large.
Taking its floor changes its logarithm by $o(1)$; apply
Theorem~\ref{dsr:nh:thm:robustintro}.
\end{proof}
Rounding is used only to produce integer coefficients. The assertion does
not require $d$ to be computable or algebraic, although an arbitrary real
$d$ need not furnish an effective algorithm for its rounded sequence.

\subsection{Positive polynomial observables}
\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{dsr:nh:thm:polyintro}]
Let $d\ge1$ be the largest total degree in the $X$-variables of a monomial
occurring in $\Phi$. Put $b_n=\tht(qn+r)$. For each fixed $j\le s$,
Lemma~\ref{dsr:nh:lem:ratios} gives
\[
\tht(qn+r+j)=b_n\exp(O(\log n)).
\]
A monomial $cN^k\prod_{j=0}^{s}X_j^{m_j}$ with
$h=\sum_jm_j$ and $c>0$ therefore evaluates to
\[
b_n^h\exp(O(\log n)).
\]
For $n\ge1$, every factor is positive. Select one occurring monomial of
$X$-degree $d$. It gives a lower bound $b_n^dn^{-A}$ for a sufficiently
large constant $A$. Since $b_n\ge1$, every occurring monomial is at most
$b_n^dn^A$ after enlarging $A$; summing the finitely many monomials gives
an upper bound of the same form. Thus
\begin{equation}
\log v_n=d\log b_n+O(\log n).
\label{dsr:nh:eq:polyscale}
\end{equation}
The sequence is eventually positive and integer-valued. Apply
Theorem~\ref{dsr:nh:thm:robustintro}.
\end{proof}
For example,
\[
\tht(n)^2+7n^3\tht(n+1),\qquad
\tht(3n+2)\tht(3n+5),\qquad
\sum_{j=0}^{s}(n+1)^j\tht(n+j)
\]
are all non-P-recursive. In the first expression the term of $X$-degree
$2$ determines the exponential scale; in the second, missing intermediate
shifts simply mean that their exponents in the polynomial are zero.

\subsection{The same obstruction excludes a leading Nilsson description}
\begin{corollary}
\label{dsr:nh:cor:notnilsson}
For every $q\ge1$, $r\ge0$, the sequence $\tht(qn+r)$ cannot have a
nonzero leading layer of the form \eqref{dsr:nh:eq:leading}. The same holds for
the sequences in Theorem~\ref{dsr:nh:thm:polyintro}.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
A leading layer alone, without any P-recursive hypothesis, gives the
window law by Lemma~\ref{dsr:nh:lem:window}. The local-growth part of the proof of
Theorem~\ref{dsr:nh:thm:rigidity} would then force an $n$th-root limit. For sections
this contradicts \eqref{dsr:nh:eq:sectionphases}. For polynomial observables,
\eqref{dsr:nh:eq:polyscale} gives two limits $dq\ell_{64}$ and $dq\ell_{75}$,
and also gives subexponential forward increases.
\end{proof}
Thus replacing a hoped-for exact recurrence by a finite dominant
power-logarithmic asymptotic ansatz does not remove the obstruction.

\section{Generating-function consequences and their limits}
\label{dsr:nh:sec:functions}
\subsection{Ordinary and exponential generating functions}
The standard coefficient--differential-equation equivalence says that
$\sum u_nz^n$ is D-finite exactly when $(u_n)$ is P-recursive
\cite[Theorem~1.5]{Stanley}. Theorem~\ref{dsr:nh:thm:main} therefore proves
non-D-finiteness of $F$ and of the ordinary generating function of every
section and every positive polynomial observable above.

For the exponential generating function, write $b_n=\tht(n)/n!$.
Multiplying a recurrence for $b_n$ by $(n+d)!$ gives
\[
\sum_{j=0}^{d}p_j(n)\frac{(n+d)!}{(n+j)!}\tht(n+j)=0,
\]
whose coefficients are polynomials. Conversely, a recurrence for $\tht(n)$
can be divided by $n!$ to give one for $b_n$, because
$(n+j)!/n!$ is a polynomial in $n$. These transformations preserve
nontriviality. Hence $E$ is D-finite if and only if $F$ is D-finite,
which completes the proof of \eqref{dsr:nh:eq:generating}'s assertion.

The bounds \eqref{dsr:nh:eq:rough} show that $F$ has radius in $[1/21,1/2]$.
They also give absolute convergence of $E$ everywhere, since
$|E(z)|\le\sum21^n|z|^n/n!=e^{21|z|}$. The latter bound is deliberately
nonoptimal.

\begin{corollary}
\label{dsr:nh:cor:transcendence}
The ordinary generating function of every sequence in
Theorems~\ref{dsr:nh:thm:main} and~\ref{dsr:nh:thm:polyintro} is transcendental over
$\C(z)$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
An algebraic germ $f$ belongs to the finite extension $\C(z,f)$ of
$\C(z)$. In characteristic zero, differentiation preserves that extension:
implicit differentiation of a separable minimal polynomial expresses $f'$
in the same field, and iteration does so for every derivative. Finitely
many derivatives must therefore be linearly dependent over $\C(z)$.
Thus an algebraic germ is D-finite, contrary to the established results.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Why nonconvergence by itself was insufficient}
\begin{example}[A positive rational counterexample to the naive argument]
\label{dsr:nh:ex:parity}
Let
\[
u_n=3^n+(-3)^n+2^n.
\]
For even $n$, $u_n=2\cdot3^n+2^n$; for odd $n$, $u_n=2^n$.
The sequence is positive and integral, its $n$th roots tend to $3$ on even
indices and to $2$ on odd indices, and yet its generating function is
\[
\frac1{1-3z}+\frac1{1+3z}+\frac1{1-2z}.
\]
It satisfies a constant-coefficient recurrence. The ratio from an odd index
to the next even index is exponentially large, exactly as
Corollary~\ref{dsr:nh:cor:jumps} requires. A two-term window cannot remain small.
\end{example}
The finite-window argument is consequently not a cosmetic addition. It
eliminates a real counterexample mechanism that a bare appeal to
oscillatory growth would miss.

\subsection{No natural-boundary claim}
A non-D-finite function need not have a natural boundary. For a direct
example, the meromorphic function $1/\cos z$ has isolated poles at
$\pi/2+k\pi$, and it can be continued around each of them. It is not
D-finite, since a polynomial-coefficient linear differential equation has
only finitely many possible finite singularities, whereas these poles are
infinite in number. Thus non-D-finiteness does not say that continuation
fails across every point of a convergence circle.

Our result also does not prove differential transcendence: nonlinear
polynomial differential equations are not excluded. The radius of $F$
has not been shown transcendental or irrational. Nor does the argument
prove that $F$ has infinitely many singularities. What it excludes, in
particular, is the finite regular-singular arithmetic configuration that
would be forced by a G-function.

\section{The periodic profile as a reusable obstruction}
\label{dsr:nh:sec:profile}
The proof of the main theorem was deliberately independent of the prior
report's stronger profile results. This section connects the new criterion
to that report without treating its established construction as new.

\subsection{The abstract profile criterion}
\begin{proposition}[Continuous nonconstant phase obstruction]
\label{dsr:nh:prop:profileobstruction}
Let $(u_n)$ be positive integers and suppose, for some $b>1$ and a
continuous nonconstant $1$-periodic function $P$, that
\begin{equation}
\log u_n=nP(\log_b n)+o(n).
\label{dsr:nh:eq:abstractprofile}
\end{equation}
Then $(u_n)$ is not P-recursive over $\C$. The same conclusion holds for
positive rational coefficients with exponentially growing common
denominators.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Periodicity and continuity make $P$ bounded and uniformly continuous, so
\eqref{dsr:nh:eq:abstractprofile} bounds $u_n$ exponentially. Writing $e_n=o(n)$,
\begin{align*}
\log u_{n+1}-\log u_n
={}&P(\log_b(n+1))\\
&+n\bigl(P(\log_b(n+1))-P(\log_b n)\bigr)+e_{n+1}-e_n
=o(n).
\end{align*}
Thus subexponential forward increases hold. For any fixed $t\in\R$,
take $n_k=\lfloor b^{k+t}\rfloor$. Then
$\log_b n_k-k\to t$, and
$\log u_{n_k}/n_k\to P(t)$. Two distinct values of $P$ give two distinct
logarithmic root limits, contradicting Theorem~\ref{dsr:nh:thm:rigidity} under
P-recursiveness.
\end{proof}
Only continuity is required, not differentiability, Lipschitz regularity,
or identification of the fundamental period. In particular, the unresolved
fine regularity questions in the prior report do not obstruct this
application.

\subsection{Recovering the repository's bounded-error law}
Here is a short independent specialization of the existing interpolation
method \cite{HJT}\,\partref{Theorem~\ref{dsr:thm:profile}}. Let $A(x)$ be the affine interpolation of
$a_n$ for $x\ge1$, and put $T(x)=A(x)-2A(x/2)$ for $x\ge2$.
At even and odd integers respectively,
\[
2A(n/2)=2a_{n/2},\qquad
2A(n/2)=a_{(n-1)/2}+a_{(n+1)/2}.
\]
On every unit interval $T$ is affine, because a breakpoint of $A(x/2)$
can only occur at an even integer. Hence $T$ interpolates $\tau_n$ and
$L\le T(x)\le U$.

The series
\begin{equation}
H(x)=A(x)+\sum_{j\ge1}2^{-j}T(2^jx),\qquad x\ge1,
\label{dsr:nh:eq:H}
\end{equation}
converges uniformly. Equivalently, $H(x)=\lim_{k\to\infty}2^{-k}A(2^kx)$,
so $H$ is continuous and $H(2x)=2H(x)$. Defining
$P(t)=2^{-t}H(2^t)$ for $t\ge0$ and extending periodically gives
\begin{equation}
a_n=nP(\log_2 n)-Q(n),\qquad L\le Q(n)\le U.
\label{dsr:nh:eq:actualprofile}
\end{equation}
The dyadic limits satisfy $\ell_m=P(\log_2 m)$, so
\eqref{dsr:nh:eq:logsep} proves that this $P$ is nonconstant. Proposition
\ref{dsr:nh:prop:profileobstruction} is therefore a second route to the base
non-P-recursiveness theorem. The profile construction and the conclusion
that it is nonconstant are not additional novelty claims here.

\wtag\ In Part~I's notation $T$ is $R$ \eqref{dsr:eq:R}, $H$ is $F$
\eqref{dsr:eq:F}, and \eqref{dsr:nh:eq:actualprofile} is
Theorem~\ref{dsr:thm:profile} at integers, with the same $P$ and~$Q$.
Part~I's Lemma~\ref{dsr:lem:phase-gap} gives a second proof that $P$ is
nonconstant, from other published counts.

\subsection{Perturbed linear sampling}
\begin{corollary}
\label{dsr:nh:cor:perturbedsampling}
Fix a real $q>0$, and let $m_n$ be positive integers satisfying
\[
m_n=qn+o\left(\frac{n}{\log(n+1)}\right).
\]
Then $(\tht(m_n))$ is not P-recursive over $\C$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The two-sided adjacent bound gives
\[
\abs{a_{m_n}-a_{\lfloor qn\rfloor}}
=O\bigl((|m_n-\lfloor qn\rfloor|+1)\log(n+1)\bigr)=o(n).
\]
Using \eqref{dsr:nh:eq:actualprofile} and continuity,
\[
\log\tht(m_n)
=n\,qP(\log_2 n+\log_2 q)+o(n).
\]
The function $t\mapsto qP(t+\log_2 q)$ is continuous, periodic, and
nonconstant. Apply Proposition~\ref{dsr:nh:prop:profileobstruction}.
\end{proof}
The indices need not themselves satisfy a recurrence, and the statement
is an obstruction, not an enumeration algorithm. It shows that the
phenomenon is not an artefact of choosing a single residue class.

\section{Exact verification and reproducibility}
\label{dsr:nh:sec:verification}
The package contains a standard-library Python program, not a symbolic
recurrence guesser masquerading as a proof. Its output separates four roles:
independent enumeration, arithmetic separation, finite diagnostic recurrence
certificates, and the limitations of each check.

\subsection{A subset-state enumeration with a proved transition}
For a subset $S\subseteq[n]$, regard $S$ as the set of entries already placed.
If $x\notin S$ is placed next, then for every integer $d>0$ with
$x-d,x+d\in[n]$, the two endpoints must either both belong to $S$ or both
lie outside $S$. Define
\begin{equation}
\mathcal A(S)=\{x\notin S:\
\mathbf1_S(x-d)=\mathbf1_S(x+d)
\text{ whenever }x-d,x+d\in[n]\}.
\label{dsr:nh:eq:allowed}
\end{equation}
The continuation recurrence is
\begin{equation}
C([n])=1,\qquad
C(S)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal A(S)}C(S\cup\{x\}).
\label{dsr:nh:eq:dp}
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}
\label{dsr:nh:prop:dp}
The value $C(\varnothing)$ in \eqref{dsr:nh:eq:dp} equals $\tht(n)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
In any completed ordering, a forbidden progression occurs exactly when its
midpoint is placed with one endpoint earlier and the other endpoint later.
Condition \eqref{dsr:nh:eq:allowed} excludes precisely that event at the step when
the midpoint is placed. If all steps obey it, no midpoint can lie between
its endpoints, so the completed permutation is 3AP-free. Conversely,
every 3AP-free permutation obeys the condition at each step. The condition
for future steps depends only on $S$, not on the internal order of an
already valid prefix. Therefore memoization by the subset is sound, and
\eqref{dsr:nh:eq:dp} counts every valid completion exactly once.
\end{proof}
This subset-state approach is consistent with the dynamic-programming
viewpoint in Correll--Ho \cite{CorrellHo}. The present implementation and
its short transition proof are provided for independent reproducibility,
not as a new faster enumeration method.
\wtag\ Part~I's Section~\ref{dsr:sec:verification} proves the same
recurrence \eqref{dsr:eq:DP}, and its own verifier, a different program,
also recomputes $\tht(n)$ for $n\le32$.

\section{Further research questions and topics}
\label{dsr:nh:sec:future}
The following questions are proposed targets, not results asserted by this
article. Their status is relative to the inspected sources and repository.

\begin{question}[Analytic continuation and a natural boundary]
\label{dsr:nh:q:boundary}%
Does the ordinary generating function $F$ have a natural boundary on its
circle of convergence? If not, determine a nontrivial continuation domain
and describe the singularities encountered there.
\end{question}
The present argument removes D-finite models but does not supply the
functional equation or singularity propagation needed for a natural-boundary
proof. A productive first step would be to derive a generating-function
identity from a refined state decomposition of the permutation class,
rather than infer complex singularities from a real growth plot.

\begin{question}[Differential algebraicity]
\label{dsr:nh:q:dalg}%
Can $F$, or its entire exponential generating function $E$, satisfy a
nonlinear polynomial differential equation over $\C(z)$?
\end{question}
The distinction is substantive: all linear equations have been excluded,
while nonlinear equations can support much richer singular behavior. Any
proposed equation should first be tested against exact coefficients and
then against the two separated dyadic exponential scales.

\begin{question}[Geometry and regularity of the growth profile]
\label{dsr:nh:q:profile}%
Determine the optimal modulus of continuity of $P$ in
\eqref{dsr:nh:eq:actualprofile}, and decide where it is differentiable. Locate its
global extrema and determine whether a maximum occurs at a dyadic phase.
\end{question}
The non-holonomicity proof needs only continuity and two unequal values.
Finer profile information could instead guide sharp asymptotic algorithms,
rigorous integration of the phase distribution, and extremal growth bounds.
Existing period and modulus results in \partref{Theorem~\ref{dsr:thm:period}, Proposition~\ref{dsr:prop:modulus}} should be kept as
the baseline rather than rediscovered.
\wtag\ These are items~1 and~2 of Part~I's Section~\ref{dsr:sec:questions}.

\begin{question}[Monotonicity and sharp adjacent ratios]
\label{dsr:nh:q:monotone}%
Is $\tht(n+1)\ge\tht(n)$ always true? What is the smallest possible growth
scale of the largest adjacent ratios?
\end{question}
Monotonicity is left open in the inspected enumerative sources. Our argument
does not use it. The gap between a weak polynomial lower estimate and
combinatorial positivity suggests investigating insertion positions and
parity traces directly, rather than strengthening the analytic argument by
an unsupported monotonicity assumption.
\wtag\ This is item~5 of Part~I's Section~\ref{dsr:sec:questions} and
Sharma's open problem~(1), stated without proof in \repo\ as
\lean{Sharma2012.open_problem_1}; the Lean
\lean{insertion_count_upper_bound_holds} bounds the adjacent ratio above
by $\lfloor(n+3)/2\rfloor$.

\begin{question}[Signed observables and cancellation]
Classify nonzero signed polynomials in finitely many shifted counts whose
values remain positive or nonzero. Which of these evaluated sequences are
P-recursive, if any?
\end{question}
The positivity proof of \eqref{dsr:nh:eq:polyscale} does not extend to subtraction.
An identically zero signed observable would of course be P-recursive, so a
meaningful classification must separate exact identities from cancellation
that changes the exponential scale. Even the asymptotic size of
$\tht(n+1)-\tht(n)$ is relevant here.

\begin{question}[An elementary substitute for the arithmetic theorem]
For the particular splitting class \eqref{dsr:nh:eq:split}, can non-P-recursiveness
be proved directly from a hypothetical polynomial recurrence, without using
the arithmetic regularity theorem for G-functions?
\end{question}
Such a proof could be more accessible to formalization. A plausible route is
a rigorous classification of the pure-exponential portion of a rational
coefficient difference system, with all possible factorial scales and
cancellations controlled. A formal asymptotic ansatz alone would not suffice.

\begin{question}[Certified large enumeration]
\label{dsr:nh:q:certified}%
Produce independently auditable certificates for $\tht(64)$ and $\tht(75)$,
or replace them by a smaller structural separation that does not import a
large count table.
\end{question}
The integer comparison in \eqref{dsr:nh:eq:exactsep} is cheap; obtaining its inputs
is the harder verification task. A certificate-producing subset-state
algorithm with a small trusted checker would strengthen the computational
boundary of both Ho's theorem and the present application.
\wtag\ Still open in \repo: its Lean count table
\lean{table_1_holds} stops at $\tht(1),\ldots,\tht(20)$, behind a native trust
boundary (Subsection~\ref{dsr:nh:sec:formal}). Item~7 of Part~I's
Section~\ref{dsr:sec:questions} asks the same for the counts
$100\le n\le200$ that Part~I's certificates use.

\begin{question}[Effective recurrence obstructions]
Given prescribed order, coefficient degree, and starting index, bound an
interval of coefficients sufficient to disprove a recurrence for this
sequence. Can such bounds be obtained from a quantitative finite-window
argument rather than growing matrix searches?
\end{question}
The analytic contradiction is uniform over all hypothetical recurrences but
does not supply a universal finite certificate size: its Vandermonde
constants and asymptotic thresholds depend on that recurrence. Bridging
this qualitative--effective gap is a separate problem.

\begin{question}[Other hereditary enumerations with phase-dependent growth]
Find other exponentially bounded, integer counting sequences with a
polynomial insertion bound and a nonconstant logarithmic exponential
profile, and apply Theorem~\ref{dsr:nh:thm:rigidity} or
Proposition~\ref{dsr:nh:prop:profileobstruction}.
\end{question}
The new criterion is not restricted to arithmetic-progressions. Its useful
search signature is an exponential-scale phase oscillation together with
local insertion control. Ordinary bounded multiplicative periodic factors
are not enough: they leave $n$th-root growth unchanged.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{dsr:nh:sec:conclusion}
The obstruction to holonomicity is not oscillation by itself, but the
combination of two incompatible structures. Arithmetic P-recursive
sequences of exponential size have a dominant finite-frequency
power-logarithmic layer. A Vandermonde matrix forces that layer to be
visible in every sufficiently late fixed-length window. The 3AP-free
permutation counts, on the other hand, cannot rise by an exponential factor
in a bounded number of steps, yet they exhibit two different limiting
exponential rates along dyadic scales.

This contradiction proves non-P-recursiveness of A003407 and every
arithmetic section, non-D-finiteness of both generating functions, and
stable versions for positive polynomial observables and subexponential
multiplicative approximations. The established enumerative and G-function
theorems remain visible dependencies. Natural boundaries, nonlinear
differential equations, sharper profile structure, and full formalization
remain distinct research targets.

Exponential size and integrality would make $F$ a G-function.
\item Its nonzero leading Nilsson layer would imply, for some fixed $s$,
$\max_{0\le j<s}\tht(n+j)=R^n\exp(o(n))$.
\item By item 2, that same maximum lies between $\tht(n)$ and
$(n+s)^{s-1}\tht(n)$. Therefore $\tht(n)^{1/n}\to R$, contradicting item 3.
\end{enumerate}
The only deep analytic step is the stated G-function input in item 5.
The finite-window passage within that step is fully proved in
Lemma~\ref{dsr:nh:lem:window}. The original question is thereby reduced to a
short contradiction, without silently replacing a limsup by a limit.
