\begin{abstract}
This report prints two manuscripts as Parts~I and~II. They treat two members of one OEIS family entered by Emanuele Munarini on 4~July 2011, sums over $k$ of $k!$ times a product of two Stirling numbers. Part~I treats A192563, $a_n=\sum_kk!\sone{n+1}{k+1}\stwo{n+1}{k+1}=\sum_m\sone nm(m+1)^n$, the diagonal of the poly-Cauchy array A344639 of B\'enyi and Ram\'irez; $a_n/n!$ is the $n$th moment of one plus the cycle count of a uniform random permutation. It proves an asymptotic expansion of $a_n$ to every fixed order in $r/n$, where $r=\log n-\log\log n-\log\log\log n+o(1)$ is the exact saddle of $e^z\Gamma(n+e^z)/\Gamma(e^z)$ and the coefficients are explicit Gaussian contraction polynomials in exact cumulants; the same expansion for the entries $A_{n,k}$ of A344639, uniformly for $k/n$ in a compact subset of $(0,\infty)$; a smooth inverse with floor and ceiling bounds for both threshold indices, correct at equality cases; and a nested-logarithm inverse. Part~II treats A192561, $b_n=\sum_kk!\sone{n+1}{k+1}^2$, as the Gaussian Fock norm of $\sum_k\sone{n+1}{k+1}z^k$. It proves an exact positive expansion with an explicit tail at every $n\ge1$, a rational-centre certificate that isolates $b_{10}=1273270076895432$, the same certificate for finite products with negative real zeros, an expansion to every fixed order in $1/w$, $w=W_0(en)$, with rational coefficients, and a nested-Lambert inverse with two-ceiling threshold bounds. The two manuscripts use different methods and do not cite each other. The write adds, for the third member A192562, which neither treats, the identity $A192562(n)=a_n-nA_{n-1,n}$, and states what the two methods suggest for its asymptotics. Effective constants and onsets, growing orders, the $1/n$-scale correction for A192561 and the asymptotics of A192562 remain open. The report is unrefereed, and nothing in it is formalized.
\end{abstract}

The three entries, read by the write on 6~October 2026 (A192561 revision \#10 of June 25, 2024; A192562 revision \#4 of March 30, 2012; A192563 revision \#13 of May 26, 2021), each with the author line \verb!_Emanuele Munarini_, Jul 04 2011!. Their names, verbatim:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A192561}: \verb|a(n) = Sum_{k=0..n} Stirling1(n+1,k+1)^2*k!.|
\item \textbf{A192562}: \verb|a(n) = sum(abs(stirling1(n,k))*stirling2(n+1,k+1)*k!,k=0..n).|
\item \textbf{A192563}: \verb|a(n) = Sum_{k=0..n} abs(Stirling1(n+1,k+1))*Stirling2(n+1,k+1)*k!.|\\
with the cross-reference \verb!Diagonal of the array A344639.!
\end{itemize}
So all three are $\sum_kk!\,X_kY_k$ over a pair of Stirling rows: two first-kind rows $\sone{n+1}{k+1}$ (A192561; the square makes the sign of \texttt{Stirling1} irrelevant), a first-kind and a second-kind row, both shifted (A192563), or both with the first row unshifted, $\sone nk\stwo{n+1}{k+1}$ (A192562). The pattern is one, but A192562's index shift differs from the other two. Part~II treats A192561, Part~I treats A192563, neither treats A192562. The listed terms (17 in each entry) agree with the write's exact recomputation from these definitions, and A192563's b-file ($0\le n\le270$, Stefano Spezia) agrees with Part~I's \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:definition} at every index. None of the three entries states an asymptotic formula.

\emph{Munarini's form of A192563.} Part~I defines $a_n=\sum_m\sone nm(m+1)^n$ \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:definition}. The diagonal $k=n$ of its identity \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:stirlingproduct}, $A_{n,k}=\sum_jj!\sone{n+1}{j+1}\stwo{k+1}{j+1}$, is word for word the entry's definition, and that identity holds for all $n,k\ge0$: it is Theorem~2.6 of B\'enyi--Ram\'irez \cite{benyi}, and Part~I's Subsection~\ref{sfp:pc:sub:signs} reproves it in a few lines, a complete proof (the one step it calls standard is proved in a dated note there). So the two forms agree for every~$n$, not only for the $n\le11$ checked at intake, the $n,k\le64$ of the companion and the $n,k<30$ of the write.

 \verb|(n, k)-poly-Cauchy permutations.|

\section*{One family, two methods}\phantomsection\label{sfp:sec:family}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{One family, two methods}
The two manuscripts were written in one session, carry the same date and the same release tooling, and do not cite each other. They are printed as one report because they treat two members of one OEIS family, and because one bilinear form and one polynomial put both in the same frame. In Part~II's notation \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:inner}, $\langle f,g\rangle_{\mathcal F}=\sum_kk![t^k]f\,[t^k]g$, and with
\begin{gather*}
 n!P_n(z)=\sum_k\sone{n+1}{k+1}z^k\ \ \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:polynomial},\qquad
 \mathfrak T_n(z)=\sum_k\stwo{n+1}{k+1}z^k,\\
 z^{\overline n}=z(z+1)\cdots(z+n-1)=\sum_k\sone nkz^k,
\end{gather*}
the three definitions read
\[
 A192561(n)=\langle n!P_n,n!P_n\rangle_{\mathcal F},\qquad
 A192563(n)=\langle n!P_n,\mathfrak T_n\rangle_{\mathcal F},\qquad
 A192562(n)=\langle z^{\overline n},\mathfrak T_n\rangle_{\mathcal F}
\]
(the notation $z^{\overline n}$ and $\mathfrak T_n$ is the write's). Part~II's sequence is a norm, and Part~II exploits exactly that: a translated Gaussian norm is a sum of nonnegative terms, which gives an exact expansion with an explicit tail. Part~I's sequence is a pairing of two different polynomials, and Part~I does not use the Fock form at all. It works with the summed form $A_{n,k}=\sum_m\sone nm(m+1)^k$, equal to the pairing by \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:stirlingproduct}, and extracts a coefficient,
\[
 a_n=n![z^n]F_n(z),\qquad F_n(z)=e^z\prod_{q=0}^{n-1}(e^z+q)=n!\,e^z\,P_n(e^z-1),
\]
the last form being Part~II's polynomial after the substitution $z\mapsto e^z-1$ (both products are $\prod_{j=1}^n(j+e^z-1)$). Positivity enters Part~I differently: $F_n$ is a positive exponential sum \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:positive}, which controls the whole circle (Lemma~\ref{sfp:pc:lem:minor}).

The scales differ accordingly. Part~I's saddle is the exact root $r=\log n-\log\log n-\log\log\log n+o(1)$ in the variable of $e^z$, and its expansion is in powers of $r/n$ with coefficients depending on $n$ through exact cumulants. Part~II's saddle $a\sim\log n$ and its anchor $w-1$, $w=W_0(en)$, give an expansion in $1/w$ with rational coefficients, after a reduction whose error $O(w^2/n)$ is beyond all orders in $1/w$. The leading growths share one factorial core: $\log b_n=2n\log n-2n+O((\log n)^2)$ \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:logleading}, while Lemma~\ref{sfp:pc:lem:loggrowth} and \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:logerror} give $\log a_n=2n\log n-n\log\log n-2n+O(n\log\log n/\log n)$; so $\log(a_n/b_n)=-n\log\log n+O(n\log\log n/\log n)$. Both Parts prove every fixed order and integer-safe inverse brackets, both leave constants and onsets non-effective, and both state their priority searches as bounded. No lemma is shared, and neither manuscript applies its method to the other's sequence. The third member, A192562, is the subject of Section~\ref{sfp:sec:further}, item~\ref{sfp:q:a192562}, where the write proves an exact identity for it.

\section*{The inverses and the transseries volume}\phantomsection\label{sfp:sec:inverses}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{The inverses and the transseries volume}
How each inverse statement relates to the transseries volume \cite{TSvol} (\file{Analysis/Transseries/docs/series-and-transseries/Transseries_And_Inversion/transseries_and_inversion.tex}), statement by statement. No inverse here is an instance of the volume's Lambert core \file{p0:thm:lambert-core}.
\begin{itemize}
\item Part~II, \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:leadinginverse}: the equation $2u(\log u-1)=L$ is the factorial core $X(\kappa\log X+d)=L$ of \file{p0:prop:factorial-core} with $\kappa=2$, $d=-2$. That proposition gives $v=W_0\bigl((L/2)e^{-1}\bigr)=W_0(L/(2e))$ and $X=e^{v+1}$, and $e^{v}=L/(2ev)$ gives $X=L/(2v)$, which is \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:leadinginverse}; the side condition $2\log X-2>0$ selects the solution $u>e$. An \emph{instance}.
\item Part~II, Theorem~\ref{sfp:mc:thm:inverse} and \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:implicitbracket}: the centre $v_R=u-J_R(u)/(2D)$ is a one-step correction of the core solution, proved from the smooth model $F_R$ of \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:FR}, whose terms in $w(x)=W_0(ex)$ lie outside the volume's exponential--power model \file{p0:def:model}. The two-ceiling brackets have the form of \file{p0:thm:staircase}\,(2), but that theorem presupposes an admissible interpolation of the sequence itself, and $F_R$ is a model with an error $\pm D_Rw^{-R}$, not an interpolation of $(b_n)$. Not an instance; proved directly; analogous to the separation condition~(2).
\item Part~I, Theorem~\ref{sfp:pc:thm:inverse}, bracket \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:bracket}: likewise rounded from the smooth model $Y_R$, which is not an interpolation of $(a_n)$; not an instance of \file{p0:thm:staircase}, analogous to its condition~(2). The lower edge $\lfloor\nu_R-\eps_R\rfloor+1$ and the strict threshold $T_>$ are Part~I's own refinements (Section~\ref{sfp:pc:sec:thresholds}).
\item Part~I, nested inverse \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:nested}: the model $\log Y_R(N)=2N\log N-N\log\log N-2N+O(N\log\log N/\log N)$ \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:loggrowth} contains the term $-N\log\log N$, outside the factorial core $X(2\log X-2)$; \emph{not} an instance of \file{p0:prop:factorial-core}. After the change of variables $\xi=\log\log X$, $Z=\log N$ it reads $\xi=Z+\log Z+\log2+B(Z)$ with $B=b_1(\log Z)/Z+O((\log Z)^2/Z^2)$, $b_1(\lambda)=-\lambda/2-1$; this is the monomial--logarithmic datum $(\rho,\mu,\beta)=(1,1,\log2)$ of \file{plt:def:lw-monomial-log-datum}, but only its first coefficient $b_1$ is determined. The write checked that the first two coefficients which \file{plt:thm:lw-template} produces from $(1,1,\log2,b_1)$, namely $q_0=-\log X'$ and $q_1=\frac32\log X'+1$ in the template's variable $X'=\xi-\log2$, give exactly \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:nested}. The template is a formal statement about a complete series $B$; Part~I's remainder $O(m^2/\ell^2)$ is its own mean-value argument (Section~\ref{sfp:pc:sec:nested}). An analogue, with first-order agreement; not an instance.
\end{itemize}
Neither manuscript claims novelty for the inversion mechanics; both call the underlying tools classical.

\begin{partabstract}
The diagonal second-kind poly-Cauchy numbers satisfy
$a_n=\sum_m\sone nm(m+1)^n$ and count the $(n,n)$-poly-Cauchy
permutations of B\'enyi and Ram\'irez. Equivalently,
$a_n/n!$ is the $n$th ordinary moment of one plus the cycle count
of a uniform permutation of $n$ labels. We prove a complete
asymptotic expansion at every fixed order, using the exact saddle
of $e^z\Gamma(n+e^z)/\Gamma(e^z)$ and explicit finite Gaussian
contraction polynomials in its standardized cumulants. The small scale is
$r/n$, where
$r=\log n-\log\log n-\log\log\log n+o(1)$.
A positive exponential sum controls the whole complementary
contour, and a weighted Taylor argument proves the integrated
remainder. The result is uniform when the moment order $k$ and
permutation size $n$ have ratio in a fixed compact subset of
$(0,\infty)$. Differentiating an explicit real-parameter model
then gives arbitrarily fine smooth inverse windows, with
strictly justified floor and ceiling bounds at equality cases.
We also obtain an explicit nested-logarithm inversion,
including its relative remainder. Exact companion calculations
check the coefficient identities and the OEIS prefix; optional
high-precision diagnostics are separated from the proof.
\end{partabstract}

\subsection{Why this diagonal is a natural asymptotic problem}

If $C_n$ is the number of cycles of a uniform random permutation
of $n$ labels, then
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:moment}
 \frac{A_{n,k}}{n!}=\EE(C_n+1)^k.
\end{equation}
Thus the diagonal studies a moment order that grows with the
underlying combinatorial size. The familiar fixed-order cycle-moment
regime does not settle it. Large moments give disproportionate
weight to atypically large cycle counts, so replacing $C_n$ by its
typical order $\log n$ is not a justified approximation.

There is also a direct combinatorial class. Take a permutation of
$n$ red labels, put its cycles into canonical order with the largest
entry of each cycle first and these largest entries increasing,
and assign each of $k$ blue labels to one of the $m+1$ gaps around
its $m$ cycles. Within each gap put the blue labels in increasing
order. Removing cycle parentheses produces the second-kind
poly-Cauchy permutations of B\'enyi--Ram\'irez
\cite[Theorem 2.1]{benyi}. This description explains both positivity
and the factor $(m+1)^k$. It counts structured permutations on two
specified label sets, not arbitrary permutations of their total size.

The present results consequently serve three concrete purposes:
asymptotic enumeration of that class, evaluation of growing ordinary
cycle-count moments, and inversion of a prescribed counting or moment
scale. The compact aspect-ratio theorem permits the two label sets
to grow at comparable but unequal rates.

\subsection{The exact saddle and its coefficients}

For real $N\geq1$ and real $z$, set
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:LN}
 L_N(z)=z+\log\Gamma(N+e^z)-\log\Gamma(e^z).
\end{equation}
Let $r=r(N)>0$ solve
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:saddle}
 rL_N'(r)=N,
 \quad\text{or}\quad
 r\bigl[1+e^r\{\psi(N+e^r)-\psi(e^r)\}\bigr]=N.
\end{equation}
Here $\psi$ is the digamma function. Existence and uniqueness for
every $N\geq1$ are proved in Section~\ref{sfp:pc:sec:real}. Define
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:cumulants}
 \kappa_j(N)=\left.(z\partial_z)^jL_N(z)\right|_{z=r(N)},
 \qquad b=\kappa_2,
 \qquad h=\frac rN.
\end{equation}
The differential operator acts with $N$ held fixed, before evaluation
at the saddle. In particular $\kappa_1=N$ and $b>0$.

Put $E_0=1$. For $q\geq1$, define the finite polynomial expression
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:Ek}
 E_q=\sum_{\substack{m_3,\ldots,m_{2q+2}\geq0\\
                    \sum_{j=3}^{2q+2}(j-2)m_j=2q}}
 (-1)^{J/2}(J-1)!!
 \prod_{j=3}^{2q+2}\frac1{m_j!}
       \left(\frac{\kappa_j}{j!b^{j/2}}\right)^{m_j},
 \qquad J=\sum_{j=3}^{2q+2}jm_j.
\end{equation}
The constraint makes $J$ even; the apparent half-powers of $b$ thus
combine to integer powers in every monomial. We use $(-1)!!=1$
when representing $E_0$ by the empty product.

\begin{theorem}[All fixed orders on the diagonal]\label{sfp:pc:thm:main}
For every fixed integer $R\geq1$, as $n\to\infty$,
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:main}
 a_n=\frac{n!\exp L_n(r)}{r^n\sqrt{2\pi b}}
       \left(\sum_{q=0}^{R-1}E_q(n)+O_R\left((r/n)^R\right)\right).
\end{equation}
Moreover, for each fixed $j\geq1$,
\begin{align}
 r(N)&=\log N-\log\log N-\log\log\log N+o(1),\label{sfp:pc:eq:rscale}\\
 \kappa_j(N)&\sim Nr^{j-1},\qquad
 E_q(N)=O_q\left((r/N)^q\right).\label{sfp:pc:eq:kappascale}
\end{align}
In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:E1}
 E_1=\frac{\kappa_4}{8b^2}
       -\frac{5\kappa_3^2}{24b^3}
       \sim-\frac{r}{12N}.
\end{equation}
The implicit constants and sufficiently large onsets may depend on
$R$ or on the displayed fixed index.
\end{theorem}

The leading equivalent already involves an exact transcendental
saddle. Retaining it is essential at higher orders. The replacement
$E_1\sim-r/(12n)$ explains a sign and a scale, but does \emph{not}
replace the full $E_1$ in the $R=2$ formula. The errors in that
replacement, and in a finite nested-log approximation to $r$, can
be much larger than $(r/n)^2$. This is an exact-saddle expansion with
slowly varying coefficients, not a constant-coefficient power series
in $1/n$.

\begin{theorem}[Compact aspect ratios]\label{sfp:pc:thm:aspect}
Fix $0<\alpha\leq\beta<\infty$ and a fixed integer $R\geq1$.
For positive integers $n,k$ with $\alpha\leq k/n\leq\beta$,
let $r>0$ solve $rL_n'(r)=k$ and define $\kappa_j,b,E_q$ as in
\eqref{sfp:pc:eq:cumulants}--\eqref{sfp:pc:eq:Ek}, with $n$ held fixed. Uniformly
in this range,
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:aspect}
 A_{n,k}=\frac{k!\exp L_n(r)}{r^k\sqrt{2\pi b}}
    \left(\sum_{q=0}^{R-1}E_q
          +O_{R,\alpha,\beta}\left((r/k)^R\right)\right).
\end{equation}
Also, uniformly in the same range,
\begin{align}
 r&=\log n-\log\log n-\log\log\log n
               +\log(k/n)+o(1),\label{sfp:pc:eq:aspectr}\\
 \kappa_j&\sim kr^{j-1},\qquad b\sim kr.
\end{align}
The theorem makes no uniform assertion as $k/n$ approaches zero or
infinity.
\end{theorem}

For fixed $R\geq1$, put
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:YR}

                   {r(N)^N\sqrt{2\pi b(N)}}P_R(N).
\end{equation}
This defines an explicit smooth function for sufficiently large real
$N$. It does not assert an extension of the combinatorial sequence
to noninteger indices.

\begin{theorem}[Smooth inverse and integer thresholds]\label{sfp:pc:thm:inverse}
The function $P_R$ is eventually positive and $Y_R$ is eventually
strictly increasing, with
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:Yderivative}
 (\log Y_R)'(N)=2\log N-\log r(N)+o(1)\sim2\log N.
\end{equation}
For sufficiently large $X$, let $\nu_R$ be the unique solution of
$Y_R(\nu_R)=X$ in the eventual increasing range. There is a constant
$C_R>0$ such that, with
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:epsilon}
 \eps_R=C_R\frac{(\log\nu_R)^{R-1}}{\nu_R^R},
\end{equation}
the two thresholds
\[
 T_{\geq}(X)=\min\{n\geq0:a_n\geq X\},\qquad
 T_{>}(X)=\min\{n\geq0:a_n>X\}
\]
satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:bracket}
 \floor{\nu_R-\eps_R}+1
 \ \leq T_{\geq}(X)\leq T_{>}(X)
 \ \leq\ceil{\nu_R+\eps_R}.
\end{equation}
In addition, if $L=\log X$, $\ell=\log L$, and $m=\log\ell$,
then
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:nested}
 \nu_R=\frac{L}{2\ell}
       \left[1+\frac{\tfrac32m+1+\log2}{\ell}
               +O_R\left(\frac{m^2}{\ell^2}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

The shrinking $\eps_R$ measures uncertainty around a \emph{smooth}
center before rounding. An integer threshold cannot in general equal
$\nu_R+o(1)$ without a rounding qualification. If the interval
$[\nu_R-\eps_R,\nu_R+\eps_R]$ lies strictly between consecutive
integers $j$ and $j+1$, both thresholds equal $j+1$. If $X=a_n$,
then $T_{\geq}(X)=n$ and $T_>(X)=n+1$; the two-sided statement
\eqref{sfp:pc:eq:bracket} deliberately retains that possibility.
The explicit nested-log expression has a much coarser error than
\eqref{sfp:pc:eq:epsilon}; it must not silently replace $\nu_R$ in that
fine bracket.

\subsection{Signs and the OEIS identification}\label{sfp:pc:sub:signs}

In Komatsu's notation for second-kind poly-Cauchy numbers,
\[
 A_{n,k}=(-1)^n\widehat c_n^{(-k)}.
\]
B\'enyi--Ram\'irez write the nonnegative normalization as
$\widehat c_{n,k}$. These conventions exclude the signed first-kind
poly-Cauchy numbers, which are a different family
\cite[Theorem 4]{komatsu2013}. The final published version of
B\'enyi--Ram\'irez gives \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:definition} and its interpretation
in Theorem 2.1 and gives
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:stirlingproduct}
 A_{n,k}=\sum_{j=0}^k j!\sone{n+1}{j+1}\stwo{k+1}{j+1}
\end{equation}
in Theorem 2.6 \cite{benyi}. The diagonal of
\eqref{sfp:pc:eq:stirlingproduct} is precisely the defining formula in
A192563. The theorem numbers in the earlier preprint are different;
all theorem references here use the final 2022 volume version.

For completeness, a direct algebraic check of
\eqref{sfp:pc:eq:stirlingproduct} is short. The standard identity
\[
 (m+1)^k=\sum_{j=0}^k\stwo{k+1}{j+1}(m)_j
\]
uses the falling factorial $(m)_j=m(m-1)\cdots(m-j+1)$.
The coefficient of $x^j$ in
\[
 \sum_{m=0}^n\sone nm(1+x)^m
      =(1+x)(2+x)\cdots(n+x)
\]
is $\sone{n+1}{j+1}$, and also equals
$\sum_m\sone nm\binom mj$. Multiplying by $j!$ and interchanging
finite sums proves the formula. The companion verifies both sides
by independent recurrences.

The check above is a complete proof. \subsection{The coefficient function}

The rising-factorial identity gives the entire function
\begin{align}
 F_n(z)&=\sum_{m=0}^n\sone nm e^{(m+1)z}\label{sfp:pc:eq:positive}\\
       &=e^z\prod_{q=0}^{n-1}(e^z+q)
        =e^z\frac{\Gamma(n+e^z)}{\Gamma(e^z)}.
        \label{sfp:pc:eq:Fn}
\end{align}
The gamma quotient in \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:Fn} is understood through its
finite product wherever necessary; $F_n$ is entire. Hence
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:coefficient}
 A_{n,k}=k![z^k]F_n(z),\qquad a_n=n![z^n]F_n(z).
\end{equation}
Summing the rising-factorial exponential generating function in
$n$ also gives
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:doubleegf}
 \sum_{n,k\geq0}A_{n,k}\frac{x^n}{n!}\frac{y^k}{k!}
     =e^y(1-x)^{-e^y}.
\end{equation}
These are exact prior-art identities, including the shifted-family
specialization behind \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:doubleegf}. The one-variable form
\eqref{sfp:pc:eq:coefficient} is the convenient route for the asymptotic
analysis; it changes with $n$, so the proof must control a triangular
family of entire functions.

\section{Real parameter saddles and scale estimates}\label{sfp:pc:sec:real}

This section works with real $N\geq1$. It simultaneously supplies
integer saddle estimates and the smooth continuation required for
inversion. Write $t=e^r$ and
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:q}
 q_N(t)=t\{\psi(N+t)-\psi(t)\}.
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}[Existence and uniqueness]\label{sfp:pc:lem:exist}
For every real $N\geq1$, the equation $rL_N'(r)=N$ has exactly
one solution $r>0$. At this solution $b>0$, and $r(N)$ is smooth.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The digamma difference integral from DLMF 5.9.16 \cite{dlmf} gives
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:digammadiff}
 q_N(t)=\int_0^\infty te^{-tu}h_N(u)\dd u,
 \qquad h_N(u)=\frac{1-e^{-Nu}}{1-e^{-u}}.
\end{equation}
For $u>0$,
\[
 \frac{h_N'(u)}{h_N(u)}
 =\frac{N}{e^{Nu}-1}-\frac1{e^u-1}\leq0,
\]
because $e^{Nu}-1\geq N(e^u-1)$ for $N\geq1$.
The last inequality follows, for example, from convexity in the
exponent parameter. If $V$ is exponential with rate one,
\eqref{sfp:pc:eq:digammadiff} is $\EE h_N(V/t)$. Since $h_N$ is
nonincreasing, this expectation is nondecreasing in $t$.
The integral is smooth for $t>0$, so $q_N'(t)\geq0$.
Consequently
\[
 L_N'(r)=1+q_N(e^r)>0,\qquad L_N''(r)\geq0.
\]
Thus $rL_N'(r)$ is strictly increasing for $r>0$, begins at zero,
and tends to infinity, since it is at least $r$. Its derivative is
$L_N'(r)+rL_N''(r)=b/r>0$. Existence, uniqueness, and smoothness
follow from continuity and the implicit function theorem.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Derivative bounds with an integral remainder]\label{sfp:pc:lem:psi}
For every fixed integer $j\geq0$ and $x>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:psiremainder}
 \left|\frac{\mathrm d^j}{\mathrm dx^j}
          \{\psi(x)-\log x\}\right|
       \leq \frac{j!}{x^{j+1}}.
\end{equation}
In particular, for each fixed $j\geq1$,
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:polygamma}
 \psi(x)=\log x+O(x^{-1}),\qquad
 \psi^{(j)}(x)=(-1)^{j-1}(j-1)!x^{-j}+O_j(x^{-j-1}).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The digamma remainder integral, DLMF 5.9.13 \cite{dlmf}, is
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:G}
 \psi(x)-\log x=-\int_0^\infty G(u)e^{-xu}\dd u,
 \qquad G(u)=\frac1{1-e^{-u}}-\frac1u.
\end{equation}
For $u>0$ one has $0<G(u)<1$: the lower inequality uses
$1-e^{-u}<u$, and the upper one uses $e^u-1>u$.
On each compact subinterval of $x>0$, dominated differentiation is
valid to every fixed order. The absolute value of the $j$th
derivative of \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:G} is at most
$\int_0^\infty u^je^{-xu}\dd u=j!x^{-j-1}$.
This proves the claims without differentiating an unspecified
asymptotic remainder.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Saddle and cumulant scales]\label{sfp:pc:lem:scales}
As $N\to\infty$, with $t=e^{r(N)}$,
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:tscale}
 \frac tN\sim\frac1{r\log r},\qquad
 r\sim\log N,\qquad
 r=\log N-\log\log N-\log\log\log N+o(1).
\end{equation}
For every fixed $j\geq1$,
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:Lderivatives}
 L_N^{(j)}(r)=\frac Nr+O_j(t+1)\sim\frac Nr,
 \qquad \kappa_j\sim Nr^{j-1}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Lemma~\ref{sfp:pc:lem:psi} first yields, uniformly for $N,t\geq1$,
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:qapprox}
 q_N(t)=t\log(1+N/t)+O(1).
\end{equation}
At $r=\tfrac12\log N$, the left side of the saddle equation is
$O(\sqrt N\log^2N)=o(N)$. At $r=\log N$ it exceeds $N$ for
large $N$, since $q_N(N)=N\log2+O(1)$. Monotonicity therefore
gives
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:roughr}
 \tfrac12\log N<r<\log N
\end{equation}
for all sufficiently large $N$.

Put $x=N/t$. The saddle equation and \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:qapprox} give
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:xequation}
 \frac xr=\log(1+x)+O(t^{-1}).
\end{equation}
Here $x\geq1$, $r\to\infty$, and $t\to\infty$.
A bounded subsequence of $x$ would contradict
\eqref{sfp:pc:eq:xequation}, so $x\to\infty$. Hence
$x/(r\log x)\to1$. The function $x/\log x$ is eventually
increasing. Substitution of fixed multiples of $r\log r$ into
this function brackets $x$ between such multiples, proving
$\log x\sim\log r$ and then $x\sim r\log r$.
Taking logarithms once more gives
\[
 \log x=\log r+\log\log r+o(1).
\]
Since $r=\log N-\log x$, the refined formula in
\eqref{sfp:pc:eq:tscale} follows from \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:roughr} and
$r/\log N\to1$.

For derivatives, apply $(t\partial_t)^{j-1}$ to $q_N(t)$.
The explicit bounds \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:psiremainder} imply that the
remainder in \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:qapprox} and each of its fixed
$t\partial_t$ derivatives are $O_j(1)$. Indeed each resulting
term has a power of $t$ multiplying a derivative bounded at
$t$ or at $N+t$ by the corresponding inverse power. In the main
term $t\log(1+N/t)$, the original logarithmic term survives
once after every application of $t\partial_t$; all other terms
are $O_j(t)$. It follows that
\[
 L_N^{(j)}(r)=t\log(1+N/t)+O_j(t+1)
           =N/r+O_j(t+1).
\]
For $j=1$ the saddle equation gives the exact equality
$L_N'(r)=N/r$. Since $rt/N\sim1/\log r\to0$, these expressions
are asymptotic to $N/r$.

Finally, the Stirling differential-operator identity is
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:operator}
 (z\partial_z)^jL_N(z)
   =\sum_{v=1}^j\stwo jv z^v L_N^{(v)}(z).
\end{equation}
At $z=r$, its term $v=j$ is asymptotic to $Nr^{j-1}$ and its
other fixed terms are smaller. This proves the cumulant estimate.
\end{proof}

\subsection{A finite Bernoulli representation at integer size}

For integer $n$, the product form gives a second, elementary
calculus for the derivatives. Write
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:p}
 p_q=\frac{t}{t+q}\quad(0\leq q\leq n-1).
\end{equation}
Let $B_j(p)$ be the $j$th cumulant polynomial of a Bernoulli
variable with success probability $p$. These polynomials satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:bernoulli}
 B_1(p)=p,\qquad
 B_{j+1}(p)=p(1-p)B_j'(p),\qquad B_j(p)=p+O_j(p^2).
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:integerderivatives}
 L_n'=1+\sum_qp_q,\qquad
 L_n^{(j)}=\sum_qB_j(p_q)\quad(j\geq2).
\end{equation}
Integral comparison shows
\[
 \sum_qp_q=t\log(1+n/t)+O(1),\qquad
 \sum_qp_q^2=O(t+1).
\]
This recovers the fixed-derivative estimates without numerical
differentiation. It also furnishes the optional diagnostic program
with a numerically stable finite-sum route, independent of repeated
differentiation of the gamma quotient.

\section{Contour control and the integrated expansion}\label{sfp:pc:sec:contour}

Only integer $n$ is needed in the coefficient integral. All real
parameter arguments above serve estimates and the later inverse;
we do not integrate a noninteger-size generating function.
Fix a small constant $0<\rho<\pi/3$.

\begin{lemma}[A fixed complex neighborhood]\label{sfp:pc:lem:complex}
For each fixed $j\geq1$, the analytic logarithm of $F_n$ agreeing
with $L_n$ on the real line satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:complex}
 |L_n^{(j)}(z)|\leq C_jn/r\qquad(|z-r|\leq\rho)
\end{equation}
for all sufficiently large $n$. If
$f(\theta)=L_n(re^{\ii\theta})$, then, on intervals with
$r|\theta|=o(1)$,
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:fderivatives}
 |f^{(j)}(\theta)|\leq C_jnr^{j-1}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
In the disk $|z-r|\leq\rho$, one has
$\operatorname{Re}e^z\geq c e^r=ct$. Hence every factor
$e^z+q$ is nonzero there, and
\[
 \left|\frac{e^z}{e^z+q}\right|
       \leq C\frac{t}{t+q}=Cp_q.
\]
The logarithmic derivatives of $e^z+q$ are the same fixed
Bernoulli cumulant polynomials as in \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:bernoulli},
evaluated at $e^z/(e^z+q)$. Each has zero constant coefficient.
The sum of their absolute values is bounded by a fixed multiple
of $\sum_qp_q=O(n/r)$; the extra term $z$ is harmless.
A nonvanishing analytic function on this disk has an analytic
logarithm, establishing \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:complex}. Applying the chain
rule repeatedly, or \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:operator}, gives
\eqref{sfp:pc:eq:fderivatives}.
\end{proof}

Set $\delta=n^{-2/5}$. At $\theta=0$,
$f^{(j)}(0)=\ii^j\kappa_j$. Taylor's theorem through degree two
therefore gives, uniformly for $|\theta|\leq\delta$,
\begin{align}
 \operatorname{Re}\{L_n(re^{\ii\theta})-L_n(r)-\ii n\theta\}
 &\leq-\tfrac12b\theta^2+Cnr^2|\theta|^3\notag\\
 &\leq-\tfrac14b\theta^2.\label{sfp:pc:eq:localgaussian}
\end{align}
The last inequality follows from $b\sim nr$ and $r\delta\to0$.

\begin{lemma}[The full complementary circle]\label{sfp:pc:lem:minor}
For some fixed $c>0$ and all large $n$,
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:minor}
 \sup_{\delta\leq|\theta|\leq\pi}
 \frac{|F_n(re^{\ii\theta})|}{F_n(r)}
       \leq e^{-cn\delta^2}=e^{-cn^{1/5}}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The positive exponential sum \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:positive} implies
\begin{align*}
 |F_n(re^{\ii\theta})|
 &\leq\sum_m\sone nm e^{(m+1)r\cos\theta}\\
 &=F_n(r\cos\theta)\leq F_n(r\cos\delta)
       \qquad(\delta\leq|\theta|\leq\pi).
\end{align*}
The function $F_n$ is increasing on the entire real line because
all its exponential-sum coefficients are nonnegative and their
exponents are positive. On $s\in[r\cos\delta,r]$, one has
$s-r=o(1)$. The finite-sum estimates
\eqref{sfp:pc:eq:integerderivatives} give $L_n'(s)\sim n/r$ uniformly
there. Since $r-r\cos\delta\asymp r\delta^2$,
\[
 L_n(r\cos\delta)-L_n(r)
       =-\int_{r\cos\delta}^r L_n'(s)\dd s
       \leq-cn\delta^2.
\]
This proves the assertion over the whole complementary circle.
\end{proof}

The last argument is useful because $e^z$ has an imaginary period,
which can make an informal local saddle calculation look vulnerable
to secondary peaks. The bound handles the entire circle at once;
no alleged periodic peak has been omitted.

\subsection{Normalization and the central cutoff}

Cauchy's coefficient formula is
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:cauchy}
 \frac{a_nr^n}{n!F_n(r)}
 =\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}
      \exp\{L_n(re^{\ii\theta})-L_n(r)-\ii n\theta\}\dd\theta,
\end{equation}
where the integrand on the full circle is interpreted as
$F_n(re^{\ii\theta})e^{-\ii n\theta}/F_n(r)$, so a global
logarithm is unnecessary. Only the local arc uses its chosen
analytic logarithm. After multiplying by $\sqrt{2\pi b}$,
Lemma~\ref{sfp:pc:lem:minor} bounds the complementary contribution by
\[
 O\bigl(\sqrt b\,e^{-cn^{1/5}}\bigr),
\]
which is smaller than every fixed power of $r/n$.

Write
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:normalization}
 \eta=\sqrt{r/n},\qquad u=\sqrt b\,\theta,\qquad
 \lambda_j=\frac{\kappa_j}{b^{j/2}}.
\end{equation}
Then $\lambda_j=O_j(\eta^{j-2})$ for every fixed $j\geq3$.
Restrict further to $|u|\leq U=\eta^{-1/6}$. This interval lies
inside $|\theta|\leq\delta$, since
\[
 \frac U{\sqrt b}=O(n^{-5/12}r^{-7/12})=o(n^{-2/5}).
\]
By \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:localgaussian}, the omitted part of the local arc
has normalized integral $O(e^{-cU^2})$. This too is smaller than
any fixed power of $\eta$.

\subsection{A weighted remainder that can be integrated}

Fix $R\geq1$. Taylor expansion through degree $2R+2$, using
Lemma~\ref{sfp:pc:lem:complex} at degree $2R+3$, gives
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:centralphase}
 L_n(re^{\ii\theta})-L_n(r)-\ii n\theta
       =-u^2/2+Q(u)+\mathcal R(u),
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:Q}
 Q(u)=\sum_{j=3}^{2R+2}\frac{\lambda_j(\ii u)^j}{j!},
 \qquad
 |\mathcal R(u)|\leq C_R\eta^{2R+1}|u|^{2R+3}.
\end{equation}
Indeed the normalized derivative bound is
$nr^{2R+2}/b^{(2R+3)/2}=O_R(\eta^{2R+1})$.
On $|u|\leq U$, the bounds imply
$|Q(u)|\leq C_R\eta|u|^3=o(1)$ and
$|\mathcal R(u)|=o(1)$. Therefore
\[
 |e^{Q(u)+\mathcal R(u)}-e^{Q(u)}|
       \leq C_R|\mathcal R(u)|,
\]
and its integral against $e^{-u^2/2}$ is
$O_R(\eta^{2R+1})$.

For the remaining exponential, write
$\lambda_j=\eta^{j-2}c_j$, where all the finitely many $c_j$
are bounded uniformly in $n$. Introduce an auxiliary real variable
$s$ and set
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:H}
 H(s,u)=\exp\left(
    \sum_{j=3}^{2R+2}\frac{c_j(\ii u)^j s^{j-2}}{j!}
                       \right).
\end{equation}
The exponent is bounded uniformly for $0\leq s\leq\eta$ and
$|u|\leq U$. Differentiating $H$ exactly $2R$ times with respect
to $s$ produces $H$ times a finite polynomial in $u$, the bounded
$c_j$, and nonnegative powers of $s$. Thus there is a fixed
integer $M_R$ for which Taylor's theorem in $s$ yields
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:weightedTaylor}
 H(\eta,u)=\sum_{d=0}^{2R-1}\eta^dH_d(u)
          +O_R\bigl(\eta^{2R}(1+|u|^{M_R})\bigr).
\end{equation}
The bound is integrable against the Gaussian, uniformly in $n$.
This is the necessary integrated error argument. A pointwise Taylor
bound evaluated only at the outer cutoff would not by itself prove
the asserted relative remainder.

Every monomial of weighted degree $d$ has ordinary degree
$J=\sum j m_j$ in $u$, while
$d=\sum(j-2)m_j$. Hence $J\equiv d\pmod2$.
Odd $d$ terms integrate to zero on the symmetric interval.
For even $d=2q$, extending each polynomial Gaussian integral from
$[-U,U]$ to $\mathbb R$ incurs only an exponentially decaying
error. The identities
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:Gaussian}
 \frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-u^2/2}u^{2v}\dd u
     =(2v-1)!!,\qquad
 \int_{\mathbb R}e^{-u^2/2}u^{2v+1}\dd u=0
\end{equation}
give precisely the contraction formula \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:Ek}.
Combining \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:cauchy}--\eqref{sfp:pc:eq:weightedTaylor} proves
\eqref{sfp:pc:eq:main} with error $O_R(\eta^{2R})=O_R((r/n)^R)$.
Each monomial of $E_q$ is $O_q(\eta^{2q})$, proving its size
claim. Finally, substituting $\kappa_j\sim nr^{j-1}$ and
$b\sim nr$ into \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:E1} gives
$(1/8-5/24)r/n=-r/(12n)$. This completes the proof of
Theorem~\ref{sfp:pc:thm:main}.

\section{Exact coefficient calculus}\label{sfp:pc:sec:coefficients}

The coefficients are finite universal expressions in the exact
cumulants. For example, the next one after \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:E1} is
\begin{align}\label{sfp:pc:eq:E2}
 E_2={}&-\frac{\kappa_6}{48b^3}
      +\frac{7\kappa_3\kappa_5}{48b^4}
      +\frac{35\kappa_4^2}{384b^4}\notag\\
     &-\frac{35\kappa_3^2\kappa_4}{64b^5}
      +\frac{385\kappa_3^4}{1152b^6}.
\end{align}
No limiting replacement of a cumulant is part of this formula.

\subsection{Two independent finite generation rules}

Formula \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:Ek} can be generated by enumerating the
nonnegative integer solutions of
$\sum_{j\geq3}(j-2)m_j=2q$. There is also an algebraically
independent formal exponential recurrence. For indeterminates
$x_3,x_4,\ldots$, set
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:formalQ}
 Q_w(u)=\frac{x_{w+2}(\ii u)^{w+2}}{(w+2)!},\qquad
 \exp\left(\sum_{w\geq1}Q_w(u)s^w\right)
     =\sum_{d\geq0}H_d(u)s^d.
\end{equation}
Formal differentiation with respect to $s$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:formalrecurrence}
 H_0(u)=1,\qquad
 H_d(u)=\frac1d\sum_{w=1}^d wQ_w(u)H_{d-w}(u).
\end{equation}
Integrating $H_{2q}$ term by term against the standard Gaussian
and then putting $x_j=\kappa_j/b^{j/2}$ yields $E_q$.
Every step takes place in a finite rational polynomial ring after
a degree cutoff; convergence of the formal infinite series is not
asserted or needed. The companion compares this recurrence with
the weighted-partition formula using exact rational arithmetic.

For the Bernoulli polynomials there are likewise two exact rules:
\eqref{sfp:pc:eq:bernoulli} and
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:BernoulliStirling}
 B_j(p)=\sum_{v=1}^j(-1)^{v-1}(v-1)!\stwo jv p^v.
\end{equation}
The latter follows by expanding
$\log(1+p(e^z-1))$ in powers of $p$ and extracting the $j$th
derivative at zero. Comparison of these two constructions checks
the derivative input used by the numerical diagnostics.

\subsection{What an exact finite check certifies}

The rational contraction identities, Stirling recurrences,
Bernoulli identities, and agreement of finite sequence values can
all be certified by terminating exact calculations. They check
signs, factorial normalizations, parity, and the correct sequence
convention. They do not prove the uniform contour estimate or an
asymptotic remainder. Those claims depend on the analytic arguments
in Sections~\ref{sfp:pc:sec:real} and \ref{sfp:pc:sec:contour}.

Similarly, a small numerical residual at a finite list of sizes is
not an interval bound for all larger sizes. The optional numerical
program is explicitly a diagnostic. In particular, it cannot turn
the asymptotic threshold theorem into a certified finite-$X$
rounding oracle without explicit constants and an onset.

\section{Uniformity for comparable moment orders}\label{sfp:pc:sec:aspect}

We give the details needed for Theorem~\ref{sfp:pc:thm:aspect}, rather than
merely substituting $k$ for $n$ in the diagonal formula. Let
$\lambda=k/n\in[\alpha,\beta]$ and choose the unique positive
saddle satisfying $rL_n'(r)=k$. The same existence proof applies,
since $rL_n'(r)$ increases from zero to infinity.

The preliminary comparison at $r=\tfrac12\log n$ and
$r=\log n$ now gives
\[
 \tfrac12\log n<r<\log n
\]
for all large $n$, uniformly in $\lambda$. Put $t=e^r$ and
$x=n/t$. The saddle relation becomes
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:aspectx}
 \frac{\lambda x}{r}=\log(1+x)+O(t^{-1}).
\end{equation}
Since $\lambda$ stays bounded above and away from zero,
$x\to\infty$ uniformly and inversion gives
\[
 x\sim\frac r\lambda\log r,
 \qquad \log x=\log r+\log\log r-\log\lambda+o(1),
\]
uniformly. Taking $r=\log n-\log x$ proves
\eqref{sfp:pc:eq:aspectr}. The derivative estimates become
\[
 L_n^{(j)}(r)=k/r+O_j(t+1)\sim k/r,
 \qquad \kappa_j\sim kr^{j-1}.
\]
The constants can be chosen uniformly on the compact ratio interval.

The disk $|z-r|\leq\rho$ used in Lemma~\ref{sfp:pc:lem:complex} is
unchanged. The derivative bound is now $C_jkr^{j-1}$ for the
angular function. With $\delta=n^{-2/5}$, positivity gives a
complementary-circle bound $\exp(-ck\delta^2)$, where $c>0$
can be chosen uniformly in $\lambda$. Since $k\asymp n$, this
is at most $\exp(-c' n^{1/5})$. The central Gaussian argument
uses $\eta=\sqrt{r/k}$ and $U=\eta^{-1/6}$; both the cutoff
inclusion and every fixed-degree integrated remainder remain
uniform. Applying Cauchy's formula to
$A_{n,k}=k![z^k]F_n(z)$ proves \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:aspect}.

This theorem leaves two different boundary regimes open. Fixed
or slowly growing $k$ is not covered by a compact positive ratio;
neither is $k/n\to\infty$. A theorem connecting these regimes
would need new uniform estimates, not an unqualified appeal to
\eqref{sfp:pc:eq:aspect}.

\section{Differentiating the smooth model}\label{sfp:pc:sec:differentiation}

We now prove the derivative claim needed for inversion. All
derivatives in this section are of exact gamma, polygamma, and
finite cumulant expressions. No derivative is taken of the
asymptotic error in \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:main}.

\begin{lemma}[Parameter derivatives]\label{sfp:pc:lem:parameter}
For fixed $j\geq1$, along the real saddle,
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:kappaderivative}
 r_N\sim N^{-1},\qquad
 \frac{\mathrm d\kappa_j}{\mathrm dN}=O_j(r^{j-1}),
 \qquad \frac{b_N}{b}=O(N^{-1}).
\end{equation}
For fixed $q\geq1$,
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:Ederivative}
 E_q'(N)=O_q\left(\frac{(r/N)^q}{N}\right).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
With $r$ temporarily held fixed, direct differentiation gives
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:partialN}
 \partial_NL_N^{(j)}(r)
  =(t\partial_t)^j\psi(N+t)
  =\sum_{v=1}^j\stwo jv t^v\psi^{(v)}(N+t)
  =O_j(t/N).
\end{equation}
The last bound follows from Lemma~\ref{sfp:pc:lem:psi}, since $t/N\to0$.
Implicit differentiation of \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:saddle} gives exactly
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:rprime}
 r_N=\frac{r\{1-rt\psi'(N+t)\}}{b}.
\end{equation}
Now $rt\psi'(N+t)\sim rt/N\sim1/\log r\to0$ and
$b\sim Nr$, proving $r_N\sim1/N$.

From \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:operator}, \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:Lderivatives}, and
\eqref{sfp:pc:eq:partialN}, with the other variable fixed,
\[
 \partial_r\kappa_j=O_j(Nr^{j-1}),\qquad
 \partial_N\kappa_j=O_j(r^jt/N).
\]
The chain rule and \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:rprime} give
\[
 \frac{\mathrm d\kappa_j}{\mathrm dN}
 =\partial_N\kappa_j+r_N\partial_r\kappa_j
 =O_j(r^{j-1}),
\]
because $rt/N=O(1)$. Division by
$\kappa_j\sim Nr^{j-1}$ shows that every fixed $\kappa_j$
is eventually positive with logarithmic derivative $O_j(1/N)$.
The same applies to $b$.

Differentiate each monomial of \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:Ek} separately.
Its size is $O_q((r/N)^q)$, and the product rule multiplies
that bound by $O_q(1/N)$. Summing finitely many terms proves
\eqref{sfp:pc:eq:Ederivative}. This argument does not assume that the
sum $E_q$ itself is nonzero.
\end{proof}

It follows that
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:Pbounds}
 P_R=1+O_R(r/N),\qquad
 P_R'=O_R(r/N^2).
\end{equation}
For $R=1$, the derivative is exactly zero. Thus $P_R>0$ for
large $N$. In the logarithm of \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:YR}, all terms involving
$r_N$ from $L_N(r)-N\log r$ cancel by the saddle equation.
The exact derivative is
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:envelope}
 (\log Y_R)'=
 \psi(N+1)+\psi(N+t)-\log r
 -\frac{b_N}{2b}+\frac{P_R'}{P_R}.
\end{equation}
Lemma~\ref{sfp:pc:lem:psi} and \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:Pbounds} yield
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:logYderivative}
 (\log Y_R)'=2\log N-\log r
             +O_R(t/N+1/N)
           =2\log N-\log r+o(1).
\end{equation}
This proves eventual strict monotonicity. It also shows
$Y_R(N)\to\infty$, so every sufficiently large $X$ has one
solution in the eventual increasing range. There is no need to
assert global monotonicity of $Y_R$ at small $N$.

\section{Threshold windows and strict equality edges}\label{sfp:pc:sec:thresholds}

We first check exact monotonicity of the original sequence.
The unsigned Stirling recurrence gives, for $n\geq1$,
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:monotone}
 a_{n+1}=\sum_{m=0}^n\sone nm
       \{n(m+1)^{n+1}+(m+2)^{n+1}\}>a_n.
\end{equation}
Also $a_1=2>a_0=1$. Since $a_n\geq n!$, the sequence tends to
infinity, and both thresholds are well defined.
\begin{writenote}[6 October 2026]
The step behind \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:monotone} is $\sone{n+1}m=n\sone nm+\sone n{m-1}$. Applied to \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:stirlingproduct} instead, the same recurrence gives an identity for the third member of the family, A192562 (Proposition~\ref{sfp:prop:a192562}).
\end{writenote}

Theorem~\ref{sfp:pc:thm:main} and $P_R=1+O_R(r/n)$ imply
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:logerror}
 \log a_n-\log Y_R(n)
   =O_R\left((r(n)/n)^R\right)
   =O_R\left((\log n/n)^R\right).
\end{equation}
Fix a sufficiently large $X$, abbreviate $\nu=\nu_R$, and take
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:rounded}
 n_- =\floor{\nu-\eps},\qquad
 n_+ =\ceil{\nu+\eps},\qquad
 \eps=C_R\frac{(\log\nu)^{R-1}}{\nu^R}.
\end{equation}
Both integers are asymptotic to $\nu$ and, for large $X$, lie
beyond every onset used in the proof. On the short intervals
joining them to $\nu$, \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:logYderivative} is at least
$\log\nu$. Consequently
\begin{align}
 \log Y_R(\nu)-\log Y_R(n_-)&\geq\eps\log\nu,\notag\\
 \log Y_R(n_+)-\log Y_R(\nu)&\geq\eps\log\nu.
 \label{sfp:pc:eq:strictgaps}
\end{align}
The local errors in \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:logerror} are bounded by a constant
multiple of $(\log\nu/\nu)^R$. Choose $C_R$ strictly larger
than that constant, increasing the onset if necessary. Since
$\eps\log\nu=C_R(\log\nu/\nu)^R$, this gives the
\emph{strict} inequalities
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:strictendpoints}
 a_{n_-}<X<a_{n_+}.
\end{equation}
Exact monotonicity now proves \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:bracket}. A fixed initial
prefix causes no difficulty: increasing $X$ past its largest entry
excludes it automatically.

\subsection{Why the strict inequalities are necessary}

The lower edge is $n_-+1$, not merely $n_-$. The upper edge
bounds the strict threshold because $X<a_{n_+}$, not just
$X\leq a_{n_+}$. If $X=a_j$, the threshold definitions give
$j$ and $j+1$ respectively. Any derivation which tacitly identifies
these two thresholds at equality would be false. The strictly
larger error constant in \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:strictgaps} ensures that
\eqref{sfp:pc:eq:bracket} covers this case as written.

The theorem supplies a hierarchy of centers. At $R=1$, the
smooth uncertainty is $O(\nu^{-1})$; at $R=2$ it is
$O(\log\nu/\nu^2)$; at $R=3$ it is
$O((\log\nu)^2/\nu^3)$. The constants are not numerically
certified here. These are asymptotic precision statements, not
precomputed finite-input guarantees. A certified implementation
would require effective constants for the contour and central
errors together with interval evaluation of the saddle and inverse.

\section{First corrected nested logarithm inversion}\label{sfp:pc:sec:nested}

The fine inverse center is defined implicitly, but its leading
nested-logarithm form can be proved with an explicit relative
remainder. We begin with the required logarithmic growth estimate.

\begin{lemma}[Logarithmic size of the smooth model]\label{sfp:pc:lem:loggrowth}
For every fixed $R\geq1$,
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:loggrowth}
 \log Y_R(N)
 =2N\log N-N\log\log N-2N
   +O_R\left(\frac{N\log\log N}{\log N}\right).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The logarithmic Stirling formula with its positive-real remainder
bound \cite[5.11.1 and 5.11(ii)]{dlmf}, together with $t/N\to0$,
gives
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:Stirlingratio}
 \log\Gamma(N+t)-\log\Gamma(t)
 =N\log N-N+t\log(N/t)
      +O(t+t^2/N+\log N).
\end{equation}
For example, expand $(N+t)\log(N+t)$ around $N$ using
$\log(1+t/N)=t/N+O((t/N)^2)$; the remaining half-logarithm
terms are $O(\log N)$ and the Stirling remainders are bounded.
The saddle relation yields
$t\log(1+N/t)=N/r+O(1)$ and hence
$t\log(N/t)=O(N/r)$. Also
$t+t^2/N+\log N=O(N/r)$.
Adding the initial term $r$ in \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:LN} therefore gives
\[
 L_N(r)=N\log N-N+O(N/r).
\]
Now use
$\log\Gamma(N+1)=N\log N-N+O(\log N)$,
$\log b=O(\log N)$, and $\log P_R=O_R(r/N)$ in
\eqref{sfp:pc:eq:YR}. We obtain
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:loggrowthr}
 \log Y_R(N)=2N\log N-N\log r-2N+O_R(N/r).
\end{equation}
From \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:rscale},
$\log r=\log\log N+O(\log\log N/\log N)$.
Substitution proves \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:loggrowth}.
\end{proof}

Let $L=\log X$, $\ell=\log L$, and $m=\log\ell$.
Equation \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:loggrowth} first implies
$\nu_R\sim L/(2\ell)$: it gives
$L\sim2\nu_R\log\nu_R$, so
$\log\nu_R\sim\log L$ and then the stated equivalent.
Set
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:x0}
 c=\tfrac32m+1+\log2,
 \qquad x_0=\frac{L}{2\ell}\left(1+\frac c\ell\right).
\end{equation}
Elementary logarithmic expansions give
\begin{align}
 \log x_0&=\ell-m-\log2+c/\ell+O(m^2/\ell^2),
                  \label{sfp:pc:eq:logx0}\\
 \log\log x_0&=m-(m+\log2)/\ell+O(m^2/\ell^2).
                  \label{sfp:pc:eq:loglogx0}
\end{align}
On substituting \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:logx0}--\eqref{sfp:pc:eq:loglogx0} into
\eqref{sfp:pc:eq:loggrowth}, the coefficient of $L/\ell$ cancels:
\[
 c-\tfrac32m-1-\log2=0.
\]
The result is
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:pc:eq:x0error}
 \log Y_R(x_0)=L+O_R(Lm^2/\ell^2).
\end{equation}
Both $x_0$ and $\nu_R$ are asymptotic to $L/(2\ell)$.
Throughout the interval between them, \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:Yderivative}
is of order $\ell$. The mean value theorem applied to
\eqref{sfp:pc:eq:x0error} consequently gives
\[
 \nu_R-x_0=O_R(Lm^2/\ell^3).
\]
This is exactly \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:nested}, including its relative
$O_R(m^2/\ell^2)$ remainder, and completes the proof of
Theorem~\ref{sfp:pc:thm:inverse}.

The coefficient $3/2$ has two contributions: inversion of
$N\log N$ contributes one factor of $\log\log L$, and the
term $-N\log\log N$ contributes an additional half after
normalization by $2N\log N$. The constants $1+\log2$ likewise
come from the $-2N$ term and the leading factor two. This explanation
is an interpretation of the explicit cancellation, not a substitute
for its remainder calculation.
\begin{writenote}[6 October 2026]
With \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:logerror}, Lemma~\ref{sfp:pc:lem:loggrowth} gives $\log a_n=2n\log n-n\log\log n-2n+O(n\log\log n/\log n)$. Part~II's \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:logleading} is $\log b_n=2n\log n-2n+O((\log n)^2)$, so $\log(a_n/b_n)=-n\log\log n+O(n\log\log n/\log n)$: the two sequences share the factorial core $2N\log N-2N$, and Part~II's leading inverse \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:leadinginverse} is the exact inverse of that core. Because of the extra term $-N\log\log N$, Part~I inverts by nested logarithms \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:nested} rather than through that core.
\end{writenote}

\section{Limits and further research}\label{sfp:pc:sec:questions}

The results cover each fixed truncation order with a proved
integrated remainder. They do not provide estimates uniform in
$R\to\infty$, an optimal truncation theorem, or a decomposition
into exponentially small asymptotic sectors. The fact that the
noncentral contour is smaller than every fixed algebraic order
does not, by itself, supply any of those stronger claims.

Several extensions have concrete mathematical content:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Effective remainder constants.}
      Can one turn the disk, contour, and weighted Taylor bounds
      into practical explicit constants and onsets? Combining
      those with interval gamma and polygamma evaluation could
      produce certified finite-$X$ threshold decisions.
\item \textbf{Transition between moment regimes.}
      Determine a uniform expansion as $k/n\to0$, connecting
      fixed-order cycle moments to the present growing-order
      regime. The transition as $k/n\to\infty$ is a separate
      problem; it may involve substantially different saddle scales.
\item \textbf{Cycle statistics under the moment weight.}
      For the probability proportional to
      $\sone nm(m+1)^k$, obtain a limit law and local estimates
      for $m$. This would describe the cycle counts typical of
      large poly-Cauchy structures rather than just their total
      number. No such limit law is asserted here.
\item \textbf{Further explicit inverse terms.}
      Sharpen \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:loggrowthr} using higher saddle-scale and
      gamma expansions, and derive a full hierarchy in nested
      logarithms with proved remainders. Its error should remain
      distinct from the much finer exact-saddle inverse window.
\item \textbf{Restricted and shifted families.}
      Investigate restricted cycle lengths and shifted poly-Cauchy
      parameters after a complete comparison with Yakymiv and the
      shifted-family literature. Positivity may still control
      global contours, but the local derivative scales require
      separate proofs.
\item \textbf{Large truncation orders.}
      Study the growth of the contraction polynomials and their
      analytic remainder constants. A valid growing-order or
      exponential-improvement theorem would need information beyond
      the fixed-order estimates established here.
\end{enumerate}

These questions are proposed research directions, not claims of
proved results or of absence from all prior literature. In particular,
reading the uninspected cycle-moment source is a priority before

\section{Sequence and principal conclusions}\label{sfp:mc:sec:overview}

Throughout, $\stir{m}{j}$ denotes an unsigned Stirling number of the
first kind. Define
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:definition}
 b_n=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\stir{n+1}{k+1}^{\!2}k!,\qquad
 P_n(z)=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left(1+\frac zj\right),\qquad
 B_n=\frac{b_n}{(n!)^2}.
\end{equation}
An empty product is $1$, so $b_0=B_0=1$.
The sequence is OEIS A192561~\cite{oeis}; the indexing in
\eqref{sfp:mc:eq:definition} is maintained throughout. All logarithms of
positive real quantities are natural logarithms, and $W_0$ denotes
the principal real Lambert function: $W_0(x)e^{W_0(x)}=x$ for $x>0$.

In the notation \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:inner}, $b_n=\langle n!P_n,n!P_n\rangle_{\mathcal F}$, while Part~I's $a_n=\langle n!P_n,\mathfrak T_n\rangle_{\mathcal F}$ with $\mathfrak T_n(z)=\sum_k\stwo{n+1}{k+1}z^k$: the two sequences pair the same polynomial with itself and with a second-kind polynomial (front matter, ``One family, two methods'').
\end{writenote}

The factorial weight is essential. It rewards configurations with
many cycles and changes the analytic norm from a circle norm to a
planar Gaussian norm. An asymptotic for the unweighted sum of squares
cannot be substituted for \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:definition}.

\subsection{An exact expansion before taking any limit}
For $n\geq1$, let $a=a_n>0$ solve
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:saddle}
 a=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac1{j+a},
\end{equation}
and let $S_r=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(j+a)^{-r}$. Write
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:centerdef}
 e^{-at}\frac{P_n(a+t)}{P_n(a)}=\sum_{k\geq0}c_k t^k,
 \qquad C_{n,K}=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}k!c_k^2.
\end{equation}
The coefficients are real, $c_0=1$, $c_1=0$, and the recurrence
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:recintro}
 kc_k=\sum_{r=2}^{k}(-1)^{r-1}S_r c_{k-r}\qquad(k\geq2)
\end{equation}
uses only finite sums. We prove the exact statement
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:certificateintro}
 B_n=e^{-a^2}P_n(a)^2(C_{n,K}+\varepsilon_{n,K}),\qquad
 0\leq\varepsilon_{n,K}\leq
 (K+1)\left(\frac{K+1}{K}S_2\right)^K
\end{equation}
for every $n\geq1$ and $K\geq2$. In particular, the error is at most
$e(K+1)\varphi^{-K}$. This is an absolute bound on the normalized
factor in parentheses; since that factor is at least $1$, it also
bounds the relative error made by retaining $C_{n,K}$.

A rational center replaces the algebraic root $a$ when an exact
arithmetic certificate is desired. The resulting recurrence has a
nonzero linear term, and its explicit tail contains one additional
positive exponential factor. Section~\ref{sfp:mc:sec:rational} supplies
both the theorem and a rational exponential enclosure.

\subsection{All fixed logarithmic orders}
Put $w=W_0(en)$. For every fixed integer $R\geq1$, we prove
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:mainintro}
 B_n=\frac{e^{w^2+1}}{2\pi w}
 \left\{\sum_{r=0}^{R-1}\frac{A_r}{w^r}+O_R(w^{-R})\right\}.
\end{equation}
The first terms are
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:firstintro}
 \frac{2\pi wB_n}{e^{w^2+1}}
 =1-\frac1{6w}+\frac{55}{72w^2}
  +\frac{1381}{6480w^3}+\frac{76541}{155520w^4}+O(w^{-5}).
\end{equation}
The constant in the exponential is $+1$. Every coefficient is
rational and generated symbolically, rather than fitted to data.
No convergence of the series in $1/w$, and no growing-order error
bound, is asserted.

\subsection{Threshold inversion with rounding retained}
Let $N(T)=\min\{n\geq0:b_n\geq T\}$ for $T>1$. With
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:inverseintrodefs}
 L=\log T,\qquad
 u=\frac{L}{2W_0(L/(2e))},\qquad D=\log u,\qquad w_u=W_0(eu),
\end{equation}
define $Q_R(w)=\sum_{r=0}^{R-1}A_rw^{-r}$ and
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:inverseintro}
 v_R=u-\frac{w_u^2+\log(u/w_u)+1+\log Q_R(w_u)}{2D}.
\end{equation}
For each fixed $R$, there are constants $C_R,T_R>0$ such that, for
$T\geq T_R$,
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:inverseintrobracket}
 \ceil{v_R-C_RD^{-R-1}}\leq N(T)
 \leq\ceil{v_R+C_RD^{-R-1}}.
\end{equation}
The constants and onset are not numerically provided. Consequently,
\eqref{sfp:mc:eq:inverseintrobracket} is an asymptotic theorem, not a
ready-to-use finite-$T$ numerical certificate. Actual certified
threshold decisions can instead use the explicit Fock bounds and
exact integer checks.

\section{Combinatorial interpretation and Gaussian orthogonality}
\label{sfp:mc:sec:fock}

\subsection{Matched cycles and the bivariate generating function}
Consider a permutation on an $n$-element labeled set together with
one special element. If the permutation has $k+1$ cycles, exactly
one cycle contains the special element. Thus $\stir{n+1}{k+1}$
counts a distinguished cycle and $k$ other cycles. Take two such
permutations on separate labeled sets of size $n$, and a bijection
between their $k$ nondistinguished cycles. There are $k!$ choices
of this bijection. Summing gives \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:definition}. We use
``matched cycles'' as a descriptive name for this interpretation,
without a historical naming claim.

Let $L(x)=-\log(1-x)$. A cycle containing the special element is
an ordered list of ordinary labels, with exponential generating
function $(1-x)^{-1}$. An ordinary cycle has generating function
$L(x)$. A set of matched pairs of cycles on the two label types
has generating function $\exp(L(x)L(y))$. Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:egf}
 \frac{\exp(L(x)L(y))}{(1-x)(1-y)}
 =\sum_{m,n\geq0}\left(\sum_{k\geq0}
   \stir{m+1}{k+1}\stir{n+1}{k+1}k!\right)
   \frac{x^m y^n}{m!n!}.
\end{equation}
In particular, $B_n$ is its diagonal coefficient. Substituting
$x=y$ would sum all coefficients on an antidiagonal, not extract
this diagonal. That distinction matters when comparing univariate
alg-log results with the present problem.
\begin{writenote}[6 October 2026]
Equation~\eqref{sfp:mc:eq:egf} also follows termwise from $\sum_m\stir{m+1}{k+1}x^m/m!=L(x)^k/(k!\,(1-x))$, the derivative of $\sum_N\stir N{k+1}x^N/N!=L(x)^{k+1}/(k+1)!$. Part~I's double generating function \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:doubleegf} is the counterpart for the array A344639; there the diagonal is extracted by the one-variable coefficient formula \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:coefficient}, here by the norm \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:polynomial}.
\end{writenote}

\subsection{The classical Fock identity}
For an entire function $f(t)=\sum_{k\geq0}f_kt^k$, define
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:norm}
 \Fock{f}=\frac1\pi\int_{\CC}e^{-|t|^2}|f(t)|^2\dd A(t),
\end{equation}
where $A$ is planar Lebesgue area; the value is allowed to be
infinite. Polar coordinates and circle orthogonality give
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:fock}
 \Fock{f}=\sum_{k\geq0}k!|f_k|^2.
\end{equation}
Indeed, for each radius $r$, uniform convergence on the circle
allows termwise integration and gives
$(2\pi)^{-1}\int_0^{2\pi}|f(re^{i\theta})|^2\dd\theta
=\sum_k|f_k|^2r^{2k}$. Tonelli's theorem then applies to the
nonnegative sum, and
$2\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2}r^{2k+1}\dd r=k!$.
Thus no exchange of a conditionally convergent series is involved.
This is the standard Gaussian Fock norm~\cite{alpayporat}.

The rising-factorial identity yields
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:polynomial}
 P_n(z)=\frac1{n!}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\stir{n+1}{k+1}z^k,
 \qquad B_n=\Fock{P_n}.
\end{equation}
This norm representation will be used directly; a two-variable
contour deformation is unnecessary.

\subsection{Translation and a global product bound}
For any $b\geq0$, set
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:offcenterdefs}
 H_b(t)=e^{-bt}\frac{P_n(b+t)}{P_n(b)},\quad
 S_r(b)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(j+b)^{-r},\quad
 d=S_1(b)-b,\quad s=S_2(b).
\end{equation}
Translating $z=b+t$ in \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:norm} cancels the linear terms
from $e^{-|z|^2}$ and $e^{bt}$, giving the exact identity
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:translation}
 B_n=e^{-b^2}P_n(b)^2\Fock{H_b}.
\end{equation}
The decisive estimate is global in $t$:
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:global}
 |H_b(t)|^2\leq\exp\{2d\operatorname{Re}t+s|t|^2\}.
\end{equation}
To prove it, write $x_j=(j+b)^{-1}>0$. Then
$|1+x_jt|^2=1+2x_j\operatorname{Re}t+x_j^2|t|^2$.
The elementary inequality $1+u\leq e^u$ for real $u$ applies,
including when the left side vanishes. Multiply over $j$ and
include $|e^{-bt}|^2$. There is no logarithmic branch choice and
no excluded zero in this argument.

\section{The exact saddle and its uniform positive certificate}
\label{sfp:mc:sec:exact}

\begin{lemma}[The center and its moments]\label{sfp:mc:lem:saddle}
For every $n\geq1$, equation~\eqref{sfp:mc:eq:saddle} has exactly one
positive solution. At that solution,
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:golden}
 0<S_2\leq\min\left\{\frac{a}{a+1},\frac1a\right\}
 \leq\frac1\varphi<\frac23.
\end{equation}
Moreover, as $n\to\infty$,
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:momentorders}
 a\sim\log n,\qquad S_r\leq\frac{a^{1-r}}{r-1}\quad(r\geq2).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The function $g(a)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(j+a)^{-1}-a$ is continuous,
strictly decreasing, positive at $0$, and tends to $-\infty$.
This proves existence and uniqueness. Since
$(j+a)^{-1}\leq(a+1)^{-1}$,
$S_2\leq S_1/(a+1)=a/(a+1)$.
The decreasing-integral estimate gives
\[
 S_r\leq\int_0^n(a+x)^{-r}\dd x
 <\frac{a^{1-r}}{r-1}.
\]
The functions $a/(a+1)$ and $1/a$ meet at $a=\varphi$,
where both equal $1/\varphi$. One is increasing and the other
decreasing, which proves \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:golden}.

Integral comparison in the defining equation also gives
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:abounds}
 \log\frac{a+n+1}{a+1}\leq a\leq\log\frac{a+n}{a}.
\end{equation}
First $a\to\infty$, since a bounded $a$ would make the sum in
\eqref{sfp:mc:eq:saddle} diverge. Also $a\leq H_n=O(\log n)$.
The left inequality in \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:abounds} is then
$a\geq\log n-O(\log\log n)$, proving $a/\log n\to1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Positive expansion and explicit tail]\label{sfp:mc:thm:exact}
Let $n\geq1$ and use \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:saddle}--\eqref{sfp:mc:eq:centerdef}.
Then
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:exactnorm}
 B_n=e^{-a^2}P_n(a)^2 C_n,\qquad
 C_n=\sum_{k\geq0}k!c_k^2\geq1,
\end{equation}
where $c_0=1$, $c_1=0$, and \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:recintro} holds.
For every integer $K\geq1$ and every $S_2<q<1$,
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:tail}
 0\leq C_n-C_{n,K}\leq\frac{(S_2/q)^K}{1-q}.
\end{equation}
For fixed $K$, the right side is minimized at $q=K/(K+1)$
whenever that point is admissible. It is admissible for every
$n\geq1$ if $K\geq2$, giving \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:certificateintro}.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
At $b=a$, the linear coefficient $d$ in \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:offcenterdefs}
is zero. Hence \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:global} becomes
$|H_a(t)|^2\leq e^{S_2|t|^2}$. By \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:golden}, this
is integrable against the standard Gaussian. Equations
\eqref{sfp:mc:eq:translation} and \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:fock} prove the positive
expansion. Near $t=0$,
\[
 \log H_a(t)=\sum_{r\geq2}\frac{(-1)^{r-1}S_r}{r}t^r
 \qquad(|t|<a+1).
\]
Differentiate this local identity and compare Taylor coefficients
to obtain \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:recintro}. Although the displayed logarithmic
series is local, $H_a$ itself is entire and the recurrence gives
all its Taylor coefficients.

For the tail, apply \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:fock} to
$H_a(\sqrt{q/S_2}\,t)$. The global bound yields
\[
 \sum_{k\geq0}k!c_k^2\left(\frac q{S_2}\right)^k
 \leq\frac1\pi\int_{\CC}e^{-(1-q)|t|^2}\dd A(t)
 =\frac1{1-q}.
\]
Since $q/S_2>1$, deleting the first $K$ terms and multiplying
by $(S_2/q)^K$ proves \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:tail}. Finally,
\[
 \frac\dd{\dd q}\log\frac{(S_2/q)^K}{1-q}
 =-\frac Kq+\frac1{1-q}
\]
changes sign from negative to positive at $K/(K+1)$.
For $K\geq2$, that point is at least $2/3>1/\varphi\geq S_2$.
The estimate $(1+1/K)^K\leq e$ finishes the stated simplification.
\end{proof}

A particularly simple choice works even at $K=1$: $q=3/4$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:uniformsimple}
 C_n-C_{n,K}\leq4(4S_2/3)^K
 \leq4\left(\frac4{3\varphi}\right)^K.
\end{equation}
Thus the exact expansion is uniformly convergent in this normalized
sense over all positive indices. For a tolerance $\epsilon>0$,
choosing a $K$ with the last expression at most $\epsilon$ is a
fully explicit universal rule. It is conservative: the actual
$S_2$ becomes small with $n$.

\subsection{A broader negative real root theorem}
\begin{theorem}[Finite products with negative zeros]\label{sfp:mc:thm:generic}
Let $m\geq1$, $\lambda_j>0$, and
$P(z)=\prod_{j=1}^{m}(1+z/\lambda_j)$.
Let $a>0$ be the unique solution of
$a=\sum_{j=1}^{m}(a+\lambda_j)^{-1}$, and put
$S_r=\sum_{j=1}^{m}(a+\lambda_j)^{-r}$.
Then $S_2<1$ and
\[
 \Fock{P}=e^{-a^2}P(a)^2\sum_{k\geq0}k!c_k^2,
 \qquad
 \sum_{k\geq K}k!c_k^2\leq\frac{(S_2/q)^K}{1-q}
\]
for $S_2<q<1$, where $c_0=1$, $c_1=0$, and the recurrence
\eqref{sfp:mc:eq:recintro} uses these moments.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Existence and uniqueness follow as before. Since $a+\lambda_j>a$,
\[
 S_2<\frac1a\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac1{a+\lambda_j}=1.
\]
The translation, product inequality, coefficient recurrence, and
scaled Gaussian argument are unchanged.
\end{proof}

Repeated roots are allowed. A nonzero constant multiplier is
handled by multiplying the norm and prefactor by its squared
modulus. The universal golden-ratio bound is specific to the
arithmetic progression $\lambda_j=j$; it is not asserted for
arbitrary negative-root polynomials. The general certificate is an
elementary consequence of classical Fock theory, and its scholarly
priority in this formulation has not been established.

\section{Rational centers and certified finite arithmetic}
\label{sfp:mc:sec:rational}

\begin{theorem}[Off-saddle certificate]\label{sfp:mc:thm:rational}
Let $n\geq1$, $b\geq0$, and use \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:offcenterdefs}.
Suppose $0<s<q<1$, and write $H_b(t)=\sum_{k\geq0}c_k(b)t^k$.
Then $c_0(b)=1$, $c_1(b)=d$, and
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:rationalrec}
 kc_k(b)=d\,c_{k-1}(b)+
 \sum_{r=2}^{k}(-1)^{r-1}S_r(b)c_{k-r}(b).
\end{equation}
For every integer $K\geq1$,
\begin{align}
 B_n&=e^{-b^2}P_n(b)^2
    \left(\sum_{k<K}k!c_k(b)^2+\varepsilon_{b,K}\right),
    \label{sfp:mc:eq:rationalidentity}\\
 0\leq\varepsilon_{b,K}
 &\leq\frac{(s/q)^K}{1-q}
     \exp\left(\frac{qd^2}{s(1-q)}\right).
    \label{sfp:mc:eq:rationaltail}
\end{align}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Translation proves \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:rationalidentity}; the local logarithmic
derivative proves \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:rationalrec}. Scale \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:global}
by $t\mapsto\sqrt{q/s}\,t$. Writing $t=x+iy$, the Gaussian
integral is
\[
 \frac1\pi\int_{\mathbb R^2}
 \exp\{-(1-q)(x^2+y^2)+2d\sqrt{q/s}\,x\}\dd x\dd y
 =\frac1{1-q}\exp\left(\frac{qd^2}{s(1-q)}\right).
\]
Apply Fock orthogonality, remove the first $K$ terms, and use
$(q/s)^k\geq(q/s)^K$ for $k\geq K$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{A rational center with a guaranteed admissible scale}
The root lies in $[0,n]$: $g(0)>0$ and
$g(n)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(j+n)^{-1}-n<0$.
Bisection evaluates $g$ using rational arithmetic at every rational
midpoint. After $J$ steps, keep endpoints $\ell\leq a\leq h$
with $h-\ell=n2^{-J}$, and choose $b=h$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:uppercenter}
 S_2(b)\leq S_2(a)\leq1/\varphi,\qquad
 d=g(b)\leq0,\qquad b-a\leq|d|.
\end{equation}
The last inequality follows from $g'(x)=-1-S_2(x)\leq-1$.
For example, $|d|\leq(1+S_2(a))(b-a)$ by integration, so the
extra exponential factor tends to $1$ as the bracket narrows.
No algebraic-number representation of $a$ is needed.

The scales $q=3/4$ and, for $K\geq2$, $q=K/(K+1)$ remain
admissible. When $d\ne0$, the latter is \emph{not generally the
minimizer} of \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:rationaltail}: the extra exponential also
depends on $q$. Calling it the optimal off-saddle scale would be
incorrect. It is a convenient explicit admissible choice.

\subsection{Rational upper and lower bounds for the exponential}
For rational $x\geq0$ and integer $M\geq0$ with $x<M+2$, put
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:expbounds}
 E_M^-(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{M}\frac{x^j}{j!},\qquad
 E_M^+(x)=E_M^-(x)+
 \frac{x^{M+1}}{(M+1)!}\frac1{1-x/(M+2)}.
\end{equation}
Every quantity is rational, and
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:expineq}
 E_M^-(x)\leq e^x\leq E_M^+(x).
\end{equation}
The first omitted term is $x^{M+1}/(M+1)!$, and every successive
ratio in the tail is at most $x/(M+2)<1$. A geometric series
therefore bounds the omitted tail. For $x<0$, reciprocation gives
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:expnegative}
 \frac1{E_M^+(-x)}\leq e^x\leq\frac1{E_M^-(-x)}.
\end{equation}
Range reduction or increasing $M$ can improve efficiency; neither
is needed for the displayed finite certificate.

To make the interval explicit, abbreviate
\[
 C=\sum_{k<K}k!c_k(b)^2,\quad
 V=\frac{(s/q)^K}{1-q},\quad
 E=\frac{qd^2}{s(1-q)},\quad
 Z=(n!)^2P_n(b)^2.
\]
Suppose rational bounds $l_1\leq e^{-b^2}\leq u_1$ and
$l_2\leq e^{E-b^2}\leq u_2$ have been computed by
\eqref{sfp:mc:eq:expbounds}--\eqref{sfp:mc:eq:expnegative}. Then
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:fullyrational}
 \mathcal L=ZCl_1\leq b_n\leq Z(Cu_1+Vu_2)=\mathcal U.
\end{equation}
All comparisons can be performed by exact integer arithmetic on
rational numerators and denominators. In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:uniqueinteger}
 \ceil{\mathcal L}=\floor{\mathcal U}=m
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad b_n=m.
\end{equation}
A narrow floating-point interval alone would not establish this
conclusion. The exponential-tail inequalities and the exact
rational comparisons are the certification mechanism.

\section{Exact center asymptotics and the Gaussian correction}
\label{sfp:mc:sec:moment}

The explicit tail already contains an all-orders exact-center
expansion. For each fixed $K$, take $q=3/4$ in \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:tail}
and use $S_2=O(a^{-1})$ to obtain
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:centerasymptotic}
 B_n=e^{-a^2}P_n(a)^2\{C_{n,K}+O_K(a^{-K})\}.
\end{equation}
The coefficients $c_k$ in this statement are the exact finite-$n$
moment polynomials. Replacing moments by their leading values
without controlling the accumulated errors would change the claim.

The exponential formula gives a second finite description:
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:partitioncoeff}
 c_k=\sum_{\substack{m_2,\ldots,m_k\geq0\\
                    \sum_{r=2}^{k}r m_r=k}}
       \prod_{r=2}^{k}\frac1{m_r!}
       \left(\frac{(-1)^{r-1}S_r}{r}\right)^{m_r}.
\end{equation}
Assign weight $r-1$ to $S_r$ because $S_r=O(a^{1-r})$.
In a monomial of $c_k$, the weight is
$k-\sum m_r\geq\ceil{k/2}$. Hence the product of two monomials
from $c_k$ has weight at least $2\ceil{k/2}\geq k$.
Only finitely many $k$ can contribute below any fixed weight.
This observation, together with the tail bound, rigorously justifies
collecting the expansion by weight.

The first coefficients are
\begin{align*}
 c_2&=-S_2/2,&
 c_3&=S_3/3,&
 c_4&=S_2^2/8-S_4/4,\\
 c_5&=S_5/5-S_2S_3/6,&
 c_6&=-S_6/6+S_2S_4/8+S_3^2/18-S_2^3/48.
\end{align*}
Squaring with the $k!$ weights and retaining total weight at most
six gives
\begin{align}
 C_n={}&1+\frac12S_2^2+\frac38S_2^4+\frac23S_3^2
       -\frac32S_2^2S_4 \notag\\
      &+\frac5{16}S_2^6+\frac{10}{3}S_2^2S_3^2
       +\frac32S_4^2+O(a^{-7}).\label{sfp:mc:eq:weight6}
\end{align}
For example, $24c_4^2=3S_2^4/8-3S_2^2S_4/2+3S_4^2/2$.
The $c_5^2$ term contributes $10S_2^2S_3^2/3$ at weight six,
and the $c_6^2$ term contributes $5S_2^6/16$ there.

Checked by the write symbolically (SymPy): $c_2,\dots,c_6$ above and every term of \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:weight6}.
\end{writenote}

Keeping just the quadratic part of $\log H_a$ gives
$e^{-S_2t^2/2}$. Its norm is exactly
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:gaussian}
 \Fock{e^{-S_2t^2/2}}
 =\sum_{j\geq0}\frac{(2j)!}{(j!)^2}
                  \left(\frac{S_2^2}{4}\right)^j
 =\frac1{\sqrt{1-S_2^2}}.
\end{equation}
Thus the two-dimensional Gaussian approximation is
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:gaussianapprox}
 B_n=\frac{e^{-a^2}P_n(a)^2}{\sqrt{1-S_2^2}}
      \{1+O(a^{-4})\}.
\end{equation}
Its first nonquadratic contribution in the normalized norm is
$2S_3^2/3$, of order $a^{-4}$. The complete finite positive sum,
with \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:tail}, is preferable when a numerical certificate
is required; \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:weight6} is an explanatory asymptotic
rearrangement.

\section{A universal Gamma norm at the Lambert anchor}
\label{sfp:mc:sec:gamma}

The exact center is ideal for finite-$n$ certification. To express
coefficients as rational numbers independent of $n$, use the
Lambert anchor
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:anchor}
 w=W_0(en),\qquad b_*=w-1,\qquad
 \log n=w+\log w-1.
\end{equation}
For $w\geq2$, define the entire function and its norm
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:gammanorm}
 h_w(t)=\frac{w^t\Gamma(w)}{\Gamma(w+t)},\qquad
 \mathcal H(w)=\Fock{h_w}.
\end{equation}
Here $w^t=e^{t\log w}$. We will use
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:gamma-ds}
 d_w=\log w-\psi(w),\qquad
 s_w=\zeta(2,w)=\sum_{j\geq0}(w+j)^{-2},
\end{equation}
where $\psi=\Gamma'/\Gamma$.

The reciprocal-Gamma canonical product gives
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:canonicalgamma}
 h_w(t)=e^{d_wt}
    \prod_{j\geq0}\left(1+\frac{t}{w+j}\right)e^{-t/(w+j)}.
\end{equation}
The product converges locally uniformly. Applying the same factor
inequality as before to its finite partial products and passing to
the limit yields
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:gammaglobal}
 |h_w(t)|^2\leq e^{2d_w\operatorname{Re}t+s_w|t|^2}.
\end{equation}
As $w\to\infty$, $d_w=O(w^{-1})$ and $s_w=O(w^{-1})$.
In particular the norm is finite for all sufficiently large $w$,
and $\mathcal H(w)\geq1$ by the constant coefficient in
\eqref{sfp:mc:eq:fock}. In fact $s_w<1$ for $w\geq2$.

\begin{proposition}[Reduction to the universal norm]\label{sfp:mc:prop:reduction}
As $n\to\infty$, with $w=W_0(en)$,
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:reduction}
 B_n=\frac{n^{2(w-1)}e^{-(w-1)^2}}{\Gamma(w)^2}
       \mathcal H(w)\{1+O(w^2/n)\}.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let
$h_{n,w}(t)=e^{-(w-1)t}P_n(w-1+t)/P_n(w-1)$.
Its linear logarithmic coefficient and quadratic majorant are
\begin{align*}
 d_{n,w}&=\psi(n+w)-\psi(w)-(w-1)
          =O(w^{-1})+O(w/n),\\
 s_{n,w}&=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}(w+j)^{-2}=O(w^{-1}).
\end{align*}
The global estimate \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:global} therefore shows that both
$e^{-|t|^2}|h_{n,w}(t)|^2$ and
$e^{-|t|^2}|h_w(t)|^2$ are bounded by
$C e^{-|t|^2/2}$ for all sufficiently large $n$, with a fixed $C$.
This follows, for example, by absorbing the linear term into a
small multiple of $|t|^2$ and a constant.

The Gamma product identity gives
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:gammaratio}
 h_{n,w}(t)=h_w(t)R_{n,w}(t),\qquad
 R_{n,w}(t)=\frac{\Gamma(n+w+t)}{\Gamma(n+w)n^t}.
\end{equation}
On $|t|\leq\sqrt n/4$, the arguments of the Gamma function in
$R_{n,w}$ lie in the positive half-plane. Thus $R_{n,w}$ has a
consistent analytic logarithm there. Identity
\eqref{sfp:mc:eq:gammaratio} is interpreted by analytic continuation at
zeros of $h_w$, so division by a vanishing function is not needed.
The standard uniform digamma estimate in that half-plane gives
\[
 \psi(n+w+u)-\log n
   =O\left(\frac{w+|u|+1}{n}\right)
 \qquad(|u|\leq\sqrt n/4).
\]
Integrating along the segment from $0$ to $t$ shows that
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:ratiobound}
 \log R_{n,w}(t)
 =O\left(\frac{(w+1)|t|+|t|^2}{n}\right).
\end{equation}
The digamma estimate follows from its usual sectorial asymptotic
expansion~\cite{dlmf-mc}, here used far from the negative axis.

Use $|e^v-1|\leq |v|e^{|v|}$ in
\eqref{sfp:mc:eq:gammaratio}. The exponential factor from
\eqref{sfp:mc:eq:ratiobound} can be absorbed into the global Gaussian
majorant, since its quadratic coefficient is $O(1/n)$ and its
linear coefficient is $O(w/n)$. Gaussian integrals of $|t|$ and
$|t|^2$ then imply a norm difference $O(w/n)$ on this disk.
Outside the disk, both global bounds give tails $O(e^{-cn})$
for some $c>0$. Consequently,
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:normcompare}
 \Fock{h_{n,w}}=\mathcal H(w)+O(w/n)
               =\mathcal H(w)\{1+O(w/n)\}.
\end{equation}
The last step uses $\mathcal H(w)\geq1$.

Finally,
$P_n(w-1)=\Gamma(n+w)/(\Gamma(n+1)\Gamma(w))$ and
\[
 \frac{\Gamma(n+w)}{\Gamma(n+1)n^{w-1}}
       =1+O(w^2/n).
\]
This follows by integrating the real digamma estimate between
$n+1$ and $n+w$, an interval of length $O(w)$.
Combine this prefactor estimate, translation, and
\eqref{sfp:mc:eq:normcompare}.
\end{proof}

The error $w^2/n$ is smaller than every fixed power $w^{-1}$,
since $n=we^{w-1}$. This observation permits an all-orders
inverse-logarithm expansion, but does not identify a complete
exponentially small correction or transseries.

\section{All orders by a finite coefficient argument}
\label{sfp:mc:sec:allorders}

The majorant \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:gammaglobal} makes the all-orders proof
particularly short: truncate the positive norm first, and only
then expand the finitely many retained coefficients. There is no
need to expand the integrand on a growing complex disk.

\subsection{The coefficient generator}
Use the Bernoulli convention $B_1(t)=t-1/2$, and set
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:gm}
 g_m(t)=\frac{(-1)^m}{m(m+1)}
             \{B_{m+1}(t)-B_{m+1}(0)\}\qquad(m\geq1).
\end{equation}
Define real polynomials $p_r(t)$ by
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:pr}
 p_0(t)=1,\qquad
 p_r(t)=\frac1r\sum_{m=1}^{r}m g_m(t)p_{r-m}(t).
\end{equation}
Equivalently,
$\sum_{r\geq0}p_r(t)x^r=\exp(\sum_{m\geq1}g_m(t)x^m)$
as a formal series. Define the finite polynomial inner product
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:inner}
 \langle f,g\rangle_{\mathcal F}
 =\sum_{k\geq0}k![t^k]f(t)[t^k]g(t),\qquad
 H_r=\sum_{j=0}^{r}\langle p_j,p_{r-j}\rangle_{\mathcal F}.
\end{equation}
No complex conjugation is needed here because all coefficients
are real.

The shifted Gamma expansion~\cite[Eq.~5.11.8]{dlmf-mc} gives,
on each fixed $t$-disk,
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:loggammaexp}
 \log h_w(t)\sim\sum_{m\geq1}g_m(t)w^{-m}.
\end{equation}
For example,
\[
 g_1(t)=-\frac{t^2}{2}+\frac t2,\qquad
 g_2(t)=\frac{t^3}{6}-\frac{t^2}{4}+\frac t{12},
\]
and
\[
 p_1(t)=g_1(t),\qquad
 p_2(t)=\frac{t^4}{8}-\frac{t^3}{12}-\frac{t^2}{8}+\frac t{12}.
\]
The nonzero linear term in $p_1$ reflects the fact that $w-1$
is an anchor rather than the exact saddle.

\begin{theorem}[Universal norm expansion]\label{sfp:mc:thm:H}
For every fixed integer $R\geq1$,
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:Hexp}
 \mathcal H(w)=\sum_{r=0}^{R-1}\frac{H_r}{w^r}+O_R(w^{-R})
 \qquad(w\to\infty),
\end{equation}
where \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:gm}--\eqref{sfp:mc:eq:inner} give the coefficients.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Write $h_w(t)=\sum_{k\geq0}h_k(w)t^k$. The scaled-Fock
argument used in Theorem~\ref{sfp:mc:thm:rational}, now applied to
\eqref{sfp:mc:eq:gammaglobal}, gives
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:gammatail}
 \sum_{k\geq K}k!h_k(w)^2
 \leq\frac{(s_w/q)^K}{1-q}
       \exp\left(\frac{qd_w^2}{s_w(1-q)}\right)
 \quad(s_w<q<1).
\end{equation}
For fixed $K$ and all sufficiently large $w$, take $q=1/2$.
Since $s_w=O(w^{-1})$ and $d_w=O(w^{-1})$, while
$s_w\geq\int_0^\infty(w+x)^{-2}\dd x=1/w$,
the exponent is $O(w^{-1})$. The right side is therefore
$O_K(w^{-K})$.

Choose $K=R$. The norm differs from the finite sum
$\sum_{k<R}k!h_k(w)^2$ by $O_R(w^{-R})$.
Each fixed $h_k$ is determined by
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:hkrec}
 kh_k=d_wh_{k-1}
       +\sum_{j=2}^{k}(-1)^{j-1}\zeta(j,w)h_{k-j},
       \qquad h_0=1.
\end{equation}
The fixed-order digamma and Hurwitz-zeta expansions yield a
rigorous finite asymptotic expansion of every such coefficient.
Equivalently, Cauchy's coefficient formula on one fixed disk
applied to the uniformly valid fixed-disk expansion
\eqref{sfp:mc:eq:loggammaexp} gives
\[
 h_k(w)=\sum_{r=0}^{R-1}[t^k]p_r(t)w^{-r}+O_{R,k}(w^{-R}).
\]
Only finitely many coefficients occur, so multiplication and
summation preserve an $O_R(w^{-R})$ remainder.

It remains to check that truncating $k<R$ does not discard any
coefficient $H_r$ for $r<R$. From \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:pr},
$\deg p_r\leq2r$, and $p_r(0)=0$ for $r>0$.
If $a+b=r$ and a monomial degree $k$ occurs in both $p_a$ and
$p_b$, then $k\leq\min(2a,2b)\leq r<R$; the cases $a=0$
or $b=0$ obey the same bound. Thus all the terms defining
$H_0,\ldots,H_{R-1}$ survive the finite norm truncation.
Together with \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:gammatail}, this proves \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:Hexp}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Stirling normalization and the constant term}
Define a formal series $E(x)=\sum_{r\geq0}E_rx^r$ by
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:Edef}
 E(x)=\exp\left(-\sum_{j\geq1}
          \frac{2B_{2j}}{2j(2j-1)}x^{2j-1}\right),\qquad
 A_r=\sum_{j=0}^{r}E_jH_{r-j}.
\end{equation}
Here $B_{2j}=B_{2j}(0)$. Stirling's expansion and
$\log n=w+\log w-1$ show that
\begin{align}
 \log\frac{n^{2(w-1)}e^{-(w-1)^2}}{\Gamma(w)^2}
 ={}&w^2+1-\log(2\pi w)\notag\\
 &-\sum_{j\geq1}\frac{2B_{2j}}{2j(2j-1)w^{2j-1}},
 \label{sfp:mc:eq:normalization}
\end{align}
where the sum is interpreted asymptotically to each fixed order.
For clarity, the elementary part of the cancellation is
\[
 2(w-1)(w+\log w-1)-(w-1)^2
 -2(w-1/2)\log w+2w=w^2+1-\log w.
\]
This fixes both the $+1$ and the factor $w^{-1}$.

\begin{theorem}[Complete inverse logarithm expansion]\label{sfp:mc:thm:main}
With $A_r$ defined by \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:gm}--\eqref{sfp:mc:eq:Edef}, for each
fixed integer $R\geq1$,
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:main}
 b_n=\frac{(n!)^2e^{W_0(en)^2+1}}{2\pi W_0(en)}
 \left(\sum_{r=0}^{R-1}\frac{A_r}{W_0(en)^r}
            +O_R(W_0(en)^{-R})\right).
\end{equation}
In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:equivalent}
 b_n\sim\frac{(n!)^2e^{W_0(en)^2+1}}{2\pi W_0(en)}.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Multiply Proposition~\ref{sfp:mc:prop:reduction},
Theorem~\ref{sfp:mc:thm:H}, and the fixed-order exponential of
\eqref{sfp:mc:eq:normalization}. The relative error $O(w^2/n)$ is
$o(w^{-R})$ for every fixed $R$, and the remaining products are
finite asymptotic algebra. Their coefficients are exactly the
convolution \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:Edef}.
\end{proof}

\section{Inverses and exact integer thresholds}\label{sfp:mc:sec:inverse}

\subsection{Strict increase and the leading inverse}
The sequence is strictly increasing. One direct proof uses
\eqref{sfp:mc:eq:definition} and the unsigned Stirling recurrence
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:stirlingrec}
 \stir{m+1}{j}=m\stir mj+\stir m{j-1}.
\end{equation}
Alternatively, $P_{n+1}(z)=P_n(z)(1+z/(n+1))$ has nonnegative
coefficients strictly larger in at least one degree, so its squared
Fock norm strictly increases; $(n!)^2$ also increases except at
the initial harmless equality $0!=1!$. In particular $b_1=2>b_0$.
Since $b_n\geq(n!)^2$, the sequence is unbounded, and $N(T)$ is
well defined for $T>1$.

By Stirling's formula and Theorem~\ref{sfp:mc:thm:main},
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:logleading}
 \log b_n=2n(\log n-1)+O((\log n)^2).
\end{equation}
The equation $2u(\log u-1)=L$ has the exact positive solution
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:leadinginverse}
 u=\frac{L}{2W_0(L/(2e))},\qquad L=\log T.
\end{equation}
Monotonicity and \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:logleading} show that $N(T)\sim u$.
The omitted logarithmic correction induces an additive shift
of order $\log u$, so the leading inverse alone is not an
integer threshold rule.

\subsection{The smooth model and an implicit two-sided bracket}
For fixed $R\geq1$, let
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:FR}
 F_R(x)=2\log\Gamma(x+1)+w(x)^2+1-\log(2\pi w(x))
                       +\log Q_R(w(x)),
 \qquad w(x)=W_0(ex).
\end{equation}
For all sufficiently large real $x$, $Q_R(w(x))>0$, so this is
smooth. Since $w'(x)=w(x)/(x(1+w(x)))$,
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:FRderivatives}
 F_R'(x)\sim2\log x>0,\qquad F_R''(x)=O(1/x).
\end{equation}
The forward expansion gives
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:logmodelerror}
 \log b_n=F_R(n)+O_R(w(n)^{-R}).
\end{equation}
The logarithm is legitimate because $Q_R(w)\to1$.

Choose a sufficiently large fixed constant $D_R$ so that the
absolute remainder in \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:logmodelerror} is at most
$D_Rw(n)^{-R}$ for all sufficiently large $n$. For large $T$,
let $x_-$ and $x_+$ be the large roots of
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:modelroots}
 F_R(x_-)+D_Rw(x_-)^{-R}=\log T,\qquad
 F_R(x_+)-D_Rw(x_+)^{-R}=\log T.
\end{equation}
The perturbed model functions are increasing in the relevant
range, by \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:FRderivatives}. If an integer $n<x_-$,
then its upper logarithmic bound is strictly below $\log T$,
so $b_n<T$. If $n\geq x_+$, its lower bound is at least
$\log T$, so $b_n\geq T$. This proves
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:implicitbracket}
 \ceil{x_-}\leq N(T)\leq\ceil{x_+},\qquad
 x_+-x_-=O_R((\log u)^{-R-1}).
\end{equation}
For sufficiently large $T$, every index below the asymptotic onset
is also excluded, because the finitely many corresponding $b_n$
have a finite maximum. The equality case at the threshold is
preserved by the upper bound's weak inequality. Replacing the lower
ceiling by a floor would give a valid but weaker bound; automatic
rounding of a single real root is not justified.

\subsection{An explicit nested Lambert center to all fixed orders}
\begin{theorem}[Explicit inverse center]\label{sfp:mc:thm:inverse}
Let $u,D,w_u$ be as in \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:inverseintrodefs}, and let
$v_R$ be as in \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:inverseintro}. For every fixed $R\geq1$,
there exist $C_R,T_R>0$ such that
\eqref{sfp:mc:eq:inverseintrobracket} holds for $T\geq T_R$.
The unrounded real interval has width $O_R(D^{-R-1})$;
its two ceilings may still differ by one.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Put
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:inverseB}
 J_R(u)=w_u^2+\log(u/w_u)+1+\log Q_R(w_u).
\end{equation}
Stirling's expansion of $2\log\Gamma(u+1)$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:FRu}
 F_R(u)-L=J_R(u)+\frac1{6u}+O(u^{-3}).
\end{equation}
Here $w_u=O(D)$, $J_R(u)=O(D^2)$, and
$J_R'(u)=O(D/u)$. The definition $v_R=u-J_R(u)/(2D)$
therefore has $v_R-u=O(D)$.
Uniformly on this $O(D)$ neighborhood of $u$, direct
 differentiation of \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:FR} yields
\[
 F_R'(x)=2D+O(D/u),\qquad F_R''(x)=O(1/u).
\]
Taylor's theorem and \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:FRu} then show that
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:mc:eq:FRv}
 F_R(v_R)-L=O(D^2/u).
\end{equation}
Indeed the leading term $J_R(u)$ is canceled exactly by
$2D(v_R-u)$, while the derivative correction times the shift
and the quadratic remainder are both $O(D^2/u)$.

The smooth root $y_R$ of $F_R(y_R)=L$ consequently satisfies
$y_R-v_R=O(D/u)$. Because $D/u$ is smaller than every fixed
power $D^{-1}$, this error is negligible relative to
$D^{-R-1}$. The perturbed roots in \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:modelroots} each
differ from $y_R$ by $O_R(D^{-R-1})$: their perturbation is
$O_R(D^{-R})$ and their derivative is of order $D$.
Enclose both roots in
$[v_R-C_RD^{-R-1},v_R+C_RD^{-R-1}]$ and apply
\eqref{sfp:mc:eq:implicitbracket}.
\end{proof}

This expression uses two principal Lambert evaluations and the
finite coefficient polynomial $Q_R$. Its shift-Taylor error is
smaller than every inverse-logarithm order. That statement does
not supply an exponentially improved inverse, nor a full
transseries. The constants and a numerical starting threshold
for the asymptotic windows have not been derived in this report;
there is no claim that they are intrinsically noncomputable.

\subsection{A practical certificate protocol}\label{sfp:mc:sub:protocol}
For a finite threshold $T$, the exact interval
\eqref{sfp:mc:eq:fullyrational} supports direct decisions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item At a candidate index $n$, compute rational
$\mathcal L_n\leq b_n\leq\mathcal U_n$. If
$\mathcal U_n<T$, then $n<N(T)$; if
$\mathcal L_n\geq T$, then $n\geq N(T)$.
\item If the interval straddles $T$, increase the Fock truncation
and the exponential accuracy, and if useful refine the rational
center. Do not convert an inconclusive comparison into a decision.
\item When equality could be present, use the integer isolation
criterion \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:uniqueinteger} or the exact recurrence
\eqref{sfp:mc:eq:stirlingrec}. Convergence to a boundary from both sides
alone need not decide equality.
\item Use monotonicity to bracket the index, then test adjacent
integers. A certificate $b_{m-1}<T\leq b_m$ proves $N(T)=m$.
\end{enumerate}
All comparisons with an exact rational or integer threshold can
remain rational. For a threshold specified only approximately,
its own uncertainty must also be enclosed. The asymptotic center
is useful for choosing a candidate, while the finite arithmetic
certificate supplies the actual decision.

\section{Sources and relation to earlier methods}\label{sfp:mc:sec:sources}

\subsection{What the source inspection establishes}
The inspected OEIS entry and official data mirror give the
defining factorial-weighted sum and finite terms~\cite{oeis};
no asymptotic was present in the inspected snapshot. Focused
searches covered the identifier, number prefix, factorial-weighted
Stirling squares, matched cycles, rising-factorial Fock norms,
and relevant multivariate or alg-log methods. No source containing
this sequence-specific equivalent and coefficient series was
located. This is a bounded search result, not proof of novelty.

Wilf~\cite{wilf} proves that the probability that two independent
uniform permutations on $n$ letters have the same cycle count is
asymptotic to $1/(2\sqrt{\pi\log n})$. His proof turns the
unweighted squared-Stirling sum into a circle norm and applies
Laplace's method. This is a close conceptual precedent. The $k!$
weight in the present sequence leads instead to the standard
planar Gaussian norm. No new general norm identity or saddle
method is being claimed.

Alpay and Porat~\cite{alpayporat} discuss generalized Fock spaces
whose coefficient norms involve factorial powers; their classical
case includes \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:fock}. Their work concerns a broader
functional-analytic setting and Stirling-number operators. The
identity used here is classical, regardless of whether the
sequence-specific evaluation appears in that paper.

The shifted Gamma and digamma expansions used in
Sections~\ref{sfp:mc:sec:gamma} and~\ref{sfp:mc:sec:allorders} are standard;
DLMF equations 5.11.1, 5.11.2, and 5.11.8 are direct
references~\cite{dlmf-mc}. The proof added here organizes these
inputs around an exact positive expansion and a scaled tail,
so that the all-orders norm remainder involves finitely many
coefficient expansions.

\subsection{General asymptotic frameworks and remaining overlap}
Gittenberger and Mandlburger~\cite{gittenberger} develop
multivariate Hayman admissibility and Gaussian coefficient
asymptotics. Their cited admissibility setup uses entire
functions, whereas \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:egf} has logarithmic singularities.
That particular theorem is not directly applicable without
additional work. The general idea of a multivariate Gaussian
coefficient estimate is established prior art.

Bender and Richmond~\cite{bender} give a general framework for
labelled structures by number of components. The official abstract
and indexed primary theorem material were inspected, but a full
PDF reading was not completed in the source pass. Their theorem
may provide an alternative route to a leading saddle estimate
once its hypotheses are checked; this report does not claim
otherwise. It also does not infer that the positive certificate
or the specific coefficients are absent from every related paper.

Gao, Laferri\`ere, and Panario~\cite{gao} treat a univariate
$\exp(C(z))$ schema of alg-log type with positive logarithmic
exponent. The official abstract was inspected; the full PDF could
not be read through the available reader in the source pass.
The substitution $x=y$ in \eqref{sfp:mc:eq:egf} gives an antidiagonal
sum and cannot by itself identify $b_n$. Detailed overlap beyond
that schema distinction remains unverified.

Accordingly, the defensible contribution of this report is a
self-contained sequence-specific derivation, its explicit
all-orders rational coefficients and inverse, and an exact
finite-$n$ certification procedure. A first-ever claim for either
the general certificate or the leading method would require a
broader literature investigation.

\section{Further questions and limitations}\label{sfp:mc:sec:future}

\subsection{Root classes beyond the arithmetic progression}\label{sfp:mc:sub:roots}
Theorem~\ref{sfp:mc:thm:generic} allows arbitrary positive root parameters,
but only gives the instance-specific condition $S_2<1$.
For regularly spaced roots, roots with a limiting density, or
families with a quantitative gap from zero, one can ask for
sharp uniform bounds on $S_2$ and explicit rates of decay as the
degree grows. Such hypotheses may produce other universal
positive certificates and asymptotic scales. Polynomials with
nonreal or positive zeros need separate arguments; the present
product estimate alone does not provide the same real saddle
or normalized tail.

\subsection{Computational complexity and interval algorithms}\label{sfp:mc:sub:complexity}
The demonstration uses exact fractions because they make every
comparison transparent. Their numerators and denominators can
become large. Useful next steps include complexity estimates in
$n$, $K$, and requested accuracy; rigorous ball arithmetic;
range reduction for exponential enclosures; and adaptive choices
of the center and scale. For an off-saddle center, minimizing
\eqref{sfp:mc:eq:rationaltail} rather than reusing $K/(K+1)$ may
substantially improve bounds. An efficiency comparison with
integer Stirling recurrence or fast polynomial methods would
require a dedicated benchmark and is not settled by the example.

\subsection{Beyond fixed logarithmic orders}\label{sfp:mc:sub:beyond}
The proof separates an $O(w^2/n)$ finite-product correction from
a complete fixed-order expansion in $w^{-1}$. It does not compute
the first correction on the $1/n$ scale, establish optimal
truncation, control coefficients at growing order, or prove
Borel summability. A systematic two-scale expansion could improve
both the forward formula and its inverse. Quantifying explicit
constants and starting indices for the logarithmic remainders
would also turn the asymptotic inverse windows into independent
numerical tools. Those tasks are distinct from the already
explicit finite-$n$ Fock certificate.

\subsection{Other factorial weights and statistics}\label{sfp:mc:sub:weights}
Generalized Fock norms suggest other factorial weights, such as
$\sum_k\stir{n+1}{k+1}^{2}(k!)^\alpha$, which may require
different saddle geometry. Only the weight $k!$ and the
negative-root polynomial extension are proved here. Weighted
cycle-count distributions, local limits, and concentration
remain separate research questions.

\subsection{The third member of the family: A192562}\label{sfp:sub:a192562}
Let $c_n=\sum_{k=0}^nk!\sone nk\stwo{n+1}{k+1}$, the sequence A192562 \cite{oeisA192562} (the write's letter; $c_n$ is not used in this sense elsewhere in the report). Here $a_n$, $A_{n,k}$ and $F_n$ are as in Part~I.

\begin{proposition}[\wtag]\label{sfp:prop:a192562}
For every $n\ge1$,
\begin{equation}\label{sfp:eq:a192562}
 c_n=a_n-nA_{n-1,n}
    =\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}\sone{n-1}m\bigl\{(m+2)^n-(m+1)^n\bigr\}
    =n![z^n]\,(e^z-1)F_{n-1}(z).
\end{equation}
In particular $0<c_n<a_n$; and $c_0=1$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
All three equalities use the recurrence $\sone{N+1}{i}=N\sone Ni+\sone N{i-1}$ ($N\ge0$, $i\ge1$; $\sone N{-1}=0$). \emph{First equality.} Apply it with $N=n$, $i=k+1$ to the diagonal of \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:stirlingproduct}:
\[
 a_n=\sum_{k=0}^nk!\sone{n+1}{k+1}\stwo{n+1}{k+1}
    =n\sum_{k=0}^nk!\sone n{k+1}\stwo{n+1}{k+1}+\sum_{k=0}^nk!\sone nk\stwo{n+1}{k+1}.
\]
The last sum is $c_n$. The first sum is \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:stirlingproduct} with $(n,k)$ replaced by $(n-1,n)$, so it equals $A_{n-1,n}$. \emph{Second equality.} Apply the recurrence with $N=n-1$, $i=m$ inside \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:definition}:
\[
 a_n=\sum_{m=0}^n\sone nm(m+1)^n
    =(n-1)\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}\sone{n-1}m(m+1)^n+\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}\sone{n-1}m(m+2)^n ,
\]
and subtract $nA_{n-1,n}=n\sum_m\sone{n-1}m(m+1)^n$. \emph{Third equality.} By \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:Fn}, $F_n(z)=(e^z+n-1)F_{n-1}(z)$, so $(e^z-1)F_{n-1}=F_n-nF_{n-1}$, and by \eqref{sfp:pc:eq:coefficient} $n![z^n]F_n=A_{n,n}=a_n$ and $n![z^n]F_{n-1}=A_{n-1,n}$. Finally, every term of the middle sum is nonnegative and the term $m=n-1$ is $(n+1)^n-n^n>0$; and $A_{n-1,n}\ge\sone{n-1}{n-1}n^n>0$. For $n=0$ the definition gives $c_0=0!\sone00\stwo11=1$.
\end{proof}

The write checked \eqref{sfp:eq:a192562} by exact integers for $1\le n\le55$, and the 17 listed terms of A192562 against the definition. Since the first equality is a difference of two quantities that Part~I's theorems describe, it is natural to ask how much cancellation it contains.
