\begin{abstract}
An inversion sequence of length $n$ is an integer vector $(e_1,\ldots,e_n)$ with $0\le e_i<i$. Britt and Beaton (arXiv:2512.21943v3) gave exact generating trees and catalytic functional equations for the classes avoiding a triple of relations, and stated numerically, and explicitly as non-rigorous, laws $a_n\sim C\mu^nn^{-3/2}$ for four classes whose generating functions they believe to be nonalgebraic. This report prints three manuscripts that prove these four laws. Part~I (Report~118) treats class~214 (OEIS A279544, $\mu=4$) and class~1509 (A279567, $\mu=3+2\sqrt2$); Part~II (Report~119) treats class~1953A (A279569, $\mu=27/4$), and hence, by the Wilf-equivalence that Britt and Beaton record, class~1953B; Part~III (Report~122) treats class~830 (A279558, $\mu=27/4$). In each case a kernel substitution reduces the catalytic equation to a scalar affine functional equation, which is solved by a normally convergent sum or quotient along contracting M\"obius-type orbits seeded at an algebraic kernel root. A global continuation argument excludes competing singularities on the circle of convergence, and an exact rational certificate proves that the square-root amplitude does not vanish. The result is a convergent local Puiseux expansion and coefficient asymptotics to every fixed order, $a_n=C\mu^nn^{-3/2}(1+c_1/n+\cdots+c_K/n^K+O(n^{-K-1}))$, with $C$, $c_1$ and $c_2$ enclosed in certified intervals. Each Part also proves a threshold inverse with a shrinking two-ceiling bracket, centred at a Lambert-$W_{-1}$ root in Parts~I and~II and expanded in powers of $1/\log M$ with coefficients polynomial in $\log\log M$ in Part~III; these inverse theorems are instances of the Lambert-core, reversion and staircase theorems of the repository's transseries volume. (Dated note, 5~October 2026: corrected after the independent check recorded at the end of Section~\ref{ivk:anc:sub:further}. The first wording, ``Each Part also proves a Lambert-$W_{-1}$ threshold inverse with a shrinking two-ceiling bracket; these inverse theorems are instances of the Lambert-core and staircase theorems'', is wrong for Part~III, whose inverse contains no $W_{-1}$.) V\'aclav Kot\v{e}\v{s}ovec's OEIS amplitudes agree with every certified interval, so no digits are new: the contribution is proof and certification. Nonalgebraicity, $D$-finiteness, effective inverse constants and the higher corrections remain open. The report is unrefereed, and nothing in it is formalized.
\end{abstract}

Three ingredients recur in the same form, each proved in every Part where it is used:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Transfer coefficients.} With $\Gamma(n-\alpha)/\Gamma(n+1)\sim n^{-\alpha-1}\sum_{j\ge0}g_j(\alpha)n^{-j}$ and the local expansion $\sum_jh_jt^j$ in $t=\sqrt{1-z/\rho}$, the relative corrections are $c_1=\frac38-\frac32h_3/h_1$ and $c_2=\frac{25}{128}-\frac{45}{16}h_3/h_1+\frac{15}{4}h_5/h_1$: \eqref{ivk:orb:c12} with the Bernoulli recursion \eqref{ivk:orb:grec}, \eqref{ivk:two:d12} with \eqref{ivk:two:bernoulli}, and \eqref{ivk:anc:d1formula}--\eqref{ivk:anc:d2formula} with \eqref{ivk:anc:gratio}. The Puiseux coefficients are called $h_j$, $\widehat h_j$ in Part~I and $b_m$, $b_j$ in Parts~II and~III; Part~II's $T_k$ and Part~III's $g_k$ are Part~I's $g_j$. The transfer itself is the standard theorem of Flajolet and Sedgewick \cite[Chapter VI]{fs}.
\item \emph{Certified constants.} Each Part encloses $\pi$ by Machin's identity with alternating-series remainders, takes square roots by integer square roots, and rounds every interval operation outward, on decimal lattices from $10^{-60}$ to $10^{-120}$ where interval arithmetic is used; the infinite orbit tails are proved bounds, and Taylor coefficients are bounded by Cauchy's estimate.
\item \emph{The inverse.} The four threshold inverses are one theorem: Part~I states it for ``either sequence'', Part~II for A279569 (adding the explicit $\log\log$ form \eqref{ivk:two:logloginverse}), and Part~III in the coordinates $L=\log M$ with polynomials $P_j(\log L)$ (its \eqref{ivk:anc:inversefirst} is Part~II's \eqref{ivk:two:logloginverse}, see the note there). All three are instances of results of the repository's transseries volume \cite{tsvol}: the Lambert centre solves $aX+b\log X=\log Y-\log C$ with $a=\log\mu>0$ and $b=-\frac32<0$, whose large root the branch rule of \texttt{p0:thm:lambert-core} assigns to $W_{-1}$; the expansion in powers of the centre has the form of the all-orders reversion \texttt{p0:thm:lambert-centered}; Part~III's expansion in powers of $1/L$, like Part~II's \eqref{ivk:two:logloginverse}, has the form of the direct polynomial--logarithmic reversion \texttt{p0:thm:flattening}, whose remainder $O((1+H)^{N+1}/L^{N+1})$ is Part~III's factor $(1+\log L)^{J+1}$, the only rigorous difference from the Lambert-centred form according to \texttt{p0:rem:accuracy-claim} (added 5~October 2026, after the independent check); the counting index is, for targets above~$1$, the integer staircase of \texttt{p0:def:three-inverses} with $n_1=1$ (all four sequences begin $1,1,2$), and a two-ceiling bracket whose ceilings agree determines it, as in the separation condition of \texttt{p0:thm:staircase}; and the mean-value step that moves the forward error to the inverse is the estimate of \texttt{p0:cor:forward-to-inverse}. The manuscripts bracket the staircase between the inverses of two smooth envelopes $F_K\pm Dx^{-K-1}$ rather than through an admissible interpolation; the conclusion is the same. These are second routes to known mechanics, not new inverse theory.
\end{itemize}

\section{Model and main conclusions}
\subsection{The counted objects and source statement}
An inversion sequence of length $n$ is a vector $e=(e_1,\ldots,e_n)$
of integers with $0\le e_i<i$. Let $a_n$ count those vectors for which
there are no indices $i<j<k$ satisfying
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:avoid214}
e_j\ge e_k\quad\hbox{and}\quad e_i\ge e_k.
\end{equation}
Set $a_0=1$ and $A(z)=\sum_{n\ge0}a_nz^n$. This is an ordinary generating
function, with no factorial normalization. The sequence is
OEIS A279544~\cite{oeis214}; its initial terms are
\[
1,1,2,4,10,26,73,214,651,2040,6549,21453,71485,241702,827603.
\]
The relation notation is $(-,\ge,\ge)$, where the dash imposes no
condition on $e_i,e_j$. Equivalently, the forbidden reduced patterns
are $000,010,100,110,120,210$.

Britt and Beaton~\cite[Section 3.5]{bb} give an exact auxiliary generating
tree and catalytic equation for this class. Their label ``214'' is the
length-seven count, not a shift in the sequence index. Their Section 4.2,
equation (4.8), predicts $a_n\sim C4^n n^{-3/2}$ with $C\approx0.0549097$;
the section explicitly identifies its asymptotics as numerical and
non-rigorous. We use version 3, dated 29 September 2026. The proof below
starts from the exact enumerative model and establishes this specific
leading law together with stronger fixed-order and inverse statements. Section~\ref{ivk:orb:sec1509}
gives the same complete treatment of class 1509, whose growth constant
is $3+2\sqrt2$.

\subsection{The result and its scope}
\begin{theorem}\label{ivk:orb:main214}
The generating function $A$ has radius of convergence $\rho=1/4$.
It extends holomorphically to
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:slit214}
\{z:|z|<R\}\setminus[1/4,R)\qquad(1/4<R<1/2),
\end{equation}
and $1/4$ is its unique singularity on the circle of convergence.
For every fixed integer $K\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:asym214}
a_n=C4^n n^{-3/2}\left(1+\sum_{j=1}^{K}\frac{c_j}{n^j}
+O(n^{-K-1})\right),\qquad n\longrightarrow\infty,
\end{equation}
where $C>0$. The constants are uniquely specified by the convergent
orbit representation and explicit recurrences below. Moreover the
minimum counting index $N(Y)=\min\{n\ge0:a_n\ge Y\}$ admits the
Lambert-$W_{-1}$ expansion and two-ceiling brackets of
Theorem~\ref{ivk:orb:inverse}.
\end{theorem}
The constants hidden in \eqref{ivk:orb:asym214} may depend on $K$.
No assertion of convergence as $K\to\infty$ is part of this theorem.
The positive amplitude is certified without fitting coefficients.

The proof has four separate requirements: identify the formal generating
function; prove normal convergence and global continuation; prove that
the square-root term is genuinely nonzero; and transfer with the correct
remainder before inverting. A kernel discriminant alone meets none of
the last three requirements.

\section{Exact generating tree and kernel cancellation}
\subsection{Auxiliary labels and coefficient extraction}
The auxiliary class of~\cite[Section 3.5]{bb} permits the patterns
$000,010,100$ only when their zero symbols have actual value zero.
Its labels $(p,s)$ are lengths of the initial and possible second run of
zeros. The root has label $(0,0)$. Each occurrence of a label creates the
following children, retaining all multiplicities:
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:tree214}
\begin{aligned}
(p,s)&\longmapsto(p+1,s),\\
(p,s)&\longmapsto(p+1,s-1)&& (s>0),\\
(p,0)&\longmapsto(p-\ell,\ell)&& (0\le\ell\le p-1),\\
(p,0)&\longmapsto(p-\ell-1,\ell)&& (0\le\ell\le p-2).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
An empty index range contributes no child. The desired objects have
$s=0$ and $p\le2$. This completely specifies an integer state recurrence
for $a_n$, and is source equation (3.31).

Let $B(z,x,y)$ record length and the two label coordinates. Put
$F(z,x)=B(z,x,0)$. Every nonroot label has $p\ge1$, so $F(z,0)=1$.
Write $F_p(z)=[x^p]F(z,x)$. The transitions into $(1,0)$ give
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:normalize214}
F_1=z(1+F_1+F_2),\qquad
A=1+F_1+F_2=F_1/z.
\end{equation}
The last quotient is removable at zero.

Summing \eqref{ivk:orb:tree214}, or clearing the denominators in source
(3.33), gives the exact catalytic relation
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:catalytic214}
0=(y+z)(y-x)-(y-zx-zxy)(y-x)B(z,x,y)
+xz(1+y)F(z,y)-z(y+2xy-x^2)F(z,x).
\end{equation}
Each coefficient of $z$ is a polynomial in $x,y$; thus substitutions
below are justified formally after denominator clearing.

\subsection{The root orbit}
Define
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:kernel214}
f_z(x)=\frac{zx}{1-zx},\qquad
P(z,x)=zx^2+(2z-1)x+z.
\end{equation}
Substitute $y=f_z(x)$ in \eqref{ivk:orb:catalytic214}. Its $B$ term vanishes,
and elementary algebra gives
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:F214}
F(z,f_z(x))=P(z,x)F(z,x)+Q(z,x),\qquad
Q(z,x)=\frac{(1+(1-z)x)(1-z-zx)}{1-zx}.
\end{equation}
Set $F(z,x)=1+xG(z,x)$. Then \eqref{ivk:orb:F214} becomes
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:G214}
G(z,f_z(x))=a(z,x)G(z,x)+b(z,x),
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:ab214}
a(z,x)=\frac{(1-zx)P(z,x)}z,
\qquad b(z,x)=x(1-z-zx),\qquad A(z)=G(z,0)/z.
\end{equation}
The apparent $z^{-1}$ is addressed explicitly when its iterated arguments
are substituted; it causes no unproved analytic assumption.

The small root of $P$ is
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:r214}
r(z)=\frac{1-2z-\sqrt{1-4z}}{2z}
=\frac{1-\sqrt{1-4z}}{1+\sqrt{1-4z}}\in z\Q[[z]].
\end{equation}
For an arbitrary seed $x$, the iterates have the exact form
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:orbit214}
x_j=f_z^{\,j}(x)=
\frac{z^jx}{1-zx(1-z^j)/(1-z)}\qquad(j\ge0).
\end{equation}
It follows either by induction or by summing the geometric series
in the denominator. Define the finite sums and their intended limit by
\begin{align}
\Phi_M(z,x)&=\sum_{k=0}^{M}b(z,x_k)\prod_{j=k+1}^{M}a(z,x_j),\label{ivk:orb:partial214}\\
\Phi(z,x)&=\sum_{k\ge0}b(z,x_k)\prod_{j>k}a(z,x_j).\label{ivk:orb:phi214}
\end{align}
An empty product is one. No reciprocal of an $a$ factor is used.
The finite sums satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:partialrec214}
\Phi_0=b(z,x),\qquad
\Phi_M=a(z,x_M)\Phi_{M-1}+b(z,x_M).
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}\label{ivk:orb:formal214}
As an identity of formal power series,
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:representation214}
A(z)=\Phi(z,r(z))/z.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Use seed $x=r(z)$. The initial factor $a(z,r)=0$, so repeated use of
\eqref{ivk:orb:G214} gives $G(z,x_{M+1})=\Phi_M(z,r)$. Formula
\eqref{ivk:orb:orbit214} gives $x_j=O(z^{j+1})$. For $j\ge1$,
$a(z,x_j)=1+O(z^j)$, while $b(z,x_j)=O(z^{j+1})$.
All products and sums therefore stabilize coefficientwise, and
$G(z,x_{M+1})\to G(z,0)$. Now use \eqref{ivk:orb:ab214}.
In particular, this argument never assumes that the original catalytic
series $F(z,x)$ converges at the eventual critical seed $x=1$.
\end{proof}

\section{Normal convergence and dominant singularity control}
\subsection{A holomorphic two-variable orbit sum}
\begin{lemma}\label{ivk:orb:normal214}
Choose $0<R<1/2$ and $0<X<(1-R)/R$. The series \eqref{ivk:orb:phi214}
and its products converge normally on $|z|\le R$, $|x|\le X$.
They define a jointly holomorphic function near each compact set
with strict room in these inequalities.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Put $\delta=1-RX/(1-R)>0$. The orbit denominator in
\eqref{ivk:orb:orbit214} has modulus at least $\delta$, since
$|z+\cdots+z^j|\le R/(1-R)$. Consequently
\[
|x_j|\le (X/\delta)|z|^j.
\]
For $j\ge1$, $x_j/z$ is holomorphic at zero and uniformly bounded
by $(X/\delta)R^{j-1}$. Expand the first factor as
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:expand_a214}
a(z,u)=1+(2-z-1/z)u+(2-2z)u^2-zu^3.
\end{equation}
There are constants $K,K'$ independent of $j,k$ such that
\[
|a(z,x_j)-1|\le KR^{j-1}\quad(j\ge1),\qquad
|b(z,x_k)|\le K'R^k\quad(k\ge0).
\]
The infinite products converge normally and are bounded by
$\exp(K/(1-R))$. The outer sum is therefore dominated by a geometric
series. Uniform limits on slightly larger polydiscs give joint
holomorphy and termwise differentiation on the chosen compact set.
Zeros in finite factors cause no problem because there is no division
by such factors.
\end{proof}
At $z=0$, the first composed factor $a(z,x_1)$ tends to $1-x$, rather
than to one; later factors tend to one. Thus $\Phi(0,x)=x(1-x)$.
This is consistent with the lemma. Since $r(0)=0$, the composition
$\Phi(z,r(z))$ vanishes at zero and its quotient in
\eqref{ivk:orb:representation214} is holomorphic there.

\subsection{Continuation on a slit disc}\label{ivk:orb:sec:slit}
Use the principal square root on $\C\setminus[1/4,\infty)$.
Then $\operatorname{Re}\sqrt{1-4z}>0$, so the Cayley transform
\eqref{ivk:orb:r214} satisfies $|r(z)|<1$. For any $1/4<R<1/2$, one can choose
$1<X<(1-R)/R$. Lemma~\ref{ivk:orb:normal214} now proves that
\eqref{ivk:orb:representation214} extends the exact germ $A$ to
\eqref{ivk:orb:slit214}. In particular, the entire circle $|z|=1/4$ except
$z=1/4$ is crossed holomorphically. This global argument rules out
unexamined competing singularities; inspection of the kernel roots
alone would not do so.

Put $t=\sqrt{1-4z}$ and define
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:H214}
H(t)=\frac4{1-t^2}\,
\Phi\left(\frac{1-t^2}{4},\frac{1-t}{1+t}\right)
=\sum_{m\ge0}h_mt^m.
\end{equation}
The lemma applies near $(z,x)=(1/4,1)$, so $H$ is holomorphic for
$|t|$ sufficiently small. This Taylor series is genuinely convergent.
It follows that $A(z)=H(\sqrt{1-4z})$ on the local slit neighborhood,
and differentiation at $t=0$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:h1}
h_1=-8\Phi_x(1/4,1).
\end{equation}
It remains to show that this coefficient is nonzero.

\section{Positive amplitude by exact arithmetic}\label{ivk:orb:sec:amplitude}
\subsection{Rational critical recurrence and a proved tail}
At $z=1/4$ and seed $x=1$, the orbit and its seed derivative are
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:criticalorbit}
u_j=\frac3{2\cdot4^j+1},\qquad
v_j^{\mathrm{orb}}=\left.\partial_xx_j\right|_{x=1}
=\frac{9\cdot4^j}{(2\cdot4^j+1)^2}.
\end{equation}
For this subsection write $d_j=v_j^{\mathrm{orb}}$ and
\[
\mathrm a(u)=(1-u/4)(1-u)^2,\qquad
\mathrm b(u)=u(3-u)/4.
\]
Then
\[
\mathrm a'(u)=-\tfrac34(1-u)(3-u),\qquad
\mathrm b'(u)=(3-2u)/4.
\]
If $q_M=\Phi_M(1/4,1)$ and $v_M=\partial_x\Phi_M(1/4,1)$,
\eqref{ivk:orb:partialrec214} gives the entirely rational recurrence
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:criticalrec}
\begin{aligned}
q_0&=\tfrac12,&v_0&=\tfrac14,\\
q_m&=\mathrm a(u_m)q_{m-1}+\mathrm b(u_m),\\
v_m&=\mathrm a(u_m)v_{m-1}
+d_m\bigl(\mathrm a'(u_m)q_{m-1}+\mathrm b'(u_m)\bigr)
&& (m\ge1).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The derivative uses the old value $q_{m-1}$.

For $0\le u\le1$, one has
\[
0\le\mathrm a\le1,\quad 0\le\mathrm b\le3u/4,\quad
|\mathrm a'|\le9/4,\quad|\mathrm b'|\le3/4,\quad
1-\mathrm a\le9u/4.
\]
For $j\ge1$, $u_j\le(3/2)4^{-j}$ and $d_j\le(9/4)4^{-j}$.
In particular $q_M\le7/8\le9/8$, and the convenient uniform bound
$|\mathrm a'q+\mathrm b'|\le105/32$ yields
\[
|v_M|\le\tfrac14+\tfrac{105}{32}\sum_{j\ge1}d_j
\le\tfrac{347}{128}.
\]
Therefore
\[
|v_m-v_{m-1}|\le\tfrac{347}{128}\tfrac94 u_m
+\tfrac{105}{32}d_m\le\tfrac{16929}{1024}4^{-m}.
\]
Normal convergence identifies the derivative limit, and summation proves
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:derivtail}
\left|\Phi_x(1/4,1)-v_M\right|
\le E_M:=\frac{16929}{3072\cdot4^M}.
\end{equation}
At $M=8$, direct exact fraction arithmetic gives
\[
v_8>0.02433,\qquad E_8<0.000085,
\]
so $\Phi_x(1/4,1)>0.024245>0$. The finite inequalities are reproducible
from \eqref{ivk:orb:criticalrec}, and no floating-point sign decision is needed.
Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:amplitude}
C=-\frac{h_1}{2\sqrt\pi}
=\frac{4\Phi_x(1/4,1)}{\sqrt\pi}>0.
\end{equation}
Together with \eqref{ivk:orb:h1}, this proves that $1/4$ is an actual square-root
singularity. The continuation already established proves the radius and
uniqueness assertions in Theorem~\ref{ivk:orb:main214}.

\]
For either arctangent, its alternating rational series is enclosed by

\section{Every fixed coefficient order}
\subsection{Transfer of the convergent local expansion}
For nonintegral $\alpha$, the exact binomial coefficient identity is
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:gammaratio}
[z^n](1-4z)^\alpha
=4^n\frac{\Gamma(n-\alpha)}{\Gamma(-\alpha)\Gamma(n+1)}.
\end{equation}
The slit disc provides a standard $\Delta$-domain at $1/4$.
Singularity analysis~\cite[Chapter VI]{fs}, applied to the convergent
Taylor expansion \eqref{ivk:orb:H214}, gives, for every fixed $m\ge1$,
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:finitegamma}
a_n=4^n\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}
\frac{h_{2j+1}}{\Gamma(-j-1/2)}
\frac{\Gamma(n-j-1/2)}{\Gamma(n+1)}
+O(4^nn^{-m-3/2}).
\end{equation}
For clarity about the remainder, expand $H$ through degree $2m+2$.
The even terms are polynomials in $z$ and have zero coefficients for
all sufficiently large $n$. The first omitted odd term contributes
$O(4^nn^{-m-3/2})$; the remaining Taylor remainder has still smaller
transfer order. No differentiation of an uncontrolled asymptotic
remainder is involved.

Define the standard gamma-ratio polynomials by
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:gdef}
\frac{\Gamma(n-\alpha)}{\Gamma(n+1)}
\sim n^{-\alpha-1}\sum_{j\ge0}g_j(\alpha)n^{-j},
\qquad g_0(\alpha)=1.
\end{equation}
Then the exact prescription for the coefficients in \eqref{ivk:orb:asym214} is
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:Crec}
C_m:=C c_m=\sum_{k=0}^{m}
\frac{h_{2k+1}g_{m-k}(k+1/2)}{\Gamma(-k-1/2)},
\qquad c_0=1.
\end{equation}
In particular, $C_0=C$. For a fully explicit recurrence, let $B_j(x)$
be the Bernoulli polynomials, defined by
$we^{xw}/(e^w-1)=\sum_{j\ge0}B_j(x)w^j/j!$. Set
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:grec}
\lambda_j(\alpha)=
\frac{(-1)^{j+1}\bigl(B_{j+1}(-\alpha)-B_{j+1}(1)\bigr)}{j(j+1)},
\qquad
g_m(\alpha)=\frac1m\sum_{j=1}^{m}j\lambda_j(\alpha)g_{m-j}(\alpha).
\end{equation}
This follows by exponentiating the logarithmic Stirling expansion of
the ratio in \eqref{ivk:orb:gdef}. It determines any fixed order using rational
arithmetic at the half-integer arguments.

The first two relative corrections are
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:c12}
\begin{aligned}
c_1&=\frac38-\frac32\frac{h_3}{h_1},\\
c_2&=\frac{25}{128}-\frac{45}{16}\frac{h_3}{h_1}
+\frac{15}{4}\frac{h_5}{h_1}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Here $g_1(1/2)=3/8$, $g_1(3/2)=15/8$, and $g_2(1/2)=25/128$.
These identities also provide immediate normalization checks.

\subsection{Certified local jets and the first corrections}
The Taylor coefficients $h_j$ can be computed by rational series
arithmetic in \eqref{ivk:orb:partialrec214}, after substituting
$z=(1-t^2)/4$ and $x=(1-t)/(1+t)$. A uniform complex-circle bound
certifies the truncation. On $|t|=1/10$ choose

R=101/400,\qquad X=11/9,\qquad
\delta=1-RX/(1-R),\qquad T=X/\delta.
\]
Then $|z|\le R$, $|x|\le X$ and $|x_j|\le TR^j$. Define

A_*&=T+(2+R)TR+(2+2R)T^2R^2+T^3R^3,\\
B_*&=T(1+R+RT).
\end{align*}
Equation \eqref{ivk:orb:expand_a214} gives
$|a(z,x_j)-1|\le A_*R^{j-1}$ for $j\ge1$, and
$|b(z,x_j)|\le B_*R^j$. Exact rational inequalities give
\[
A_* /(1-R)<6,\qquad B_*<4,\qquad (1-R)^{-1}<3/2.
\]
Using $e<3$, the finite sums obey
$|\Phi_M|\le B_*e^{A_* /(1-R)}/(1-R)<5000$.
Telescoping \eqref{ivk:orb:partialrec214} therefore yields

|\Phi-\Phi_M|
\le\frac{5000A_*+B_*R}{1-R}R^M<40000R^M.
\]
Since $|1/z|\le400/99<5$, if $H_M(t)=\Phi_M(z(t),x(t))/z(t)$, then

\sup_{|t|=1/10}|H-H_M|<200000R^M,
\qquad
|h_j-[t^j]H_M|<200000\cdot10^jR^M.
\end{equation}
The second inequality is Cauchy's estimate. This supplies an effective

Degree-five rational jets with $M=64$, followed by interval arithmetic

27.604095556555475840191975&<c_1<27.604095556555475840191976,\\
43.988908237439005751766276&<c_2<43.988908237439005751766277.

\end{equation}

\subsection{What is and is not an all-order result}
The series \eqref{ivk:orb:H214} converges in a neighborhood of $t=0$.
The expansion \eqref{ivk:orb:asym214} means that each fixed truncation has its
own proved algebraic remainder. These are different statements.
No uniform estimate in a truncation order growing with $n$ has been
proved. We do not claim optimal truncation, all subdominant exponential
sectors, Stokes constants, or a complete resurgent transseries. Nor do
the finite coefficient checks replace the analytic continuation and
tail arguments above.

\section{A quotient kernel for class 1509}\label{ivk:orb:sec1509}
\subsection{The second counted class and exact equations}
Let $b_n$ count inversion sequences with no $i<j<k$ satisfying
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:avoid1509}
e_j\ge e_k\quad\hbox{and}\quad e_i>e_k.
\end{equation}
This is class $(-,\ge,>)$, or avoidance of $100,110,120,210$,
counted by OEIS A279567~\cite{oeis1509}. Put
$\mathcal A(z)=\sum_{n\ge0}b_nz^n$. Its initial terms are
\[
1,1,2,6,21,82,343,1509,6893,32419,156058,765578,3815062.
\]
Source~\cite[Section 3.6, equation (3.34)]{bb} gives the root $(0,0)$
and these multiplicity-preserving transitions:
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:tree1509}
\begin{aligned}
(p,s)&\longmapsto(p+1,s),\\
(p,s)&\longmapsto(p+1,s-1)&& (s>0),\\
(p,s)&\longmapsto(p-i,0)&& (s\le1,\ 0\le i<p),\\
(p,s)&\longmapsto(p-\ell,\ell-k)&&
(s\le1,\ 1\le\ell<p,\ 0\le k<\ell).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The target is the sum of all states with $s\le1$. The auxiliary class
allows $100$ when both zero symbols have actual value zero. Let
$\mathcal B(z,x,y)$ be its generating function, and set
\[
J(z,x)=\mathcal B(z,x,0)+[y]\mathcal B(z,x,y),\qquad
\mathcal A(z)=J(z,1).
\]
Write $B_0(x)=\mathcal B(z,x,0)$ and $B_1(x)=[y]\mathcal B(z,x,y)$,
suppressing only the $z$ argument. The source's equations (3.36)--(3.37)
are, equivalently,
\begin{align}
B_0(x)&=1+zxJ(z,x)+\frac{zx}{1-x}\bigl(\mathcal A-J(z,x)\bigr),
\label{ivk:orb:B01509}\\
\mathcal B(z,x,y)&=B_0(x)-zxB_1(x)
+\frac{zx(y+1)}y\bigl(\mathcal B(z,x,y)-B_0(x)\bigr)\notag\\
&\quad-\frac{zxy}{(1-x)(x-y)}J(z,x)
+\frac{zxy}{(1-x)(1-y)}\mathcal A
+\frac{zxy}{(1-y)(x-y)}J(z,y).
\label{ivk:orb:B1509}
\end{align}
Clear denominators before making formal substitutions. Equation
\eqref{ivk:orb:B01509} gives
$B_0(x)=1+zx\mathcal A/(1-x)-zx^2J(z,x)/(1-x)$.
Set $y=f_z(x)=zx/(1-zx)$ in \eqref{ivk:orb:B1509}, substitute this expression,
and collect terms. The result is
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:J1509}
J(z,f_z(x))=c(z,x)J(z,x)+d(z,x)+e(z,x)\mathcal A(z),
\end{equation}
where, with $K(z,x)=1-x+zx+zx^2$,
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:cde1509}
\begin{aligned}
c(z,x)&=\frac{(2zx-1)K(z,x)}{z(x-1)},\\
d(z,x)&=\frac{(2zx-1)(zx+z-1)}{z(zx-1)},\qquad
e(z,x)=-\frac{2x(zx+z-1)}{x-1}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Thus the scalar reduction follows by rational algebra from the exact
source model, not from a guessed coefficient recurrence.

Define
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:rho1509}
\sigma=3-2\sqrt2,\quad \mu=\sigma^{-1}=3+2\sqrt2,\qquad
X(z)=\frac{1-z-\sqrt{1-6z+z^2}}{2z},\quad X(0)=1.
\end{equation}
It satisfies $X=1+zX+zX^2$, so its Taylor coefficients are nonnegative.
Put
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:y1509}
y(z)=\frac{X(z)-1}{2}=f_z(X(z)),\qquad
X(\sigma)=1+\sqrt2,\quad y(\sigma)=1/\sqrt2.
\end{equation}
At $x=X(z)$, the kernel $K$ vanishes. The substitution is legitimate
because every coefficient in $z$ of $J$ is a polynomial in $x$;
intermediate divisions by $X-1=2z+O(z^2)$ are interpreted in Laurent
series, or removed by clearing denominators. At that root,
$d=y-1/y$ and $e=1/y$, whence
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:boundary1509}
\mathcal A=1-y^2+yJ(z,y).
\end{equation}

\subsection{Iterating in the contracting direction}
Rearrange \eqref{ivk:orb:J1509} in the opposite direction from class 214:
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:back1509}
J(z,x)=p(z,x)J(z,f_z(x))+q(z,x)+r_*(z,x)\mathcal A(z),
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:pqr1509}
\begin{aligned}
p(z,x)&=\frac{z(1-x)}{(1-2zx)K(z,x)},\\
q(z,x)&=\frac{(1-x)(1-z-zx)}{(1-zx)K(z,x)},\\
r_*(z,x)&=\frac{2zx(1-z-zx)}{(1-2zx)K(z,x)}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
For the orbit $x_j=f_z^{\,j}(x)$ of \eqref{ivk:orb:orbit214}, put
$P_0=1$, $P_j=\prod_{i=0}^{j-1}p(z,x_i)$, and define
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:UV1509}
U(z,x)=\sum_{j\ge0}P_jq(z,x_j),\qquad
V(z,x)=\sum_{j\ge0}P_jr_*(z,x_j).
\end{equation}
Let $U_N,V_N$ stop at $j=N-1$. Finite iteration gives exactly
\[
J(z,x)=U_N(z,x)+V_N(z,x)\mathcal A(z)+P_NJ(z,x_N).
\]
For $x=y(z)=O(z)$, every $p(z,x_j)$ has positive $z$ valuation, so
the last term tends coefficientwise to zero. Combining with
\eqref{ivk:orb:boundary1509} proves the formal identity
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:F1509}
\mathcal A(z)=\mathcal F(z,y(z)),\qquad
\mathcal F(z,x)=\frac{1-x^2+xU(z,x)}{1-xV(z,x)}.
\end{equation}
Its denominator has constant term one. The essential additional analytic
issue is to exclude zeros of this new denominator throughout the whole
disc, not merely at the positive critical point.

\subsection{A global nonzero denominator certificate}
For $|z|\le\sigma$, positivity and \eqref{ivk:orb:rho1509}--\eqref{ivk:orb:y1509} give
\[
|z|<R:=43/250,\qquad |X(z)|<M:=483/200,\qquad
|y(z)|<Y:=177/250.
\]
The algebraic kernel factors as
\[
K(z,x)=(1-x/X(z))(1-zX(z)x).
\]
Since $X$ is never zero and $y/X=z/(1-zX)$, at the first orbit point
$x_0=y(z)$ the lower bound is
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:k01509}
|K(z,x_0)|\ge k_0:=(1-a)(1-RMY)>0,\qquad
 a=R/(1-RM).
\end{equation}
The exact rational upper bounds
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:first1509}
\begin{aligned}
\frac{R(1+Y)}{(1-2RY)k_0}&<\frac45,&
\frac{2RY(1+R+RY)}{(1-2RY)k_0}&<\frac{17}{20},\\
\frac{RY}{1-RY}&<\frac7{50}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
imply respectively $|p(z,x_0)|<4/5$, $|r_*(z,x_0)|<17/20$,
and $|x_1|<7/50$. Also
$|q(z,x_0)|\le(1+Y)(1+R+RY)/((1-RY)k_0)$ is finite.

For $|x|\le w:=7/50$, the simpler lower bound
$|K(z,x)|\ge k_s:=1-w-Rw-Rw^2>0$ suffices. Direct rational
inequalities give
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:tailbounds1509}
|p|<\tfrac14,\qquad |q|<2,\qquad
|r_*|<\tfrac35|x|,\qquad |f_z(x)|<\tfrac9{50}|x|.
\end{equation}
For example, the upper bounds for $p$ and $r_*/x$ are
$R(1+w)/((1-2Rw)k_s)$ and
$2R(1+R+Rw)/((1-2Rw)k_s)$; the corresponding bounds for $q$ and
$f_z(x)/x$ are $(1+w)(1+R+Rw)/((1-Rw)k_s)$ and $R/(1-Rw)$.
This displays every finite inequality needed for \eqref{ivk:orb:tailbounds1509}.

The orbit satisfies $|x_j|\le w(9/50)^{j-1}$ for $j\ge1$.
Normal convergence of both series \eqref{ivk:orb:UV1509} follows by geometric
majorants. In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:denom1509}
|yV(z,y)|\le Y\left(\frac{17}{20}
+\frac{(4/5)(3/5)(7/50)}{1-(1/4)(9/50)}\right)<\frac23.
\end{equation}
Hence $|1-yV(z,y)|>1/3$ throughout the closed disc. The factorization
\eqref{ivk:orb:k01509} is important: using only the crude first-step triangle
bound would lose the strength required for this global conclusion.
The formal identity \eqref{ivk:orb:F1509} is therefore a convergent analytic
representation in $|z|<\sigma$ without a presupposed growth estimate
for the coefficients.

There is also a two-variable neighborhood for $U,V$. On
$|z|\le43/250$, $|x|\le709/1000$, one has
\[
|K(z,x)|\ge1-|x|-R|x|-R|x|^2>0,\qquad |p(z,x)|<5,
\qquad |f_z(x)|<7/50.
\]
The later iterates obey \eqref{ivk:orb:tailbounds1509}. All these inequalities
have strict margins, so slightly larger polydiscs still work.
Thus $U,V$ are holomorphic on a neighborhood of this closed polydisc;
$\mathcal F$ is holomorphic near the compact critical curve
$\{(z,y(z)):|z|\le\sigma\}$ by \eqref{ivk:orb:denom1509}.

Away from $z=\sigma$ on the boundary circle, the discriminant in
\eqref{ivk:orb:rho1509} is nonzero, so $X,y$ extend holomorphically. The
nonzero denominators and contraction estimates persist locally.
At $\sigma$, use a square-root coordinate for $y$ and the just-proved
two-variable holomorphy. Compactness then supplies $\eta>0$ and
$0<\phi<\pi/2$ such that $\mathcal A$ continues to the $\Delta$-domain
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:delta1509}
\{z:|z|<\sigma+\eta,\ z\ne\sigma,\ |\arg(z-\sigma)|>\phi\}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{An exact positive critical derivative}
Put $y_*=1/\sqrt2$. For fixed $z=\sigma$, the disc
$|x-y_*|\le1/1000$ lies in $|x|<709/1000$. Its first product factor
has modulus less than five; subsequent factors have modulus less than
$1/4$. Since both subsequent $q$ and $r_*$ have modulus less than two,
the omitted tails in \eqref{ivk:orb:UV1509} satisfy, for $N\ge1$,
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:UVtail1509}
|U-U_N|,\ |V-V_N|\le E_N:=\frac{40}{3}4^{1-N}.
\end{equation}
Indeed, after the first factor the remaining geometric majorant is
$2/(1-1/4)=8/3$. Cauchy's formula gives at $x=y_*$
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:UVderiv1509}
|U_x-(U_N)_x|,\ |V_x-(V_N)_x|\le1000E_N.
\end{equation}

The finite quantities are reproducible with rational interval arithmetic.
Start with zero terminal values and use, backwards for $j=N-1,\ldots,0$,
\[
U_j=q(z,x_j)+p(z,x_j)U_{j+1},\qquad
V_j=r_*(z,x_j)+p(z,x_j)V_{j+1}.
\]
For derivatives, set $\dot x_0=1$,
$\dot x_{j+1}=z\dot x_j/(1-zx_j)^2$, and use, for example,
\[
\dot U_j=q_x(z,x_j)\dot x_j+
 p_x(z,x_j)\dot x_j U_{j+1}+p(z,x_j)\dot U_{j+1}.
\]
Use the identical rule with $q,U$ replaced by $r_*,V$.
All functions being differentiated are the displayed rational functions.
Enclose $\sqrt2$ by scaled integer square roots, then form the intervals
for $\sigma$ and $y_*$. At $N=24$, interval operations on the rational
grid $10^{-60}\mathbb Z$, rounding every operation outward, followed by
\eqref{ivk:orb:UVtail1509}--\eqref{ivk:orb:UVderiv1509} and quotient differentiation in
\eqref{ivk:orb:F1509}, give
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:Fx1509}
0.1631959191663560<\mathcal F_x(\sigma,y_*)
<0.1631959201118698.
\end{equation}
In particular, the derivative is strictly positive. This is an exact
finite interval certificate plus a proved infinite tail, not a
floating-point sign test.

Set $t=\sqrt{1-z/\sigma}$ and $z=\sigma(1-t^2)$. The local branch is
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:local1509}
y(z)=\frac{1-3z-t\sqrt{1-\sigma z}}{4z}
=y_*-\kappa t+O(t^2),\qquad
\kappa=\frac{\sqrt{1-\sigma^2}}{4\sigma}>0.
\end{equation}
Since $\mathcal F$ is holomorphic near $(\sigma,y_*)$, the expansion
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:Blocal1509}
\mathcal F\left(\sigma(1-t^2),
\frac{1-3\sigma(1-t^2)-t\sqrt{1-\sigma^2(1-t^2)}}{4\sigma(1-t^2)}
\right)=\sum_{j\ge0}\widehat h_j t^j
\end{equation}
converges for sufficiently small $|t|$. In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:C1509}
\widehat h_1=-\kappa\mathcal F_x(\sigma,y_*)<0,\qquad
\widehat C=\frac{\kappa\mathcal F_x(\sigma,y_*)}{2\sqrt\pi}>0.
\end{equation}
A rational Machin-series enclosure for $\pi$, combined with the same
outward interval arithmetic, proves
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:C1509digits}
0.0660857086342079<\widehat C<0.0660857090170910.
\end{equation}
The more precise numerical value $0.066085708825649431\ldots$ was
already reported by V\'aclav Kot\v{e}\v{s}ovec in A279567 on
7 October 2021~\cite{oeis1509}. The short certificate \eqref{ivk:orb:C1509digits} suffices for positivity;

\section{The counting index inverse}\label{ivk:orb:secinverse}
\subsection{Leading inverse and formal correction recurrence}
The inverse considered here takes a target count to its required index.
It is not the compositional inverse of the generating function $A(z)$.
The following notation applies to either sequence: let $\mu_0$ be its
growth constant, $C_0$ its positive amplitude, and $c_j^{(0)}$ its
relative corrections. Thus $(\mu_0,C_0,c_j^{(0)})$ equals
$(4,C,c_j)$ for A279544 and $(3+2\sqrt2,\widehat C,\widehat c_j)$
for A279567. In this section abbreviate them to $\mu,C,c_j$,
and write $L=\log\mu$ and $\beta=3/2$. For sufficiently large
real $Y>0$, let
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:Wleading}
u(Y)=-\frac{\beta}{L}\,
W_{-1}\left(-\frac{L}{\beta}\left(\frac CY\right)^{1/\beta}\right).
\end{equation}
The branch $W_{-1}$ is the real branch with values at most $-1$ on
$[-1/e,0)$~\cite{lambert}. It selects the large solution of
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:leadingeq}
C e^{Lu}u^{-\beta}=Y.
\end{equation}
The principal branch would select the small solution and would be
incorrect for this counting inverse. In logarithmic form,
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:logleading}
u(Y)=\frac{\log Y+\beta\log\log Y-\log C-\beta\log L}{L}
+O\left(\frac{\log\log Y}{\log Y}\right).
\end{equation}

Let the logarithmic correction coefficients be defined formally by
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:ddef}
\log\left(1+\sum_{j\ge1}c_jw^j\right)=\sum_{j\ge1}d_jw^j.
\end{equation}
Thus $d_1=c_1$, $d_2=c_2-c_1^2/2$, and in general
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:drec}
d_m=c_m-\frac1m\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}j d_jc_{m-j}.
\end{equation}
There is a unique formal inverse expansion
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:yinverse}
y(Y)=u(Y)+\sum_{j\ge1}e_j u(Y)^{-j},
\end{equation}
obtained by setting all powers of $u^{-1}$ to zero in
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:erec}
L(y-u)-\beta\log(y/u)+\sum_{j\ge1}d_jy^{-j}.
\end{equation}
At order $u^{-m}$, the new unknown occurs only as $Le_m$; hence this
is an unambiguous recursion. In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:e123}
\begin{aligned}
e_1&=-d_1/L,\\
e_2&=-\beta d_1/L^2-d_2/L,\\
e_3&=-\beta^2d_1/L^3-\beta d_2/L^2-d_1^2/L^2-d_3/L.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
This is the smooth inverse of the asymptotic model. It does not impose
a canonical interpolation on the discrete sequence $a_n$.

\subsection{Strict increase and integer threshold brackets}
Appending the value $n$ to any admissible length-$n$ sequence gives
an admissible length-$(n+1)$ sequence: a new forbidden triple would
have its last, maximal entry at $k=n+1$, which is impossible in
\eqref{ivk:orb:avoid214}. The sequence
$(0,1,\ldots,n-1,n-1)$ is also admissible and is not in the image.
For $n=1$ it is $(0,0)$. Therefore $a_{n+1}>a_n$ for $n\ge1$.
For class 1509, appending the maximum gives the same injection,
and the all-zero sequence supplies an extra valid object. Thus
$b_{n+1}>b_n$ for $n\ge1$ as well. In the theorem below, $a_n$
denotes either of these two sequences with its matching constants.

\begin{theorem}\label{ivk:orb:inverse}
Let $N(Y)=\min\{n\ge0:a_n\ge Y\}$ and, for a fixed integer $K\ge0$,
put
\[
y_K(Y)=u(Y)+\sum_{j=1}^{K}e_j u(Y)^{-j}.
\]
There are constants $E_K>0$ and $Y_K$ such that, for every $Y\ge Y_K$,
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:orb:ceilingbracket}
\ceil{y_K(Y)-E_Ku(Y)^{-K-1}}
\le N(Y)\le
\ceil{y_K(Y)+E_Ku(Y)^{-K-1}}.
\end{equation}
For sufficiently large $Y$, these bounds contain at most two adjacent
integer candidates. The convention at equality is $N(a_n)=n$ for
$n\ge2$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The coefficient expansion implies, for every fixed $K$,
\[
\log a_n=Ln-\beta\log n+\log C+
\sum_{j=1}^{K}d_jn^{-j}+O(n^{-K-1}).
\]
Let $F_K(x)=Lx-\beta\log x+\log C+\sum_{j=1}^{K}d_jx^{-j}$.
Choose $D$ and a starting index so that the error at every subsequent
integer is bounded in absolute value by $Dx^{-K-1}$. The smooth
functions $F_K(x)\pm Dx^{-K-1}$ are eventually strictly increasing,
because their derivatives tend to $L>0$.

Invert these two envelopes at $\log Y$. Formal substitution from
\eqref{ivk:orb:erec} gives $F_K(y_K)-\log Y=O(u^{-K-1})$; the mean value
theorem, using a fixed positive lower derivative bound, puts both
inverse roots at $y_K+O(u^{-K-1})$. If an integer lies below the
inverse root of the upper envelope, its count is below $Y$. Every
integer at or above the inverse root of the lower envelope has count
at least $Y$. Applying the corresponding ceilings gives
\eqref{ivk:orb:ceilingbracket}, after enlarging the fixed constant $E_K$.
Since the real interval has length $2E_Ku^{-K-1}\to0$, its ceilings
are eventually equal or adjacent.
\end{proof}
Already at $K=0$, the real bracket width is $O(1/u)$, not $O(1)$.
It is essential to retain both ceilings: a target may lie arbitrarily
close to an exact sequence value, so unconditional rounding of $y_K$
or an unconditional single ceiling is not justified. The proof gives
existence of $E_K,Y_K$, but this report supplies no effective numerical
values for them. The theorem is therefore not an executable certified
threshold routine for a specified finite target.

 e_i>e_k\quad\hbox{and}\quad e_j>e_k.

\subsection{The theorem}
Write $\rho=4/27$ and $\lambda=\log(27/4)$.
\begin{theorem}\label{ivk:two:main}
The generating function $A$ has radius of convergence $\rho$, and $\rho$
is its unique singularity on the circle of convergence. For some
$\eta>0$ and $0<\phi<\pi/2$, it extends holomorphically to the
$\Delta$-domain
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:two:delta}
 \{z:|z|<\rho+\eta,\ z\ne\rho,\ |\arg(z-\rho)|>\phi\}.
\end{equation}
Near $\rho$ it has a convergent expansion
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:two:puiseux}
 A(z)=\sum_{m\ge0}b_m t^m,\qquad t=\sqrt{1-z/\rho},
 \qquad b_1<0,
\end{equation}
with the branch $t>0$ for real $z<\rho$. For every fixed integer $J\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:two:allorders}
 a_n=\left(\frac{27}{4}\right)^n n^{-3/2}
 \left(C_0+\frac{C_1}{n}+\cdots+\frac{C_J}{n^J}
       +O(n^{-J-1})\right),
 \qquad C_0=C>0.
\end{equation}
The coefficients $b_m,C_j$ are specified by convergent recurrences and
explicit coefficient-transfer formulas below. In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:two:Cinterval}
 0.01116841071267033797867<C<0.01116841071267033797869.
\end{equation}
For $N(T)=\min\{n\ge0:a_n\ge T\}$, Theorem~\ref{ivk:two:inverse} gives
Lambert-$W_{-1}$ expansions with an error retained inside two ceilings.
\end{theorem}
The constant in the remainder of \eqref{ivk:two:allorders} may depend on $J$.
Convergence of the local series \eqref{ivk:two:puiseux} does not assert
convergence of the inverse-power expansion as $J\to\infty$. The
inverse error constants and their starting thresholds are not
numerically certified here.

\subsection{How the continuation argument differs}\label{ivk:two:sec:contrast}
This is a self-contained companion to report118, which treated
classes 214 and 1509 using iterated kernels. The present representation
needs both small cubic roots and a quotient of two orbit limits.

positive real segment. Normal convergence first gives meromorphic
continuation around the rest of the critical circle; positivity then

boundary pole to be removable. This distinction matters: a real-axis

\section{The exact succession rule and scalar equation}
Every state at depth $n$ of the generating tree contributes one object,
with multiplicity. Start with the label $(p,s)=(0,0)$. Its children are
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:two:succession}
\begin{aligned}
 (p,s)&\longmapsto(p+1,s-i),&&0\le i\le s,\\
      &\longmapsto(p+1-\ell,k),&&1\le\ell\le p,\quad0\le k<\ell.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The labels record the lengths of the initial and possible second run
of zeros in the source's left-growing construction. Let $b_{n,p,s}$ be
the number of labels, counting multiplicity, with
$b_{0,p,s}=\mathbf1_{(p,s)=(0,0)}$. Rule \eqref{ivk:two:succession} determines
$b_{n+1,p,s}$ by summing contributions of every child. Thus
\[
 B(z,x,y)=\sum_{n,p,s\ge0}b_{n,p,s}z^nx^py^s,\qquad
 H(z,x)=B(z,x,1),\qquad A(z)=H(z,1).
\]
In particular each coefficient of $z$ in $H$ is a polynomial in $x$.

Summing the two finite child sums in \eqref{ivk:two:succession} gives
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:two:catalytic}
\begin{aligned}
 B(z,x,y)={}&1-\frac{zxy}{1-y}B(z,x,y)\\
 &-\frac{xz(x^2+y-2xy)}{(1-x)(x-y)(1-y)}H(z,x)\\
 &+\frac{zxy}{(1-y)(x-y)}H(z,y)
  +\frac{zx}{(1-x)(1-y)}A(z).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The displayed rational denominators encode polynomial finite sums;
identities at their exceptional values are interpreted after clearing
denominators.

Define
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:two:definitions}
\begin{aligned}
 f_z(x)&=\frac1{1-zx},& K(z,x)&=(x-1)^2-zx^3,\\
 c(z,x)&=\frac{K(z,x)}{1-x},& d(z,x)&=x-f_z(x),\\
 e(z,x)&=1+\frac{zx^2}{1-x}.&&
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Multiplying \eqref{ivk:two:catalytic} by $(1-y)(1-x)(x-y)$ makes its left
kernel $(1-y+zxy)(1-x)(x-y)$. Set $y=f_z(x)$, cancel the kernel and
simplify the remaining identity. The result is the scalar equation
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:two:scalar}
 H(z,f_z(x))=c(z,x)H(z,x)+d(z,x)+e(z,x)A(z),
\end{equation}
with the useful exact relation
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:two:ce}
 c(z,x)=1-xe(z,x).
\end{equation}
No analytic assumption on $A$ is needed for this formal calculation.

\section{Two roots and the formal counting germ}
\subsection{The two small kernel branches}
Put $u=\sqrt z$. Let $X(u)$ be the unique formal solution of
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:two:Ximplicit}
 X=1+uX^{3/2},\qquad X(0)=1,
\end{equation}
where the fractional power is expanded at $1$. The implicit function
theorem also makes this an analytic germ. Lagrange inversion gives
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:two:lagrange}
 [u^n](X(u)-1)=\frac1n\binom{3n/2}{n-1}>0\qquad(n\ge1).
\end{equation}
The two roots of $K(z,x)=0$ with finite limit at zero are
$X_+(z)=X(u)$ and $X_-(z)=X(-u)$, beginning $1+u+O(u^2)$ and
$1-u+O(u^2)$. At either root $X$,
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:two:kernelinitial}
 Y=f_z(X)=\frac{X^2}{2X-1},\qquad
 e(z,X)=\frac1X,\qquad
 H(z,Y)=X-Y+\frac{A(z)}X.
\end{equation}
These statements follow by replacing $z$ with $(X-1)^2/X^3$ in
\eqref{ivk:two:definitions} and using $c(z,X)=0$.

\subsection{The orbit and its canceled coefficients}
For independent $z,X$ define
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:two:orbit}
 x_{-1}=X,\qquad x_0=f_z(X),\qquad x_{j+1}=f_z(x_j)\quad(j\ge0).
\end{equation}
The identity $x_j-1=zx_{j-1}x_j$ yields the cancellation
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:two:cancellation}
 e_j:=e(z,x_j)=1-\frac{x_j}{x_{j-1}},\qquad
 c_j=1-x_je_j,\qquad d_j^{\rm orb}=x_j-x_{j+1}.
\end{equation}
We use the right-hand side to define $e_j$ at $z=0$ as well. It removes
the apparent division by $1-x_j$. The superscript on $d_j^{\rm orb}$
distinguishes this orbit increment from the relative asymptotic
corrections introduced later.

Initialize and iterate
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:two:QRrec}
\begin{aligned}
 Q_0&=X-x_0,& R_0&=1/X,\\
 Q_{j+1}&=c_jQ_j+d_j^{\rm orb},&
 R_{j+1}&=c_jR_j+e_j.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Let $Q(z,X),R(z,X)$ be their limits. Section~\ref{ivk:two:holomorphy} proves
normal convergence on an explicit product domain. The recurrence is
also a definition by convergent product-sum expressions, but requires
no $c_j$ to be nonzero.

The attracting fixed point is
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:two:fixed}
 \alpha(z)=\frac2{1+\sqrt{1-4z}},\qquad
 f_z(\alpha)=\alpha,\qquad f'_z(\alpha)=\alpha-1.
\end{equation}
For the substitutions $z=u^2$, $X=X(\pm u)$, the exact difference
formula
\[
 f_z(x)-f_z(y)=\frac{z(x-y)}{(1-zx)(1-zy)}
\]
shows, coefficientwise and uniformly on a sufficiently small $u$-disk,
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:two:formalorders}
\begin{aligned}
 x_j-\alpha&=O(u^{2j+3}),&
 e_j,\ c_j-1&=O(u^{2j+1}),&
 d_j^{\rm orb}&=O(u^{2j+3}).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Indeed $x_0-\alpha=O(u^3)$ and each application of the difference formula
gains a factor $O(u^2)$. These estimates make \eqref{ivk:two:QRrec}
coefficientwise convergent in $\C[[u]]$.

\begin{proposition}\label{ivk:two:germ}
The formal counting series has the exact representation
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:two:quotient}
 A(z)=\frac{Q(z,X_-(z))-Q(z,X_+(z))}
             {R(z,X_+(z))-R(z,X_-(z))}.
\end{equation}
This quotient defines an analytic germ in $z$ at zero, with value $1$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Start with \eqref{ivk:two:kernelinitial} and iterate \eqref{ivk:two:scalar}. Then
$H(z,x_j)=Q_j+R_jA(z)$. Since each $z$-coefficient of $H$ is polynomial
in its second argument, the coefficientwise limit in
\eqref{ivk:two:formalorders} gives
\[
 H(z,\alpha)=Q(z,X_+)+R(z,X_+)A
           =Q(z,X_-)+R(z,X_-)A.
\]
Solving these two equations gives \eqref{ivk:two:quotient}. This argument uses
the original formal counting series; it does not assume it already has
a positive radius.

The joint holomorphy lemma below applies near $(z,X)=(0,1)$.
From \eqref{ivk:two:QRrec} and \eqref{ivk:two:formalorders},
\[
 Q(u^2,X(u))=u+O(u^2),\qquad
 R(u^2,X(u))=1-u+O(u^2).
\]
The numerator and denominator of \eqref{ivk:two:quotient} are both odd analytic
functions of $u$, each with leading term $-2u$. Their quotient is even
and holomorphic at $u=0$, with value $1$. An even holomorphic $u$-germ
is holomorphic in $z=u^2$. It equals $A$ coefficientwise by the formal
identity, establishing positive radius of convergence.
\end{proof}

\section{An explicit joint holomorphy domain}\label{ivk:two:holomorphy}
\begin{lemma}\label{ivk:two:domainlemma}
The limits $Q(z,X),R(z,X)$ in \eqref{ivk:two:QRrec} are jointly holomorphic on
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:two:productdomain}
 \mathcal D=\{(z,X)\in\C^2:|z|<3/20,\quad0<|X|<31/10\}.
\end{equation}
The recurrences converge normally on every compact subset of
$\mathcal D$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For $(z,X)\in\mathcal D$,
\[
 |x_0|<\frac1{1-(3/20)(31/10)}=\frac{200}{107}<\frac{19}{10}.
\]
The closed disk $|x|\le19/10$ is invariant under $f_z$, since
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:two:contractbounds}
 |f_z(x)|<\frac{200}{143}<\frac{19}{10},\qquad
 |f'_z(x)|<\frac{6000}{20449}<\frac3{10}.
\end{equation}
Every orbit point is nonzero. Moreover,
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:two:reciprocalbounds}
 |1/x_0|<\frac{293}{200},\qquad
 |1/x_j|<\frac{257}{200}\quad(j\ge1).
\end{equation}
The first bound follows from $1/x_0=1-zX$, and the second from
$1/x_j=1-zx_{j-1}$. The common bound $293/200$ is therefore valid even
when the preceding point in \eqref{ivk:two:cancellation} is $x_0$.

Contraction gives, for $j\ge1$,
\[
 |x_j-x_{j-1}|\le(3/10)^{j-1}|x_1-x_0|.
\]
Consequently \eqref{ivk:two:cancellation} and \eqref{ivk:two:reciprocalbounds} make
$e_j,c_j-1,d_j^{\rm orb}$ geometrically small. The coefficient $e_0$
uses $1/X$, which is uniformly bounded on each compact subset of
$\mathcal D$. All finite recurrences are holomorphic there, including
at $z=0$.

If $\sum_j|c_j-1|$ is bounded, every finite product of $|c_j|$ is
bounded by $\exp(\sum_j|c_j-1|)$. Formula \eqref{ivk:two:QRrec} and the
summable forcing terms then bound $Q_j,R_j$. The differences
\[
 Q_{j+1}-Q_j=(c_j-1)Q_j+d_j^{\rm orb},\qquad
 R_{j+1}-R_j=(c_j-1)R_j+e_j
\]
are uniformly summable on compact subsets. Thus $Q_j,R_j$ converge
normally and their limits are jointly holomorphic. The orbit itself
converges to the fixed point \eqref{ivk:two:fixed}, determined by
$\alpha(0)=1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{ivk:two:seedbounds}
Let $r=\sqrt\rho=2/(3\sqrt3)$. The series $X(u)$ has radius $r$,
is absolutely convergent for $|u|\le r$, and satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:two:Xbounds}
 |X(u)|\le3\quad(|u|\le r),\qquad X(r)=3,\quad X(-r)=3/4.
\end{equation}
The relevant kernel branches extend analytically across each point
$|z|=\rho$ other than $z=\rho$. At $\rho$ they are analytic in
$t=\sqrt{1-z/\rho}$; the lower branch is analytic in $t^2$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Stirling's formula in \eqref{ivk:two:lagrange} gives radius $r$ and convergence
at $r$. The positive real solution of \eqref{ivk:two:Ximplicit} reaches
$X=3$ at $u=r$. Substitution at $u=-r$ gives $X=3/4$ on the branch
from $1$. Positivity of the coefficients then proves the disk bound.
No finite kernel root is zero, since $K(z,0)=1$.

The simultaneous equations $K=K_x=0$ have only the nonzero critical
value $z=4/27$, with $x=3$; the cubic discriminant is
$z(4-27z)$. The double root at $\rho$ has an ordinary square-root
parametrization, while the other root $3/4$ is simple. Thus the
implicit function theorem gives the asserted local continuations.
\end{proof}
The strict inequalities $\rho<3/20$ and $3<31/10$ place both selected
seeds within the joint domain \eqref{ivk:two:productdomain}, with room to
continue outside the critical circle. Hence \eqref{ivk:two:quotient} is
meromorphic in a neighborhood of every noncritical boundary point,
and meromorphic in $t$ near $\rho$. This is a genuinely complex
continuation statement, not a consequence of real-axis convergence.

\section{The real determinant and removal of boundary poles}
\subsection{A strict determinant gap}
For $0<z\le\rho$, the two real seeds obey
\[
 1<X_+(z)\le3,\qquad 3/4\le X_-(z)<1.
\]
The upper seed lies above $\alpha$. A crossing is impossible because
$K(z,\alpha)=-z\alpha^2<0$, and the ordering holds near zero.
The fixed-point quadratic and monotonicity of $f_z$ then give
\[
 \alpha<f_z(X_+)<X_+,\qquad 1<f_z(X_-)<\alpha.
\]
The upper orbit decreases to $\alpha$; the lower orbit increases to it.

\begin{lemma}\label{ivk:two:gap}
For $0<z\le\rho$,
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:two:detgap}
 R(z,X_-(z))\ge\frac1{X_-(z)}>1>R(z,X_+(z)).
\end{equation}
In particular the denominator in \eqref{ivk:two:quotient} never vanishes on
this positive segment, including at $\rho$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
On the upper orbit, $e_j>0$ by \eqref{ivk:two:cancellation}. Also
$0<c_j<1$: the kernel is negative between $1$ and $X_+$, so
$c_j=K(z,x_j)/(1-x_j)>0$, while \eqref{ivk:two:ce} gives $c_j<1$.
Starting from $0<R_0=1/X_+<1$, the recurrence preserves positivity and
\[
 R_{j+1}-1=c_j(R_j-1)+(1-x_j)e_j<0.
\]
More quantitatively, $1-R_{j+1}\ge c_j(1-R_j)$. Since
$\sum_j|1-c_j|<\infty$ and every $c_j>0$, the infinite product
$\prod_jc_j$ is positive. Thus
$1-R\ge(1-R_0)\prod_jc_j>0$.

On the lower orbit, $x_j>1$, $e_j<0$ and $c_j>1$. Starting from
$R_0=1/X_->1$, induction gives $R_j>1$ and
\[
 R_{j+1}-R_j=e_j(1-x_jR_j)>0.
\]
Its limit is at least $1/X_->1$, proving \eqref{ivk:two:detgap}.
\end{proof}
The strict lower-branch gap alone would suffice: finite upper bounds
$R_j<1$ give $R\le1$ in the limit. The product argument above also
establishes strictness of the upper limit; finite strict inequalities
alone would not do so.

\subsection{The radius and the boundary}
\begin{proposition}\label{ivk:two:boundary}
The radius of $A$ is at least $\rho$ and
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:two:absconv}
 \sum_{n\ge0}a_n\rho^n=A(\rho)<\infty.
\end{equation}
Every apparent pole of \eqref{ivk:two:quotient} on $|z|=\rho$, except possibly
at $\rho$, is removable. The counting function has a $\Delta$-domain
of the form \eqref{ivk:two:delta} and is holomorphic in $t$ near $\rho$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Proposition~\ref{ivk:two:germ} gives a positive convergence radius. If it were
less than $\rho$, Pringsheim's theorem for nonnegative coefficients
would make its positive real endpoint singular. Lemmas~\ref{ivk:two:domainlemma}
and \ref{ivk:two:gap}, and the regular positive kernel branches before $\rho$,
continue \eqref{ivk:two:quotient} holomorphically through that endpoint. This
contradiction proves that the radius is at least $\rho$.

At $\rho$ the orbit limits are finite and their determinant is nonzero,
so the quotient has a finite radial limit. Since $a_n\ge0$, monotone
convergence gives \eqref{ivk:two:absconv}. The series is therefore uniformly
absolutely convergent on the closed disk, and
$|A(z)|\le A(\rho)$ there.

Fix $\zeta$ on the critical circle with $\zeta\ne\rho$.
Lemma~\ref{ivk:two:seedbounds} and joint holomorphy provide a meromorphic
quotient on a full neighborhood of $\zeta$. Its denominator is not
identically zero: on the $u$-sheet it is a single analytic function
with expansion $-2u+O(u^3)$, and the identity theorem applies along
the continued branches. The quotient agrees with $A$ on the interior
overlap by continuation of the germ. Any nonremovable singularity
of this meromorphic function at $\zeta$ would be a pole, forcing
unbounded values along the inward radius. That contradicts
\eqref{ivk:two:absconv}. The singularity is therefore removable.

At $\rho$, Lemma~\ref{ivk:two:gap} makes the determinant nonzero, so the
quotient is holomorphic in $t$. Small neighborhoods of the other
circle points have holomorphic continuations after removal. A finite
cover of the circle away from a small neighborhood of $\rho$, combined
with the local slit neighborhood at $\rho$, gives some $\eta,\phi$
in \eqref{ivk:two:delta}. Identity on overlapping interior sets makes the
continuations consistent.
\end{proof}
Bounded radial values do not generally remove arbitrary singularities.
The proof first establishes meromorphic continuation, where the only
possible nonremovable isolated singularities are poles. It makes no
claim that the orbit determinant has no complex zeros everywhere in
\eqref{ivk:two:productdomain}; it shows that any relevant boundary zeros
cancel in the quotient.

\section{The positive amplitude and its exact certificate}\label{ivk:two:sec:amplitude}
\subsection{The critical derivative}
Treat the two seeds as independent variables and define near
$(\rho,3,3/4)$
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:two:Fdef}
 F(z,v,w)=\frac{Q(z,w)-Q(z,v)}{R(z,v)-R(z,w)}.
\end{equation}
It is holomorphic there by Lemmas~\ref{ivk:two:domainlemma} and \ref{ivk:two:gap}.
The critical root expansion is
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:two:rootexpansion}
 z=\rho(1-t^2),\qquad X_+=3-2\sqrt3\,t+O(t^2),\qquad
 X_-=3/4+O(t^2).
\end{equation}
Since $z$ and $X_-$ are analytic in $t^2$, the coefficient of $t$ is
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:two:amplitude}
 b_1=-2\sqrt3\,F_v(\rho,3,3/4),\qquad
 C=\frac{-b_1}{2\sqrt\pi}=\sqrt{\frac3\pi}\,F_v(\rho,3,3/4).
\end{equation}
It remains essential to prove $F_v>0$; the kernel discriminant alone
does not exclude cancellation of this term.

\subsection{Uniform complex tail bounds}
The following deliberately generous constants make a finite rational
calculation an infinite-orbit certificate. Fix $z=\rho$ and let the
seed $X$ range over either of the closed disks
\[
 |X-3|\le h\quad\hbox{or}\quad|X-3/4|\le h,
 \qquad h=1/1000.
\]
Set $M=181/100$, $q=7/25$ and $P=2^{230}$. The rational inequalities
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:two:certbounds}
 \frac1{1-\rho(3+h)}<M,\qquad
 \frac1{1-\rho M}<M,\qquad
 \frac{\rho}{(1-\rho M)^2}<q
\end{equation}
show that all orbit points satisfy $|x_j|<M$ and that $f_\rho$ is
$q$-contracting on their disk. Lower bounds follow from
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:two:certlower}
\begin{aligned}
 \frac1{|1-\rho a|+\rho h}&>\frac34,
 &\frac{\rho(a-h)}{1+\rho(a+h)}&>\frac1{12}
 &&(a=3,3/4),\\
 \frac1{1+\rho M}&>\frac34,
 &\frac{\rho(3/4)}{1+\rho M}&>\frac1{12}.&&
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The first line bounds $|x_0|$ and $|x_0-1|$ on the seed disks; the
second line propagates $|x_j|>3/4$ and $|x_j-1|>1/12$ for later points.

For the two fixed points $\alpha,\beta$ of $f_\rho$,
\[
 1<\alpha<5/4,\qquad
 \beta=\frac{1+\sqrt{1-4\rho}}{2\rho}<28/5,
 \qquad |x_j-\alpha|<4q^j.
\]
The last estimate follows from $|x_0-\alpha|<M+5/4<4$ and contraction.
Using
\[
 e(\rho,x)=-\frac{\rho(x-\alpha)(x-\beta)}{x-1},
 \qquad \rho\,12(M+28/5)<14,
\]
we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:two:coeffbounds}

\section{Every fixed algebraic correction}
\subsection{The convergent local series}
The local kernel branches may be written explicitly as
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:two:trigbranches}
\begin{aligned}
 s_+(t)&=\frac{1+2\cos((\pi+2\arcsin t)/3)}3,
 &s_-(t)&=\frac{1-2\cos(2\arcsin(t)/3)}3,\\
 X_+(t)&=\frac1{1-s_+(t)},
 &X_-(t)&=\frac1{1-s_-(t)}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
They satisfy $z=s^2(1-s)=\rho(1-t^2)$ and $K(z,X)=0$; their values
and first derivatives agree with \eqref{ivk:two:rootexpansion}. These are
local analytic branch formulas. The orbit functions $Q,R$ are still
infinite normally convergent recurrences.

Define $b_m$ by
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:two:bdef}
 F\bigl(\rho(1-t^2),X_+(t),X_-(t)\bigr)=\sum_{m\ge0}b_mt^m.
\end{equation}
Joint holomorphy and the nonzero local determinant prove that this
Taylor series converges for sufficiently small $|t|$. Arbitrarily
many coefficients are obtainable by Taylor arithmetic in
\eqref{ivk:two:QRrec}, with tails bounded using Cauchy's estimates on an
appropriate fixed local domain.

\subsection{Transfer coefficients and remainders}
For $\gamma\notin\{0,1,2,\ldots\}$, define $T_k(\gamma)$ by
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:two:gammaexp}
 \frac{\Gamma(n-\gamma)}{\Gamma(n+1)}
 \sim n^{-\gamma-1}\sum_{k\ge0}T_k(\gamma)n^{-k}.
\end{equation}
They are obtained by exponentiating the formal series
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:two:bernoulli}
 \sum_{m\ge1}\frac{(-1)^{m+1}
 [B_{m+1}(-\gamma)-B_{m+1}(1)]}{m(m+1)}n^{-m},
\end{equation}
where $B_j(x)$ are Bernoulli polynomials. In particular,
$T_0=1$ and $T_1(\gamma)=\gamma(\gamma+1)/2$.
The exact coefficient identity
\[
 [z^n](1-z/\rho)^\gamma
 =\rho^{-n}\frac{\Gamma(n-\gamma)}{\Gamma(-\gamma)\Gamma(n+1)}
\]
then gives
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:two:Ck}
 C_k=\sum_{j=0}^k
 \frac{b_{2j+1}T_{k-j}(j+1/2)}{\Gamma(-j-1/2)}.
\end{equation}

For any specified $J$, expand \eqref{ivk:two:bdef} through $t^{2J+4}$ with
remainder $O(t^{2J+5})$. The local even powers form a polynomial in
$1-z/\rho$ and contribute no large-$n$ algebraic terms. Transfer in
the proved $\Delta$-domain~\cite[Chapter VI]{fs} applies to the odd
powers and the remainder. The last retained odd power $t^{2J+3}$
contributes $O(\rho^{-n}n^{-J-5/2})$ and may be absorbed into the
error in \eqref{ivk:two:allorders}; the transferred local remainder is of
still smaller order. Equations \eqref{ivk:two:gammaexp}--\eqref{ivk:two:Ck} prove
\eqref{ivk:two:allorders} at every fixed depth. This completes the coefficient
part of Theorem~\ref{ivk:two:main}.

Let $d_j=C_j/C$ denote the relative corrections. The first two are
specified exactly by
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:two:d12}
 d_1=\frac38-\frac32\frac{b_3}{b_1},\qquad
 d_2=\frac{25}{128}-\frac{45}{16}\frac{b_3}{b_1}
                  +\frac{15}{4}\frac{b_5}{b_1}.
\end{equation}
These formulas are rigorous independently of a decimal evaluation of
$b_3,b_5$. The following certificate also supplies their first two
numerical enclosures.

\section{Counting index inverses and integer thresholds}\label{ivk:two:sec:inverses}
\subsection{Monotonicity and the Lambert approximation}
Appending the new maximum $n$ to a length-$n$ inversion sequence
preserves \eqref{ivk:two:avoid}: the new entry can only be the last member of
a triple, and no preceding value exceeds it. This is an injection
into the size-$n+1$ class. For $n\ge1$, the all-zero sequence is
outside its image, so $a_{n+1}>a_n$. Thus the threshold $N(T)$ is
well defined for all sufficiently large $T$.

Write $p=3/2$, $\lambda=\log(27/4)$ and define
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:two:udef}
 u(T)=-\frac p\lambda
 W_{-1}\!\left(-\frac\lambda p\left(\frac CT\right)^{1/p}\right).
\end{equation}
Here $W_{-1}$ is the real Lambert branch with values at most $-1$~\cite{lambert}.
For large $T$, $u$ is the large positive solution of
$C\exp(\lambda u)u^{-p}=T$, and $u\to\infty$.

Distinguish the relative corrections $d_j$ from the logarithmic
corrections $\ell_j$ defined formally by
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:two:logcorrections}
 \log\left(1+\sum_{j\ge1}d_jn^{-j}\right)
       =\sum_{j\ge1}\ell_jn^{-j}.
\end{equation}
In particular $\ell_1=d_1$, $\ell_2=d_2-d_1^2/2$. Define $\eta_j$
recursively by cancellation in
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:two:inverserec}
 0=\lambda(v-u)-p\log(v/u)+\sum_{j\ge1}\ell_jv^{-j},
 \qquad v=u+\sum_{j\ge1}\eta_ju^{-j}.
\end{equation}
The coefficient of the new unknown $\eta_j$ is always $\lambda$,
so the recursion determines every fixed order uniquely. Its first
terms are
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:two:eta12}
 \eta_1=-\frac{\ell_1}{\lambda},\qquad
 \eta_2=-\frac{p\ell_1}{\lambda^2}-\frac{\ell_2}{\lambda}.
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}\label{ivk:two:inverse}
For any fixed $K\ge0$, set
$v_K(T)=u(T)+\sum_{j=1}^K\eta_ju(T)^{-j}$. There exist constants
$M_K>0$ and $T_K$ such that, for every real $T\ge T_K$,
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:two:bracket}
 \ceil{v_K(T)-M_Ku(T)^{-K-1}}
 \ \le N(T)\le\
 \ceil{v_K(T)+M_Ku(T)^{-K-1}}.
\end{equation}
Equivalently, some $\eps_K(T)=O(u(T)^{-K-1})$ satisfies
$N(T)=\ceil{v_K(T)+\eps_K(T)}$. The two integers in
\eqref{ivk:two:bracket} are eventually equal or adjacent.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The coefficient expansion and $C>0$ give, at integer $n$,
\[
 \log a_n=\lambda n-p\log n+\log C
          +\sum_{j=1}^K\ell_jn^{-j}+O(n^{-K-1}).
\]
Let $G_K(x)=\lambda x-p\log x+\log C+
\sum_{j=1}^K\ell_jx^{-j}$ for real $x$ large. Some constant $D_K$
makes $G_K(n)-D_Kn^{-K-1}\le\log a_n\le
G_K(n)+D_Kn^{-K-1}$ at all sufficiently large integers. Both smooth
envelopes have derivative $\lambda-p/x+O(x^{-2})>\lambda/2$ for
large $x$. This differentiates explicit finite smooth expressions,
not the unspecified remainder of an integer sequence.

Formal cancellation \eqref{ivk:two:inverserec}, used at a fixed finite order,
gives $G_K(v_K)-\log T=O(u^{-K-1})$. The mean value theorem puts
the inverse roots of both envelopes at $v_K+O(u^{-K-1})$. An integer
strictly below the inverse root of the upper envelope has count
below $T$; an integer at or above the inverse root of the lower
envelope has count at least $T$. Taking ceilings and enlarging
$M_K$ gives \eqref{ivk:two:bracket}. Finite initial counts are below $T$
once $T$ is sufficiently large. The real bracket width tends to
zero, so its two ceilings are eventually equal or adjacent.
\end{proof}
The unknown constants $M_K,T_K$ are existential. The theorem is not
an effective certified algorithm for a prescribed finite target.
Dropping the error inside the ceiling is invalid uniformly over all
real $T$: a target arbitrarily close to an exact count can change
the last integer.

\section{Further research questions}\label{ivk:two:sec:questions}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
\item \textbf{Effective coefficient and inverse errors.}
Can the qualitative $\Delta$-domain be replaced by an explicit domain
and usable bounds on it, yielding certified $M_K,T_K$ in
\eqref{ivk:two:bracket}? This would turn the asymptotic inverse into a
finite-target procedure requiring at most a small final exact search.
\item \textbf{Complex determinant zeros.}
Where do the zeros of $R(z,X_+)-R(z,X_-)$ lie beyond the critical
circle, and which cancel with its numerator? The present proof
removes possible boundary poles but does not classify farther zeros.
\item \textbf{Higher coefficients and late-order behavior.}
Can efficiently certified Taylor jets expose the sign pattern and
large-order growth of $C_j$? Fixed-order existence does not determine
convergence, optimal truncation or exponentially small corrections.
\item \textbf{Global algebraic and differential status.}
Can the orbit representation prove or disprove algebraicity or
$D$-finiteness? This requires information beyond the single dominant
square-root expansion established here.

\item \textbf{Other two-root catalytic models.}
Which other avoidance classes permit a real determinant gap and
nonnegative boundary convergence in place of a global denominator
majorant? Each model still needs proofs of formal-germ identification,
complex continuation and noncancellation.\par

\end{enumerate}

\section{Definitions and principal results}
An inversion sequence of length $n$ is an integer sequence
$e=(e_1,\ldots,e_n)$ satisfying $0\le e_i<i$. A pattern occurs when a
subsequence has the indicated order and equality relations. Avoidance
of $010$, $120$, and $210$ is equivalent to the absence of indices
$i<j<k$ for which
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:avoid}
 e_i\ne e_j,\qquad e_j>e_k,\qquad e_i\ge e_k.
\end{equation}
Indeed, if $e_i=e_k$ the pattern is $010$; otherwise the two possible
orders of the distinct first two entries give $120$ or $210$.
Let $a_n$ count these sequences, include the empty sequence by $a_0=1$,
and put
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:constants}
 F(z)=\sum_{n\ge0}a_nz^n,\qquad \rho=\frac4{27},\qquad
 \lambda=\log\frac{27}{4},\qquad p=\frac32.
\end{equation}
This is OEIS A279558 and Class 830 in Britt--Beaton~\cite{bb,oeis830}.
Its first terms are
\[
 1,1,2,5,15,52,200,830,3654,16869,80963,401300,\ldots.
\]

\begin{theorem}\label{ivk:anc:thm:main}
The generating function $F$ has radius of convergence $\rho$ and a
unique singularity on $|z|=\rho$, namely $z=\rho$. It has analytic
continuation to a $\Delta$-domain there and a convergent local expansion
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:puiseux}
 F(z)=\sum_{j\ge0}b_j\left(1-\frac z\rho\right)^{j/2},
 \qquad b_1<0.
\end{equation}
For every fixed integer $J\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:mainexp}
 a_n=\rho^{-n}n^{-3/2}
       \left(\sum_{j=0}^{J}\frac{C_j}{n^j}
                         +O_J(n^{-J-1})\right),
 \qquad C_0=C=-\frac{b_1}{2\sqrt\pi}>0.
\end{equation}
All coefficients are computable from a normally convergent orbit
representation. In particular, with $d_j=C_j/C$,
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:Cinterval}
\begin{gathered}
 C>0.0001809637754651179072704308064957871691430,\\
 C<0.0001809637754651179072704308064957871691431.
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
\begin{align}
 137.22155058455044742556&<d_1<137.22155058455044742557,\label{ivk:anc:d1interval}\\
 4914.56133466923234229799&<d_2<4914.56133466923234229800.\label{ivk:anc:d2interval}
\end{align}
\end{theorem}

Here a $\Delta$-domain can be taken to have the form
\[
 \{z:|z|<\rho+\eta,\ z\ne\rho,
                  |\arg(z-\rho)|>\theta\},
 \qquad \eta>0,\quad 0<\theta<\pi/2.
\]
Only the existence of suitable $\eta,\theta$ is needed. We do not
claim an explicit numerical threshold $n_0$ for the asymptotic
remainders. The series in \eqref{ivk:anc:puiseux} converges locally. The
infinite expansion in powers of $1/n$ represented by \eqref{ivk:anc:mainexp}
is an asymptotic expansion; its convergence is not asserted.

\begin{theorem}\label{ivk:anc:thm:inverse}
For $M>0$ define the integer threshold
$N(M)=\min\{n\ge0:a_n\ge M\}$. Put $L=\log M$ and
$A=\log C+p\log\lambda$. The smooth asymptotic inverse begins
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:inversefirst}
 \nu_1(L)=\frac1\lambda\left[
 L+p\log L-A+
 \frac{p^2\log L-pA-\lambda d_1}{L}\right].
\end{equation}
There are a constant $B>0$ and a sufficiently large $L_0$ such that,
for $L\ge L_0$, with $\eps_1(L)=B(\log L)^2/L^2$,
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:inverseceil}
 \ceil{\nu_1(L)-\eps_1(L)}\le N(e^L)
                         \le\ceil{\nu_1(L)+\eps_1(L)}.
\end{equation}
An analogous statement holds to every fixed order, with recursively
computable polynomials in $\log L$ and an error tending to zero.
If the two ceilings agree, their common value is the exact threshold.
If they do not, rounding uncertainty remains.
\end{theorem}

\section{The generating tree and the all zero correction}\label{ivk:anc:sec:tree}
For a nonempty sequence let $h$ be its maximum and $k$ its greatest
entry strictly below $h$, taking $h=k=0$ for an all-zero sequence.
Every sequence in the class ends on its maximum or its premaximum.
To see this, if its final entry $v$ were below two earlier distinct
values $h,k$, those two values, in either order, followed by $v$
would form $120$ or $210$.

Use type $S$ when the last entry is $h$, and type $T$ when the last
entry is $k<h$. There is no $T$ type among all-zero sequences.
Set
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:labels}
 a=n-h,\qquad b=h-k.
\end{equation}
The empty root is $(0,0)_S$. From either type, appending the current
maximum gives $(a+1,b)_S$; appending a new maximum $h+d$, for
$1\le d\le n-h=a$, gives $(a+1-d,d)_S$.

The possible smaller appended values depend on the type. From $S$,
a new value $v<h$ must satisfy $k<v<h$. A value below $k$ would
produce a forbidden triple using $k$ and $h$; a value equal to $k$
would produce $010$ using an earlier $k$ and the final $h$.
Conversely, when $k<v<h$, every earlier value below $h$ is strictly
below $v$, so a triple ending at $v$ cannot satisfy \eqref{ivk:anc:avoid}.
The new premaximum is $v$. From $T$, the value $k$ itself may also
be appended: repeating the final value cannot create a new forbidden
triple that was absent when this same final value was first present.
Values $k<v<h$ are again valid, and values below $k$ are not.
Thus the complete rule is
\begin{align}
 (a,b)_{S\text{ or }T}&\longrightarrow(a+1,b)_S,
                                      &&\label{ivk:anc:rule1}\\
 (a,b)_{S\text{ or }T}&\longrightarrow(a+1-d,d)_S,
                  &&1\le d\le a,\label{ivk:anc:rule2}\\
 (a,b)_S&\longrightarrow(a+1,j)_T,
                  &&1\le j\le b-1,\label{ivk:anc:rule3}\\
 (a,b)_T&\longrightarrow(a+1,j)_T,
                  &&1\le j\le b.\label{ivk:anc:rule4}
\end{align}
An empty index range means no children. In particular,
\eqref{ivk:anc:rule3} produces none when $b=0$.

Let $S(z,x,y)$ and $T(z,x,y)$ mark size by $z$ and labels $a,b$ by
$x,y$, respectively. Write
\[
 B(z,x,y)=S(z,x,y)+T(z,x,y),\quad
 H(z,x)=B(z,x,1),\quad F(z)=H(z,1).
\]
When $z$ is fixed in notation, $H(x)$ means $H(z,x)$, and similarly
for $B,S,T$. Coefficients of each power of $z$ are polynomials in
$x,y$, so the divided differences below are formal polynomial
identities.

\begin{proposition}\label{ivk:anc:prop:fe}
The generating tree gives
\begin{align}
 S&=1+zxB+\frac{zxy}{x-y}\bigl(H(x)-H(y)\bigr),\label{ivk:anc:Sfe}\\
 (1-zx)T&=\frac{zx}{1-y}\bigl(yH(x)-B\bigr)
                                      +\frac{zx}{1-zx}.\label{ivk:anc:Tfe}
\end{align}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For new maxima, the finite sum for a parent with first label $a$ is
\[
 \sum_{d=1}^a x^{a+1-d}y^d
       =\frac{xy}{x-y}(x^a-y^a),
\]
which gives \eqref{ivk:anc:Sfe}. For $T$ children, the two sums are
\[
 \sum_{j=1}^{b-1}y^j=\frac{y-y^b}{1-y}\quad(b\ge1),
 \qquad \sum_{j=1}^b y^j=\frac{y(1-y^b)}{1-y}.
\]
The first rational expression would equal $-1$ at $b=0$, whereas
the correct empty sum is zero. Exactly the all-zero sequences
have type $S$ with $b=0$, and their generating function is
\[
 S(z,x,0)=\sum_{n\ge0}(zx)^n=\frac1{1-zx}.
\]
Consequently
\[
 T=\frac{zx}{1-y}\bigl(yH(x)-S-yT\bigr)+\frac{zx}{1-zx}.
\]
Using $S+yT=B-(1-y)T$ proves \eqref{ivk:anc:Tfe}.
\end{proof}
The final term in \eqref{ivk:anc:Tfe} is essential. Omitting it changes
both the exact enumeration and the later scalar equation.

\section{A scalar equation and an algebraic anchor}\label{ivk:anc:sec:anchor}
Eliminate $T=B-S$ from \eqref{ivk:anc:Sfe}--\eqref{ivk:anc:Tfe}. After multiplication
by $1-y$, the coefficient of $B$ is
\[
 \mathcal K(z,x,y)=(1-zx)^2(1-y)+zx.
\]
Its formal root in $y$ is the rational map
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:phi}
 \ph(x)=1+\frac{zx}{(1-zx)^2}.
\end{equation}
Substitution of $y=\ph(x)$ is valid coefficientwise. The remaining
terms simplify to the one-variable affine equation
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:scalar}
 H(z,\ph(x))=c(z,x)H(z,x)+d(z,x),
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:cd}
 d(z,x)=\frac{x-\ph(x)}{zx},\qquad
 c(z,x)=1-(1-zx)d(z,x).
\end{equation}
For a general indeterminate $x$, equation \eqref{ivk:anc:scalar} is read
after clearing denominators. The apparent negative powers of $z$
cancel in that identity. Along the orbits used below they are
removable singularities; this will be proved explicitly.

Let $R(z)$ be the unique formal series satisfying
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:ternary}
 R=1+zR^3,\qquad R(0)=1.
\end{equation}
Writing $R=1+W$ and applying Lagrange inversion gives
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:Rcoeff}
 [z^n]R(z)=\frac1{2n+1}\binom{3n}{n}\quad(n\ge0).
\end{equation}
Put $X=R^2$. Since $zR^2=(R-1)/R$, direct algebra gives
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:anchoridentities}
 \ph(X)=R^2-R+1=:Y,\qquad d(z,X)=R,\qquad c(z,X)=0.
\end{equation}
For instance, $X-Y=R-1$ and $zX=(R-1)/R$ yield $d=R$;
then $1-zX=1/R$ yields $c=0$. Equation \eqref{ivk:anc:scalar} therefore
has the exact anchor
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:anchor}
 H(z,Y)=R.
\end{equation}

\subsection{The two orbit construction}
For analytic purposes, regard $z$ and $R$ as independent variables.
The upper orbit carries the anchor value:
\begin{align}
 y_0&=\ph(R^2),&v_0&=R,\notag\\
 y_{j+1}&=\ph(y_j),&v_{j+1}&=c(z,y_j)v_j+d(z,y_j).
                                                        \label{ivk:anc:upper}
\end{align}
The lower orbit starts at the desired specialization $x=1$:
\begin{align}
 x_0&=1,&p_0&=1,&q_0&=0,\notag\\
 x_{j+1}&=\ph(x_j),&p_{j+1}&=c(z,x_j)p_j,
 &q_{j+1}&=c(z,x_j)q_j+d(z,x_j).\label{ivk:anc:lower}
\end{align}
Denote the limits, when established, by
\[
 V(z,R)=\lim v_j,\qquad P(z)=\lim p_j,\qquad Q(z)=\lim q_j.
\]
Only $V$ depends on $R$. The solution will be
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:G}
 G(z,R)=\frac{V(z,R)-Q(z)}{P(z)},\qquad
 F(z)=G(z,R(z)).
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}\label{ivk:anc:lem:formal}
Equation \eqref{ivk:anc:G} holds as an identity of formal power series.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The exact difference identity is
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:difference}
 \ph(x)-\ph(y)=
 \frac{z(x-y)(1-z^2xy)}{(1-zx)^2(1-zy)^2}.
\end{equation}
For $x(0)=y(0)=1$, this increases the $z$-adic order of their
difference by at least one. Each orbit therefore converges
coefficientwise to the same formal fixed point $\alpha=1+O(z)$.
On setting $R=R(z)$, equations \eqref{ivk:anc:anchor}, \eqref{ivk:anc:scalar} give
\[
 v_j=H(z,y_j),\qquad p_jF(z)+q_j=H(z,x_j).
\]
Since every coefficient of $H(z,x)$ is a polynomial in $x$, passage
to the common formal limit is legitimate. The lower first step has
$d(z,1)=-(1-z)^{-2}$ and $c(z,1)=(2-z)/(1-z)$. All subsequent
$c$ factors have constant term 1 and their $d$ terms have constant
term zero. Thus $P(0)=2$, $Q(0)=-1$. At $R=1,z=0$, the upper
limit is 1, so $G(0,1)=1$. In particular $P$ is a formal unit and
\eqref{ivk:anc:G} follows.
\end{proof}

The finite quotient $(v_m-q_m)/p_m$ is generally not equal to $F$.
As a formal approximation on the algebraic branch, however,
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:finiteseries}
 \frac{v_m-q_m}{p_m}-F=O(z^{m+2}).
\end{equation}
Indeed $y_0-x_0=O(z)$ and \eqref{ivk:anc:difference} yields
$y_m-x_m=O(z^{m+1})$, whereas $H(z,x)=1+O(z)$ has
$\partial_xH=O(z)$. Subtract the two displayed identities in the
proof and divide by the unit $p_m$.

\section{Normal convergence and joint holomorphy}\label{ivk:anc:sec:normal}
\begin{proposition}\label{ivk:anc:prop:normal}
The functions $V(z,R)$, $P(z)$ and $Q(z)$ are holomorphic on
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:bidomain}
 |z|<\frac3{20},\qquad |R|<\frac{151}{100}.
\end{equation}
The finite orbit expressions converge normally there.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For the upper seed, $|R^2|<23/10$, and hence
\[
 |\ph(R^2)-1|
 <\frac{69/200}{(131/200)^2}<\frac{81}{100}.
\]
Both initial orbit points lie in $|x|\le181/100$. On this disk,
\begin{align}
 |\partial_x\ph(x)|
 &\le\frac{(3/20)(1+(3/20)(181/100))}
 {(1-(3/20)(181/100))^3}<\frac12,\label{ivk:anc:contraction}\\
 |\ph(x)-1|&<\frac{52}{100}.\label{ivk:anc:invariance}
\end{align}
The disk is invariant and convex, so the derivative bound gives a
uniform contraction. It yields a common holomorphic fixed point
$\alpha(z)$ and geometrically decaying consecutive differences.
All orbit arguments are nonzero: the upper initial point has
modulus greater than $19/100$, and all its subsequent points have
modulus greater than $48/100$. The lower initial point is 1 and
its subsequent points satisfy the latter bound as well. Also the
factors $1-zx$ stay away from zero.

It remains to remove the apparent $1/z$ in \eqref{ivk:anc:cd}. The lower
initial step has the explicit regular expression already given.
For an upper initial point $u=\ph(S)$, one has the regular identity
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:removable}
 d(z,u)=\frac{S/(1-zS)^2-u/(1-zu)^2}{u}.
\end{equation}
For later steps, use \eqref{ivk:anc:difference} with two consecutive orbit
points. It expresses $d(z,x_j)$ as a bounded holomorphic factor
times $x_{j-1}-x_j$, for $j\ge1$, and the same holds for the upper
orbit. Therefore both $d(z,x_j)$ and
$c(z,x_j)-1=-(1-zx_j)d(z,x_j)$ decrease geometrically, uniformly
on compact subsets of \eqref{ivk:anc:bidomain}.

For any consecutive range of factors,
\[
 \prod |c_j|\le\prod(1+|c_j-1|)
                    \le\exp\left(\sum|c_j-1|\right).
\]
These products are bounded. Iterating an affine recurrence gives
\[
 w_N=w_0\prod_{j=0}^{N-1}c_j+
             \sum_{k=0}^{N-1}d_k\prod_{j=k+1}^{N-1}c_j,
\]
so its partial values are bounded as well. Finally
$w_{j+1}-w_j=(c_j-1)w_j+d_j$ is summable uniformly on compact
subsets. This proves normal convergence of $v_j,q_j,p_j$ and
holomorphy of their limits, by the Weierstrass theorem.
No division by an individual $c_j$ is used.
\end{proof}

Since $P(0)=2$, the quotient in \eqref{ivk:anc:G} is holomorphic near
$(0,1)$. Its composition with the analytic germ $R(z)$ agrees
coefficientwise with the counting series. Thus $F$ has a positive
radius of convergence as a conclusion, without assuming a prior
exponential bound on the counts.

\section{A zero free denominator on a larger disk}\label{ivk:anc:sec:zero}
A local representation alone would not exclude a pole of $1/P$ or
another competing singularity on the critical circle. We now give
an explicit uniform exclusion.

\begin{proposition}\label{ivk:anc:prop:zero}
On the closed disk $|z|\le Z=149/1000$,
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:Pbound}
 |P(z)|>e^{-32/3}>3^{-11}.
\end{equation}
In particular $P$ has no zeros there. It is holomorphic on a
neighborhood of this disk.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $B_*=4/3$; this symbol is a radius, not the generating function
$B(z,x,y)$. Exact rational inequalities give
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:zeroineq}
 \rho<Z<\frac3{20},\qquad
 \frac{ZB_*}{(1-ZB_*)^2}<B_*-1=\frac13.
\end{equation}
Thus the lower orbit stays in $|x|\le B_*$ and, after the initial
point, satisfies $|x_j-1|<1/3$. In particular $|x_j|>2/3$.
A derivative bound on this disk is
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:LK}
 \begin{split}
 L_*&=\frac{Z(1+ZB_*)}{(1-ZB_*)^3}
       =\frac{150694875}{434163602}<\frac7{20},\\
 K_*&=\frac{1+ZB_*}{(1-Z)^2(2-B_*)}
       =\frac{1798000}{724201}<\frac52.
 \end{split}
\end{equation}
The first difference is $x_1-x_0=z/(1-z)^2$. Contraction and
\eqref{ivk:anc:cd} therefore imply, for $j\ge1$,
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:cfactors}
 |x_{j+1}-x_j|\le\frac{|z|L_*^j}{(1-Z)^2},\qquad
 |c(z,x_j)-1|\le K_*L_*^j.
\end{equation}
At zero these inequalities hold by removable continuation; indeed
\eqref{ivk:anc:difference} gives $x_{j+1}-x_j=O(z^{j+1})$.

The exact rational premises also give
\[
 K_*L_*<\frac78,\qquad
 \frac{K_*L_*}{1-L_*}<\frac43.
\]
Every factor with $j\ge1$ is therefore nonzero, and its deviation
from 1 has a summable majorant. The initial factor is
\[
 c(z,1)=\frac{2-z}{1-z},\qquad
 |c(z,1)|\ge\frac{2-Z}{1+Z}>1.
\]
Let $u_j=K_*L_*^j$. For $0\le u\le7/8$,
$\log(1-u)\ge-8u$, as follows by integrating
$(1-u)^{-1}\le8$. Hence
\[
 |P(z)|\ge |c(z,1)|\prod_{j\ge1}(1-u_j)
 >\exp\left(-8\sum_{j\ge1}u_j\right)
 >e^{-32/3}>3^{-11}.
\]
The last comparison follows from $32/3<11$ and $e<3$.
Strict inequalities leave room to increase $Z$ slightly; normal
convergence and holomorphy also follow from Proposition
\ref{ivk:anc:prop:normal}. This establishes the neighborhood assertion.
\end{proof}

For $0<z\le Z$, the lower real orbit is strictly increasing and
stays below its attracting fixed point in $[1,4/3]$. Thus $d_j<0$
and $c_j>1$, giving $P(z)>2$; at zero $P(0)=2$ exactly. This
positivity is useful as a check, but the complex disk bound
\eqref{ivk:anc:Pbound}, rather than positivity alone, is what rules out
all competing denominator poles.

\section{The dominant singularity and its local expansion}\label{ivk:anc:sec:sing}
From \eqref{ivk:anc:Rcoeff} and Stirling's formula, $R$ has radius
$\rho=4/27$, and its nonnegative coefficients give
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:Rbound}
 |R(z)|\le R(\rho)=\frac32\qquad(|z|\le\rho).
\end{equation}
The branch value $3/2$ can also be obtained by following the real
solution of $z=(R-1)/R^3$ from $R=1$ to its first stationary point.
The coefficient formula ensures convergence at $z=\rho$.

The holomorphy margins $\rho<Z<3/20$ and $3/2<151/100$, together
with \eqref{ivk:anc:Pbound}, show that \eqref{ivk:anc:G} continues the original
counting germ throughout $|z|<\rho$, continuously to its boundary.
Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:boundarysum}
 \sum_{n\ge0}a_n\rho^n=F(\rho)<\infty.
\end{equation}
For example, this follows first on the positive radius and then by
monotone convergence of the nonnegative coefficient sum.

For the algebraic equation $R-1-zR^3=0$, a finite nonzero critical
value must satisfy
\[
 R-1-zR^3=0,\qquad 1-3zR^2=0.
\]
These equations give only $R=3/2$, $z=4/27$. At every other point
of $|z|=\rho$, the implicit-function derivative is nonzero, so the
selected branch $R(z)$ continues analytically across that point.
The same then holds for $F$ by \eqref{ivk:anc:G}. Near $z=\rho$, the
algebraic root has its ordinary square-root continuation. A finite
cover of the remaining compact arcs of the critical circle, plus
that local slit neighborhood, gives a $\Delta$-domain for $F$.
This is global control of the full dominant circle, not merely a
real-axis singular expansion.

Put
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:tdef}
 t=\sqrt{1-z/\rho},
\end{equation}
with $t>0$ when $z<\rho$ is real and close to $\rho$. The selected
root has the convergent expansion
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:Rpuiseux}
 \begin{split}
 R(t)={}&\frac32-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}t+\frac23t^2
       -\frac{35\sqrt3}{108}t^3+\frac{40}{81}t^4\\
       &\hspace{24mm}-\frac{1001\sqrt3}{3888}t^5+O(t^6).
 \end{split}
\end{equation}
One way to verify both convergence and the branch choice is to set
$R=(3/2)(1-u)$. The defining equation becomes
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:uimplicit}
 t=u\sqrt{\frac{3-u}{(1-u)^3}}.
\end{equation}
The right side has nonzero derivative $\sqrt3$ at zero, so it
has a holomorphic inverse, with $u=t/\sqrt3+O(t^2)$. Expanding
this inverse gives \eqref{ivk:anc:Rpuiseux}.

Joint holomorphy of $G$ around $(\rho,3/2)$ now yields a convergent
power series
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:bseries}
 G(\rho(1-t^2),R(t))=\sum_{j\ge0}b_jt^j.
\end{equation}
The chain rule gives
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:b1C}
 b_1=-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}G_R(\rho,3/2),\qquad
 C=\frac{\sqrt3}{4\sqrt\pi}G_R(\rho,3/2).
\end{equation}
The exact certificate in Section~\ref{ivk:anc:sec:certificate} proves
$G_R(\rho,3/2)>0$. Thus $b_1\ne0$, so the singularity at $\rho$
is genuine. It follows that the actual radius of $F$ is $\rho$,
and the preceding continuation proves uniqueness on that circle.

\section{Exact amplitude and correction certificates}\label{ivk:anc:sec:certificate}
The analytic proof does not depend on floating-point fitting.
We give an explicit infinite-tail bound so finite rational interval
calculations can certify the constants in the theorems.

\subsection{A uniform complex neighborhood}
Take
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:certparams}
 h=10^{-5},\quad r=10^{-3},\quad q=\frac12,\quad
 M_*=\frac{176}{100},\quad Z=\frac{149}{1000},
\end{equation}
and work on
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:certdisk}
 |z-\rho|\le\rho h^2,\qquad |R-3/2|\le r.
\end{equation}
Here $M_*$ is a radius for orbit arguments and is unrelated to
the target size $M$ used for inversion. All inequalities in the
following derivation have exact rational premises checked by the
certificate programs.

On $|x|\le M_*$, $|z|\le Z$, the two derivative bounds are
\[
 |\partial_x\ph(x)|
 \le\frac{Z(1+ZM_*)}{(1-ZM_*)^3}<q,
 \qquad
 |\partial_z\ph(x)|
 \le\frac{M_*(1+ZM_*)}{(1-ZM_*)^3}<6.
\]
At the initial argument $R^2$, whose modulus is less than $226/100$,
the corresponding bounds are $11/10$ and $11$. Since
$|R^2-9/4|\le(3+r)r$, the upper initial point differs from its
real reference value $\phi_\rho(9/4)=7/4$ by less than
\[
 \frac{11}{10}(3+r)r+11\rho h^2<\frac1{250}.
\]
The lower initial point is exactly its reference value 1.

The real reference orbits at $(z,R)=(\rho,3/2)$ stay in
$[1,7/4]$. Comparing one complex step with a reference step gives
an error recurrence $e_{j+1}\le q e_j+6\rho h^2$, and therefore
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:referenceerror}
 e_j\le\frac1{250}q^j+12\rho h^2<\frac1{200}.
\end{equation}
This is a self-consistent induction because $7/4+1/200<M_*$.
It also gives $|x_j|,|y_j|>99/100$.

The upper reference first difference is
$7/4-589/400=111/400<28/100$; the lower one has size
$\rho/(1-\rho)^2<21/100$. Incorporating the initial perturbation
and then applying contraction gives, for either orbit $w_j$,
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:tailpremises}
 \begin{split}
 |w_{j+1}-w_j|&<\frac3{10}q^j,\\
 |d(z,w_j)|&<\frac{11}{5}q^j,\\
 |c(z,w_j)-1|&<3q^j.
 \end{split}
\end{equation}
For the second inequality use $1/|z|<7$, $1/|w_j|<100/99$;
the third follows from $1+ZM_*$. This neighborhood is away from
$z=0$, so no removable-limit issue arises in these estimates.

\subsection{An explicit infinite tail}
Every finite product of absolute $c$ factors is less than
\[
 \exp\left(3\sum_{j\ge0}q^j\right)=e^6<3^6<2^{10}=:K_0.
\]
An affine orbit with initial modulus at most 2 has all partial
values bounded by
\[
 K_0\left(2+\sum_{j\ge0}\frac{11}{5}q^j\right)<7K_0.
\]
This covers $v_j,q_j,p_j$, with zero additive term for $p_j$.
By summing their increments from $N$ onward, we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:tailbound}
 |V-v_N|, |Q-q_N|, |P-p_N|
 <2^{16}q^N=:E_N.
\end{equation}
Indeed the increment majorant is
$(3\cdot7K_0+11/5)q^j$, and its tail sum is less than
$2^{16}q^N$. In the implemented certificates
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:E}
 N=512,\qquad E=E_{512}=2^{-496}.
\end{equation}
For the $R$ derivative at the center, Cauchy's estimate on the disk
of radius $r=10^{-3}$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:derivtail}
 |V_R-(v_N)_R|<1000E.
\end{equation}
No derivative tail of $P$ or $Q$ is needed because those functions
do not depend on $R$.

\subsection{Directed arithmetic and the amplitude}
The real interval implementation uses integer endpoints on the
lattice $10^{-120}\mathbb Z$. Sums are exact on this lattice;
products and reciprocals round the lower endpoint downward and
the upper endpoint upward. Reciprocals are taken only after
checking that zero is excluded. Square roots use integer square
roots and outward rounding. The constant $\pi$ is enclosed using
Machin's identity
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:machin}
 \pi=16\arctan(1/5)-4\arctan(1/239)
\end{equation}
and the alternating-series remainder after 120 and 40 terms,
respectively. These operations have no floating-point input.
Dual-number interval arithmetic propagates first derivatives
through the same finite recurrence.

After widening finite values by \eqref{ivk:anc:tailbound} and the upper
$R$ derivative by \eqref{ivk:anc:derivtail}, the quotient \eqref{ivk:anc:G} gives
\begin{align*}
 F(\rho)&=1.22624332667254683644642931221778\ldots,\\
 P(\rho)&=3.13161052808937020027269114323476\ldots,\\
 G_R(\rho,3/2)&=0.0007407402584171465268932549321887829\ldots.
\end{align*}
In particular the certified derivative enclosure lies strictly
between $0.000740740$ and $0.000740741$. Equation \eqref{ivk:anc:b1C}
then gives \eqref{ivk:anc:Cinterval}, including its strict positivity.
The displayed long decimals here are abbreviated enclosures;
the complete interval endpoints are supplied in the companion.

\subsection{Cauchy bounds for the Puiseux coefficients}
For the first two coefficient corrections we need $b_1,b_3,b_5$.
To control the infinite-orbit error on a complex $t$ disk, write
\eqref{ivk:anc:uimplicit} as
\[
 u=t\psi(u),\qquad
 \psi(u)=\sqrt{\frac{(1-u)^3}{3-u}}.
\]
On $|u|=1/100$, $|\psi(u)|<3/5$. Lagrange inversion and Cauchy's
coefficient estimate give
\[
 |[t^k]u(t)|\le\frac1k\left(\frac35\right)^k100^{k-1}
       \le\frac35\,60^{k-1}.
\]
Thus $u(t)$ converges for $|t|<1/60$, and
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:ubound}
 |u(t)|\le\frac{(3/5)|t|}{1-60|t|}.
\end{equation}
For $|t|\le h$, this implies $|R(t)-3/2|<h<r$, while
$|z-\rho|\le\rho h^2$. Hence \eqref{ivk:anc:tailbound} applies throughout
that disk.

Complex rectangular interval arithmetic encloses the finite
quotient on the larger boxes containing this disk. The exact
checks establish
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:finitequotient}
 \Ree p_N>3,\qquad |v_N-q_N|<5.
\end{equation}
The rectangles are used only to bound these finite expressions;
the infinite-tail theorem is used on the actual $t$ disk above.
Since each of the three tails is at most $E<1/2$,
\[
 \left|\frac{V-Q}{P}-\frac{v_N-q_N}{p_N}\right|
 \le\frac{2E}{3-E}+\frac{5E}{3(3-E)}<2E.
\]
Cauchy's coefficient estimate therefore bounds the error in each
Taylor coefficient of the finite quotient by
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:bjtails}
 |b_j-[t^j](v_N-q_N)/p_N|<2Eh^{-j}.
\end{equation}
Interval Taylor jets through degree five compute the finite
coefficients; their widths are then enlarged by \eqref{ivk:anc:bjtails}.
For orientation, the resulting values are
\begin{align*}
 b_1&=-0.00064149988139509874897893846423296835\ldots,\\
 b_3&=\phantom{-}0.05852469731287832893335588719712865501\ldots,\\
 b_5&=-0.79679053577631879378776442471360871456\ldots.
\end{align*}
Section~\ref{ivk:anc:sec:transfer} derives the formulas converting these
coefficients to $d_1,d_2$, and the exact intervals prove
\eqref{ivk:anc:d1interval}--\eqref{ivk:anc:d2interval}.

\section{Transfer to every fixed order}\label{ivk:anc:sec:transfer}
For nonintegral $\alpha$, the exact coefficient identity is
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:gammaexact}
 [z^n](1-z/\rho)^\alpha
 =\rho^{-n}\frac{\Gamma(n-\alpha)}{\Gamma(-\alpha)\Gamma(n+1)}.
\end{equation}
Define universal polynomials $g_k(\alpha)$ by the gamma-ratio
asymptotic expansion
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:gratio}
 \frac{\Gamma(n-\alpha)}{\Gamma(n+1)}
 \sim n^{-\alpha-1}\sum_{k\ge0}g_k(\alpha)n^{-k}.
\end{equation}
For example,
\[
 g_0=1,\quad g_1=\frac{\alpha(\alpha+1)}2,\quad
 g_2=\frac{\alpha(\alpha+1)(\alpha+2)(3\alpha+1)}{24}.
\]
The gamma-ratio expansion, with a remainder after each fixed
number of terms, follows from Stirling's expansion with remainder
applied to the two gamma functions and exponentiation. It is
not an assertion of convergence of the infinite series.

\begin{proposition}\label{ivk:anc:prop:transfer}
For every fixed $J\ge0$, equation \eqref{ivk:anc:mainexp} holds with
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:Cjformula}
 C_j=\sum_{m=0}^j
 \frac{b_{2m+1}}{\Gamma(-m-1/2)}
                  g_{j-m}(m+1/2),\qquad 0\le j\le J.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Truncate the convergent local expansion \eqref{ivk:anc:bseries} after
degree $2J+2$. Its remainder is $O(t^{2J+3})$, equivalently
$O((1-z/\rho)^{J+3/2})$, uniformly in a smaller sector at the
singularity. The even powers of $t$ in this finite truncation
are polynomials in $z$. Their coefficients vanish once $n$ is
larger than their degrees. For the odd powers use
\eqref{ivk:anc:gammaexact}, and then \eqref{ivk:anc:gratio} to the order needed.
The term $b_{2m+1}$ begins at the scale
$\rho^{-n}n^{-m-3/2}$, so collection at relative order $n^{-j}$
gives precisely \eqref{ivk:anc:Cjformula}.

The $\Delta$-domain established in Section~\ref{ivk:anc:sec:sing} permits
the standard singularity-analysis remainder transfer: a remainder
$O((1-z/\rho)^\beta)$ with this nonintegral $\beta$ contributes
$O(\rho^{-n}n^{-\beta-1})$ here. With
$\beta=J+3/2$, this is
$O(\rho^{-n}n^{-J-5/2})$, exactly the remainder in
\eqref{ivk:anc:mainexp}. One can see the scale directly by deforming
Cauchy's coefficient contour to two rays around the cut and a
circle of radius $1/n$ around $\rho$: set
$z=\rho(1+w/n)$, so the remainder contributes $n^{-\beta}$,
the differential $dz$ contributes $n^{-1}$, and
$z^{-n-1}$ provides an exponentially decaying factor along the
rays. The contour away from the singularity is exponentially
smaller. The standard $\Delta$-transfer theorem formalizes this
argument; see Flajolet--Sedgewick~\cite[Chapter VI]{fs}.
\end{proof}

For the leading term,
$\Gamma(-1/2)=-2\sqrt\pi$, which gives \eqref{ivk:anc:b1C}.
Using $\Gamma(-1/2)/\Gamma(-3/2)=-3/2$ and
$\Gamma(-1/2)/\Gamma(-5/2)=15/4$, the next coefficients reduce to
\begin{align}
 d_1&=\frac38-\frac32\frac{b_3}{b_1},\label{ivk:anc:d1formula}\\
 d_2&=\frac{25}{128}-\frac{45}{16}\frac{b_3}{b_1}
                                  +\frac{15}{4}\frac{b_5}{b_1}.
                                                        \label{ivk:anc:d2formula}
\end{align}
These are the formulas used by the certificates. The corrections
are large: at $n=100$, even $d_1/n$ exceeds 1. This explains why
moderate-size data can make direct extraction of the very small
leading amplitude delicate. It does not, by itself, provide an
error estimate at $n=100$ or justify truncating there.

Every $b_j$ is a derivative of the locally holomorphic composition
\eqref{ivk:anc:bseries}. Finite jets of the orbits plus a Cauchy tail
estimate analogous to \eqref{ivk:anc:bjtails} give an algorithm for any
fixed coefficient and any prescribed rational accuracy. Higher
orders may require a larger orbit length and lattice precision.
Only the stated first coefficients are numerically certified in
this package; the all-order result is a theorem with an algorithm,
not a claim to have run certificates at every order.

\section{All order inversion and integer thresholds}\label{ivk:anc:sec:inverse}
We now justify Theorem~\ref{ivk:anc:thm:inverse} without pretending that
$a_n$ is a specified continuous function of $n$.

\subsection{Monotonicity and the logarithmic expansion}
Appending the current maximum to a valid sequence produces a
valid sequence of length $n+1$: no earlier entry exceeds the
appended maximum, so a new forbidden triple cannot occur.
Deletion of that final entry recovers the original object.
This is an injection and proves $a_{n+1}\ge a_n$ (including the
empty-sequence step). Moreover \eqref{ivk:anc:mainexp} implies
\[
 \frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\longrightarrow\frac{27}{4}>1,
\]
so the counts are eventually strictly increasing and unbounded.
The threshold $N(M)$ is consequently well-defined for every
positive target $M$.

Parts~\ref{ivk:orb:part} and~\ref{ivk:two:part} prove strict increase for every $n\ge1$ by exhibiting an object outside the image of their injection; the same can be done here: Remark~\ref{ivk:anc:rem:strict} at the end of this section.
\end{writenote}


For each fixed $K$, taking the logarithm of \eqref{ivk:anc:mainexp} gives
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:logasym}
 \log a_n=\lambda n-p\log n+\log C
          +\sum_{k=1}^K\frac{\ell_k}{n^k}+O_K(n^{-K-1}),
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:ellcoeff}
 \log\left(1+\sum_{j\ge1}d_jw^j\right)
       =\sum_{k\ge1}\ell_kw^k
\end{equation}
is a formal identity. In particular,
$\ell_1=d_1$ and $\ell_2=d_2-d_1^2/2$.
Set $y=\lambda n$, $L=\log M$, and
$A=\log C+p\log\lambda$. The smooth asymptotic equation reads
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:yequation}
 L=y-p\log y+A+
       \sum_{k\ge1}\ell_k\lambda^ky^{-k}.
\end{equation}
This is an equation for asymptotic series, to be truncated at a
fixed order with a controlled remainder.

\subsection{The inverse polynomials}
There are unique polynomials $P_j$ such that the formal inverse
has the form
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:inverseall}
 y=L+p\log L-A+
            \sum_{j\ge1}\frac{P_j(\log L)}{L^j}.
\end{equation}
To construct them, substitute the partial expression into the
right side of \eqref{ivk:anc:yequation}, expand the logarithm and
reciprocals in $L^{-1}$, and cancel successive powers. At order
$L^{-j}$ the new unknown $P_j$ appears with coefficient 1, while
all other terms are already determined. This proves both existence
and uniqueness recursively. The degree of $P_j$ is at most $j$.

Write $q_L=p\log L-A$. The first two polynomials are
\begin{align}
 P_1(\log L)&=pq_L-\lambda d_1,\label{ivk:anc:P1}\\
 P_2(\log L)&=pP_1(\log L)-\frac p2q_L^2
           +\lambda d_1q_L-\lambda^2(d_2-d_1^2/2).
                                                        \label{ivk:anc:P2}
\end{align}
For example, substitute $y=L+q_L+P_1/L+P_2/L^2$ and use
\[
 \log y=\log L+\frac{q_L}{L}
                   +\frac{P_1-q_L^2/2}{L^2}
                   +O\!\left(\frac{(\log L)^3}{L^3}\right).
\]
Together with $y^{-1}=L^{-1}-q_LL^{-2}+O((\log L)^2L^{-3})$
and $y^{-2}=L^{-2}+O((\log L)L^{-3})$, this yields
\eqref{ivk:anc:P1}--\eqref{ivk:anc:P2}. Dividing \eqref{ivk:anc:inverseall} by $\lambda$
and retaining $P_1$ gives \eqref{ivk:anc:inversefirst}.

\subsection{A rigorous discrete localization}
For $J\ge0$ let
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:nuJ}
 \nu_J(L)=\frac1\lambda\left[
 L+p\log L-A+\sum_{j=1}^J\frac{P_j(\log L)}{L^j}\right].
\end{equation}
In particular, even the $J=0$ truncation retains the constant
$-A$. Keeping only $L/\lambda$, or omitting another nonvanishing
term, would not justify an error interval tending to zero.

\begin{proposition}\label{ivk:anc:prop:ceil}
For every fixed $J\ge0$ there exist $B_J>0$ and $L_J$ such that,
for all $L\ge L_J$, setting
\[
 \eps_J(L)=B_J\frac{(1+\log L)^{J+1}}{L^{J+1}},
\]
one has
\begin{equation}\label{ivk:anc:ceilall}
 \ceil{\nu_J(L)-\eps_J(L)}\le N(e^L)
                    \le\ceil{\nu_J(L)+\eps_J(L)}.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Choose $K\ge J+1$ in \eqref{ivk:anc:logasym}, and let
\[
 g_K(x)=\lambda x-p\log x+\log C+
                   \sum_{k=1}^K\ell_kx^{-k},\qquad x>0.
\]
There are $B>0,n_0$ such that for integers $n\ge n_0$,
\[
 g_K(n)-Bn^{-K-1}\le\log a_n
                  \le g_K(n)+Bn^{-K-1}.
\]
Both bounding smooth functions $g_-(x)=g_K(x)-Bx^{-K-1}$ and
$g_+(x)=g_K(x)+Bx^{-K-1}$ are strictly increasing for sufficiently
large $x$, because their derivatives tend to $\lambda>0$.
For large $L$, let their unique large inverse roots be
$x_-(L)=g_-^{-1}(L)$ and $x_+(L)=g_+^{-1}(L)$; the ordering is
$x_+(L)\le x_-(L)$. If an integer $n<x_+(L)$, its upper bound is
less than $L$, so $a_n<e^L$. If $n\ge x_-(L)$, its lower bound is
at least $L$, so $a_n\ge e^L$. Early integers are irrelevant
once $L$ exceeds their finitely many logarithmic counts. Therefore
\[
 \ceil{x_+(L)}\le N(e^L)\le\ceil{x_-(L)}.
\]
The substitution construction of \eqref{ivk:anc:inverseall} gives a
residual at $\nu_J(L)$ of
$O((1+\log L)^{J+1}/L^{J+1})$ in each smooth equation.
Their derivatives are bounded below by, say, $\lambda/2$ there.
The mean value theorem thus places both inverse roots within
$\eps_J(L)$ of $\nu_J(L)$ after increasing $B_J$. Taking ceilings
proves the result.
\end{proof}

The constants $B_J,L_J$ are existence constants here. The exact
coefficient intervals do not automatically make these threshold
bounds numerically effective at a specified moderate $M$.
An explicit certified value of $N(M)$ would require either a
quantified asymptotic remainder and threshold or exact enumeration
near the candidate. The theorem nevertheless gives rigorous
all-order localization as $M\to\infty$, and in particular
$N(M)=\nu_1(\log M)+O(1)$.

\begin{remark}[\wtag, 5 October 2026]\label{ivk:anc:rem:strict}
For every $n\ge1$, $a_{n+1}\ge a_n+1$; in particular $a_{n+1}>a_n$ for $n\ge1$, while $a_1=a_0=1$.
\end{remark}
\begin{proof}
Let $n\ge1$. For $e$ in the class of length $n$, with maximum $h$, appending $h$ gives a sequence in the class (Section~\ref{ivk:anc:sec:inverse}: a forbidden triple ending at the new entry would need an earlier entry $e_j>h$), and deleting the last entry recovers $e$. So appending the current maximum is an injection from length $n$ to length $n+1$, and every sequence in its image has last entry equal to the maximum of its first $n$ entries. Now consider $f=(0,\ldots,0,n)$ of length $n+1$. It is an inversion sequence, since $n<n+1$. It avoids \eqref{ivk:anc:avoid}: a triple $i<j<k$ needs $e_j>e_k$, but $e_j=0$ for every $j\le n$, and $e_k\ge0$. Its last entry $n\ge1$ differs from the maximum $0$ of its first $n$ entries, so $f$ is not in the image. Hence $a_{n+1}\ge a_n+1$. (A brute-force count at the write gives $1,1,2,5,15,52,200,830,3654,16869,80963$ for $n=0,\ldots,10$, the displayed terms of A279558.)
\end{proof}

\section{Further questions and limitations}\label{ivk:anc:sec:questions}
The orbit solution settles the leading equivalent and the regular
all-fixed-order scale for this class. Several natural questions
remain.
\begin{enumerate}
 \item \textbf{Effective remainder constants.} The present argument
 gives $O_J$ remainders and existence thresholds. Explicit contour
 bounds could turn them into numerical inequalities for all
 $n\ge n_0(J)$, making the inverse thresholds directly executable.
 \item \textbf{Higher correction coefficients.} The jet and Cauchy
 method computes any prescribed fixed order. An efficient
 implementation could optimize the choice of disk radius, orbit
 length and lattice precision for large orders.
 \item \textbf{The large correction regime.} The first relative
 corrections are large despite the small leading amplitude.
 Quantifying optimal truncation or obtaining uniform finite-$n$
 bounds would explain the crossover more sharply than leading
 asymptotics alone.
 \item \textbf{Global analytic structure.} The disk $|z|\le149/1000$
 suffices for dominant-circle control. It does not describe all
 farther singularities, zeros of $P$, or the maximal analytic
 continuation of the orbit quotient.
 \item \textbf{Algebraicity and D-finiteness.} A representation using
 an algebraic anchor and infinite rational orbits does not decide
 either property. No conclusion about them is drawn here.
 \item \textbf{Refined statistics.} The catalytic variables retain
 maximum and premaximum information. Extending the orbit analysis
 to marked observables may yield distributional results, but those
 are not proved by the univariate argument in this report.
\end{enumerate}

The numerical amplitude was known before this proof, and the
published source already proposed the regular scale. The proof,
all-order transfer and honest discrete inversion are the objects
to assess. A limited source and repository search found no matching
proof during preparation; it is not a comprehensive priority
certification. No publication or external repository modification
is part of the accompanying reproducibility work.
