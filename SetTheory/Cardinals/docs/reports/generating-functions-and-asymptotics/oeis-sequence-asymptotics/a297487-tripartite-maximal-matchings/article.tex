\begin{abstract}
Let $A_n$ be the number of maximal matchings of the complete tripartite graph $K_{n,n,n}$ with labelled vertices (OEIS A297487, with $A_0=1$). This report prints two manuscripts of one research session. Part~I proves an expansion of the marked counts to every fixed algebraic order, uniformly for the marking variable in compact subsets of $(0,\infty)$, with a finite coefficient algorithm and four explicit correction polynomials; its leading term is V\'aclav Kot\v{e}\v{s}ovec's OEIS equivalent $A_n\sim3\sqrt2\,(2n/e)^{3n/2}e^{\sqrt{n/2}-3/8}$, credited as such. It shows that the number of unmatched vertices of a uniformly chosen maximal matching is within $O(n^{-1/4})$ in total variation of a parity-conditioned Poisson law of mean $\sqrt{n/2}$, with refined mean and variance, a central limit theorem and a local law; it expands the exponentially small probability that a uniform maximal matching is maximum to every fixed relative order; and it brackets the threshold $\min\{n:A_n\ge y\}$ between two ceilings, with a Lambert-$W$ approximation of the smooth inverse. Part~II passes to unequal parts. For compact sets of strictly triangular part proportions it compares the three unmatched-part branches to leading order, with a softmax law for the unmatched part near balance. For $K_{2n+d,n,n}$, across the boundary of the triangle inequality, it proves an all-fixed-orders expansion in powers of $n^{-1/3}$, uniform while $d/n^{2/3}$ stays in a compact interval, with exponential suppression of the other branches, Gaussian laws for the unmatched count on the scale $n^{1/3}$, four explicit corrections for the boundary counts $M(2n,n,n)$ (a sequence with no OEIS entry), a two-ceiling threshold bracket, and, for $d/n$ in a compact subset of $(0,\infty)$, an all-orders Poisson expansion. Part~II settles one instance of the unequal-parts half of Part~I's last question; the case of more parts, uniformity between the regimes and unequal small parts near a degenerating triangle remain open. The write checks the 100 b-file terms of A297487 against Part~I's exact counts, recomputes both printed prefixes from the generating function, places the inverses relative to the repository's transseries volume, and collects the open questions. All error constants and onsets are existential. The report is unrefereed, and nothing in it is formalized.
\end{abstract}

\section*{The OEIS entry, and the boundary sequence}\phantomsection\label{tmm:sec:oeis}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{The OEIS entry, and the boundary sequence}
The write read \textbf{A297487} \cite{oeis} on 6~October 2026 (revision \#18 of 6~February 2026). Its name is ``Number of maximal matchings in the complete tripartite graph K\_\{n,n,n\}.'' The author is Eric W.~Weisstein (30~December 2017); the terms from $a(6)$ on (30~December 2017), the PARI program (dated 30~December 2017) and the b-file ``Table of n, a(n) for n = 1..100'' are Andrew Howroyd's; the offset is~1, so the entry starts at $A_1=3$ and the Parts add $A_0=1$. On 6~February 2026 V\'aclav Kot\v{e}\v{s}ovec added an order-five recurrence with polynomial coefficients and the equivalent
\begin{center}
\verb|a(n) ~ 3 * 2^((3*n+1)/2) * exp(sqrt(n/2) - 3*n/2 - 3/8) * n^(3*n/2)|,
\end{center}
which is Part~I's \eqref{tmm:bal:eq:known}, since $2^{(3n+1)/2}n^{3n/2}e^{-3n/2}=\sqrt2\,(2n/e)^{3n/2}$. The entry's Mathematica line, which carries no name, is the ``hypergeometric expression'' of Part~I's Section~\ref{tmm:bal:sec:convention}:
\begin{quote}
\raggedright\texttt{3 n! HypergeometricPFQ[\{(1 - n)/2, -n, -n/2\}, \{1\}, -4] - If[Mod[n, 2] == 0, 2 (n!/(n/2)!)\^{}3, 0]}.
\end{quote}
Howroyd's program computes
\[
 [2\mid n]\Bigl(\binom n{n/2}(n/2)!\Bigr)^3+\sum_{0\le k\le(n-1)/2}3\binom nk^2\binom n{2k}\binom{2k}k\,k!^2(n-k)!.
\]
\wtag\ This is \eqref{tmm:bal:eq:Amark} at $v=1$ term by term: the bracket is $(n!/(n/2)!)^3=B_n$, and the $k$-th summand is $3(n!)^3/\bigl((n-k)!\,k!^2\,(n-2k)!\bigr)=3t(n,n-2k)$ by \eqref{tmm:bal:eq:t}, so $k$ is the number of edges joining the unmatched part to each of the other two parts (Part~I's $a=b=(n-K)/2$ for $K=n-2k$ unmatched vertices), and $0\le k\le(n-1)/2$ is exactly $K\ge1$. Hence Howroyd's sum is $[2\mid n]B_n+3\sum_{K\ge1}t(n,K)=3S_n(1)-2[2\mid n]B_n$, which is Part~I's ``positive finite sum'' and its Section~\ref{tmm:bal:sec:exact}.

\emph{The b-file.} Part~I checked the 16 displayed terms and says that the b-file was not retrieved. The write retrieved it (100 terms) and compared it with the exact counts $n=0,\dots,100$ of Part~I's certificate (\file{data/176-balanced-data-certificates.json}): all 100 terms agree, and Howroyd's formula, evaluated independently, gives the same numbers.

\emph{The boundary sequence.} Part~II's $A_n=M(2n,n,n)$, beginning $1$, $3$, $74$, $4506$, $489240$, $82306920$, \dots, is \emph{not} A297487 (Part~II says so), and Part~II found no OEIS entry for it. The write's OEIS searches of 6~October 2026 for \texttt{74,4506,489240,82306920} and for \texttt{4506,489240,82306920,19743705360} returned ``No results''. So the sequence has no OEIS entry as far as the write can see; that is evidence, not a proof of absence. Nothing was submitted to the OEIS.

\section*{Part~II and the question of Part~I}\phantomsection\label{tmm:sec:sequel}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Part~II and the question of Part~I}
Part~I's last question (Section~\ref{tmm:bal:sec:questions}, item~5) reads: ``\textbf{Unequal parts and more parts.} For $K_{a,b,c}$ or a growing number of parts, identify the feasibility boundaries and competing unmatched-part contributions. Symmetry is central to the simple scalar gamma ratio used here.'' For part sizes $p_1,p_2,p_3$, Part~II's branch formula \eqref{tmm:bd:eq:branch} shows which unmatched part is possible: with $k$ vertices unmatched in part~$i$ the three edge populations are $a_i+k/2$, $a_j-k/2$, $a_\ell-k/2$, $a_i=(p_j+p_\ell-p_i)/2$, so a branch other than $i$ is feasible only if $a_i\ge0$. When $p_i>p_j+p_\ell$ only branch~$i$ survives; inside the triangle all three compete. The \emph{feasibility boundaries} are therefore the faces $p_i=p_j+p_\ell$ of the triangle inequality.

Part~II's object, $K_{2n+d,n,n}$, is literally a $K_{a,b,c}$ (with $a=2n+d$, $b=c=n$), and $d=0$ is the face $a=b+c$. Part~II therefore \emph{settles one instance of the unequal-parts half} of the question: for this isosceles family it identifies the boundary behaviour to every fixed order in the window $d/n^{2/3}$ compact (Theorem~\ref{tmm:bd:thm:window}), proves that the competing branches are exponentially suppressed there (\eqref{tmm:bd:eq:suppress}), and gives the exterior law for $d/n$ compact in $(0,\infty)$ (Theorem~\ref{tmm:bd:thm:exterior}). It also \emph{advances} the general three-part case: for any compact set of strictly triangular proportions, Theorem~\ref{tmm:bd:thm:strict} identifies the competing contributions to leading order (relative error $O(n^{-1/2})$, not all orders), and Corollary~\ref{tmm:bd:cor:softmax} gives the softmax law for the unmatched part near balance. Open, and recorded in Section~\ref{tmm:sec:further}: the \emph{more parts} half (item~\ref{tmm:q:unequal}); faces with unequal small parts, $p_1=p_2+p_3$ with $p_2\ne p_3$, and the regime where several branches stay comparable near a degenerating triangle (item~\ref{tmm:q:degenerate}, Part~II's own question~4); a theorem uniform as $d/n^{2/3}\to\pm\infty$ joining the three regimes (item~\ref{tmm:q:overlap}); and higher orders in the strict triangle.

\section*{The inverses and the transseries volume}\phantomsection\label{tmm:sec:inverses}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{The inverses and the transseries volume}
The repository's transseries volume \cite{TSvol} has a Part~0 apparatus for inverting rapidly growing functions. Its symbols are subscripted ``vol'' here where they meet this report's letters ($\kappa_\vol$, $d_\vol$, $v_\vol$, $X_\vol$, $L_\vol$, $\Phi_\vol$, $\varrho_\vol$): Part~II's $d$ is the imbalance and its $\kappa_r$ are coefficients, and $v$ is a marking variable in Part~I and a variance in Part~II. Statement by statement \wtag:
\begin{itemize}
\item Part~I, the initializer of Theorem~\ref{tmm:bal:thm:W}: $x_0=2\ell/(3w)$ with $w=W(4\ell/(3e))$ and $\ell=\log y$, the root of $H(x)=(3x/2)\log(2x/e)=\ell$. An \emph{instance} of the factorial core \file{p0:prop:factorial-core} with $\kappa_\vol=\frac32$, $d_\vol=\frac32(\log2-1)$ and $L_\vol=\ell>0$, since $H(X)=X(\kappa_\vol\log X+d_\vol)$. The proposition gives $v_\vol=W_0\bigl((2\ell/3)e^{\log2-1}\bigr)=W_0(4\ell/(3e))=w$ and $X_\vol=e^{v_\vol-d_\vol/\kappa_\vol}=e^{w+1}/2$; as $we^w=4\ell/(3e)$, $e^{w+1}/2=2\ell/(3w)=x_0$. Its side condition is $\kappa_\vol\log X_\vol+d_\vol=\frac32w>0$, and its slope formula gives $H'(x_0)=\kappa_\vol(v_\vol+1)=\frac32(w+1)$, which is Part~I's $D$.
\item Part~I, the corrections of \eqref{tmm:bal:eq:inverseW}. Part~I's proof inverts $H+g$, $g(x)=\sqrt{x/2}+C$, by Taylor expansion and finds $\delta=-g/D+g'g/D^2-H''g^2/(2D^3)+\cdots$. These three terms are exactly the terms $m=1$ and $m=2$ of the perturbation-operator series \file{p0:eq:operator-series} (\file{p0:prop:operator-form}) with $\Phi_\vol=H$, $\varrho_\vol=g$, $\varepsilon=1$ and $X=x_0$: the term $m=1$ is $-g/H'$, and the term $m=2$ is $\frac12\,(H')^{-1}\frac{d}{dX}\bigl[g^2/H'\bigr]=gg'/(H')^2-g^2H''/(2(H')^3)$. The proposition is a formal series; the error $O_J(1/(\sqrt{x_0}D))$ and the localization are Part~I's own. An \emph{analogue}, not an instance of a theorem of the volume.
\item Part~II, the initializer of Section~\ref{tmm:bd:sec:inverse}: $x_0=\log y/(2W(\log y/e))$, the root of $2x\log(2x/e)=\log y$. An \emph{instance} of \file{p0:prop:factorial-core} with $\kappa_\vol=2$, $d_\vol=2(\log2-1)$, $L_\vol=\log y$: $v_\vol=W_0(\log y/e)$, $X_\vol=e^{v_\vol+1}/2=\log y/(2v_\vol)=x_0$, and the slope $\kappa_\vol(v_\vol+1)=2\log(2x_0)$ is the ``leading derivative'' Part~II names.
\item Part~II, the leading displacement $x_M-x_0\sim-3ax_0^{2/3}/(2\log(2x_0))$: the term $m=1$ of \file{p0:eq:operator-series} about the same core with $\varrho_\vol(x)=3ax^{2/3}$, as an asymptotic statement proved in Part~II. An \emph{analogue}. Part~II says that it is ``only a leading displacement, not a complete ordered reversion series''.
\item Part~I's Theorem~\ref{tmm:bal:thm:enclosure} and Part~II's Theorem~\ref{tmm:bd:thm:inverse}, the two-ceiling brackets: each is proved directly at the integers, from the monotonicity of the sequence, a smooth model ($F_J$, resp.\ $B_M$) and the relative error at integer arguments, without an interpolation of the sequence. They are \emph{not} instances of \file{p0:thm:staircase}. When the two ceilings agree they determine the threshold, the conclusion of item~(2) of that theorem; both Parts' warnings that a bare ceiling of the smooth root is not proved describe the failure mode of its items~(2) and~(5).
\end{itemize}
Neither Part claims novelty for the inversion mechanics.

\section{Counting convention and attribution}\label{tmm:bal:sec:convention}

It is \emph{maximal} if no edge can be added, and \emph{maximum} if its cardinality is largest possible. These are different conditions. We count matchings without identifying graph automorphisms. Set $A_0=1$ for the empty graph; asymptotic assertions concern positive integers $n\to\infty$.

\section{Exact enumeration and the marked polynomial}\label{tmm:bal:sec:exact}
\begin{lemma}\label{tmm:bal:lem:maximal}
A matching of a complete multipartite graph is maximal if and only if all its unmatched vertices lie in at most one part.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Two unmatched vertices in different parts could be joined by an edge. Conversely, if all unmatched vertices lie in one part, there is no edge between them and no edge can be added to the matching.
\end{proof}

Suppose that $k$ vertices in a distinguished part are unmatched. If $a,b,c$ are the numbers of edges between the three pairs of parts, then
\[
 a+b=n-k,\quad a+c=n,\quad b+c=n.
\]
Thus $a=b=(n-k)/2$, $c=(n+k)/2$. Admissible values of $k$ are exactly
\[
 0\le k\le n,\qquad k\equiv n\pmod2.
\]
Partition the vertices of each part according to their destinations, and then bijectively pair the classes assigned to each edge type. The resulting count is
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bal:eq:t}
 t(n,k)=\frac{(n!)^3}{k!\,((n-k)/2)!^2\,((n+k)/2)!}.
\end{equation}
For $k>0$ the unmatched part is unique. At $k=0$ the three choices represent the same perfect matching. Define
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bal:eq:S}
 S_n(v)=\sum_{\substack{0\le k\le n\\k\equiv n\ (2)}}t(n,k)v^k.
\end{equation}
If $A_n(v)$ counts maximal matchings with weight $v^k$ for $k$ unmatched vertices, then
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bal:eq:Amark}
 A_n(v)=3S_n(v)-2\one_{2\mid n}B_n,\qquad A_n=A_n(1).
\end{equation}
Here $B_n=t(n,0)$ when $n$ is even; for odd $n$ it is only a useful positive normalization. Formula~\eqref{tmm:bal:eq:Amark} also gives $A_0(v)=1$ directly, before introducing $\lambda$.

For part sizes $a,b,c$, let $M_{a,b,c}(v)$ be the analogous marked count. The multivariate exponential generating function is
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bal:eq:egf}
 \sum_{a,b,c\ge0}M_{a,b,c}(v)\frac{x^ay^bz^c}{a!b!c!}
  =(e^{vx}+e^{vy}+e^{vz}-2)e^{xy+xz+yz}.
\end{equation}
The first factor chooses the unmatched set in one part and counts its empty choice once; the second is a labeled set of cross-part edges. In particular $A_n(v)=(n!)^3[x^ny^nz^n]$ of the right side.

The first values for $n=0,\ldots,8$ are
\[
 1,\ 3,\ 14,\ 342,\ 5256,\ 252360,\ 7950960,\ 582346800,\ 30400755840.
\]

\section{A globally bounded Poisson reduction}\label{tmm:bal:sec:poisson}
Set $x=\lambda^2=n/2$ and define, for integers $0\le k\le n$,
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bal:eq:R}
 R_n(k)=\frac{\Gamma(x+1)^3}
 {\Gamma(x-k/2+1)^2\Gamma(x+k/2+1)\lambda^k}.
\end{equation}
Then $t(n,k)=B_n\lambda^kR_n(k)/k!$ on the admissible parity class.

\begin{lemma}\label{tmm:bal:lem:bound}
For $n\ge1$ and every integer $0\le k\le n$,
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bal:eq:globalbound}
 0<R_n(k)\le e^2.
\end{equation}
Consequently, for $P\sim\Poi(v\lambda)$ and real $v>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bal:eq:poisson}
 \frac{S_n(v)}{B_ne^{v\lambda}}
  =\E\left[R_n(P)\one_{P\le n}\one_{P\equiv n\ (2)}\right].
\end{equation}
The product with the cutoff is understood as zero when $P>n$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Write $\psi=(\log\Gamma)'$. Convexity of $\log\Gamma$ and the functional equation imply
\[
 \psi(t)\le\log t\le\psi(t+1)\le\log(t+1)\quad(t>0).
\]
With $a=k/2$,
\begin{align*}
 \log\frac{\Gamma(x+1)}{\Gamma(x-a+1)}
 &=\int_{x-a}^x\psi(t+1)\,dt\le a\log(x+1),\\
 \log\frac{\Gamma(x+1)}{\Gamma(x+a+1)}
 &=-\int_x^{x+a}\psi(t+1)\,dt\le-a\log x.
\end{align*}
The first integral is still valid when $x-a=0$, since $\psi(t+1)$ is continuous there. Twice the first bound plus the second, minus $a\log x$, gives
\[
 \log R_n(k)\le k\log(1+1/x)\le k/x\le2.
\]
Positivity follows from the positive gamma arguments. Equation~\eqref{tmm:bal:eq:poisson} is the exact Poisson probability formula.
\end{proof}

Fix a marking compact $K=[a,b]\subset(0,\infty)$ and the larger interval
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bal:eq:compact}
 K^*=[a/2,2b].
\end{equation}
The positive margins are important. Uniform Poisson Chernoff bounds give
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bal:eq:tail}
 \Pp(P/\lambda\notin K^*)=O_K(e^{-c_K\lambda})
\end{equation}
for some $c_K>0$, uniformly in $v\in K$. A fixed polynomial in $P/\lambda$ can be inserted without changing the exponential nature of this bound. Also $2b\lambda<n=2\lambda^2$ eventually. Lemma~\ref{tmm:bal:lem:bound} therefore controls both tails and the hard cutoff in the following argument.

\section{Uniform gamma expansions and the coefficient algorithm}\label{tmm:bal:sec:gamma}
On $K^*$ write $z=k/\lambda$ and $D(h,z)=\log R_n(k)$. For each fixed $J$, Stirling's expansion gives
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bal:eq:D}
 D(h,z)=-\frac{3z^2}{8}+\sum_{j=1}^Jd_j(z)h^j+O_{K,J}(h^{J+1}),
\end{equation}
uniformly with any prescribed finite number of $z$ derivatives. The first four polynomials are
\begin{align}
 d_1(z)&=\frac z4-\frac{z^3}{48},&
 d_2(z)&=\frac{3z^2}{16}-\frac{z^4}{64},\label{tmm:bal:eq:d12}\\
 d_3(z)&=-\frac z{24}+\frac{z^3}{48}-\frac{z^5}{640},&
 d_4(z)&=-\frac{z^2}{16}+\frac{3z^4}{128}-\frac{z^6}{640}.\label{tmm:bal:eq:d34}
\end{align}

For clarity, a finite procedure defines all further $d_j$. In the formal Stirling expression
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bal:eq:stirlingalg}
 G(X)=\left(X+\frac12\right)\log X-X+\frac12\log(2\pi)
 +\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{B_{2m}}{2m(2m-1)X^{2m-1}},
\end{equation}
substitute $X=h^{-2}$ and $X=h^{-2}\pm z/(2h)$, and take the coefficient of $h^j$ in
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bal:eq:dalg}
 3G(h^{-2})-2G(h^{-2}-z/(2h))-G(h^{-2}+z/(2h))
             +\frac zh\log h.
\end{equation}
Here $G(X)$ is the expansion of $\log\Gamma(X+1)$, and only finitely many Bernoulli terms contribute to a fixed coefficient. The leading term of~\eqref{tmm:bal:eq:dalg} is $-3z^2/8$; logarithms cancel. Taylor expansion of $\log(1\pm zh/2)$ performs the extraction using rational arithmetic.

The uniformity and differentiated remainder are standard consequences of the sectorial log-gamma expansion~\cite[\S5.11]{dlmf}. Indeed, for $z$ in a fixed complex neighborhood of $K^*$, both gamma arguments tend uniformly to positive infinity inside a sector bounded away from the negative real axis. Use a sufficiently long uniform Stirling expansion there, and then Cauchy's estimate on fixed-radius $z$ discs. This justifies finitely many $z$ derivatives; it does not prove a complex-$v$ version of the marked theorem.

Define $p_j(z)$ by
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bal:eq:p}
 \exp\left(\sum_{s\ge1}d_s(z)h^s\right)=\sum_{j\ge0}p_j(z)h^j,
 \quad p_0=1,\quad
 p_j=\frac1j\sum_{s=1}^j s\,d_s p_{j-s}.
\end{equation}
Thus $d_j$ has rational coefficients, degree at most $j+2$, and parity $j$, while $p_j$ has degree at most $3j$ and parity $j$.

\begin{theorem}[Marked expansion]\label{tmm:bal:thm:marked}
There are rational polynomials $q_j(v)$, with $q_0=1$, degree at most $3j$, and parity $j$, such that for every fixed $J\ge0$ and compact $K\subset(0,\infty)$,
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bal:eq:marked}
 S_n(v)=\frac{B_n}{2}e^{v\lambda-3v^2/8}
 \left(\sum_{j=0}^Jq_j(v)\lambda^{-j}
                    +O_{K,J}(\lambda^{-J-1})\right)
\end{equation}
uniformly for real $v\in K$. A finite coefficient algorithm is
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bal:eq:qalg}
 q_j(v)=e^{3v^2/8}[h^j]\left.
 \exp\left(\sum_{r\ge2}\frac{v h^{r-1}}{r!}\partial_z^r\right)
 \left[e^{-3z^2/8}\exp\left(\sum_{s\ge1}d_s(z)h^s\right)\right]
 \right|_{z=v}.
\end{equation}
The parameter $v$ is held fixed during differentiation in $z$. The operator is a formal coefficient algorithm, not an asserted convergent infinite operator.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Using~\eqref{tmm:bal:eq:tail} and~\eqref{tmm:bal:eq:globalbound}, restrict~\eqref{tmm:bal:eq:poisson} to $P/\lambda\in K^*$. Uniform exponentiation of~\eqref{tmm:bal:eq:D} gives
\[
 R_n(P)=\sum_{j=0}^Jh^j f_j(P/\lambda)+O(h^{J+1}),
 \qquad f_j(z)=e^{-3z^2/8}p_j(z).
\]
Taylor-expand $f_j$ at $z=v$ through degree $2(J-j)+1$. Its remainder is bounded by a constant times $|P/\lambda-v|^{2(J-j)+2}$ on the central interval. Exact even centered Poisson moments show
\[
 \E|P/\lambda-v|^{2m}=O_{K,m}(\lambda^{-m}).
\]
Hence the expectation of this remainder after multiplication by $h^j$ is $O(h^{J+1})$. The Taylor polynomial can be averaged over the whole Poisson distribution because its omitted tail is exponentially small. The exact centered moment generating function is
\[
 \E e^{s(P/\lambda-v)}
   =\exp\left(\sum_{r\ge2}\frac{v h^{r-1}s^r}{r!}\right),
\]
which gives~\eqref{tmm:bal:eq:qalg} for the unsigned expectation.

It remains to impose the parity. Exactly,
\[
 \one_{P\equiv n\ (2)}=\frac{1+(-1)^{P-n}}2,
 \qquad \E[(-1)^P\fall P r]=(-v\lambda)^r e^{-2v\lambda}.
\]
Every finite Taylor polynomial is a polynomial in $P$, so its signed expectation is an exponential times a fixed polynomial in $\lambda$ and $\lambda^{-1}$. The signed Taylor remainder has the same absolute bound as the unsigned one. Thus the parity-restricted expectation is half the unsigned expansion to every fixed algebraic order, proving~\eqref{tmm:bal:eq:marked}.

For the degree assertion, an operator monomial of $h$ order $m$ uses $\ell$ derivative blocks, has total derivative order $r=m+\ell$, and carries a factor $v^\ell$, where $\ell\le m$. After extracting $e^{-3z^2/8}$, differentiation increases polynomial degree by at most $r$. Therefore it increases the eventual degree by at most $r+\ell\le3m$. Starting with $p_{j-m}$ proves the bound $3j$. Derivative parity and the factor $v^\ell$ change parity by $r+\ell\equiv m\pmod2$, proving parity $j$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{tmm:bal:rem:parity}
The signed Taylor remainder above is bounded algebraically, not exponentially. The theorem therefore does not identify the exponentially small parity sector of the count. In particular, the exact subtraction in~\eqref{tmm:bal:eq:Amark} cannot be promoted into an exponentially accurate count transseries by combining it with~\eqref{tmm:bal:eq:marked}.
\end{remark}

An alternative exact implementation of~\eqref{tmm:bal:eq:qalg} uses the centered moments $\mu_r(t)=\E(P-t)^r$ for $P\sim\Poi(t)$:
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bal:eq:mu}
 \mu_0=1,\quad\mu_1=0,\quad
 \mu_{r+1}(t)=t\{\mu_r'(t)+r\mu_{r-1}(t)\}.
\end{equation}
Only $r\le2J$ can affect order $h^J$. If $\mathcal D=\partial_z-3z/4$, then $f_j^{(r)}=e^{-3z^2/8}\mathcal D^rp_j$, allowing every coefficient to be computed in rational polynomial arithmetic.

\section{Marked laws and directly inserted moments}\label{tmm:bal:sec:moments}
Let $U_n$ be the number of unmatched vertices in a uniformly chosen maximal matching. Its probability generating polynomial is exactly $A_n(v)/A_n$. From Theorem~\ref{tmm:bal:thm:marked}, for every fixed $J$ and compact $K\subset(0,\infty)$,
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bal:eq:mgf}
 \E v^{U_n}=e^{\lambda(v-1)-3(v^2-1)/8}
 \left(\frac{Q_J(v,\lambda)}{Q_J(1,\lambda)}
                 +O_{K,J}(\lambda^{-J-1})\right).
\end{equation}
The error is inside the displayed exponential normalization. This is a real-parameter mod-Poisson type statement only; no uniform fixed complex neighborhood of $v=1$ is asserted.

Differentiating an unspecified real remainder would not justify moment expansions. Instead insert factorial powers in the exact sum. If $r\ge1$ is fixed and $P\sim\Poi(\lambda)$, the exact factorial-shift identity
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bal:eq:shift}
 \E[\fall P r f(P)]=\lambda^r\E f(P+r)
\end{equation}
implies
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bal:eq:insert}
 \E\fall{U_n}r=
 \frac{3B_ne^\lambda\lambda^r}{A_n}
 \E\left[R_n(P+r)\one_{P+r\le n}\one_{P+r\equiv n\ (2)}\right].
\end{equation}
The perfect term has no contribution because $\fall0r=0$.

A finite inserted coefficient algorithm follows from~\eqref{tmm:bal:eq:qalg} by replacing every $z$ inside its square bracket by $z+rh$, retaining $v=1$ in the operator, and then evaluating at $z=1$. In detail let
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bal:eq:Tr}
 T_r(h)=e^{3/8}\left.
 \exp\left(\sum_{m\ge2}\frac{h^{m-1}}{m!}\partial_z^m\right)
 \left[e^{-3(z+rh)^2/8}
       \exp\left(\sum_{s\ge1}d_s(z+rh)h^s\right)\right]
 \right|_{z=1}
\end{equation}
as a formal series. Then $T_0(h)=\sum a_jh^j$. Applying the same enlarged-central-interval proof to the shifted argument gives, for each fixed $M$,
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bal:eq:momentseries}
 \E\fall{U_n}r
   =h^{-r}\left(\frac{\sum_{j=0}^M[h^j]T_r(h)\,h^j}
                         {Q_M(1/h)}+O_{M,r}(h^{M+1})\right).
\end{equation}
Changing parity in~\eqref{tmm:bal:eq:insert} changes only the signed negligible polynomial contribution. The hard cutoff and tails are controlled as before. Formula~\eqref{tmm:bal:eq:momentseries} proves direct moment expansions at every fixed order; one chooses $M$ sufficiently large before cancellations.

\begin{theorem}\label{tmm:bal:thm:moments}
The first two moments have the expansions
\begin{align}
 \E U_n&=\lambda-\frac34+\frac{21}{32\lambda}
                         -\frac9{32\lambda^2}+O(\lambda^{-3}),\label{tmm:bal:eq:mean}\\
 \Var U_n&=\lambda-\frac32+\frac{71}{32\lambda}
                         -\frac{41}{16\lambda^2}+O(\lambda^{-3}).\label{tmm:bal:eq:variance}
\end{align}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Use~\eqref{tmm:bal:eq:momentseries} for $r=1,2$ through $h^5$, and form
$\Var U_n=\E\fall{U_n}2+\E U_n-(\E U_n)^2$.
The error for the mean is then $O(h^5)$ and for the second factorial moment $O(h^4)$; the subsequent square and subtraction leave an error at most $O(h^4)$ before truncating the displayed series, so $O(h^3)$ in~\eqref{tmm:bal:eq:mean}--\eqref{tmm:bal:eq:variance} is justified.

For a compact way to check the retained coefficients, the already justified finite formal calculation agrees with applying $v\partial_v$ and its square to the formal logarithm in~\eqref{tmm:bal:eq:mgf}. In that calculation
\[
 q_2(v)-\frac12q_1(v)^2=-\frac{v^2(16v^2-23)}{64}.
\]
The leading correction $-3v^2/8$, together with this polynomial and $q_1(v)$, gives exactly the displayed values. This algebraic check is used only after the inserted-moment remainder proof.
\end{proof}

\section{Conditioned Poisson comparison and local limits}\label{tmm:bal:sec:limits}
Let $\Pi_n$ denote the distribution of a Poisson random variable of mean $\lambda$ conditioned to have parity $n$. We use the convention
$\TV(\mu,\nu)=\frac12\sum_k|\mu(k)-\nu(k)|$.

\begin{theorem}\label{tmm:bal:thm:tv}
As $n\to\infty$,
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bal:eq:tv}
 \TV(\mathcal L(U_n),\Pi_n)=O(\lambda^{-1/2})=O(n^{-1/4}),
 \qquad \frac{U_n-\lambda}{\sqrt\lambda}\ \Longrightarrow\ N(0,1).
\end{equation}
For every fixed $M>0$, uniformly over integers $k\equiv n\pmod2$ with $|k-\lambda|\le M\sqrt\lambda$,
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bal:eq:local}
 \Pp(U_n=k)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi\lambda}}
 e^{-(k-\lambda)^2/(2\lambda)}(1+o(1)).
\end{equation}
The factor two is the lattice-span correction.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
First ignore the multiplicity adjustment at $k=0$, and let $V_n$ have probabilities $t(n,k)/S_n(1)$ on the admissible support. It is the $\Pi_n$ law tilted by $R_n(k)\one_{k\le n}$. On, for example, $k/\lambda\in[1/2,2]$, the smooth gamma expansion yields
\[
 |R_n(k)-e^{-3/8}|
   \le C\left(\frac{|k-\lambda|}{\lambda}+\lambda^{-1}\right).
\]
The probability of each parity under $\Poi(\lambda)$ is
$(1+(-1)^ne^{-2\lambda})/2$, bounded away from zero. Cauchy--Schwarz and Poisson variance therefore give a conditioned first absolute deviation $O(\sqrt\lambda)$. Outside the central interval, Chernoff bounds and $R_n\le e^2$ apply. Hence
\[
 \E_{\Pi_n}|R_n(P)\one_{P\le n}-e^{-3/8}|=O(\lambda^{-1/2}).
\]
The normalizing expectation tends to $e^{-3/8}>0$. Normalizing a nonnegative tilt changes its total variation distance by at most a constant times this $L^1$ error, proving $\TV(\mathcal L(V_n),\Pi_n)=O(\lambda^{-1/2})$. For odd $n$, $U_n=V_n$ in distribution. For even $n$, the weights at zero change from $3B_n$ to $B_n$ when passing from $3S_n$ to $A_n$; their total variation effect is $O(B_n/S_n)=O(e^{-\lambda})$ by~\eqref{tmm:bal:eq:marked}. This proves the first assertion.

For completeness the conditioned-Poisson central limit theorem follows from its exact moment generating function. With $\epsilon_n=(-1)^n$ and every real $t$,
\begin{align}
 &\E\left[e^{t(P-\lambda)/\sqrt\lambda}\mid P\equiv n\ (2)\right]\notag\\
 &\quad=\exp\left(\lambda(e^{t/\sqrt\lambda}-1)-t\sqrt\lambda\right)
 \frac{1+\epsilon_n e^{-2\lambda e^{t/\sqrt\lambda}}}
      {1+\epsilon_n e^{-2\lambda}}.\label{tmm:bal:eq:condmgf}
\end{align}
For each fixed $t$ this tends to $e^{t^2/2}$. Moment generating function convergence in a real neighborhood of zero proves the conditional central limit theorem. Total variation decreases under a measurable transformation, so the first part of~\eqref{tmm:bal:eq:tv} transfers it to $U_n$. This transfer is not used to infer moments; those were proved separately.

For the local assertion, $k>0$ eventually in the stated window, so the exact probability is $3t(n,k)/A_n$. Uniform Stirling expansion of $k!$ gives the ordinary Poisson local Gaussian approximation there. Also $R_n(k)=e^{-3/8}(1+o(1))$ uniformly, while $A_n\sim(3/2)B_ne^{\lambda-3/8}$. Substitution gives~\eqref{tmm:bal:eq:local}.
\end{proof}

The number of edges is $(3n-U_n)/2$, and the largest possible number is $\lfloor3n/2\rfloor$. The gap from maximum size is therefore exactly
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bal:eq:gap}
 G_n=\frac{U_n-(n\bmod2)}2.
\end{equation}
In particular its mean is $\lambda/2+O(1)$ and its standard deviation is $\sqrt\lambda/2\,(1+o(1))$. Thus the typical gap has order $\sqrt n$ and its fluctuations have order $n^{1/4}$.

\section{Rare maximum matchings}\label{tmm:bal:sec:rare}
For even $n$, maximum matchings have $U_n=0$; for odd $n$, they have $U_n=1$. These are the smallest allowed unmatched counts, and~\eqref{tmm:bal:eq:t} proves that they occur.

\begin{theorem}\label{tmm:bal:thm:rare}
For every fixed $J\ge0$, the probability $p_n^{\max}$ that a uniform maximal matching is maximum satisfies
\begin{align}
 p_n^{\max}
 &=\frac23e^{-\lambda+3/8}
       \left(Q_J(\lambda)^{-1}+O_J(\lambda^{-J-1})\right),
       &&n\ \text{even},\label{tmm:bal:eq:evenrare}\\
 p_n^{\max}
 &=2\lambda e^{-\lambda+3/8}R_n(1)
       \left(Q_J(\lambda)^{-1}+O_J(\lambda^{-J-1})\right),
       &&n\ \text{odd}.\label{tmm:bal:eq:oddrare}
\end{align}
The fixed-shift ratio has an expansion to every fixed order in $\lambda^{-2}$, beginning
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bal:eq:R1}
 R_n(1)=1-\frac1{8\lambda^2}+\frac{17}{128\lambda^4}
           -\frac{75}{1024\lambda^6}+\frac{1035}{32768\lambda^8}
           +O(\lambda^{-10}).
\end{equation}
These are relative expansions of exponentially small probabilities.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The exact numerators are $B_n$ and $3t(n,1)=3B_n\lambda R_n(1)$, respectively. Divide by~\eqref{tmm:bal:eq:count}, using eventual positivity of $Q_J$. The relative error in the denominator remains a relative error in the quotient regardless of the small numerator.

For the odd ratio, the gamma functional equation gives, with $x=\lambda^2$,
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bal:eq:R1exact}
 R_n(1)=\frac{[\Gamma(x+1)/\Gamma(x+1/2)]^3}{(x+1/2)\sqrt x}.
\end{equation}
The standard fixed-shift log-gamma expansion, obtained by substituting into Stirling's formula, gives for any fixed $M$
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bal:eq:shiftgamma}
 \log R_n(1)=\sum_{m=1}^M\ell_m x^{-m}+O_M(x^{-M-1}),
\end{equation}
where the Bernoulli-polynomial formula is
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bal:eq:ellm}
 \ell_m=\frac{3(-1)^{m+1}\{B_{m+1}(1)-B_{m+1}(1/2)\}}{m(m+1)}
                       +\frac{(-1)^m}{m2^m}.
\end{equation}
The first four values are $-1/8,1/8,-11/192,1/64$. Exponentiation gives~\eqref{tmm:bal:eq:R1} and all further fixed orders.
\end{proof}

For explicit use, the four relative corrections after removing the leading prefactors are
\begin{align}
 \frac{p_n^{\max}}{(2/3)e^{-\lambda+3/8}}
 ={}&1-\frac{13}{96\lambda}-\frac{1847}{18432\lambda^2}
 +\frac{4912087}{26542080\lambda^3}\notag\\
 &-\frac{2299743979}{10192158720\lambda^4}+O(\lambda^{-5}),
 \qquad n\text{ even},\label{tmm:bal:eq:even4}\\
 \frac{p_n^{\max}}{2\lambda e^{-\lambda+3/8}}
 ={}&1-\frac{13}{96\lambda}-\frac{4151}{18432\lambda^2}
 +\frac{5361367}{26542080\lambda^3}\notag\\
 &-\frac{818433259}{10192158720\lambda^4}+O(\lambda^{-5}),
 \qquad n\text{ odd}.\label{tmm:bal:eq:odd4}
\end{align}
Theorem~\ref{tmm:bal:thm:rare} does not require an exponentially accurate expansion for $A_n$. Its exact rare numerator avoids subtracting nearly equal approximate quantities. This is the reason these probability results coexist with the limitation in Remark~\ref{tmm:bal:rem:parity}.

\section{Smooth inversion and integer thresholds}\label{tmm:bal:sec:inverse}
Define the integer threshold
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bal:eq:N}
 N(y)=\min\{n\ge1:A_n\ge y\},\qquad y>0.
\end{equation}
It exists because~\eqref{tmm:bal:eq:known} tends to infinity.

\begin{lemma}\label{tmm:bal:lem:monotone}
The sequence $A_n$ is nondecreasing, and is eventually strictly increasing.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Given a maximal matching on the old vertices, add one new vertex to each part. If the old matching has unmatched vertices, leave the new vertex of that part unmatched and join the other two new vertices. If it is perfect, use the first distinguished part for the new unmatched vertex. All unmatched vertices of the result lie in one part, so it is maximal. Restricting to the old vertices recovers the original matching, proving an injection.

By~\eqref{tmm:bal:eq:logcount}, the difference of consecutive logarithms is
$\log A_{n+1}-\log A_n\sim(3/2)\log(2n)>0$. Here even a leading log expansion with an $o(1)$ remainder suffices to bound the consecutive remainder difference. This proves eventual strict increase.
\end{proof}

For each fixed $J\ge0$ define the smooth positive model, for all sufficiently large real $x$,
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bal:eq:FJ}
 F_J(x)=\frac32\frac{\Gamma(x+1)^3}{\Gamma(x/2+1)^3}
 e^{\sqrt{x/2}-3/8}Q_J(\sqrt{x/2}).
\end{equation}
Its logarithmic derivative is
\begin{align}
 \frac{d}{dx}\log F_J(x)
 ={}&3\psi(x+1)-\frac32\psi(x/2+1)+\frac1{4\sqrt{x/2}}\notag\\
 &+\frac{Q_J'(\sqrt{x/2})}{4\sqrt{x/2}\,Q_J(\sqrt{x/2})}
 =\frac32\log(2x)+O_J(x^{-1/2})>0.\label{tmm:bal:eq:derivative}
\end{align}
Consequently $F_J$ has an eventual real inverse, denoted $x_J(y)$.

\begin{theorem}[Boundary-safe threshold enclosure]\label{tmm:bal:thm:enclosure}
For every fixed $J\ge0$ there are constants $c_J>0$ and $y_J$ such that, for $y\ge y_J$, with $x=x_J(y)$ and
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bal:eq:epsilon}
 \varepsilon_J(y)=c_J\frac{x^{-(J+1)/2}}{\log x},
\end{equation}
one has
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bal:eq:ceil}
 \ceil{x-\varepsilon_J(y)}\le N(y)\le\ceil{x+\varepsilon_J(y)}.
\end{equation}
Eventually this integer bracket has width zero or one. It is an exact asymptotic existence statement, with no numerical error constant or onset certified here.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Write $a=(J+1)/2$ and $f_J=\log F_J$. The relative expansion~\eqref{tmm:bal:eq:count} gives
\[
 |\log A_n-f_J(n)|\le C_Jn^{-a}
\]
for all sufficiently large integers $n$. In a fixed-width neighborhood of $x$, equation~\eqref{tmm:bal:eq:derivative} bounds $f_J'$ below by a positive multiple of $\log x$, while $n^{-a}$ is comparable with $x^{-a}$. Choose $c_J$ larger than the corresponding error-to-slope constant. At $n_+=\ceil{x+\varepsilon_J}$ the mean value theorem gives $\log A_{n_+}\ge\log y$. At $n_- =\ceil{x-\varepsilon_J}-1<x-\varepsilon_J$ it gives $\log A_{n_-}<\log y$. Monotonicity proves~\eqref{tmm:bal:eq:ceil}. Since $2\varepsilon_J<1$ eventually, its endpoints differ by at most one.
\end{proof}

Away from integer boundaries, if the two ceilings agree they determine $N(y)$ exactly. A bare rule $N(y)=\ceil{x_J(y)}$ is not proved: an arbitrarily small real error can change the ceiling near an integer. The existential constants in Theorem~\ref{tmm:bal:thm:enclosure} also prevent interpreting an uncalibrated numerical bracket as a certified one.
\begin{writenote}[6 October 2026]
Theorem~\ref{tmm:bal:thm:enclosure} is proved directly at the integers, from Lemma~\ref{tmm:bal:lem:monotone} and the relative error of the model $F_J$; it is not an instance of the staircase theorem of the transseries volume \cite{TSvol}, whose item~(2) reaches the same conclusion when the two ceilings agree (front matter, ``The inverses and the transseries volume''). \subsection{An explicit Lambert W approximation}
Let $\ell=\log y$ and use the positive real branch of Lambert $W$:
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bal:eq:lambert}
 w=W\left(\frac{4\ell}{3e}\right),\qquad
 x_0=\frac{2\ell}{3w},\qquad
 D=\frac32(w+1),\qquad \lambda_0=\sqrt{x_0/2}.
\end{equation}
These quantities are used only for sufficiently large $y$, so $\ell,w>0$.

\begin{theorem}\label{tmm:bal:thm:W}
For every fixed $J\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bal:eq:inverseW}
 x_J(y)=x_0-\frac{\lambda_0+C}{D}
             +\frac1{4D^2}-\frac3{8D^3}
             +O_J\left(\frac1{\sqrt{x_0}D}\right).
\end{equation}
In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bal:eq:simpleW}
 x_J(y)=x_0-\frac{\lambda_0+C}{D}+O(D^{-2}),
\end{equation}
which has $o(1)$ absolute real error.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $H(x)=(3x/2)\log(2x/e)$ and $g(x)=\sqrt{x/2}+C$. Equations~\eqref{tmm:bal:eq:logB} and~\eqref{tmm:bal:eq:FJ} imply
\[
 \log F_J(x)=H(x)+g(x)+O_J(x^{-1/2}).
\]
The identity $we^w=4\ell/(3e)$ gives $H(x_0)=\ell$, and
\[
 H'(x_0)=D,\qquad H''(x_0)=\frac3{2x_0},\qquad
 g'(x_0)=\frac1{4\lambda_0}.
\]
Monotonicity and the mean value theorem first localize the solution to $x_0+O(\sqrt{x_0}/D)$. The $O(x^{-1/2})$ term in the logarithm changes the solution by $O(1/(\sqrt{x_0}D))$. Taylor inversion of $H+g$ then yields
\[
 \delta=-\frac gD+\frac{g'g}{D^2}-\frac{H''g^2}{2D^3}
                   +O\left(\frac1{\sqrt{x_0}D}\right),
\]
with functions evaluated at $x_0$. Terms involving $C$ in the last two corrections fit the stated remainder. The remaining coefficients follow from
\[
 g'g=\frac14+O(x_0^{-1/2}),\qquad
 H''g^2=\frac34+O(x_0^{-1/2}).
\]
For the Taylor remainder, $H'''(x)=O(x^{-2})$ and $g''(x)=O(x^{-3/2})$ throughout the localized interval. Substituting $\delta=O(\sqrt{x_0}/D)$ and dividing the resulting residual by the slope $\asymp D$ gives terms of size $O(1/(\sqrt{x_0}D^3))$ or smaller. The unretained substitutions in the second-order correction obey the same bound. This proves~\eqref{tmm:bal:eq:inverseW}. Finally $D\to\infty$ and $D/\sqrt{x_0}\to0$, yielding~\eqref{tmm:bal:eq:simpleW}.
\end{proof}
\begin{writenote}[6 October 2026]
$x_0$ and $D=H'(x_0)$ are the factorial core of the transseries volume \cite{TSvol} with $\kappa_\vol=\frac32$, $d_\vol=\frac32(\log2-1)$, and $w$ is that proposition's $v_\vol$ (an instance). The three correction terms of \eqref{tmm:bal:eq:inverseW} are the terms $m=1,2$ of the volume's perturbation-operator series with $\varrho_\vol=g$ (an analogue: the error bound is this Part's own). Both are proved in the front matter.
\end{writenote}

The models~\eqref{tmm:bal:eq:FJ}, together with~\eqref{tmm:bal:eq:qalg}, specify inverse approximations at arbitrarily high fixed algebraic orders. Each $x_J$ may be obtained by solving a one-dimensional eventually monotone equation. Formal Newton expansion about~\eqref{tmm:bal:eq:lambert} gives further explicit terms when desired; the threshold theorem uses the model itself and does not require writing a long mixed expansion in $x_0^{-1/2}$ and $D^{-1}$.

\section{Exact enumeration and classical identities}\label{tmm:bd:sec:exact}
\begin{lemma}\label{tmm:bd:lem:maximal}
A matching of a complete multipartite graph is maximal if and only if its unmatched vertices lie in at most one part.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Two unmatched vertices in different parts can be joined by an edge. Conversely, if all unmatched vertices lie in one part, no edge joins two of them, so no edge can be added.
\end{proof}

Let $M_{p_1,p_2,p_3}(w)$ weight a matching by $w$ to the number of unmatched vertices. The multivariate exponential generating function is
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bd:eq:egf}
 \sum_{p_1,p_2,p_3\ge0}M_{p_1,p_2,p_3}(w)
 \frac{x^{p_1}y^{p_2}z^{p_3}}{p_1!p_2!p_3!}
 =(e^{wx}+e^{wy}+e^{wz}-2)e^{xy+xz+yz}.
\end{equation}
Indeed the second factor is the labeled set of cross-part edges. The first chooses the unmatched set in a single part; subtraction of two counts its empty choice once. This is the general EGF already recorded in Report 176.
\begin{writenote}
Lemma~\ref{tmm:bd:lem:maximal} is Part~I's Lemma~\ref{tmm:bal:lem:maximal}, with the same proof, and \eqref{tmm:bd:eq:egf} is Part~I's \eqref{tmm:bal:eq:egf} with $w$ for Part~I's $v$. Both are printed again because this Part's proofs cite them.
\end{writenote}

For distinct $i,j,\ell$ put
\[
 a_i=\frac{p_j+p_\ell-p_i}{2},\qquad F=p_1!p_2!p_3!.
\]
If exactly $k$ vertices are unmatched in part $i$, the edge populations opposite the three parts are $a_i+k/2$, $a_j-k/2$, $a_\ell-k/2$. Consequently the exact branch term is
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bd:eq:branch}
 T_i(k)=\frac{F}{k!\,\Gamma(a_i+k/2+1)
                    \Gamma(a_j-k/2+1)\Gamma(a_\ell-k/2+1)}.
\end{equation}
This expression is used only when $k\ge0$ and all three edge populations are nonnegative integers. Equivalently the feasibility constraints include $k\equiv p_1+p_2+p_3\pmod2$. To prove the count, partition the vertices of each part by their destinations and unmatched class, and choose a bijection for each of the three edge types. The factorials cancel to~\eqref{tmm:bd:eq:branch}. Positive-$k$ branches are disjoint. A feasible perfect term occurs in all three branch sums and must be subtracted twice.

\subsection{The large-part branch}
Let $J$ denote the number of edges joining the two parts of size $n$. On the branch whose unmatched vertices are in the part of size $2n+d$, the three edge populations are $n-J,n-J,J$ and the unmatched count is $U=d+2J$. Therefore
\begin{align}
 A_{\rm big}(n,d)&=\Gamma(2n+d+1)Z(n,d),\label{tmm:bd:eq:big}\\
 Z(n,d)&=\sum_{j=L}^{n}t(n,d,j),\qquad
 L=\max(0,\ceil{-d/2}),\label{tmm:bd:eq:Z}\\
 t(n,d,j)&=\frac{\Gamma(n+1)^2}
 {\Gamma(n-j+1)^2\Gamma(d+2j+1)\Gamma(j+1)}.\label{tmm:bd:eq:term}
\end{align}
For $L\le j<n$ its positive-term ratio is
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bd:eq:ratio}
 r(n,d,j)=\frac{(n-j)^2}{(d+2j+2)(d+2j+1)(j+1)}.
\end{equation}
It is strictly decreasing. If $d>0$, all vertices of the large part cannot be matched outside it, so there are no other branches. If $d=0$, the other two formal branches contain only the common perfect term, which their duplicate correction cancels. Thus $A(n,d)=A_{\rm big}(n,d)$ for $d\ge0$. For $d<0$ this equality generally fails; Section~\ref{tmm:bd:sec:suppression} proves an exponentially small relative difference in the moving window.

\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bd:eq:hyperHermite}
 Z_n={}_2F_2\!\left(\begin{matrix}-n,-n\\1/2,1\end{matrix};\frac14\right),

\end{equation}
where $X\sim\Normal(0,1)$ and $\operatorname{He}$ denotes probabilists' Hermite polynomials. The first follows from $(1/2)_j=(2j)!/(4^jj!)$. For the second, the product of the three Hermite generating functions has expectation

 \E\exp\{(X+1)t-t^2/2+Xs-s^2/2+Xu-u^2/2\}

\section{Strictly triangular proportions and competing parts}\label{tmm:bd:sec:strict}
This comparison is proved independently of the boundary theorem. Suppose that the part ratios $(p_1/n,p_2/n,p_3/n)$ range in a fixed compact subset of the positive strict triangle region. Thus each $a_i$ is bounded above and below by positive multiples of $n$. Define
\[
 B=\frac{F}{\prod_i\Gamma(a_i+1)},\qquad
 \lambda_i=\sqrt{\frac{a_ja_\ell}{a_i}},\qquad
 C_i=\frac{\lambda_i^2}{8}\sum_{r=1}^3a_r^{-1}.
\]
When total parity is odd, $B$ is a positive gamma reference, not a count of perfect matchings. Let $S_i$ be the feasible branch sum including $k=0$ when allowed.

\begin{theorem}\label{tmm:bd:thm:strict}
Uniformly in such a compact strict triangle region,
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bd:eq:strictlead}
 S_i=\frac B2\exp(\lambda_i-C_i)\{1+O(n^{-1/2})\}.
\end{equation}
The full count is $\sum_iS_i-2B$ for even total size and $\sum_iS_i$ for odd total size. The subtraction, when present, is exponentially small relative to the sum. Within each nonempty branch the unmatched count is asymptotically, in total variation, a Poisson variable of mean $\lambda_i$ conditioned on the required parity.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Write $T_i(k)=B\lambda_i^kR_i(k)/k!$ on its feasible range. The elementary digamma inequalities
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bd:eq:digamma}
 \log t\le\psi(t+1)\le\log(t+1)\qquad(t>0)
\end{equation}
follow from convexity of $\log\Gamma$ and its functional equation. Applying the upper bound to downward shifts and the lower bound to the upward shift gives
\[
 \log R_i(k)\le\frac k2\{\log(1+1/a_j)+\log(1+1/a_\ell)\}\le C.
\]
The endpoint with a downward shift reaching zero is handled by continuity of $\psi(t+1)$ there. Thus the corresponding Poisson expectation has a uniform bounded tilt on its full feasible range.

For $k$ in a fixed positive compact multiple of $\sqrt n$, Taylor expansion of $\log\Gamma$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bd:eq:Rstrict}
 \log R_i(k)=-\frac{k^2}{8}\sum_ra_r^{-1}+O(n^{-1/2}).
\end{equation}
For completeness, the leading linear gamma terms cancel $k\log\lambda_i$; the quadratic terms all have negative sign and sum to the displayed coefficient. Remaining linear terms are $O(k/n)$ and cubic terms are $O(k^3/n^2)$, both $O(n^{-1/2})$. The estimates and their first two derivatives hold uniformly on that central range.

Let $P\sim\Poi(\lambda_i)$, so $\lambda_i\asymp\sqrt n$. Outside a fixed central range its probability is $O(e^{-c\sqrt n})$ by the exponential-moment bound for a Poisson variable. The bounded tilt controls this tail, including the infeasible cutoff. On the central range, $R_i'(k)=O(n^{-1/2})$ and $R_i''(k)=O(n^{-1})$. Taylor's formula about $\lambda_i$ has expected second-order remainder $O(n^{-1}\E(P-\lambda_i)^2)=O(n^{-1/2})$.

For either parity $\epsilon$, $\Pp(P\equiv\epsilon\ (2))=(1+(-1)^\epsilon e^{-2\lambda_i})/2$, and
\[
 \E[(P-\lambda_i)\one_{P\equiv\epsilon\ (2)}]
 =-(-1)^\epsilon\lambda_i e^{-2\lambda_i}.
\]
The linear Taylor term is therefore exponentially small even with parity. Using~\eqref{tmm:bd:eq:Rstrict} at $k=\lambda_i$ proves~\eqref{tmm:bd:eq:strictlead}. The perfect subtraction is smaller than $Be^{c\sqrt n}$ by an exponential factor. Finally $R_i(P)/R_i(\lambda_i)\to1$ in probability on each parity class, and the global bound gives convergence in $L^1$. Normalizing proves the total-variation assertion; excluding the exponentially rare $k=0$ term has no effect.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Near-balanced softmax law]\label{tmm:bd:cor:softmax}
Suppose $p_i=n+\delta_i\sqrt n+o(\sqrt n)$ are integers, with fixed real $\delta_i$, and put $D=\delta_1+\delta_2+\delta_3$. Condition away the perfect case, or give it its own label. The label $I$ of the unmatched part has limiting probabilities
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bd:eq:softmax}
 \Pp(I=i)\longrightarrow
 \frac{e^{\sqrt2\delta_i}}{\sum_{r=1}^3e^{\sqrt2\delta_r}}.
\end{equation}
Jointly with this label,
\[
 \frac{U-\sqrt{n/2}}{(n/2)^{1/4}}\ \Longrightarrow\ \Normal(0,1),
\]
and the limiting Gaussian and label are independent.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Expansion of $a_i$ gives
\[
 \lambda_i=\sqrt{n/2}+\frac{4\delta_i-D}{2\sqrt2}+o(1),
 \qquad C_i\longrightarrow3/8.
\]
The common factors in~\eqref{tmm:bd:eq:strictlead} cancel to give~\eqref{tmm:bd:eq:softmax}. Parity-conditioned Poisson variables with means tending to infinity have the usual centered Gaussian limit: their characteristic functions follow directly from $(e^{\lambda(z-1)}+(-1)^\epsilon e^{-\lambda(z+1)})/(1+(-1)^\epsilon e^{-2\lambda})$. The branch means differ from $\sqrt{n/2}$ by only $O(1)$, negligible on the $n^{1/4}$ scale. The conditional Gaussian limit is the same for all three labels, proving joint independence.
\end{proof}
The compact strict-triangle assumption is essential here. This proof supplies no estimate uniform as a triangle inequality degenerates.

\section{The compact moving-window theorem}\label{tmm:bd:sec:window}
Let $K\subset\mathbb R$ be any fixed compact interval. For each integer pair $(n,d)$ put
\[
 \delta=\frac d{N^2}\in K.
\]
All functions below use this \emph{exact} parameter, not a rounded limit. There is a unique $u>0$ satisfying
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bd:eq:u}
 \delta=u-\frac2{u^2}.
\end{equation}
The right side is strictly increasing from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$. Define
\begin{align}
 s&=u^{-2},&\chi&=\frac4u+\frac1s,&v&=\chi^{-1},&b&=-\frac{2s}{\chi},\label{tmm:bd:eq:params}\\
 \Phi&=u+s-\delta\log u,& C&=-\frac{s^3}3+\frac{2s^2}{\chi}.&&\label{tmm:bd:eq:phase}
\end{align}
Equivalently $s(\delta+2s)^2=1$ and $s>\max(0,-\delta/2)$, with $u=\delta+2s$. These functions are real analytic in $\delta$; their positive quantities and reciprocals stay bounded on $K$. Set
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bd:eq:H}
 H(n,d)=\frac{\Gamma(2n+d+1)N^{-2d}}
 {N\sqrt{2\pi us\chi}}\exp\{N^2\Phi-s^2N+C\}.
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}\label{tmm:bd:thm:window}
For every fixed nonnegative integer $M$, there are rational functions $c_r(u)$, with $c_0=1$, such that uniformly for integer $d$ with $d/N^2\in K$,
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bd:eq:window}
 A(n,d)=H(n,d)\left\{\sum_{r=0}^Mc_r(u)N^{-r}
                    +O_{K,M}(N^{-M-1})\right\}.
\end{equation}
They are analytic for $u>0$, and Section~\ref{tmm:bd:sec:algorithm} gives a finite coefficient algorithm. Moreover, for some positive $\gamma_K$,
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bd:eq:suppress}
 0\le\frac{A(n,d)-A_{\rm big}(n,d)}{A_{\rm big}(n,d)}
 \le C_K e^{-\gamma_KN^2}.
\end{equation}
The latter difference is exactly zero for $d\ge0$.
\end{theorem}
We prove suppression next, then the complete local expansion and summation remainder. In particular this theorem covers $d=-1,-2,0,1$, both total parities, and all rounding offsets in the prescribed compact window.

\section{Uniform suppression of the other branches}\label{tmm:bd:sec:suppression}
It remains to consider $d\le0$. Put
\[
 a_1=-d/2,\qquad a_2=a_3=n+d/2,\qquad
 B=\frac{\Gamma(2n+d+1)\Gamma(n+1)^2}
 {\Gamma(a_1+1)\Gamma(a_2+1)\Gamma(a_3+1)}.
\]
If $d$ is odd, these are half-integer $a_i$ and $B$ is only a positive reference. All comparisons below use gamma functions, so this distinction causes no parity gap.

Suppose first that $a_1>0$. A small-part branch has $0\le k\le2a_1$ of the required parity. Its Poisson scale in~\eqref{tmm:bd:eq:branch} is $\lambda=\sqrt{a_1}$. The same integral inequalities~\eqref{tmm:bd:eq:digamma} give
\[
 \frac{T_2(k)}B\le\frac{\lambda^k}{k!}
 (1+1/a_1)^{k/2}(1+1/a_3)^{k/2}
 \le\frac{\lambda^k}{k!}\exp\{k/(2a_1)+k/(2a_3)\}.
\]
Here $k/(2a_1)\le1$, and $a_1/a_3\le1$ uniformly for sufficiently large $n$. With $D=\max(0,-\inf K)$, summing even over all nonnegative $k$ yields
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bd:eq:smallupper}
 S_2+S_3\le2e^2B\exp(\sqrt{a_1})
 \le2e^2B\exp(\sqrt{D/2}\,N).
\end{equation}
No lower bound proportional to $N^2$ on $a_1$ was used; the estimate includes $a_1=1/2$. At $a_1=0$, necessarily $d=0$, the two small branches contain just their $k=0$ terms. These contribute $2B$ and cancel exactly against the perfect duplicate correction.

To lower-bound the large branch, set $C_D=D/2+2$ and $c=1/(8\sqrt{C_D})$. Take the largest nonnegative integer $k\le cN^2$ with the required parity. For large $N$, uniformly,
\[
 k\ge cN^2/2,\qquad a_2-k/2\ge n/2,\qquad
 a_1+k/2+1\le C_DN^2.
\]
Its edge populations are nonnegative integers. The opposite directions of~\eqref{tmm:bd:eq:digamma} give
\begin{align*}
 \frac{T_1(k)}B
 &\ge\frac{(n/2)^k}{k!(a_1+k/2+1)^{k/2}}\\
 &\ge\left(\frac1{2c\sqrt{C_D}}\right)^k
 =4^k\ge\exp(c\log2\,N^2).
\end{align*}
In the second line use $k!\le k^k$ and $k\le cN^2$. This construction works for odd total size as well. Combining it with~\eqref{tmm:bd:eq:smallupper} proves~\eqref{tmm:bd:eq:suppress}, after decreasing the positive exponential constant. Subtracting perfect duplicates can only reduce the nonnegative excess over the large branch. Since all statistics used here are polynomially bounded in $n$, every fixed polynomial moment transfers between the conditional large branch and the full model with superpolynomially small error.

\section{Finite coefficient algorithm}\label{tmm:bd:sec:algorithm}
For an admissible integer $j$, exactly
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bd:eq:logterm}
 \log t=2j\log n+2\sum_{h=0}^{j-1}\log(1-h/n)
         -\log\Gamma(d+2j+1)-\log\Gamma(j+1).
\end{equation}
Set $j=sN^2+Ny$ and $k=d+2j=uN^2+2Ny$. Stirling expansion and the stationarity identity $\log s+2\log u=0$ give the phase
\begin{align}
 \log t={}&-2d\log N+N^2\Phi-s^2N-2\log N-\log(2\pi\sqrt{us})\notag\\
 &-s^3/3-2sy-\chi y^2/2+\sum_{r=1}^Mh_r(y)N^{-r}
       +\text{remainder}.\label{tmm:bd:eq:localphase}
\end{align}
Completing the square yields
\[
 -s^3/3-2sy-\chi y^2/2=C-(y-b)^2/(2v).
\]
The first two phase polynomials are
\begin{align}
 h_1(y)={}&s-s^4/6-(s^2+1/(2s)+1/u)y-y^2
             +(1/(6s^2)+4/(3u^2))y^3,\label{tmm:bd:eq:h1}\\
 h_2(y)={}&-s^5/10-(2s^3/3)y+s^2/2-sy^2+y
             -(1/s+1/u)/12\notag\\
 &+y^2/(4s^2)+y^2/u^2-y^4/(12s^3)-4y^4/(3u^3).\label{tmm:bd:eq:h2}
\end{align}

Here is an explicit finite prescription for every further coefficient. Let $S_m(x)=\sum_{h=0}^{x-1}h^m$ mean the Faulhaber polynomial, and put $x=s/q^2+y/q$, $z=u/q^2+2y/q$. Expand formally
\begin{align}
 G(q,y)={}&3y/q-x\log(1+yq/s)-z\log(1+2yq/u)\notag\\
 &-\tfrac12\log(1+yq/s)-\tfrac12\log(1+2yq/u)\notag\\
 &-2\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{q^{3m}}mS_m(x)
 -\sum_{r\ge1}\frac{B_{2r}}{2r(2r-1)}
       \{x^{1-2r}+z^{1-2r}\}.\label{tmm:bd:eq:G}
\end{align}
Its initial terms are $-s^2/q-s^3/3-2sy-\chi y^2/2$; $h_r=[q^r]G$. The separately displayed $N^2\Phi$ and logarithmic prefactors in~\eqref{tmm:bd:eq:localphase} are not part of $G$. To obtain $h_1,\ldots,h_M$, retain only $m\le M+2$ and Stirling terms with $4r-2\le M$, and Taylor-expand the logarithms only through degrees which can affect $q^M$. This is a finite formal operation, not a convergent representation of the untruncated phase.

Define polynomials and Gaussian moments by
\begin{align}
 P_0(y)&=1,&
 P_k(y)&=\frac1k\sum_{r=1}^krh_r(y)P_{k-r}(y),\label{tmm:bd:eq:P}\\
 \mu_0&=1,&\mu_1&=b,&
 \mu_{r+1}&=b\mu_r+rv\mu_{r-1}.\label{tmm:bd:eq:mu}
\end{align}
For $Y\sim\Normal(b,v)$, the count coefficients are
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bd:eq:ck}
 c_k(u)=\E P_k(Y).
\end{equation}
Equations~\eqref{tmm:bd:eq:G}--\eqref{tmm:bd:eq:ck} involve only rational operations in $u$, since $s=u^{-2}$. Gaussian insertion $\E[Y^mP_k(Y)]$, followed by formal series division, supplies moment coefficients. This proves the rational form of the coefficients once the analytic remainder below establishes the expansion.

\section{Localization and the all-orders remainder}\label{tmm:bd:sec:proof}
\subsection{A uniformly interior saddle}
On the admissible real range the ratio derivative is
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bd:eq:rderivative}
 \frac{d}{dx}\log r(n,d,x)
 =-\frac2{n-x}-\frac2{d+2x+2}-\frac2{d+2x+1}-\frac1{x+1}<0.
\end{equation}
Choose $\eta>0$ smaller than $\min_Ks/2$ and $\min_Ku/4$. On $|x-sN^2|\le\eta N^2$, both $x$ and $d+2x$ are positive constant multiples of $N^2$, uniformly, and $n-x\asymp n$. Hence the derivative lies between $-C_K/N^2$ and $-c_K/N^2$. For bounded $y$,
\[
 \log r(n,d,sN^2+yN)=N^{-1}(-2s-\chi y)+O_K(N^{-2}).
\]
It follows by monotonicity that an integer mode $m$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bd:eq:mode}
 m=sN^2+bN+O_K(1).
\end{equation}
Summing logarithms of consecutive ratios on this bulk interval gives
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bd:eq:gausstail}
 t(n,d,j)/t(n,d,m)\le C_K\exp\{-c_K(j-m)^2/N^2\}.
\end{equation}
Conversely at least $c_KN$ terms within a fixed multiple of $N$ of the mode are bounded below by a fixed multiple of the modal term. Thus $Z\ge c_KNt(n,d,m)$.

At either bulk endpoint the limiting ratio $1/[z(\delta+2z)^2]$, with $z=x/N^2$, is separated from $1$ uniformly. Monotonicity bounds both exterior tails by geometric series whose initial terms already lose $e^{-c_KN^2}$. This reaches all the way to the lower cutoff, even when the unmatched count there is $0$ or $1$. The cutoff is separated from the saddle by $uN^2/2+O(1)$ when $\delta<0$, and by $sN^2$ when $\delta\ge0$.

For every fixed sufficiently small $\varepsilon>0$, discarding $|y|>N^\varepsilon$ now loses less than any prescribed inverse power of $N$, also after insertion of a fixed polynomial in $y$. In the bulk this follows from $e^{-cN^{2\varepsilon}}$; outside it follows from the geometric bounds. The full support has $|y|=O_K(N^2)$, so fixed polynomial weights cost only powers of $N$.

\subsection{A polynomial-weighted local error}
On $|y|\le N^\varepsilon$, both $j$ and $k$ are comparable to $N^2$, and $j/n=O_K(N^{-1})$. Truncate $\log(1-h/n)$ in~\eqref{tmm:bd:eq:logterm} at degree $L=M+2$. Its total remainder is bounded by
\[
 C\sum_{h=0}^{j-1}(h/n)^{L+1}
 \le Cj^{L+2}/n^{L+1}=O_{K,M}(N^{-M-1}).
\]
The power sums are exact polynomials. Stirling's real-positive remainder, after sufficiently many Bernoulli terms, is $O_{K,M}(N^{-M-1})$ for both factorial arguments. Taylor expansion in $y/N$ then gives
\begin{align}
 \log t&=\log D_{n,d}-(y-b)^2/(2v)
           +\sum_{r=1}^MN^{-r}h_r(y)+E_{M,n,d}(y),\label{tmm:bd:eq:E}\\
 D_{n,d}&=\frac{N^{-2d}e^{N^2\Phi-s^2N+C}}{2\pi\sqrt{us}\,N^2},\label{tmm:bd:eq:D}\\
 |E_{M,n,d}(y)|&\le C_{K,M}N^{-M-1}(1+|y|^{D_M})\label{tmm:bd:eq:Ebound}
\end{align}
for some finite integer $D_M$. Every $h_r$ has degree at most $r+2$. For the fixed order choose $\varepsilon>0$ smaller, if necessary, so all Taylor remainders and retained perturbations are bounded by a constant plus an arbitrarily small multiple of $y^2$. For the retained terms this follows from
\[
 N^{-r}|y|^{r+2}=(|y|/N)^r y^2;
\]
for~\eqref{tmm:bd:eq:Ebound} it follows by choosing $\varepsilon$ relative to $D_M$. Any such positive $\varepsilon$ still leaves superpolynomially small tails.

Taylor's integral remainder for exponentiation yields
\begin{align}
 t(n,d,j)&=D_{n,d}e^{-(y-b)^2/(2v)}
              \sum_{r=0}^MN^{-r}P_r(y)+R_{M,n,d}(y),\label{tmm:bd:eq:expand}\\
 |R_{M,n,d}(y)|&\le C_{K,M}D_{n,d}N^{-M-1}
                      (1+|y|^{E_M})e^{-c_Ky^2}.\label{tmm:bd:eq:Rbound}
\end{align}
One may obtain~\eqref{tmm:bd:eq:Rbound} by differentiating the finite perturbation polynomial in $q$ and applying Taylor's integral formula; its derivatives are polynomials in $y$, while the exponential of the perturbation is absorbed in a weaker Gaussian. This explains why the error is polynomial-weighted Gaussian, rather than merely the supremum of a polynomial on a growing window. The latter weaker approach could introduce unwanted logarithmic factors. Multiplication by any prescribed polynomial in $y$ preserves the needed form of~\eqref{tmm:bd:eq:Rbound}.

\subsection{Summation on the actual lattice}
The $y$ values lie on the mesh $j/N-sN$, of spacing $1/N$, with arbitrary fractional offset. For every fixed polynomial $P$, Poisson summation for a polynomial times a Gaussian gives
\begin{align}
 &\frac1N\sum_{j\in\mathbb Z}P(j/N-sN)
     e^{-(j/N-sN-b)^2/(2v)}\notag\\
 &\hspace{15mm}=\int_{\mathbb R}P(y)e^{-(y-b)^2/(2v)}\,dy
                   +O_{K,P}(N^A e^{-c_KN^2}).\label{tmm:bd:eq:lattice}
\end{align}
To see uniformity, its Fourier transform is a polynomial times a Gaussian; the nonzero dual lattice points have size proportional to $N$, and the offset contributes only a unit-modulus phase. The compact bounds on $b,v$ make all constants uniform. No assertion that odd moments vanish on an uncentered discrete lattice is needed.

Extend the central Gaussian-polynomial sum to the full lattice, losing less than any inverse power. Summing~\eqref{tmm:bd:eq:Rbound} costs $O(N)$ because shifted-mesh sums of a fixed polynomial times a Gaussian are uniformly $O(N)$. Finally
\[
 \Gamma(2n+d+1)D_{n,d}N\sqrt{2\pi v}=H(n,d).
\]
Equations~\eqref{tmm:bd:eq:ck}, \eqref{tmm:bd:eq:expand}, and~\eqref{tmm:bd:eq:lattice}, followed by the suppression result, prove Theorem~\ref{tmm:bd:thm:window} with its full stated remainder. The argument also proves all fixed polynomial-insertion expansions used below.

\section{Window corrections and probability laws}\label{tmm:bd:sec:windowlaws}
Put $x=u^3$. The first relative coefficient is
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bd:eq:c1window}
 c_1(u)=\frac{60x^4+315x^3+372x^2+80x-64}{6u^8(x+4)^3}.
\end{equation}
For the second logarithmic coefficient, write
\begin{align*}
 P(x)={}&5x^8-340x^7-900x^6+6056x^5+17240x^4\\
        &-4800x^3-30720x^2-7680x+6144.
\end{align*}
Then
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bd:eq:ell2window}
 \ell_2(u)=c_2(u)-\frac{c_1(u)^2}{2}
          =-\frac{P(x)}{60u^{10}(x+4)^5},
\end{equation}
so
\[
 \log\frac{A(n,d)}{H(n,d)}=\frac{c_1(u)}N+\frac{\ell_2(u)}{N^2}
                        +O_K(N^{-3}).
\]
These expressions follow by substituting~\eqref{tmm:bd:eq:h1}--\eqref{tmm:bd:eq:h2} into $c_1=\E h_1(Y)$ and $c_2=\E[h_2(Y)+h_1(Y)^2/2]$, and reducing with $s=u^{-2}$. The formulas provide checkable rational expressions rather than numerical fits.

\begin{theorem}\label{tmm:bd:thm:windowlaw}
For a uniform maximal matching of $K_{2n+d,n,n}$ in the compact window,
\begin{align}
 \E J&=sN^2+bN+m_0(u)+O_K(N^{-1}),\label{tmm:bd:eq:EJwindow}\\
 \Var J&=vN^2+v_1(u)N+O_K(1),\label{tmm:bd:eq:VJwindow}\\
 m_0(u)&=-\frac{3u^6+7u^3-8}{(u^3+4)^3},&
 v_1(u)&=-\frac{4(u^6+2u^3+4)}{u(u^3+4)^3}.\label{tmm:bd:eq:momwindow}
\end{align}
For the actual unmatched count,
\begin{align}
 \E U&=uN^2+2bN+2m_0(u)+O_K(N^{-1}),\label{tmm:bd:eq:EUwindow}\\
 \Var U&=4vN^2+4v_1(u)N+O_K(1).\label{tmm:bd:eq:VUwindow}
\end{align}
Let $g_\delta$ be the $\Normal(b,v)$ density, and define the normalized lattice Gaussian on all integers by
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bd:eq:Qwindow}
 Q_{n,d}(j)=\frac{g_\delta((j-sN^2)/N)}
 {\sum_{r\in\mathbb Z}g_\delta((r-sN^2)/N)}.
\end{equation}
Then, uniformly,
\begin{align}
 \sup_{j\in\mathbb Z}\left|N\Pp(J=j)-g_\delta((j-sN^2)/N)\right|
       &=O_K(N^{-1}),\label{tmm:bd:eq:localwindow}\\
 \TV(\mathcal L(J),Q_{n,d})&=O_K(N^{-1}).\label{tmm:bd:eq:TVwindow}
\end{align}
The Kolmogorov distance from the law of $(J-sN^2-bN)/(\sqrt vN)$ to $\Normal(0,1)$ is $O_K(N^{-1})$. The same conclusion holds for $(U-uN^2-2bN)/(2\sqrt vN)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Polynomial insertion in~\eqref{tmm:bd:eq:expand}, followed by normalization, gives all fixed orders of the moments of $y=(J-sN^2)/N$. At first order, Gaussian integration by parts gives
\[
 m_0=v\E h_1'(Y),\qquad v_1=v^2\E h_1''(Y),
\]
which evaluate to~\eqref{tmm:bd:eq:momwindow}. Run the insertion expansion one order further before multiplying by $N$ or $N^2$, so the remainders in~\eqref{tmm:bd:eq:EJwindow}--\eqref{tmm:bd:eq:VJwindow} follow without differentiation of an uncontrolled $O$ term.

On the large branch $U=d+2J$. When $d<0$ this is \emph{not} an identity on the full set of maximal matchings. Equation~\eqref{tmm:bd:eq:suppress} and polynomial boundedness of $J,U$ transfer the moments to the full model, proving~\eqref{tmm:bd:eq:EUwindow}--\eqref{tmm:bd:eq:VUwindow}.

The normalized first-order pointwise expansion, its Gaussian-weighted error, and the ratio tail estimates imply~\eqref{tmm:bd:eq:localwindow}. Summing its weighted form gives total variation $O_K(N^{-1})$ against~\eqref{tmm:bd:eq:Qwindow}; the denominator is $N$ up to an exponentially small relative error by~\eqref{tmm:bd:eq:lattice}. The full and conditional large-branch laws differ in total variation by at most the exceptional branch probability, so this is valid unconditionally. For $U$, couple it to $d+2J$ on the same matching; equality fails only on that exceptional event.

Finally Riemann-sum approximation of a Gaussian distribution function on mesh $1/N$ has uniform error $O_K(N^{-1})$: bound each cell's error by the integral of the density derivative times its width, and use the finite uniformly bounded $L^1$ norm of that derivative. Combining this with the total-variation bound proves the Kolmogorov assertions.
\end{proof}

If $\delta_n\to\tau$, the centering using $s_n,b_n$ gives convergence of $(J-s_nN^2-b_nN)/N$ to $\Normal(0,1/\chi(\tau))$. Centering by the actual mean and standardizing by the actual variance gives the standard normal limit as well. These are statements with the exact $\delta_n$ retained in the centering; replacing it by its limit requires separate error control.

\subsection{Why rounding cannot be dropped}
The phase satisfies $\Phi'(\delta)=-\log u$. If $d=\tau N^2+r_n$ with bounded $r_n$, then
\[
 N^2\{\Phi(d/N^2)-\Phi(\tau)\}=-r_n\log u(\tau)+O(N^{-2}).
\]
Thus replacement by $\tau$ can introduce a nonvanishing factor $u(\tau)^{-r_n}$ in the phase contribution. The factors $N^{-2d}$ and $\Gamma(2n+d+1)$ also require the actual integer $d$. Formula~\eqref{tmm:bd:eq:window} includes arbitrary rounding and parity automatically by using $\delta=d/N^2$ exactly.

\section{The exact boundary and four corrections}\label{tmm:bd:sec:boundary}
At $d=0$, the parameters simplify to
\[
 s=a,\quad u=2a,\quad\chi=3/a,\quad b=-2a^2/3,\quad v=a/3,
 \quad\Phi=3a,\quad C=1/12.
\]
In particular the constant $1/12$ is forced by completing the square: the original constant is $-a^3/3$, and the shift contributes $2a^3/3$, leaving $a^3/3=1/12$.

\begin{corollary}\label{tmm:bd:cor:boundary}
For each fixed $M\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bd:eq:boundaryasym}
 A_n=\frac{(2n)!e^{3aN^2-a^2N+1/12}}{\sqrt{12\pi a}\,N}
       \left\{\sum_{k=0}^Mc_kN^{-k}+O_M(N^{-M-1})\right\}.
\end{equation}
Here $c_0=1$, and the first four relative coefficients are
\begin{align}
 c_1&=\frac{211a}{216},&
 c_2&=\frac{172781a^2}{466560},\notag\\

\section{Boundary-safe inverse enclosures}\label{tmm:bd:sec:inverse}
\begin{lemma}\label{tmm:bd:lem:monotone}
$A_{n+1}\ge2A_n$ for every $n\ge0$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Adjoin two labeled vertices to the large part and one to each small part. Match the new large vertices to the new small vertices in either of the two bijections. Either extension remains maximal by Lemma~\ref{tmm:bd:lem:maximal}. The two images are disjoint, and restriction to old vertices recovers the old matching.
\end{proof}
\begin{writenote}
This lemma concerns the boundary sequence $M(2n,n,n)$. Part~I's Lemma~\ref{tmm:bal:lem:monotone} is the balanced counterpart (nondecreasing, by a different injection).
\end{writenote}
For fixed $M$ define, for sufficiently large real $x$,
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bd:eq:BM}
 B_M(x)=\frac{\Gamma(2x+1)e^{3ax^{2/3}-a^2x^{1/3}+1/12}}
 {\sqrt{12\pi a}\,x^{1/3}}\sum_{k=0}^Mc_kx^{-k/3}.
\end{equation}
The finite sum is eventually positive. Its logarithmic derivative is
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bd:eq:BMder}
 (\log B_M)'(x)=2\log(2x)+O(x^{-1/3}),
\end{equation}
so it is eventually strictly increasing and unbounded. For sufficiently large $y$, let $x_M(y)$ be its unique large solution of $B_M(x)=y$ and let
\[
 H_*(y)=\min\{n\ge0:A_n\ge y\}.
\]
\begin{theorem}\label{tmm:bd:thm:inverse}
There exist constants $C_M,y_M>0$ such that for $y\ge y_M$, with $x_M=x_M(y)$ and
\[
 \Delta_M=C_M\frac{x_M^{-(M+1)/3}}{\log x_M},
\]
one has the boundary-safe enclosure
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bd:eq:inverse}
 \ceil{x_M-\Delta_M}\ \le H_*(y)\le\ \ceil{x_M+\Delta_M}.
\end{equation}
It eventually contains at most two consecutive integers.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Corollary~\ref{tmm:bd:cor:boundary} implies
\[
 |\log A_n-\log B_M(n)|\le Cn^{-(M+1)/3}
\]
for sufficiently large $n$. On a neighborhood of $x_M$,~\eqref{tmm:bd:eq:BMder} is bounded below by a positive multiple of $\log x_M$. The mean-value theorem, with $C_M$ enlarged, implies $A_n<y$ for every nearby integer $n<x_M-\Delta_M$, and $A_n>y$ for every nearby integer $n\ge x_M+\Delta_M$. Monotonicity extends these comparisons outside the neighborhood, and the finitely many small $n$ are below $y$ eventually. Taking ceilings proves~\eqref{tmm:bd:eq:inverse}. Since $2\Delta_M\to0$, its endpoints are eventually equal or consecutive.
\end{proof}
All constants and the onset here are existential. This is not a numerically certified inverse algorithm for arbitrary $y$, and no unconditional rule $H_*(y)=\ceil{x_M(y)}$ follows when $x_M$ is near an integer.

The leading Lambert approximation is
\[
 x_0=\frac{\log y}{2W((\log y)/e)},
\]
which solves $2x_0\log(2x_0/e)=\log y$. Its first displacement is
\[
 x_M-x_0\sim-\frac{3ax_0^{2/3}}{2\log(2x_0)}.
\]
Indeed the missing leading term in the logarithmic count is $3ax_0^{2/3}$, and the leading derivative is $2\log(2x_0)$; the relative displacement tends to zero, making the Taylor remainder smaller. This is only a leading displacement, not a complete ordered reversion series. Implicit inversion of~\eqref{tmm:bd:eq:BM} avoids mixing logarithmic and fractional-power scales.
\begin{writenote}[6 October 2026]
$x_0$ is the factorial core of the transseries volume \cite{TSvol} with $\kappa_\vol=2$, $d_\vol=2(\log2-1)$ (an instance), and $2\log(2x_0)$ is its slope $\kappa_\vol(v_\vol+1)$. The displayed displacement is the first term of the volume's perturbation-operator series with $\varrho_\vol(x)=3ax^{2/3}$ (an analogue). Theorem~\ref{tmm:bd:thm:inverse} is proved directly at the integers and is not an instance of the volume's staircase theorem. Details in the front matter.
\end{writenote}

\section{The fixed exterior ratio and its Poisson expansion}\label{tmm:bd:sec:exterior}
Now suppose the exact ratio $c_n=d/n$ ranges in a fixed compact subset $K_+\subset(0,\infty)$. Write $c=c_n$ and $\lambda=c^{-2}$. Only the large branch is possible. The $j=0$ term including its factorial carrier is
\[
 T_0=\frac{\Gamma(2n+d+1)}{\Gamma(d+1)}.
\]
Relative to it, the terms are
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bd:eq:exteriorR}
 R_j=\frac{\fall nj^2\Gamma(d+1)}{j!\Gamma(d+2j+1)},\quad0\le j\le n,
 \qquad A(n,d)=T_0\sum_{j=0}^nR_j.
\end{equation}
Here $\fall nj=n(n-1)\cdots(n-j+1)$, with empty product $1$. In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bd:eq:exteriordom}
 0\le R_j\le\frac{(n/d)^{2j}}{j!}=\frac{\lambda^j}{j!}.
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}\label{tmm:bd:thm:exterior}
For every fixed $M\ge0$, rational functions $\kappa_r(c)$ with $\kappa_0=1$ satisfy, uniformly on $K_+$,
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bd:eq:exteriorall}
 A(n,d)=T_0e^\lambda\left\{\sum_{r=0}^M\kappa_r(c)n^{-r}
                                    +O_{K_+,M}(n^{-M-1})\right\}.
\end{equation}
In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bd:eq:exteriorfirst}
 \kappa_1(c)=-(c^{-4}+2c^{-5}+3c^{-3}),\qquad
 \TV(\mathcal L(J),\Poi(c^{-2}))=O_{K_+}(n^{-1}).
\end{equation}
Here $U=d+2J$ exactly. A maximum matching has $2n$ edges; $J$ is exactly its edge-count deficit. If $c_n\to c_*>0$, then $J\Rightarrow\Poi(c_*^{-2})$ in total variation and $U-d$ converges to twice that Poisson variable.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For fixed $j$, finite products in~\eqref{tmm:bd:eq:exteriorR} give $R_j/(\lambda^j/j!)\to1$. Bound~\eqref{tmm:bd:eq:exteriordom} already proves the leading count and total-variation convergence by dominated summation. To prove the full expansion and rate, write
\begin{align}
 \log\frac{R_j}{\lambda^j/j!}
 &=2\sum_{h=0}^{j-1}\log(1-h/n)-\sum_{h=1}^{2j}\log(1+h/(cn))\notag\\
 &=\sum_{m=1}^M\beta_m(j;c)n^{-m}
       +O_{K_+,M}\bigl(n^{-M-1}(1+j^{M+2})\bigr),\label{tmm:bd:eq:betaseries}\\
 \beta_m(j;c)&=-\frac2mS_m(j)
           +\frac{(-1)^m}{mc^m}\sum_{h=1}^{2j}h^m.\label{tmm:bd:eq:beta}
\end{align}
These are exact coefficient polynomials in $j$; the remainder is uniform on $j\le n^\varepsilon$ for a sufficiently small fixed positive $\varepsilon$. Define $E_0=1$ and
\[
 E_r(j;c)=\frac1r\sum_{m=1}^rm\beta_m(j;c)E_{r-m}(j;c).
\]
Then a finite exponential Taylor expansion gives a polynomial-weighted remainder of order $n^{-M-1}$ on this central range. Choose $\varepsilon$ small enough to keep the retained perturbation bounded there. Every resulting polynomial may be summed against $\lambda^j/j!$. The complementary tail $j>n^\varepsilon$ is smaller than every fixed inverse power, also after any fixed polynomial insertion, because $\lambda$ remains in a fixed positive compact interval and~\eqref{tmm:bd:eq:exteriordom} holds globally. Thus
\begin{equation}\label{tmm:bd:eq:kappaalg}
 \kappa_r(c)=\E[E_r(P;c)],\qquad P\sim\Poi(c^{-2}),
\end{equation}
proves~\eqref{tmm:bd:eq:exteriorall}. Poisson moments are finite polynomials in $\lambda$ (for example via factorial moments $\E\fall Pk=\lambda^k$), so the algorithm is rational and finite.

The first phase coefficient is $\beta_1=-j(j-1)-j(2j+1)/c$. Taking Poisson expectation gives~\eqref{tmm:bd:eq:exteriorfirst}. The same weighted expansion of the masses, followed by normalizing and summing absolute errors, gives total variation $O(n^{-1})$. Finally the graph has only $2n$ vertices outside the large part, so no matching has more than $2n$ edges, and the $j=0$ term attains that number. This proves the deficit interpretation.
\end{proof}
The maximum-matching probability here also has an exact numerator, $T_0$, so its expansion is the reciprocal of the factor multiplying $T_0$ in~\eqref{tmm:bd:eq:exteriorall}. In particular it equals
\[
 e^{-\lambda}\{1+(c^{-4}+2c^{-5}+3c^{-3})/n+O_{K_+}(n^{-2})\}.
\]
It is no longer exponentially small in $n$ when $c$ stays in a positive compact interval.
