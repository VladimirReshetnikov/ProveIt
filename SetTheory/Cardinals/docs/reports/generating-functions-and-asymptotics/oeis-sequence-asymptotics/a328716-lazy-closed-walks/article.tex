\begin{abstract}
Let $a_n$ count the $n$-step walks from the origin to itself in $\mathbb Z^n$, with steps $0,\pm e_1,\ldots,\pm e_n$. This is OEIS A328716. We prove its known parity-dependent leading equivalent, give exact Bessel definitions of its constants, and construct every fixed inverse-power correction, including three explicit terms. The marked expansion has an absolute complex-uniform remainder, so it remains valid when its leading amplitude vanishes.

For a uniformly chosen bridge, the zero-step count has an all-orders exponentially weighted $\ell^1$ expansion about a parity-conditioned Poisson law. The number of occupied coordinate axes has Gaussian fluctuations, asymptotically independent of that residual Poisson variable, and an explicit parity-dependent constant mean correction. Separate inverse carriers retain both parity constants and give an eventual global integer bracket of width at most one. A compact proportional length-to-dimension extension retains the moving Poisson parameter.

The exact generating function and numerical leading equivalent were already recorded in the OEIS. Bessel walk enumeration and full large-powers expansions are classical. This is a family-specific derivation and refinement, with bounded source comparison, not a claim of an open-problem resolution or historical priority. Finite exact verification and optional high-precision diagnostics accompany the proofs; no effective inverse error constant or certified numerical starting threshold is claimed.
\end{abstract}

\part{Lazy closed walks in growing dimension: all fixed orders, parity conditioned Poisson laws, and occupied coordinate fluctuations for OEIS A328716}\label{lcw:part:one}

\section{The model and the scope of the results}\label{lcw:sec:scope}
A walk is an ordered sequence of steps from
$\{0,+e_1,-e_1,\ldots,+e_D,-e_D\}$, where the axes are labeled and the zero step is a single available step. A bridge has total displacement zero. Let
\[
 A_{N,D}(u)=\sum_{\text{$N$-step bridges in $\mathbb Z^D$}}u^{\text{number of zero steps}},
 \qquad A_n(u)=A_{n,n}(u),\qquad a_n=A_n(1).
\]
The empty bridge is counted once, including in dimension zero. Random bridges below are uniform over this finite set; they are equivalently uniform-step random walks conditioned to return to the origin. We write $J_n$ for their zero-step count and $K_n$ for the number of axes used at least once. An axis is counted once regardless of how many steps use it.

OEIS A328716~\cite{oeis} records the exact diagonal formula, attributed to Ilya Gutkovskiy on 26 October 2019, and a leading equivalent with numerical parity constants, attributed to V\'aclav Kot\v e\v sovec on 27 October 2019. The general array A328718~\cite{array} identifies A328716 as its diagonal; its entry is due to Seiichi Manyama, and its walk interpretation is attributed to Alois P. Heinz. The classical Bessel-product walk generating function is given by Gessel, Weinstein, and Wilf~\cite{gww}, equation (2). Our lazy step appends an elementary exponential factor.

The large-powers method, including full descending-power expansions and compact-ratio uniformity, is classical; see Flajolet and Sedgewick~\cite{fs}, Theorem VIII.8 and its proof. Their subsequent remarks also discuss vanishing multipliers. We give complete arguments specialized to this period-two family, rather than interpreting the existence of a full expansion as a new analytic theorem. The explicit coefficients, marked probability refinements, occupation calculation, and inverse brackets are the objects of this report. No exhaustive historical novelty claim is made.

Every all-orders statement means a Poincar\'e expansion: the order $M$ is fixed before the size parameter tends to infinity. Constants and onsets may depend on $M$ and any specified compact sets. We do not assert convergence of the infinite series, allow $M$ to grow, or supply computable remainder constants.

\section{Exact enumeration and tilted coordinates}\label{lcw:sec:exact}
Put
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:eq:F}
 F(z)=I_0(2z)=\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{z^{2k}}{(k!)^2},\qquad
 \DD=z\frac{d}{dz}.
\end{equation}
The defining series is sufficient for all arguments; no Bessel asymptotic formula is required.

\begin{proposition}\label{lcw:prop:exact}
For nonnegative integers $N,D$,
\begin{align}
 A_{N,D}(u)&=N![z^N]e^{uz}F(z)^D,\label{lcw:eq:exact}\\
 A_n(u,w)&:=\sum_{\text{bridges}}u^{J_n}w^{K_n}
 =n![z^n]e^{uz}\{1+w(F(z)-1)\}^n.\label{lcw:eq:joint}
\end{align}
In particular $J_n\equiv n\pmod2$, $0\le K_n\le\lfloor(n-J_n)/2\rfloor$, and
$a_{n+1}>a_n$ for every $n\ge1$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
If there are $j$ zero steps and $k_i$ positive and $k_i$ negative steps in axis $i$, then
$j+2\sum_i k_i=N$ and the number of orderings is
\[
 \frac{N!}{j!\prod_i(k_i!)^2}.
\]
Summing proves \eqref{lcw:eq:exact}; marking each nonzero $k_i$ proves \eqref{lcw:eq:joint}. The parity and support assertions follow from the same constraint. To prove strict monotonicity, embed a bridge in $\mathbb Z^{n+1}$ and append a zero step. This is injective. A bridge using $+e_{n+1}$ once, $-e_{n+1}$ once, and $n-1$ zero steps lies outside the image. The only initial equality is $a_0=a_1=1$.
\end{proof}

For any $r>0$ define an even-valued variable $X_r$ by

\section{The saddle and exact constants}\label{lcw:sec:constants}
\begin{lemma}\label{lcw:lem:saddle}
There is a unique $r>0$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:eq:saddle}
 \DD\log F(r)=\frac{2rI_1(2r)}{I_0(2r)}=1.
\end{equation}
Writing $b=\DD^2\log F(r)$, one has $b=4r^2-1>0$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The mean in \eqref{lcw:eq:tilt} is strictly increasing in $\log r$, since its derivative is the positive variance. It tends to zero as $r\downarrow0$. It tends to infinity as $r\to\infty$: for any finite support cutoff, comparison with one higher positive term of $F$ shows that the probability below the cutoff tends to zero. Thus the intermediate value theorem gives the unique solution.

Termwise differentiation of \eqref{lcw:eq:F} gives
$z^2F''+zF'-4z^2F=0$. With $a(z)=\DD\log F(z)$ this becomes
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:eq:riccati}
 \DD a=4z^2-a^2.
\end{equation}
At $a(r)=1$ it gives the asserted expression for $b$.
\end{proof}

\section{An absolute expansion to every fixed order}\label{lcw:sec:expansion}
For an amplitude $A$ analytic near $r$, define a linear functional $\TT_\ell A$ as follows. Sum over $j\ge0$ and finitely supported nonnegative integers $(m_h)_{h\ge3}$ satisfying
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:eq:index}
 j+\sum_{h\ge3}(h-2)m_h=2\ell,\qquad
 q=j+\sum_{h\ge3}hm_h.
\end{equation}
The integer $q=2\ell+2\sum_hm_h$ is even. Set
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:eq:operator}
 \TT_\ell A=
 \sum_{\eqref{lcw:eq:index}}
 \frac{(\DD^jA)(r)}{j!}
 \frac{(-1)^{q/2}(q-1)!!}{b^{q/2}}
 \prod_{h\ge3}\frac{\kappa_h^{m_h}}{m_h!(h!)^{m_h}}.
\end{equation}
Here $(-1)!!=1$. The sum is finite, uses amplitude derivatives only through $2\ell$, and cumulants only through $2\ell+2$. In particular $\TT_0A=A(r)$.

Let $s_j$ be the classical Stirling coefficients, specified to all fixed orders by the formal identity
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:eq:stirling}
 \sum_{j\ge0}s_jt^j=
 \exp\left(\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{B_{2k}}{2k(2k-1)}t^{2k-1}\right),
 \qquad
 \frac{x}{e^x-1}=\sum_{j\ge0}B_j\frac{x^j}{j!}.
\end{equation}
Thus $s_0=1$, $s_1=1/12$, $s_2=1/288$, $s_3=-139/51840$. Define
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:eq:Poperator}
 \PP_\ell=\sum_{h=0}^{\ell}s_h\TT_{\ell-h},\qquad
 E_{\epsilon,u}(z)=e^{uz}+\epsilon e^{-uz}.
\end{equation}
All series used for coefficient extraction in this report are formal when an infinite expansion is displayed.

\begin{theorem}\label{lcw:thm:absolute}
For each fixed $M\ge0$ and $R<\infty$, uniformly for complex $|u|\le R$ as $n\to\infty$,
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:eq:absolute}
 A_n(u)=\frac{(dn)^n}{\sqrt b}
 \left\{\sum_{\ell=0}^M\frac{\PP_\ell E_{\epsilon,u}}{n^\ell}
       +O_{M,R}(n^{-M-1})\right\},\qquad \epsilon=(-1)^n.
\end{equation}
The error inside braces is absolute, including at zeros of $E_\epsilon(ru)$.
In particular, with $c_{\epsilon,\ell}=\PP_\ell E_{\epsilon,1}/E_\epsilon(r)$,
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:eq:main}
 a_n=C_\epsilon(dn)^n
 \left\{\sum_{\ell=0}^M\frac{c_{\epsilon,\ell}}{n^\ell}
          +O_M(n^{-M-1})\right\},\qquad c_{\epsilon,0}=1.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\subsection{The two dominant arcs}
Cauchy's formula on $|z|=r$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:eq:cauchy}
 [z^n]e^{uz}F(z)^n
 =\frac{F(r)^nr^{-n}}{2\pi}
 \int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{ur e^{i\theta}}
 \left(\frac{F(re^{i\theta})}{F(r)}\right)^n e^{-in\theta}\,d\theta.
\end{equation}
Equality in $|F(re^{i\theta})|\le F(r)$ requires $e^{2ik\theta}=1$ for every $k\ge0$, since every even coefficient is strictly positive and the constant coefficient is nonzero. Hence it occurs only at $\theta=0,\pi$ modulo $2\pi$. Outside fixed small neighborhoods of these points the ratio is bounded by a constant $\gamma<1$. The amplitude is uniformly bounded for $|u|\le R$, so those arcs are exponentially small.

Near zero an analytic logarithm exists and
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:eq:phase}
 \phi(\theta):=\log\frac{F(re^{i\theta})}{F(r)}-i\theta
 =-\frac{b\theta^2}{2}+\sum_{h\ge3}\frac{\kappa_h(i\theta)^h}{h!}.
\end{equation}
By shrinking the neighborhood, $\Re\phi(\theta)\le-b\theta^2/4$. Near $\pi$, translating the angle by $\pi$ gives the identical phase and amplitude
$\epsilon e^{-ur e^{i\theta}}$. Thus the two local integrals combine into a single symmetric integral with amplitude $E_{\epsilon,u}(re^{i\theta})$.

\subsection{A controlled integrated remainder}\label{lcw:sec:remainder}
Fix any $0<\delta<1/2$ and restrict further to $|\theta|\le n^{-1/2+\delta}$. The removed intermediate arcs contribute $O(\exp(-c n^{2\delta}))$ relative to the saddle scale, even after multiplication by any fixed power of $n$. Put $t=\sqrt n\theta$ and $v=n^{-1/2}$. After extracting $e^{-bt^2/2}$, the remaining phase exponent is
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:eq:H}
 H(v,t)=\sum_{h\ge3}\frac{\kappa_h(it)^h v^{h-2}}{h!},
\end{equation}
an analytic function of $v$ at zero. On $|t|\le n^\delta$, uniformly for $0\le v'\le v$, convergence of the phase series yields
\[
 |H(v',t)|\le C v|t|^3\le C n^{-1/2+\delta}t^2.
\]
For all sufficiently large $n$ the last constant is smaller than, for example, $b/8$. Each fixed derivative of $H$ in $v'$ is bounded by a fixed polynomial in $|t|$, uniformly on this set. This follows by differentiating its convergent series with $|v't|$ in a fixed sufficiently small disk. Likewise the $k$th $v'$ derivative of $A(re^{iv't})$ is bounded by $C_k|t|^k$ for amplitudes in any compact analytic family.

The product $A(re^{iv't})e^{H(v',t)}$ therefore has every fixed $v'$ derivative bounded by a polynomial in $|t|$ times $e^{bt^2/8}$. Taylor's formula through degree $2M+1$ in $v$ has, after multiplication by $e^{-bt^2/2}$ and integration, a remainder
\[
 O(v^{2M+2})\int_{\mathbb R}(1+|t|)^L e^{-3bt^2/8}\,dt
 =O(n^{-M-1}).
\]
This bound is uniform for $A=E_{\epsilon,u}$, $|u|\le R$. A coefficient of odd degree in $v$ is an odd polynomial in $t$: in the notation \eqref{lcw:eq:index}, its total $t$ degree differs from its $v$ degree by an even integer. Such coefficients integrate to zero on the symmetric interval. Replacing each remaining finite-range Gaussian moment by the full moment has an exponentially small error.

The normalized Gaussian moments are
\[
 \frac{\int_{\mathbb R}(it)^q e^{-bt^2/2}\,dt}
 {\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-bt^2/2}\,dt}
 =(-1)^{q/2}(q-1)!!b^{-q/2}\quad(q\text{ even}).
\]
Expanding the amplitude and the exponential in \eqref{lcw:eq:H} now gives precisely \eqref{lcw:eq:operator}, with coefficient constraint \eqref{lcw:eq:index}. We have proved
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:eq:pre-stirling}
 [z^n]e^{uz}F(z)^n
 =\frac{F(r)^nr^{-n}}{\sqrt{2\pi nb}}
 \left\{\sum_{\ell=0}^M\frac{\TT_\ell E_{\epsilon,u}}{n^\ell}
 +O_{M,R}(n^{-M-1})\right\}.
\end{equation}
Finally use the fixed-order Stirling expansion
$n!=\sqrt{2\pi n}(n/e)^n(\sum_{j=0}^M s_jn^{-j}+O_M(n^{-M-1}))$.
Multiplying gives \eqref{lcw:eq:absolute}. Since $E_\epsilon(r)>0$, dividing only at $u=1$ proves \eqref{lcw:eq:main}. This completes the proof of Theorem~\ref{lcw:thm:absolute}.

\begin{remark}\label{lcw:rem:zeros}
For even $n$, $u=i\pi/(2r)$ makes $E_+(ru)=0$; for odd $n$, $u=i\pi/r$ makes $E_-(ru)=0$. Formula \eqref{lcw:eq:absolute} still controls the first nonzero correction there. A relative error of the form $E_\epsilon(ru)(1+O(n^{-1}))$ would not give this statement and generally would be false at these points. No division by a marked leading amplitude occurs in the proof.
\end{remark}

\section{Zero steps and weighted probability expansions}\label{lcw:sec:poisson}
Let $J_\epsilon$ be $\Poi(r)$ conditioned on $(-1)^{J_\epsilon}=\epsilon$. Write its mass function and probability generating function as
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:eq:poisson}
 \pi_\epsilon(j)=
 \begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{2r^j}{j!E_\epsilon(r)},&(-1)^j=\epsilon,\\[4pt]0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}
 \qquad
 \E u^{J_\epsilon}=\frac{E_\epsilon(ru)}{E_\epsilon(r)}.
\end{equation}
In particular $\E J_\epsilon=v_\epsilon$ and $\E J_\epsilon^2=r^2+v_\epsilon$.

\begin{theorem}\label{lcw:thm:weighted}
For every fixed $M\ge0$ and $R>1$, there are polynomials $Q_{\epsilon,\ell}(j)$ of degree at most $2\ell$, with $Q_{\epsilon,0}=1$, such that
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:eq:weighted}
 \sum_{j\ge0}R^j\left|\Pp(J_n=j)-\pi_\epsilon(j)
 \sum_{\ell=0}^M\frac{Q_{\epsilon,\ell}(j)}{n^\ell}\right|
 =O_{M,R}(n^{-M-1}),\qquad\epsilon=(-1)^n.
\end{equation}
For $\ell\ge1$, $\E Q_{\epsilon,\ell}(J_\epsilon)=0$. The first polynomial is
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:eq:Q1}
 Q_{\epsilon,1}(j)=-\frac{j^2-r^2-v_\epsilon}{2b}
                       +\frac{j-v_\epsilon}{b^2}.
\end{equation}
Consequently the total-variation distance from $J_\epsilon$ is $O(n^{-1})$, and every fixed polynomial moment has an expansion to all fixed orders.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Choose $R'>R$. Theorem~\ref{lcw:thm:absolute} holds uniformly on $|u|\le R'$. Dividing by the expansion at $u=1$ is legitimate since its leading coefficient is the positive $E_\epsilon(r)$. Finite series division gives a probability-generating-function expansion with an absolute remainder uniformly $O(n^{-M-1})$ on that disk.

To identify its coefficients explicitly, define the monomial polynomials
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:eq:monomial}
 H_\ell(j)=r^{-j}\PP_\ell(z^j).
\end{equation}
Since $\DD^kz^j=j^kz^j$, these are polynomials in $j$ of degree at most $2\ell$, and
\[
 c_{\epsilon,\ell}=\E H_\ell(J_\epsilon).
\]
The probability polynomials are specified by the finite recurrence
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:eq:Qrec}
 Q_{\epsilon,0}=1,\qquad
 Q_{\epsilon,\ell}(j)=H_\ell(j)-\sum_{h=1}^\ell
 c_{\epsilon,h}Q_{\epsilon,\ell-h}(j).
\end{equation}
Indeed the coefficient of $u^j$ in $\PP_\ell E_{\epsilon,u}$ is
$(1+\epsilon(-1)^j)r^jH_\ell(j)/j!$. Dividing by the unmarked denominator gives \eqref{lcw:eq:Qrec}, including exact zeros at the wrong parity. Taking expectation in that recurrence proves centering inductively.

If $\mathcal R_n(u)$ denotes the analytic remainder after quotient expansion, Cauchy's inequality gives
\[
 |[u^j]\mathcal R_n(u)|\le C n^{-M-1}(R')^{-j}.
\]
Multiplication by $R^j$ and summation of the geometric series proves \eqref{lcw:eq:weighted}. This step controls the infinite tail; pointwise coefficient convergence alone would not suffice.

Finally \eqref{lcw:eq:T1}, $\kappa_3=2$, and the subtraction of the mean in \eqref{lcw:eq:Qrec} give \eqref{lcw:eq:Q1}. Total variation is one half the unweighted $\ell^1$ norm. Any fixed polynomial is bounded in absolute value by a constant times $R^j$, giving the moment statement.
\end{proof}

The finite correction in \eqref{lcw:eq:weighted} is a signed approximation; it is not asserted to be a probability mass function term by term. Setting $M=0$ shows $J_n\Rightarrow J_\epsilon$ along each parity subsequence. The whole sequence has no single zero-step weak limit, since even and odd support remain disjoint. In particular the zero steps stay of order one even though the walk length and dimension both grow.

\section{Occupied axes and a Gaussian product limit}\label{lcw:sec:occupation}
Put
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:eq:occupation-constants}
 f=F(r),\qquad q=f^{-1},\qquad p=1-q,\qquad
 \tau^2=pq-\frac{q^2}{b}.
\end{equation}
Numerically,
\[

 \tau^2=0.041597460122151058557181923\ldots.
\]

\begin{theorem}\label{lcw:thm:occupation}
One has $\tau^2>0$ and
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:eq:occupation-clt}
 \frac{K_n-np}{\sqrt n}\ \Rightarrow\ \Normal(0,\tau^2).
\end{equation}
Along a fixed parity $\epsilon=(-1)^n$, jointly
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:eq:product}
 \left(\frac{K_n-np}{\sqrt n},J_n\right)
 \ \Rightarrow\ (G,J_\epsilon),
\end{equation}
where $G\sim\Normal(0,\tau^2)$ is independent of $J_\epsilon$. Moreover
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:eq:meanoffset}
 \E K_n=np-\frac qb\left(v_\epsilon+\frac{b-1}{2}-\frac1b\right)
 +O(n^{-1}),\qquad \Var K_n=n\tau^2+O(1).
\end{equation}
The constant mean offsets are approximately $-0.071243028912$ for even $n$ and $-0.311664057977$ for odd $n$.
\end{theorem}

\subsection{An analytic moving saddle}
Let
\[
 B_w(z)=1+w(F(z)-1).
\]
Near $w=1$, the equation $\DD\log B_w(r(w))=1$ has an analytic solution with $r(1)=r$. The implicit-function theorem applies in the variable $\log r$, because the derivative is $b>0$. Shrink to a fixed complex neighborhood of $w=1$ in which $r(w)$, $B_w(r(w))$, and
\[
 b(w)=\DD^2\log B_w(r(w))
\]
are nonzero and $\Re b(w)$ is bounded below by a positive constant. Take logarithm and square-root branches from their real positive values at $w=1$, and put
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:eq:h}
 h(w)=\log B_w(r(w))-\log r(w).
\end{equation}

For completeness the moving contour is valid also for complex $w$. The parametrization $z=r(w)e^{i\theta}$ describes a positively oriented circle of radius $|r(w)|$, merely with a rotated starting point. The coefficient integrand is entire except for its pole at zero, so Cauchy's formula applies on this circle. At $\theta=0$ the exact saddle equation cancels the linear term and gives quadratic term $-b(w)\theta^2/2$. Since $B_w$ is even, the second saddle is exactly $-r(w)$ and its phase factor is still $\epsilon$.

At $w=1$ the compact complement of the two local arcs has a strict normalized modulus gap. Continuity preserves it uniformly for all complex $w$ in a sufficiently small closed neighborhood of one. Local logarithms exist on the two arcs; a global logarithm is not needed. The integrated Taylor-remainder argument of Section~\ref{lcw:sec:remainder} then holds with complex analytic cumulants, uniformly in $w$ and bounded $u$. The Gaussian integral is $\sqrt{2\pi/b(w)}$ with the specified branch, by analytic continuation in the right half-plane or direct Gaussian integration. In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:eq:moving-leading}
 A_n(u,w)=\left(\frac ne\right)^n\frac{e^{nh(w)}}{\sqrt{b(w)}}
 \left\{E_\epsilon(ur(w))+O(n^{-1})\right\}.
\end{equation}
The remainder inside braces is absolute and analytic in $u,w$. The full fixed-order version follows in the same way. Cauchy's derivative estimate on a smaller $w$ disk allows its uniform remainders to be differentiated any fixed number of times.

\subsection{The variance density and limiting independence}
Write $H(s)=h(e^s)$ and $x(s)=\log r(e^s)$. At $s=0$ consider the pair $(X,I)$, where $X=X_r$ and $I=\one_{\{X>0\}}$. The log partition function is
\[
 \Psi(s,x)=\log\left(1+e^s(F(e^x)-1)\right).
\]
The saddle constraint is $\Psi_x(s,x(s))=1$. At $(0,\log r)$ its derivatives are
\[
 \Psi_s=p,\quad \Psi_{ss}=pq,\quad \Psi_{xx}=b,\quad
 \Psi_{sx}=\Cov(I,X)=q.
\]
The covariance identity follows from $IX=X$ and $\E X=1$. Differentiating the constraint and $H(s)=\Psi(s,x(s))-x(s)$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:eq:Hderivatives}
 x'(0)=-\frac qb,\qquad H'(0)=p,\qquad H''(0)=pq-\frac{q^2}{b}=\tau^2.
\end{equation}
Strict Cauchy--Schwarz makes $\tau^2>0$, since $I$ is not an affine function of $X$ on its positive-probability support $0,2,4,\ldots$.

For real $t,y$, put $w=e^{it/\sqrt n}$ and $u=e^{iy}$ in \eqref{lcw:eq:moving-leading}. Divide by $A_n(1,1)$ and multiply by $e^{-itp\sqrt n}$. Taylor expansion gives
\[
 n\{H(it/\sqrt n)-H(0)\}-itp\sqrt n
 =-\frac{\tau^2t^2}{2}+O(n^{-1/2}).
\]
The remaining amplitude tends to $E_\epsilon(re^{iy})/E_\epsilon(r)$, including at its zeros because the remainder was absolute. The joint characteristic functions therefore tend to
\[
 e^{-\tau^2t^2/2}\frac{E_\epsilon(re^{iy})}{E_\epsilon(r)}.
\]
This is the characteristic function of the independent product in \eqref{lcw:eq:product}; L\'evy's continuity theorem applies. Setting $y=0$ proves \eqref{lcw:eq:occupation-clt} without a parity restriction. These are weak limits, not total-variation convergence from a lattice variable to a continuous Gaussian.

\subsection{The constant mean correction}
At $u=1$ the leading amplitude in \eqref{lcw:eq:moving-leading} is nonzero in a small $w$ neighborhood. Its analytic logarithm yields
\[
 \log\frac{A_n(1,e^s)}{A_n(1,1)}
 =n\{H(s)-H(0)\}
 +\log\frac{E_\epsilon(r(e^s))}{E_\epsilon(r)}
 -\frac12\log\frac{b(e^s)}b+O(n^{-1}).
\]
The remainder may be differentiated, as established above. At fixed $x$, differentiating $\Psi_{xx}$ with respect to $s$ gives
$\Psi_{xxs}=q(b-1)$. One direct check is
$\DD p=q$ and $\DD^2p=q(b-1)$ at the saddle. Allowing $x$ to move then gives, using $\kappa_3=2$,
\[
 \left.\frac d{ds}b(e^s)\right|_{s=0}
 =q(b-1)+\kappa_3x'(0)=q\left(b-1-\frac2b\right).
\]
The derivative of $\log E_\epsilon(r(e^s))$ is $v_\epsilon x'(0)$. Together with \eqref{lcw:eq:Hderivatives} these prove the expectation formula in \eqref{lcw:eq:meanoffset}. Two derivatives prove the variance statement. The same analytic expansion supplies all fixed cumulant corrections, although only the first mean offset is needed here.

\section{Parity sensitive inversion}\label{lcw:sec:inverse}
Let
\[
 \nu(X)=\min\{n\ge0:a_n\ge X\},\qquad X\to\infty.
\]
Strict monotonicity from Proposition~\ref{lcw:prop:exact} makes this a genuine threshold. For each parity let $\nu_\epsilon(X)$ be the first such integer of parity $\epsilon$, so $\nu(X)=\min_\epsilon\nu_\epsilon(X)$.

Define the logarithmic coefficients formally by
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:eq:logcoef}
 \ell_{\epsilon,j}=[t^j]\log\left(\sum_{k\ge0}c_{\epsilon,k}t^k\right).
\end{equation}
For example $\ell_{\epsilon,1}=c_{\epsilon,1}$,
$\ell_{\epsilon,2}=c_{\epsilon,2}-c_{\epsilon,1}^2/2$, and
$\ell_{\epsilon,3}=c_{\epsilon,3}-c_{\epsilon,1}c_{\epsilon,2}+c_{\epsilon,1}^3/3$.
For fixed $M\ge0$ put
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:eq:carrier}
 \mathcal F_{\epsilon,M}(t)=t\log(dt)+\log C_\epsilon
                     +\sum_{j=1}^M\ell_{\epsilon,j}t^{-j}.
\end{equation}
It is eventually increasing, with derivative $1+\log(dt)+O_M(t^{-2})$, and tends to infinity. For large $X$ let $t_{\epsilon,M}(X)$ be its unique sufficiently large solution of $\mathcal F_{\epsilon,M}(t)=\log X$. Write $\ceilp{t}{\epsilon}$ for the least integer at least $t$ with $(-1)^n=\epsilon$.

\begin{theorem}\label{lcw:thm:inverse}
For each fixed $M$ there exists $A_M>0$ such that, for all sufficiently large $X$, with
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:eq:eta}
 \eta_\epsilon=
 \frac{A_M t_{\epsilon,M}^{-M-1}}{1+\log(dt_{\epsilon,M})},
\end{equation}
one has
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:eq:parity-bracket}
 \ceilp{t_{\epsilon,M}-\eta_\epsilon}{\epsilon}
 \le \nu_\epsilon(X)\le
 \ceilp{t_{\epsilon,M}+\eta_\epsilon}{\epsilon}.
\end{equation}
Taking the minimum of the two lower endpoints and of the two upper endpoints gives integers $L_M(X),U_M(X)$ satisfying
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:eq:width}
 L_M(X)\le\nu(X)\le U_M(X),\qquad U_M(X)-L_M(X)\in\{0,1\}
\end{equation}
eventually. No effective value of $A_M$ or onset is asserted.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Taking logarithms in \eqref{lcw:eq:main} gives, on each parity,
\[
 \log a_n=\mathcal F_{\epsilon,M}(n)+O_M(n^{-M-1}).
\]
The leading relative factor is positive and tends to one, so the logarithm and its fixed-order remainder are valid. On a fixed-width neighborhood of $t=t_{\epsilon,M}$ the carrier derivative is comparable to $\Lambda=1+\log(dt)$, and $n^{-M-1}$ is comparable to $t^{-M-1}$. Choose $A_M$ strictly larger than the common logarithmic error bound after these comparisons.

The parity integer immediately preceding $\ceilp{t-\eta_\epsilon}{\epsilon}$ is below $t-\eta_\epsilon$ and within a fixed distance of $t$. By the mean value theorem its carrier is smaller than $\log X$ by more than the possible logarithmic error, so its actual count is below $X$. The integer $\ceilp{t+\eta_\epsilon}{\epsilon}$ has count at least $X$ by the same argument with opposite sign. Monotonicity proves \eqref{lcw:eq:parity-bracket}, including endpoint cases. Taking minima is valid because the global threshold is the minimum of the two parity thresholds.

It remains to prove the width assertion. At a common large argument the two carriers differ by $\log(C_+/C_-)+O_M(t^{-1})$. Their derivatives are of order $\log t$, so their roots differ by $O(1/\log t)$; their radii tend to zero. The convex hull of both real intervals
$[t_{\epsilon,M}-\eta_\epsilon,t_{\epsilon,M}+\eta_\epsilon]$
therefore eventually has length less than one.

If $U_M>L_M$, choose a parity attaining the minimum lower ceiling $L_M$. Its upper ceiling must exceed $L_M$, so its real interval contains the integer $L_M$ and extends above it. Every point of the other real interval is strictly below $L_M+1$, since the common hull has length less than one. The opposite-parity upper ceiling is consequently at most $L_M+1$. Thus $U_M\le L_M+1$. The alternative is $U_M=L_M$, completing \eqref{lcw:eq:width}.
\end{proof}

For $M=0$ the carriers have a closed form. With $W_0$ the positive real Lambert branch and $Y_\epsilon=\log(X/C_\epsilon)$,
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:eq:lambert}
 t_{\epsilon,0}(X)=\frac{Y_\epsilon}{W_0(dY_\epsilon)}.
\end{equation}
Substitution verifies the equation exactly. If instead one starts from
$t_*=(\log X)/W_0(d\log X)$, the first displacement is
\[
 t_{\epsilon,0}-t_*=-\frac{\log C_\epsilon}{1+\log(dt_*)}
 +o\!\left(\frac1{\log t_*}\right).
\]
The two constants cannot be silently merged for sharp parity rounding. Newton iteration on \eqref{lcw:eq:carrier} computes higher real carriers; interval rounding still requires \eqref{lcw:eq:parity-bracket}. A ceiling is certified only when its two parity endpoints coincide, or the global endpoints coincide. The existential width-one theorem is not by itself a certified finite-input inverse calculator.

\section{Proportional length and dimension}\label{lcw:sec:ratio}
Let $D\to\infty$ and $N$ be an integer such that $\rho=N/D$ belongs to a fixed compact interval $K\subset(0,\infty)$. Define $r_\rho>0$ by
\[
 \DD\log F(r_\rho)=\rho,\qquad b_\rho=\DD^2\log F(r_\rho)>0,
 \qquad\kappa_{\rho,h}=\DD^h\log F(r_\rho).
\]
The argument of Lemma~\ref{lcw:lem:saddle} proves existence and uniqueness. Compactness of $K$ puts the saddles in a compact positive interval and bounds $b_\rho$ away from zero. Let $\TT_{\rho,\ell}$ denote \eqref{lcw:eq:operator} with $r,b,\kappa_h$ replaced by these quantities.

\begin{theorem}\label{lcw:thm:ratio}
For fixed $M\ge0$, $R<\infty$, uniformly for $\rho\in K$ and complex $|u|\le R$,
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:eq:ratio}
 A_{N,D}(u)=\frac{N!F(r_\rho)^D r_\rho^{-N}}{\sqrt{2\pi D b_\rho}}
 \left\{\sum_{\ell=0}^M\frac{\TT_{\rho,\ell}E_{\epsilon,u}}{D^\ell}
                  +O_{M,R,K}(D^{-M-1})\right\},\quad\epsilon=(-1)^N.
\end{equation}
The remainder is absolute inside braces. Here $E_{\epsilon,u}(z)=e^{uz}+\epsilon e^{-uz}$ as before; $N!$ is retained exactly.

Let $J_{N,D}$ count zero steps. It has an all-fixed-orders weighted $\ell^1$ expansion, uniform for $\rho\in K$, about $\Poi(r_\rho)$ conditioned to have parity $N$. In particular, for every fixed $R>1$,
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:eq:moving-poisson}
 \sum_{j\ge0}R^j\left|\Pp(J_{N,D}=j)-\pi_{\rho,\epsilon}(j)\right|=O_{R,K}(D^{-1}),
\end{equation}
where $\pi_{\rho,\epsilon}$ is \eqref{lcw:eq:poisson} with $r$ replaced by $r_\rho$.
If additionally $\rho\to\rho_0\in(0,\infty)$ and $N$ has fixed parity $\epsilon$, then $J_{N,D}$ converges weakly to $\Poi(r_{\rho_0})$ conditioned on that parity.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Use the circle $|z|=r_\rho$ and centered phase
\[
 \log\frac{F(r_\rho e^{i\theta})}{F(r_\rho)}-i\rho\theta.
\]
The quadratic coefficient is $-b_\rho/2$, uniformly negative. Positivity and the even support give exactly two maximum-modulus points for every $\rho$; compactness supplies a uniform strict gap away from the two fixed neighborhoods. Local analytic derivatives and amplitude bounds are uniform on compact sets. The argument of Section~\ref{lcw:sec:remainder}, scaled by $\sqrt D$, therefore proves \eqref{lcw:eq:ratio}. Translation through $\pi$ contributes $e^{-iN\pi}=(-1)^N$, independent of the parity of $D$.

The unmarked leading amplitudes $E_\epsilon(r_\rho)$ are bounded away from zero for both signs on $K$. Quotient expansion and the larger-disk Cauchy argument of Theorem~\ref{lcw:thm:weighted} prove \eqref{lcw:eq:moving-poisson} and all its fixed-order refinements. More explicitly set
\[
 H_{\rho,\ell}(j)=r_\rho^{-j}\TT_{\rho,\ell}(z^j),\qquad
 c_{\rho,\epsilon,\ell}=\E H_{\rho,\ell}(J_{\rho,\epsilon}),
\]
where $J_{\rho,\epsilon}$ has mass $\pi_{\rho,\epsilon}$, and apply the recurrence \eqref{lcw:eq:Qrec} with these quantities. The resulting polynomials of degree at most $2\ell$ give an error $O_{M,R,K}(D^{-M-1})$. Finally continuity of $r_\rho$ gives the asserted fixed-law weak limit when $\rho$ converges and parity is fixed.
\end{proof}

If desired, expanding $N!$ in \eqref{lcw:eq:ratio} replaces $\TT_{\rho,\ell}$ by $\sum_{h=0}^\ell s_h\rho^{-h}\TT_{\rho,\ell-h}$ after extracting the corresponding Stirling leading factor. Compactness controls the additional remainder. For a moving, nonconvergent $\rho$, \eqref{lcw:eq:moving-poisson} is an approximation by a moving parameter, not convergence to one fixed distribution. Nothing here treats $\rho\downarrow0$, $\rho\to\infty$, or fixed $D$; in particular the fixed-dimensional row statement in A328718 is a separate regime.

\section{Exact checks and reproducibility}\label{lcw:sec:verification}
The proofs above establish the asymptotic results. Computation checks finite enumeration and coefficient algebra; numerical agreement does not establish a uniform remainder, an effective onset, or priority over prior literature.

\subsection{Three finite counting descriptions}
An integer convolution avoids cancellation. Put
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:eq:Qcount}
 Q_{D,k}=(k!)^2[t^k]\left(\sum_{h\ge0}\frac{t^h}{(h!)^2}\right)^D.
\end{equation}
Then $Q_{0,0}=1$, $Q_{0,k}=0$ for $k>0$, and
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:eq:Qconvolution}
 Q_{D,k}=\sum_{h=0}^k\binom kh^2 Q_{D-1,k-h}.
\end{equation}
For $N-j=2k\ge0$, the exact number of bridges with $j$ zero steps is
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:eq:exactJ}
 \binom Nj\binom{2k}k Q_{D,k}.
\end{equation}
The count is zero at other parities. To count occupied axes independently, put
\[
 R_{k,m}=(m!)^2[t^m]\left(\sum_{h\ge1}\frac{t^h}{(h!)^2}\right)^k.
\]
These satisfy $R_{0,0}=1$ and
$R_{k,m}=\sum_{h=1}^m\binom mh^2R_{k-1,m-h}$. For $n-j=2m$,
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:eq:exactJK}
 \#\{J_n=j,K_n=k\}=\binom nj\binom{2m}m\binom nk R_{k,m}.
\end{equation}
Both recurrences follow by selecting the contribution of one factor in the corresponding generating function.

A different exact calculation uses \eqref{lcw:eq:riccati}. Write
$a(z)=\sum_{k\ge1}\alpha_kz^{2k}$ and $F(z)^D=\sum_{k\ge0}\beta_{D,k}z^{2k}$. Then
\begin{align}
 \alpha_k&=\frac{4\one_{\{k=1\}}-\sum_{h=1}^{k-1}\alpha_h\alpha_{k-h}}{2k},\label{lcw:eq:alpha}\\
 \beta_{D,0}&=1,\qquad
 \beta_{D,k}=\frac{D}{2k}\sum_{h=1}^k\alpha_h\beta_{D,k-h}.\label{lcw:eq:beta}
\end{align}
The unmarked count is $N!\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor N/2\rfloor}\beta_{D,k}/(N-2k)!$. Rational arithmetic makes this comparison exact.

\subsection{Independent coefficient verification}
A raw-derivative route to the cumulants differentiates the second-order Bessel equation instead of iterating \eqref{lcw:eq:derivation}. Let $g_m=r^mF^{(m)}(r)/F(r)$, with $g_0=g_1=1$. Then
\begin{align}
 g_{m+2}={}&-(2m+1)g_{m+1}-(m^2-4r^2)g_m\notag\\
 &+8mr^2g_{m-1}+4m(m-1)r^2g_{m-2},\label{lcw:eq:rawrec}
\end{align}
where terms with negative indices have zero coefficient and are omitted. Converting these factorial moments to raw moments with Stirling numbers, then raw moments to cumulants, checks \eqref{lcw:eq:cumulants} and higher orders independently.

For the Gaussian algebra, if
$\exp(\sum_{h\ge1}e_hv^h)=\sum_{k\ge0}P_kv^k$, then
\[
 P_0=1,\qquad P_k=\frac1k\sum_{h=1}^k h e_h P_{k-h}.
\]
Take $e_h=\kappa_{h+2}(it)^{h+2}/(h+2)!$, adding $ijt$ to $e_1$ for monomial amplitude $z^j$. \section{Further questions within this treatment}\label{lcw:sec:further}
Several concrete extensions remain outside the results proved here. These are questions for extending this treatment, not claims that the corresponding problems are historically unsolved.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Make the compact saddle bounds effective: produce explicit remainder constants and verified onsets, then combine them with interval evaluations of $r$, $C_\epsilon$, and the inverse carriers. That would turn the existential brackets of Theorem~\ref{lcw:thm:inverse} into a certified finite-input inverse calculator.
\item Analyze sparse and dense ratios $N/D\to0$ and $N/D\to\infty$. The compact theorem does not control these limits; the saddle, variance, and relative sizes of the zero-step and coordinate occupancies require separate scaling arguments.
\item Strengthen the occupied-axis weak limit to a lattice local theorem or a joint Edgeworth expansion with the zero-step count. Such a result should explicitly retain the exact support and parity constraints, and specify the range of uniformity and tail norm rather than infer it from the characteristic-function limit alone.
\item Compare the finite-size polynomials in Theorem~\ref{lcw:thm:weighted} and the occupation corrections systematically with general conditional-occupancy and Bessel-distribution literature. This could clarify alternative formulations and attribution beyond the bounded source search used here.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{partabstract}
We give an exact counterexample to the universal Gaussian return bound
conjectured in Felker--Lyons (2003) and stated by Ball--Sterbenz (2005):
in dimension $16$, the nonlazy return probability after $26$ steps is
strictly greater than $2(4/(13\pi))^8$. The certificate uses only integer
arithmetic and an elementary rational bound for $\pi$.
A uniform asymptotic theorem explains the failure and proves more:
no finite constant can multiply the Gaussian expression into an upper
bound valid for every dimension and time.

The main tool is a single expansion for weighted closed walks with steps
$0,\pm e_1,\ldots,\pm e_D$, uniform in dimension and all nonnegative
activities. For length $N=2m+\epsilon$, exact parity reduction expresses
the count through Bessel and hyperbolic functions with negative real
zeros. Their tilted coefficients are Poisson--binomial probabilities,
with the decisive variance bound $m/2\le B\le m$. We prove expansions
to every fixed order, with relative remainder $O_K(m^{-K-1})$, and a
leading relative error at most $24/m$.

For isotropic nonlazy and unit-idle walks in the dense range
$N/D\to\infty$, the Gaussian estimate, including the period correction
when appropriate, is relatively valid exactly when
$D^3/N^2\to0$. At $N/D^{3/2}\to s>0$, the missing factor tends to
$\exp(1/(48s^2))$. The eventual Gaussian upper-bound thresholds
are asymptotic to $D^2/12$ and $D^2/9$, respectively. In the sparse range $m^2/D=O(1)$ with bounded
$\lambda\sqrt{m/D}$, repeated axes
follow a Poisson law of mean $m^2/(4D)$, asymptotically independent of
a parity-conditioned Poisson idle count. We prove error $O(m^{-1})$
in exponentially weighted $\ell^1$, give the first counting correction,
and quantify triple occupancy.

The results answer endpoint questions in the repository's lazy-walk
report. Proofs, source comparisons, reproducible checks, and further questions
are included. The classical
real-zero and Edgeworth methods are credited explicitly; historical
priority of the combined conclusions is not asserted.

\smallskip\noindent
Mathematical status: proved in the text, with separate computational checks.
No Lean formalization or external peer review is claimed.
\end{partabstract}

\section{A finite counterexample to the Gaussian return bound}
\label{lcw:ulb:sec:lyons-counterexample}

Ball and Sterbenz, in \emph{Explicit Bounds for the Return Probability
of Simple Random Walks}, Journal of Theoretical Probability 18 (2005),
317--326, state the following conjecture of Russ Lyons in equation (1.3)
of the available preprint dated 19 January 2004 \cite{ballsterbenz}: for the nonlazy simple random walk on $\mathbb Z^d$,
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:ulb:eq:lyons-bound}
 p_{2n}^{(d)}(0,0)\le 2\left(\frac{d}{4\pi n}\right)^{d/2}
 \qquad(d\ge1,\ n\ge1).
\end{equation}
The same universal inequality is conjectured in Felker and Lyons
\cite{felkerlyons}, equation (2.3). The leading factor $2$ is the
lattice-period correction. Ball and Sterbenz's
Proposition 1 and Remark 1 establish a sufficient large-time range
of order $d^3$, and they report checks through dimension six.
The universal inequality as stated is false.

\begin{theorem}[An exact finite counterexample]
\label{lcw:ulb:thm:lyons-counterexample}
For the nonlazy simple random walk in dimension $d=16$, after $26$
steps,
\[
 p_{26}^{(16)}(0,0)
 =\frac{5630493790109507827003978475}{2^{118}}
 >2\left(\frac4{13\pi}\right)^8.
\]
Indeed, the ratio of the left-hand side to the right-hand side is
strictly greater than $4001/4000$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Define the integers
\[
 C_{m,d}=\sum_{k_1+\cdots+k_d=m}
            \binom{m}{k_1,\ldots,k_d}^{\!2}.
\]
Splitting off the last coordinate gives the finite exact recurrence
\[
 C_{m,1}=1,\qquad
 C_{m,d+1}=\sum_{k=0}^m\binom{m}{k}^2 C_{m-k,d}.
\]
A closed walk uses the positive and negative direction of each
axis equally often. Its count is therefore
$\binom{2m}{m}C_{m,d}$, and hence
\[
 p_{2m}^{(d)}(0,0)
 =\frac{\binom{2m}{m}C_{m,d}}{(2d)^{2m}}.
\]
At $(m,d)=(13,16)$ the recurrence gives
\[
 C_{13,16}=2217420395389549070189056,
\]
and thus exactly
\[
 \binom{26}{13}C_{13,16}
   =23062502564288544059408295833600
\]
closed walks. Division by $32^{26}$ gives the displayed probability.
The accompanying verification script performs this recurrence and
independently obtains the same count by repeated squaring of the
degree-13 truncation of $\sum_{k\ge0}t^k/(k!)^2$.

No floating-point comparison with $\pi$ is required. The classical
rational lower bound $\pi>333/106$ implies
\[
 2\left(\frac4{13\pi}\right)^8
   <2\left(\frac{424}{4329}\right)^8.
\]
For a self-contained certificate of this lower bound, Machin's identity
$\pi=16\arctan(1/5)-4\arctan(1/239)$ and the alternating-series
bounds give
\[
 \pi>
 16\left(\frac15-\frac1{3\cdot5^3}
                 +\frac1{5\cdot5^5}-\frac1{7\cdot5^7}\right)
       -\frac4{239}
 >\frac{333}{106};
\]
the final comparison is rational arithmetic. Machin's identity itself
follows from the tangent addition formula and the fact that the
relevant angle lies in $(0,\pi/2)$.

Writing $A=5630493790109507827003978475$, the needed comparison reduces
to the positive integer
\begin{align*}
 A\,4329^8-2^{143}53^8
 &=241148999863792122048803143474490963808862899634921387\\
 &>0.
\end{align*}
The stronger ratio bound follows from the exact comparison
\[
 4000 A\,4329^8>4001\cdot2^{143}53^8.
\]
Every displayed integer and rational comparison is checked by the
standard-library-only script \texttt{verify\_lyons.py}.
\end{proof}

The numerical values, included only to show the size of the effect, are
\[
 p_{26}^{(16)}(0,0)\simeq1.6943650925091064\times10^{-8},\qquad
 2\left(\frac4{13\pi}\right)^8
     \simeq1.6934178227188187\times10^{-8}.
\]
Their ratio is approximately $1.0005593833828717$. The exact
certificate, rather than these decimal approximations, proves the
counterexample. No claim that this is the smallest possible dimension
or the shortest possible counterexample is needed.

\paragraph{Source locator.}
Primary paper: \url{https://doi.org/10.1007/s10959-003-2606-7}.
The author-hosted full text is
\url{https://mathweb.ucsd.edu/~bdriver/DRIVER/Miscellaneous_Files/sterbenz6.pdf};
see equation (1.3) on page 2, Proposition 1 and Remark 1 on pages 3--4.

\subsection{What this finite calculation settles}

This disproves the universal inequality \eqref{lcw:ulb:eq:lyons-bound}.
It is consistent with the eventual fixed-dimension upper bound
proved in \cite{ballsterbenz}. More substantially,
Corollary~\ref{lcw:ulb:cor:nonlazy-dense} below proves that the ratio to the
period-corrected Gaussian expression is unbounded along even lengths
$N\sim D^{4/3}$. Thus replacing the factor $2$ by any other fixed
finite constant cannot repair the universal statement.

For the historical comparison, the author-hosted Felker--Lyons version
of 6 November 2003, the Ball--Sterbenz paper, and the linked
Felker--Lyons erratum of 7 August 2012 were inspected. That erratum
concerns body-centered lattice formulas and numerical bounds
\cite{felkererratum}. A targeted search of later references located
no prior refutation of \eqref{lcw:ulb:eq:lyons-bound}; this search is not an
exhaustive priority determination. The mathematical claim here is the
explicit counterexample and the proved asymptotic failure of every
constant bound.

\subsection{Weighted bridges and exact parity reduction}

Fix a nonnegative integer $D$. Give the step $+e_i$ activity $a_i\ge0$,
the step $-e_i$ activity $b_i\ge0$, and the step $0$ activity

total weight of length-$N$ walks starting and ending at the origin.
Only $c_i=a_i b_i$ matters for a closed walk, so we also write
$W_N(\boldsymbol c,\lambda)$. The isotropic notation is
$A_{N,D}(\lambda)=W_N((1,\ldots,1),\lambda)$.

These weights include ordinary counts, colored idle steps when $\lambda$
is an integer, and transition probabilities. If
$\lambda+\sum_i(a_i+b_i)=1$, then $W_N$ is already the return
probability. For any positive total activity, division by that sum to the power $N$ normalizes
the step activities. No symmetry assumption $a_i=b_i$ is required.

Define the entire functions, by their power series,
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:ulb:eq:entire}
 F(t)=\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{t^k}{(k!)^2},\qquad
 H_0(t)=\sum_{h\ge0}\frac{t^h}{(2h)!},\qquad
 H_1(t)=\sum_{h\ge0}\frac{t^h}{(2h+1)!}.
\end{equation}
For positive arguments these are
$I_0(2\sqrt t)$, $\cosh\sqrt t$, and $\sinh\sqrt t/\sqrt t$.
Writing them by series avoids any square-root branch convention.
For $N=2m+\ep$, $\ep\in\{0,1\}$, put
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:ulb:eq:Ge}
 G_\ep(t)=H_\ep(\lambda^2t)\prod_{i=1}^D F(c_i t).
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Exact coefficient identity]\label{lcw:ulb:prop:exact}
For every nonnegative choice of activities,
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:ulb:eq:exact}
 W_N(\boldsymbol c,\lambda)
 =N!\lambda^\ep[t^m]G_\ep(t).
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
If axis $i$ is used $k_i$ times in each direction and there are $j$
idle steps, then $j+2\sum_i k_i=N$. The number of orders of these
steps is $N!/(j!\prod_i(k_i!)^2)$ and their common weight is
$\lambda^j\prod_i c_i^{k_i}$. Set $j=2h+\ep$ and sum. Equivalently,
the exponential generating function before splitting parity is
$e^{\lambda z}\prod_i I_0(2\sqrt{c_i}\,z)$.
\end{proof}

If $\ep=1$ and $\lambda=0$, the original count is exactly zero.
For all other cases with at least one nonconstant factor in $G_\ep$,
the following gives a relative asymptotic. The normalized coefficient
$[t^m]G_1(t)$ itself continues smoothly to $\lambda=0$ and also has
the stated expansion. If all $c_i$ and $\lambda$ vanish, then
$G_\ep\equiv1$ and its positive-degree coefficients vanish; no
positive saddle exists in this degenerate case.

\subsection{The saddle and the main theorem}

Write $\Dop=t\,d/dt$. For a nonconstant generator $G_\ep$, let $r>0$
be the unique solution of
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:ulb:eq:saddle}
 (\Dop\log G_\ep)(r)=m.
\end{equation}
Existence and uniqueness, for $m>0$, follow in the proof below. Define
the cumulants and leading approximation by
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:ulb:eq:cumulants}
 \kappa_j=(\Dop^j\log G_\ep)(r),\qquad B=\kappa_2,
 \qquad L_N=\frac{N!\lambda^\ep G_\ep(r)}{r^m\sqrt{2\pi B}}.
\end{equation}
When discussing the normalized odd coefficient at zero, omit the
factor $\lambda^\ep$ in both the exact count and $L_N$.

\begin{theorem}[Uniform expansion in all parameters]\label{lcw:ulb:thm:global}
For every fixed integer $K\ge0$ there are constants $C_K,m_K$, depending
only on $K$, such that for every $m\ge m_K$, every dimension $D$, every
parity, and every allowed nonnegative choice of activities with nonzero
count,
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:ulb:eq:global}
 \frac{W_N(\boldsymbol c,\lambda)}{L_N}
 =\sum_{s=0}^{K} E_s+\mathcal R_K,
 \qquad |\mathcal R_K|\le C_K m^{-K-1}.
\end{equation}
Here $E_0=1$. For $s\ge1$, sum over nonnegative integers
$\nu_3,\ldots,\nu_{2s+2}$ satisfying
$\sum_{j=3}^{2s+2}(j-2)\nu_j=2s$, and set
$J=\sum_j j\nu_j$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:ulb:eq:Es}
 E_s=\sum_{\sum(j-2)\nu_j=2s}
 \frac{(-1)^{J/2}(J-1)!!}{B^{J/2}}
 \prod_{j=3}^{2s+2}\frac{\kappa_j^{\nu_j}}{\nu_j!(j!)^{\nu_j}}.
\end{equation}
The integer $J$ is even. Each $E_s=O_s(m^{-s})$ uniformly in all
the same parameters, and
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:ulb:eq:variance}
 \frac m2\le B\le m.
\end{equation}
The dimension and the activities may depend arbitrarily on $m$.
\end{theorem}

In particular, the first two corrections are
\begin{align}
 E_1&=\frac{\kappa_4}{8B^2}-\frac{5\kappa_3^2}{24B^3},
 \label{lcw:ulb:eq:E1}\\
 E_2&=-\frac{\kappa_6}{48B^3}
 +\frac{7\kappa_3\kappa_5}{48B^4}
 +\frac{35\kappa_4^2}{384B^4}
 -\frac{35\kappa_3^2\kappa_4}{64B^5}
 +\frac{385\kappa_3^4}{1152B^6}.
 \label{lcw:ulb:eq:E2}
\end{align}
The expression is a fixed-order Poincar\'e expansion. There is no
assertion that the infinite sum of its corrections converges, or that
one may let $K$ grow without additional estimates.

\begin{corollary}[Explicit leading envelope]\label{lcw:ulb:cor:explicit}
For every $m>0$ in the nonzero cases,
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:ulb:eq:explicit}
 \left|\frac{W_N}{L_N}-1\right|\le\frac{12}{B}\le\frac{24}{m}.
\end{equation}
Consequently, for $m>24$,
\[
 L_N(1-24/m)\le W_N\le L_N(1+24/m).
\]
The same inequalities apply to the normalized odd coefficient at
$\lambda=0$.
\end{corollary}

The envelope is deliberately conservative. Its value is that its
constant and its parameter range are explicit, with no hidden
dependence on dimension or anisotropy. Evaluating $r,G_\ep(r),B$ by
outward-rounded arithmetic would turn it into a numerical interval.
The supplied floating-point examples illustrate the formula; they do
not by themselves implement outward rounding.

\subsection{Negative zeros and the variance sandwich}

Let $j_{0,k}$ denote the positive zeros of the ordinary Bessel function
$J_0$. The classical product identities \cite{dlmfzeros,dlmftrig} give
\begin{align}
 F(t)&=\prod_{k\ge1}\left(1+\frac{4t}{j_{0,k}^2}\right),
 \label{lcw:ulb:eq:Fproduct}\\
 H_0(t)&=\prod_{k\ge0}\left(1+
          \frac{t}{\pi^2(k+\tfrac12)^2}\right),
 &H_1(t)&=\prod_{k\ge1}\left(1+\frac{t}{\pi^2k^2}\right).
 \label{lcw:ulb:eq:Hproduct}
\end{align}
Their reciprocal zero moduli are summable, so the products and their
logarithmic derivatives converge locally away from the zeros. Scaling
the argument by any positive activity preserves this property; a zero
activity contributes the constant factor one.

For a factor $1+t/a$, its tilt at $r$ is the generating function
of a Bernoulli variable of parameter $r/(a+r)$. Thus the normalized
coefficient distribution of $G_\ep$ is a countable sum of independent
Bernoulli variables. More explicitly, with independent parameters
$p_j=r/(a_j+r)$ over all reciprocal-zero factors, put $S=\sum_jX_j$.
Then $\sum_jp_j<\infty$, $S$ is finite almost surely, and
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:ulb:eq:tiltedidentity}
 \E z^S=\frac{G_\ep(rz)}{G_\ep(r)},\qquad
 [t^m]G_\ep(t)=G_\ep(r)r^{-m}\Prob(S=m).
\end{equation}
At the saddle, $\E S=m$. In particular, its variance does not exceed its
mean. The lower bound follows factor by factor from a further special
property of the Bessel and parity factors.

\begin{lemma}[Variance at least half the mean]\label{lcw:ulb:lem:half}
For $Q=F,H_0,H_1$ and $t>0$, put
$\mu_Q(t)=\Dop\log Q(t)$ and $b_Q(t)=\Dop\mu_Q(t)$.
Then $b_Q(t)\ge\mu_Q(t)/2$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For $F$, set $z=2\sqrt t$ and $R(z)=I_1(z)/I_0(z)$. Then
\[
 \mu_F(t)=\frac z2R(z),\qquad
 b_F(t)=\frac{\mu_F(t)}2+\frac{z^2}{4}R'(z).
\]
The integral representation
$I_0(z)=\pi^{-1}\int_0^\pi e^{z\cos\theta}\,d\theta$
\cite{dlmfintegral} identifies $R(z)$ with the expectation of
$\cos\theta$ under the density proportional to $e^{z\cos\theta}$.
Differentiating that expectation shows
$R'(z)=\Var_z(\cos\theta)\ge0$.

For the parity factors, let $x=\sqrt t$. Direct differentiation gives
\begin{align*}
 \mu_{H_0}&=\frac{x\tanh x}{2},
 &b_{H_0}-\frac{\mu_{H_0}}2&=\frac{x^2\sech^2x}{4},\\
 \mu_{H_1}&=\frac{x\coth x-1}{2},
 &b_{H_1}-\frac{\mu_{H_1}}2&=
       \frac{1-x^2\csch^2x}{4}.
\end{align*}
The last numerator is nonnegative because $\sinh x\ge x$.
All formulas extend by continuity at zero.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{lcw:ulb:thm:global} and
Corollary~\ref{lcw:ulb:cor:explicit}]
The mean of each nonconstant factor increases continuously from zero
to infinity: this follows from the displayed formulas, or from its
Bernoulli product and the presence of infinitely many positive
Bernoulli parameters approaching one as $r\to\infty$. The total mean
is strictly increasing, so \eqref{lcw:ulb:eq:saddle} has exactly one
positive solution for every $m>0$. Variances and means add under the
product. Lemma~\ref{lcw:ulb:lem:half} and the Bernoulli upper bound give
\eqref{lcw:ulb:eq:variance}.

Theorem~\ref{lcw:ulb:thm:PB} below gives the coefficient expansion with
error $O_K(B^{-K-1})$ and corrections \eqref{lcw:ulb:eq:Es}. Since
$B\ge m/2$, all its constants are independent of the walk parameters.
Multiplication by the exact factor $N!\lambda^\ep$ proves the theorem.
The explicit estimate in Proposition~\ref{lcw:ulb:prop:PBexplicit} gives
the corollary.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Why resolving parity changes the uniformity problem}

In the original variable $z$, the factor $I_0(2z)^D$ is even. In
sparse regimes the two arcs near positive and negative real $z$ can
have comparable size, and the odd answer is a small difference of
large contributions. An error controlled relative to either arc is
then insufficient for an odd relative asymptotic. In
\eqref{lcw:ulb:eq:exact}, both parity factors have positive coefficients
and the tilted lattice has span one. The global characteristic-function
bound in the next section gives one uniform estimate for both cases.

For example, with $\lambda=1$ and $D\gg m^2$, the first equivalents are
\[
 A_{2m,D}(1)\sim\frac{(2m)!D^m}{m!},\qquad
 A_{2m+1,D}(1)\sim\frac{(2m+1)!D^m}{m!}.
\]
The typical odd bridge has one idle step. This leading behavior is
automatically retained by the factorization, including when the odd
idle activity becomes arbitrarily small.

\subsection{A direct extension to prescribed endpoints}
\label{lcw:ulb:subsec:endpoints}

The same argument already covers arbitrary endpoints, including endpoints
whose coordinates grow with the length. The parameter controlling the
relative error is the number of available pairs after the mandatory
displacement has been removed.

Let $W_{N,x}$ be the total activity of length-$N$ walks from $0$ to
$x\in\Z^D$, with the directional and idle activities used above. Put
\[
 x_i^+=\max(x_i,0),\qquad x_i^-=\max(-x_i,0),\qquad
 \nu_i=|x_i|,\qquad h=|x|_1=\sum_i\nu_i,\qquad c_i=a_ib_i.
\]
If $N<h$, then $W_{N,x}=0$. Otherwise write
$N-h=2m+\epsilon$, where $m\ge0$ and $\epsilon\in\{0,1\}$, and define
\[
 F_\nu(t)=\nu!\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{t^k}{k!(k+\nu)!},
 \qquad
 G_{\epsilon,x}(t)=H_\epsilon(\lambda^2t)
                    \prod_{i=1}^D F_{\nu_i}(c_it).
\]
In particular $F_0=F$ and $F_\nu(0)=1$. For $t>0$,
$F_\nu(t)=\nu!t^{-\nu/2}I_\nu(2\sqrt t)$, but the entire series is
the definition at zero.

\begin{corollary}[Uniform prescribed-endpoint expansion]
\label{lcw:ulb:cor:endpoint}
For all nonnegative activities,
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:ulb:eq:endpoint-exact}
 W_{N,x}=P_{N,x}[t^m]G_{\epsilon,x}(t),\qquad
 P_{N,x}=N!\lambda^\epsilon
          \prod_{i=1}^D\frac{a_i^{x_i^+}b_i^{x_i^-}}{\nu_i!}.
\end{equation}
Powers of exponent zero in the prefactor equal one, also when the
activity is zero.

Suppose $m>0$, $P_{N,x}>0$, and $G_{\epsilon,x}$ is nonconstant.
Let $r>0$ satisfy $\mathcal D\log G_{\epsilon,x}(r)=m$, and set
\[
 \kappa_j=\mathcal D^j\log G_{\epsilon,x}(r),\qquad B=\kappa_2,
 \qquad
 L_{N,x}=\frac{P_{N,x}G_{\epsilon,x}(r)}
               {r^m\sqrt{2\pi B}}.
\]
Then $m/2\le B\le m$, and the expansion in
Theorem~\ref{lcw:ulb:thm:global}, with the same corrections $E_s$ and
constants depending only on its fixed order, holds for $W_{N,x}/L_{N,x}$.
Moreover, for every such $m>0$,
\[
 \left|\frac{W_{N,x}}{L_{N,x}}-1\right|
       \le\frac{12}{B}\le\frac{24}{m}.
\]
Uniformity includes arbitrary variation of $x,D$ and all activities;
there is no upper restriction on any endpoint order $\nu_i$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
For axis $i$, the two directional counts are
$k_i+x_i^+$ and $k_i+x_i^-$ for some $k_i\ge0$. If there are
$j=2\ell+\epsilon$ idle steps, the length constraint is
$\ell+\sum_i k_i=m$. The weight of all orders of these steps is
\[
 \frac{N!\lambda^{2\ell+\epsilon}
           \prod_i a_i^{k_i+x_i^+}b_i^{k_i+x_i^-}}
      {(2\ell+\epsilon)!\prod_i k_i!(k_i+\nu_i)!}.
\]
Summing proves \eqref{lcw:ulb:eq:endpoint-exact}.

For each nonnegative integer $\nu$, the Bessel product
\cite{dlmfzeros} gives
\[
 F_\nu(t)=\prod_{k\ge1}
           \left(1+\frac{4t}{j_{\nu,k}^2}\right),
\]
where $j_{\nu,k}$ are the positive zeros of $J_\nu$ and
$\sum_k j_{\nu,k}^{-2}<\infty$. Consequently every tilted factor is
again a countable Bernoulli sum. It remains only to verify the
variance lower bound uniformly in $\nu$.

Let $z=2\sqrt t$ and $M_\nu(z)=F_\nu(z^2/4)$.
The positive integral representation
\cite[Eq.~10.32.2]{dlmfintegral} reads
\[
 M_\nu(z)=
 \frac{\Gamma(\nu+1)}{\sqrt\pi\,\Gamma(\nu+\tfrac12)}
 \int_{-1}^1 e^{zu}(1-u^2)^{\nu-1/2}\,du.
\]
At $z=0$ this normalizes a probability density. Under its exponential
tilt at $z>0$, put $R_\nu(z)=(\log M_\nu)'(z)$.
Differentiating the integral gives
\[
 R_\nu(z)=\mathbb E_{\nu,z}u,\qquad
 R_\nu'(z)=\operatorname{Var}_{\nu,z}(u)\ge0.
\]
Therefore the mean and variance of the coefficient tilt of $F_\nu$
satisfy
\[
 \mu_\nu(t)=\frac z2R_\nu(z),\qquad
 b_\nu(t)=\frac{\mu_\nu(t)}2+\frac{z^2}{4}R_\nu'(z)
             \ge\frac{\mu_\nu(t)}2.
\]
This is the same bound as for $F_0$, with no constant depending on
$\nu$. Zero activities contribute constant factors and zero mean and
variance. The means and variances add, and the parity factor has
already been treated. Each nonconstant factor has infinitely many
Bernoulli factors, so its mean increases strictly from zero to
infinity. This proves existence and uniqueness of the saddle and the
variance sandwich. Theorem~\ref{lcw:ulb:thm:PB} and
Proposition~\ref{lcw:ulb:prop:PBexplicit}, followed by multiplication by
the exact prefactor, prove the asserted expansion and envelope.
\end{proof}

The excluded boundaries are exact, not asymptotic exceptions.
If $P_{N,x}=0$, a required directional or idle step has zero
activity and $W_{N,x}=0$. If $G_{\epsilon,x}\equiv1$, its coefficient
is zero for $m>0$. For $m=0$, its constant coefficient is one and
$W_{N,x}=P_{N,x}$, whether or not the generator is constant.
When a prefactor vanishes but the generator is nonconstant, the
normalized coefficient $[t^m]G_{\epsilon,x}$ still has the same
uniform expansion; no ratio to a zero walk weight is asserted.

\section{A uniform local expansion for Bernoulli sums}
\label{lcw:ulb:sec:PB}

This section proves the probabilistic estimate used in
Theorem~\ref{lcw:ulb:thm:global}. Its hypotheses allow arbitrarily unequal
Bernoulli parameters and countably many summands. The method is classical;
the proof records the uniform constants and the cancellation needed for
an integer-power remainder at every order.

\begin{theorem}[Uniform Poisson--binomial local expansion]
\label{lcw:ulb:thm:PB}
Let $X_j$ be a finite or countable family of independent Bernoulli random
variables with parameters $p_j\in[0,1]$ and $\sum_jp_j<\infty$.
Suppose $S=\sum_jX_j$ has integer mean $m$ and variance $B>0$.
Write $\kappa_j$ for its cumulants. For every fixed $K\ge0$, as
$B\to\infty$,
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:ulb:eq:PB}
 \sqrt{2\pi B}\,\Prob(S=m)
 =\sum_{s=0}^K E_s+O_K(B^{-K-1}),
\end{equation}
where $E_s$ is given by \eqref{lcw:ulb:eq:Es}, with these cumulants and
variance. The error constant depends only on $K$. In particular,
$E_s=O_s(B^{-s})$ uniformly over all such families.
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Analytic bounds and localization}

For one Bernoulli parameter define the centered logarithm
\[
 \ell_p(z)=\log(1-p+pe^{iz})-ipz.
\]
On $|z|\le1/4$, the logarithm is the analytic branch at zero:
\[
 |1-p+pe^{iz}|\ge 1-|e^{iz}-1|\ge2-e^{1/4}>0.
\]
Direct differentiation gives
\[
 \ell_p''(z)=-\frac{p(1-p)e^{iz}}{(1-p+pe^{iz})^2}.
\]
It is bounded there by an absolute constant times $p(1-p)$.
Cauchy's estimates on a smaller fixed disk give, for each fixed $j\ge2$,
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:ulb:eq:cumulantbound}
 |\kappa_j|\le C_jB.
\end{equation}
They also give, for each fixed $L\ge2$ and real $|\theta|\le\delta$,
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:ulb:eq:logphi}
 \log\phi(\theta)
 =-\frac{B\theta^2}{2}
  +\sum_{j=3}^{L}\frac{\kappa_j(i\theta)^j}{j!}
  +O_L(B|\theta|^{L+1}),
 \qquad \phi(\theta)=\E e^{i\theta(S-m)}.
\end{equation}
Here $\delta>0$ is a fixed sufficiently small number. These statements
follow by summing the individual estimates; the summability of the
variances justifies this also for countably many factors.

For the whole Fourier interval, the elementary identity
\[
 |1-p+pe^{i\theta}|^2=1-4p(1-p)\sin^2(\theta/2)
\]
and $1-u\le e^{-u}$ imply
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:ulb:eq:localization}
 |\phi(\theta)|\le e^{-2B\sin^2(\theta/2)}
 \le e^{-2B\theta^2/\pi^2},\qquad |\theta|\le\pi.
\end{equation}
Thus the characteristic function has no competing lattice peak when
$B$ grows, regardless of how close individual parameters are to zero
or one. Fourier inversion at the integer mean reads
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:ulb:eq:inversion}
 \Prob(S=m)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\phi(\theta)\,d\theta.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Proof to every fixed order}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{lcw:ulb:thm:PB}]
Fix $K$ and take $L=2K+3$ in \eqref{lcw:ulb:eq:logphi}. Put
$h=B^{-1/2}$, $x=\theta/h$, and $b_j=\kappa_j/B$. Then
\[
 \log\phi(hx)=-x^2/2+Q(h,x)
      +O_K(h^{2K+2}|x|^{2K+4}),
 \qquad
 Q(t,x)=\sum_{j=3}^{2K+3}\frac{b_j(ix)^j}{j!}t^{j-2}.
\]
The $b_j$ are bounded solely in terms of $j$. Choose $\delta>0$
sufficiently small in terms of $K$. On $|hx|\le\delta$, for
$0\le t\le h\le1$, each term in $Q$ is bounded by a constant
times $x^2(t|x|)^{j-2}$. Consequently
\[
 |Q(t,x)|\le x^2/8,
 \qquad
 |\log\phi(hx)+x^2/2-Q(h,x)|\le x^2/8.
\]
Replacing $\phi(hx)$ by $e^{-x^2/2}e^{Q(h,x)}$ therefore has
integrated error $O_K(h^{2K+2})$: the pointwise error is bounded by
\[
 C_K h^{2K+2}|x|^{2K+4}e^{-x^2/4}.
\]

Expand $e^{Q(t,x)}$ in $t$ at zero through degree $2K+1$.
The integral form of Taylor's remainder, together with the preceding
bound on $Q$, shows that its remainder after multiplication by
$e^{-x^2/2}$ is at most
\[
 C_Kh^{2K+2}(1+|x|^{M_K})e^{-3x^2/8}.
\]
Indeed, a fixed number of derivatives of $e^Q$ is $e^Q$ times
a polynomial in the derivatives of $Q$, whose coefficients are
uniformly bounded for $0\le t\le h\le1$. The displayed bound
is integrable with a constant depending only on $K$.

The coefficient of $t^w$ is
\[
 A_w(x)=\sum_{\sum(j-2)\nu_j=w}
       \prod_j\frac{\{b_j(ix)^j/j!\}^{\nu_j}}{\nu_j!}.
\]
If $J=\sum j\nu_j$, then $J\equiv w\pmod2$. Hence $A_w(x)$
is odd and purely imaginary when $w$ is odd. Its integral against
the even Gaussian over the symmetric local interval is exactly zero.
For even $w=2s$, extend the polynomial Gaussian integral to the
whole real line, with exponentially small error. Equation
\eqref{lcw:ulb:eq:localization} bounds the remaining original integral
by an exponentially small quantity as well.

Finally,
\[
 \frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\R}x^{2j}e^{-x^2/2}\,dx=(2j-1)!!.
\]
Multiplying by $h^{2s}$ and replacing $b_j$ by $\kappa_j/B$
gives exactly \eqref{lcw:ulb:eq:Es}. For any summand in that formula,
\[
 J/2-\sum_j\nu_j=\tfrac12\sum_j(j-2)\nu_j=s,
\]
so \eqref{lcw:ulb:eq:cumulantbound} gives $E_s=O_s(B^{-s})$.
All discarded terms have integrated size $O_K(B^{-K-1})$ in the
normalization of \eqref{lcw:ulb:eq:PB}, completing the proof.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Why the odd term must be retained]
After the first correction, an absolute bound on an omitted fifth
cumulant would yield only $O(B^{-3/2})$. The proof keeps that term
until its odd integral cancels. At order $K$ it keeps the corresponding
odd degree $2K+1$ in $h$. This supplies the claimed $O(B^{-K-1})$
remainder without an unproved parity improvement.
\end{remark}

\subsection{A fully explicit leading bound}

\begin{proposition}\label{lcw:ulb:prop:PBexplicit}
Under the hypotheses of Theorem~\ref{lcw:ulb:thm:PB}, for every $B>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:ulb:eq:PBexplicit}
 \left|\sqrt{2\pi B}\,\Prob(S=m)-1\right|\le\frac{12}{B}.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For $|\theta|\le1/2$, write
$\log\phi(\theta)=-B\theta^2/2+R(\theta)+iI(\theta)$.
We first prove
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:ulb:eq:RI}
 |R(\theta)|\le B\theta^4/8,
 \qquad |I(\theta)|\le B|\theta|^3/3.
\end{equation}
For one parameter put $q=p(1-p)$ and $u=4q\sin^2(\theta/2)$.
Then $u\le1/16$, and the real remainder is
\[
 q\{\theta^2/2-2\sin^2(\theta/2)\}
       +\tfrac12\{\log(1-u)+u\}.
\]
The first term has absolute value at most $q\theta^4/24$.
For the second,
\[
 \tfrac12|\log(1-u)+u|
 \le\frac{u^2}{4(1-u)}
 \le\frac4{15}q^2\theta^4\le\frac1{15}q\theta^4.
\]
Their sum is less than $q\theta^4/8$. For the imaginary part,
\[
 \frac{d^3}{d\theta^3}\log(1-p+pe^{i\theta})
 =-\frac{iqe^{i\theta}(1-p-pe^{i\theta})}
          {(1-p+pe^{i\theta})^3}.
\]
On this real interval its modulus is at most $2q$, since the
numerator apart from $q$ has modulus at most one and the denominator
has modulus at least $\cos^3(1/4)>1/2$. The centered first derivative
vanishes and the second derivative at zero is real. Taylor's theorem
gives the imaginary bound $q|\theta|^3/3$. Summing proves
\eqref{lcw:ulb:eq:RI}.

Put $a=15/32$. The real exponent is at most $-aB\theta^2$.
Using the real mean-value inequality for exponentials and
$1-\cos I\le I^2/2$, obtain
\[
 |\Re\phi(\theta)-e^{-B\theta^2/2}|
 \le e^{-aB\theta^2}
       \left(\frac{B\theta^4}{8}+\frac{B^2\theta^6}{18}\right).
\]
Integrating over the entire real line and multiplying by
$\sqrt{B/(2\pi)}$ bounds the local normalized error by
\[
 \frac1B\left\{
 \frac{3}{32\sqrt2\,a^{5/2}}
 +\frac{5}{48\sqrt2\,a^{7/2}}\right\}<\frac2B.
\]
For example, $a>4/9$ and $1/\sqrt2<3/4$ bound the braces by
$45927/24576<2$.

On $1/2\le|\theta|\le\pi$, use \eqref{lcw:ulb:eq:localization}
and enlarge to two infinite Gaussian tails. The elementary inequality
\[
 \int_h^\infty e^{-cB\theta^2}\,d\theta
       \le\frac{e^{-cBh^2}}{2cBh}
\]
shows that the normalized characteristic-function tail is at most
$\pi^2e^{-B/(2\pi^2)}/\sqrt{2\pi B}$. The omitted Gaussian
tail is at most $4e^{-B/8}/\sqrt{2\pi B}$.
After multiplication by $B$, the suprema of these bounds over $B>0$
are, respectively,
\[
 \frac{\pi^3}{\sqrt{2\pi e}}<8,
 \qquad \frac8{\sqrt{2\pi e}}<2.
\]
The first inequality follows from $\pi<22/7$ and $e>8/3$;
the second from $\pi>3$ and $e>8/3$. Combining the local and tail
estimates proves \eqref{lcw:ulb:eq:PBexplicit}. The argument imposed
no lower restriction on $B$.
\end{proof}

For the lattice-bridge product, $B\ge m/2$, so this proposition gives
Corollary~\ref{lcw:ulb:cor:explicit}. The numerical constant $24$ is an
effective certificate; its optimal value is an open quantitative
question discussed in Section~\ref{lcw:ulb:sec:outlook}.

\section{The sparse collision and idle-step transition}\label{lcw:ulb:sp:sec:sparse}% label added by the write

Let $N=2m+\epsilon$, where $\epsilon\in\{0,1\}$, and let $D\geq1$.
A bridge is an $N$-step closed walk in $\mathbb Z^D$ with steps
$0,\pm e_1,\ldots,\pm e_D$. Write $J$ for its number of idle steps.
For each axis $i$, closure forces the positive and negative step counts
to agree; call their common value $K_i$. Thus
\[
 J\equiv\epsilon\pmod2,\qquad
 \frac{J-\epsilon}{2}+\sum_{i=1}^D K_i=m.
\]
Let
\[
 R=\#\{i:K_i\geq2\},\quad U=\#\{i:K_i\geq3\},\quad
 K_{\rm occ}=\#\{i:K_i\geq1\}.
\]
Here a repeated axis means an axis used at least twice in each direction.
This is a count of axes, not a count of individual step coincidences.

Give a bridge weight $\lambda^J$, where $\lambda\geq0$, and denote the
total weight by $A_{N,D}(\lambda)$. Define the entire functions
\[
 F(t)=\sum_{k\geq0}\frac{t^k}{(k!)^2}=I_0(2\sqrt t),\qquad
 H_0(t)=\sum_{h\geq0}\frac{t^h}{(2h)!},\qquad
 H_1(t)=\sum_{h\geq0}\frac{t^h}{(2h+1)!}.
\]
The square-root notation is only shorthand for these entire series.
The exact parity-reduced enumeration is
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:ulb:sp:eq:exact}
 A_{N,D}(\lambda)
 =N!\lambda^\epsilon[t^m]F(t)^D H_\epsilon(\lambda^2t).
\end{equation}
Indeed, a fixed vector of axis counts and idle count contributes the
multinomial number $N!/(J!\prod_i(K_i!)^2)$.

When $\epsilon=1$ and $\lambda=0$, the total weight in
\eqref{lcw:ulb:sp:eq:exact} is zero, so it cannot normalize a probability law.
We therefore define the normalized partition function by the entire
extension
\[
 \widetilde A_{N,D}(\lambda)
 :=N![t^m]F(t)^D H_\epsilon(\lambda^2t).
\]
For $\lambda>0$ this equals $A_{N,D}(\lambda)/\lambda^\epsilon$.
At $\lambda=0$, its associated limiting probability law is the uniform
law on bridges with exactly $\epsilon$ idle steps. In particular it is
the usual nonlazy bridge law for $\epsilon=0$, and the exactly-one-idle
bridge law for $\epsilon=1$. Assertions about the odd law at zero below
refer to this extension, not to normalization of zero total weight.

The two transition parameters are
\[
 \theta=\frac{m^2}{D},\qquad
 \beta=\lambda\sqrt{\frac mD}.
\]
The first controls repeated axes; the second controls the idle count.
The original unweighted bridge measure $\lambda=1$ has $\beta\to0$
throughout the regime of bounded $\theta$ considered here. A nonzero
idle transition is observed by allowing the idle weight to grow.

\begin{theorem}[Uniform sparse partition function]\label{lcw:ulb:sp:thm:count}
Fix $K,B<\infty$. Uniformly over integers $D\geq1$, parities
$\epsilon\in\{0,1\}$, and $\lambda\geq0$ such that
$0<\theta\leq K$ and $0\leq\beta\leq B$, as $m\to\infty$,
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:ulb:sp:eq:count}
 \widetilde A_{N,D}(\lambda)
 =\frac{N!D^m}{m!}e^{-\theta/4}H_\epsilon(\beta^2)
 \left(1+\frac{C_\epsilon(\theta,\beta)}m+O_{K,B}(m^{-2})\right),
\end{equation}
where, with $c=\beta^2$,
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:ulb:sp:eq:C}
 C_\epsilon(\theta,\beta)
 =\frac\theta4-\frac{\theta^2}{72}
 +\frac\theta2\frac{cH_\epsilon'(c)}{H_\epsilon(c)}
 -\frac12\frac{c^2H_\epsilon''(c)}{H_\epsilon(c)}.
\end{equation}
Multiplication by $\lambda^\epsilon$ gives the weighted count, with
the zero odd count correctly retained when $\lambda=0$.
\end{theorem}

Define a probability law $\mathcal P_{\epsilon,\beta}$ on the
nonnegative integers by its probability generating function
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:ulb:sp:eq:paritylaw}
 P_{\epsilon,\beta}(u)
 =\frac{u^\epsilon H_\epsilon(\beta^2u^2)}{H_\epsilon(\beta^2)}.
\end{equation}
For $\beta>0$ this is Poisson($\beta$) conditioned to have parity
$\epsilon$: it is $\cosh(\beta u)/\cosh\beta$ in the even case,
and $\sinh(\beta u)/\sinh\beta$ in the odd case.
At $\beta=0$ it is respectively the point mass at $0$ or at $1$.
This definition avoids conditioning a zero-parameter Poisson variable
on an event of probability zero.

\begin{theorem}[Joint sparse probability law]\label{lcw:ulb:sp:thm:law}
Under the same parameter restrictions, let $p_{m,D,\lambda}(j,r)$
be the joint law of $(J,R)$ under the weighted bridge measure, including
the minimal-idle extension at zero. For every fixed $a,b\geq1$,
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:ulb:sp:eq:l1}
 \sum_{j,r\geq0}a^j b^r
 \left|p_{m,D,\lambda}(j,r)
   -\mathcal P_{\epsilon,\beta}\{j\}
      e^{-\theta/4}\frac{(\theta/4)^r}{r!}\right|
 =O_{K,B,a,b}(m^{-1}).
\end{equation}
Consequently the idle count and the repeated-axis count are
asymptotically independent, uniformly in these moving parameters.
If $\theta\to\theta_*$ and $\beta\to\beta_*$ along a fixed parity,
their joint limit is
$\mathcal P_{\epsilon,\beta_*}\otimes\operatorname{Pois}(\theta_*/4)$.
Moreover
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:ulb:sp:eq:triple}
 \mathbb E U=\frac{\theta^2}{36m}+O_{K,B}(m^{-2}),\qquad
 \mathbb P(U\geq1)=O_{K,B}(m^{-1}).
\end{equation}
If $Q\sim\mathcal P_{\epsilon,\beta}$ and
$Z\sim\operatorname{Pois}(\theta/4)$ are independent, then
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:ulb:sp:eq:deficit}
 d_{\rm TV}\left(\mathcal L(m-K_{\rm occ}),
                 \mathcal L((Q-\epsilon)/2+Z)\right)
 =O_{K,B}(m^{-1}).
\end{equation}
Thus sparse occupied-axis fluctuations are described by an explicit
discrete law, on the natural scale of one axis.
\end{theorem}

\subsection{A uniform coefficient-extraction lemma}

For a function $f$ analytic on a neighborhood of the closed disk
$|z|\leq R$, with fixed $R>1$, define
\[
 T_m f:=\frac{m!}{m^m}[z^m]e^{mz}f(z).
\]
Writing $f(z)=\sum_{j\geq0}f_jz^j$ gives the finite exact identity
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:ulb:sp:eq:transfer}
 T_m f=\sum_{j=0}^m f_j\frac{(m)_j}{m^j}.
\end{equation}
Here $(m)_j=m(m-1)\cdots(m-j+1)$ is the falling factorial.
For $0\leq j\leq m$, the elementary product inequalities give
\[
 0\leq1-\frac{(m)_j}{m^j}
 \leq\frac{j(j-1)}{2m}.
\]
Cauchy's estimate $|f_j|\leq\|f\|_R R^{-j}$ and the exponentially
small tail $j>m$ show
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:ulb:sp:eq:transfer1}
 T_m f=f(1)+O_R(m^{-1}\|f\|_R).
\end{equation}
For a second order bound, apply the first two Bonferroni inequalities
to the product $\prod_{k=0}^{j-1}(1-k/m)$:
\[
 0\leq\frac{(m)_j}{m^j}-1+\frac{j(j-1)}{2m}
 \leq\frac12\left(\frac{j(j-1)}{2m}\right)^2.
\]
Summing again yields the uniform identity
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:ulb:sp:eq:transfer2}
 T_m f=f(1)-\frac{f''(1)}{2m}
        +O_R(m^{-2}\|f\|_R).
\end{equation}
The missing tails of both $f(1)$ and $f''(1)$ are exponentially small.
These estimates also apply to analytic families with a common sup norm
bound. This elementary lemma is what supplies uniformity for the
changing dimension; no uncontrolled saddle substitution is used.

\subsection{Proof of the count and joint law}

To mark repeated axes, put
\[
 F_w(t)=1+t+w(F(t)-1-t).
\]
The exact joint generating polynomial, after removal of the common
$\lambda^\epsilon$ factor, is
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:ulb:sp:eq:marked}
 N!u^\epsilon[t^m]F_w(t)^D
 H_\epsilon(\lambda^2u^2t).
\end{equation}
This formula is polynomial in $u,w,\lambda$, and remains meaningful
at $\lambda=0$ with the interpretation specified above.

Fix any complex disks $|u|\leq R_u$ and $|w|\leq R_w$, and a disk
$|z|\leq R$ with $R>1$. Put
$t_0=m/D=\theta/m$ and substitute $t=t_0z$.
The function $F_w(t_0z)$ is uniformly close to $1$ for large $m$;
its logarithm is the branch obtained by Taylor expansion at $0$.
Uniformly on these three fixed disks,
\[
 \log F_w(s)
 =s+\frac{w-2}{4}s^2+\frac{3-2w}{9}s^3+O_{R_w}(s^4).
\]
Hence
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:ulb:sp:eq:log}
 D\log F_w(t_0z)
 =mz+\frac{w-2}{4}\theta z^2
    +\frac{3-2w}{9m}\theta^2z^3+O_{K,R,R_w}(m^{-2}).
\end{equation}
The last estimate follows from
$Dt_0^4=m^4/D^3=\theta^3/m^2$.
Similarly $\lambda^2t_0=\beta^2$ exactly, so the idle factor is
$H_\epsilon(\beta^2u^2z)$, which is uniformly bounded on the
fixed disks because $\beta\leq B$.

Set
\[
 \alpha=\frac{w-2}{4}\theta,\quad
 b_0=\frac{3-2w}{9}\theta^2,\quad c=\beta^2u^2,\quad
 f(z)=e^{\alpha z^2}H_\epsilon(cz).
\]
Equations \eqref{lcw:ulb:sp:eq:log} and \eqref{lcw:ulb:sp:eq:transfer2} give
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:ulb:sp:eq:markedexp}
 \frac{m!}{D^m}[t^m]F_w(t)^D H_\epsilon(\lambda^2u^2t)
 =f(1)+\frac{b_0f(1)-f''(1)/2}{m}+O(m^{-2}).
\end{equation}
Expanding the derivative, the right-hand side is
\begin{multline*}
 e^\alpha\biggl[H_\epsilon(c)+\frac1m\bigl\{
 (b_0-\alpha-2\alpha^2)H_\epsilon(c)\\
 -2\alpha cH_\epsilon'(c)-\tfrac12c^2H_\epsilon''(c)
 \bigr\}\biggr]+O(m^{-2}).
\end{multline*}
All error constants here are uniform for the stipulated real parameters
and fixed complex marker disks. To justify applying the transfer to the
analytic remainder, observe from \eqref{lcw:ulb:sp:eq:transfer} and Cauchy's
bound that $|T_mg|\leq (1-1/R)^{-1}\|g\|_R$. Thus a remainder
$O(m^{-2})$ in sup norm remains $O(m^{-2})$ after transfer.

At $u=w=1$ one has $\alpha=-\theta/4$ and $b_0=\theta^2/9$.
The expression $b_0-\alpha-2\alpha^2$ then reduces to
$\theta/4-\theta^2/72$, and \eqref{lcw:ulb:sp:eq:markedexp} proves
Theorem~\ref{lcw:ulb:sp:thm:count}. The leading factor cannot approach zero:
$e^{-\theta/4}\geq e^{-K/4}$ and $H_\epsilon(\beta^2)\geq1$.
Division by the unmarked count is therefore uniform.

Let $G_{m,D,\lambda}(u,w)$ be the joint probability generating
function of $(J,R)$. Taking the ratio of the marked and unmarked
formulas, and restoring $u^\epsilon$, proves
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:ulb:sp:eq:pgf}
 G_{m,D,\lambda}(u,w)
 =\frac{u^\epsilon H_\epsilon(\beta^2u^2)}{H_\epsilon(\beta^2)}
     \exp\left(\frac\theta4(w-1)\right)+O(m^{-1})
\end{equation}
uniformly on every fixed complex polydisk in $u,w$.
Given $a,b\geq1$, choose $R_u>a$ and $R_w>b$. Cauchy's estimates
on the larger disks give a bound
$Cm^{-1}R_u^{-j}R_w^{-r}$ for each coefficient of the error.
Summing $a^jb^r$ times this bound yields
\eqref{lcw:ulb:sp:eq:l1}. This also shows why convergence of the generating
functions merely on a neighborhood of $1$ would be insufficient for
the asserted weighted norm: the present proof supplies every fixed
complex disk directly.

For the assertion about triple-use axes, introduce an extra marker
$v$ and set
\[
 F_{w,v}(t)=1+t+\frac{w t^2}{4}
          +wv\left(F(t)-1-t-\frac{t^2}{4}\right).
\]
Its logarithmic coefficient of $t^3$ is
$wv/36-w/4+1/3$. Therefore the argument above, uniformly also
on a fixed disk about $v=1$, gives a $v$ derivative at $w=v=1$
equal to
\[
 \frac{\theta^2}{36m}e^{-\theta/4}H_\epsilon(\beta^2)
 +O_{K,B}(m^{-2})
\]
for the normalized numerator. Division by the unmarked expression
proves the expectation in \eqref{lcw:ulb:sp:eq:triple}; Markov's inequality
proves its probability bound. Differentiation of the error is valid
by Cauchy's estimate on a slightly larger fixed $v$ disk.

On the event $U=0$, every occupied axis has one or two pairs, so
\[
 m-K_{\rm occ}=(J-\epsilon)/2+R.
\]
The failure probability is $O(m^{-1})$. Apply the data-processing
inequality for total variation to \eqref{lcw:ulb:sp:eq:l1} at $a=b=1$
and add this failure probability. This proves
\eqref{lcw:ulb:sp:eq:deficit} and completes the theorem.

\subsection{Consequences and scope}

For unweighted bridges $\lambda=1$, $\beta^2=m/D=\theta/m$.
Thus $J=\epsilon$ with probability $1-O_K(m^{-1})$, and the
occupied-axis deficit has the Poisson($\theta/4$) approximation
in total variation with error $O_K(m^{-1})$.
For example, the even count is
\[
 A_{2m,D}(1)=\frac{(2m)!D^m}{m!}e^{-\theta/4}
 \left(1+\frac{3\theta/4-\theta^2/72}{m}
 +O_K(m^{-2})\right),
\]
because $H_0(\theta/m)=1+\theta/(2m)+O(m^{-2})$.
For odd lengths,
\[
 A_{2m+1,D}(1)=\frac{(2m+1)!D^m}{m!}e^{-\theta/4}
 \left(1+\frac{5\theta/12-\theta^2/72}{m}
 +O_K(m^{-2})\right),
\]
using $H_1(\theta/m)=1+\theta/(6m)+O(m^{-2})$.
These statements remain uniform as $\theta\downarrow0$.

The theorem addresses the critical sparse range $m^2/D=O(1)$.
It does not itself describe a diverging repeated-axis mean
$m^2/D\to\infty$. The global Bernoulli-product saddle theorem
can cover counts in that wider range, but a Gaussian or
mod-Poisson description of the repeated-axis law would require
additional uniform marked estimates.

\section{The Gaussian transition and its dimension thresholds}
\label{lcw:ulb:sec:dense-gaussian}

For integers $N,D\ge1$, let
\[
 R_{N,D}=\frac{N!}{(2D+1)^N}[z^N]e^z I_0(2z)^D,
 \qquad
 G_{N,D}=\left(\frac{2D+1}{4\pi N}\right)^{D/2}.
\]
Thus $R_{N,D}$ is the return probability of the walk whose $2D+1$
equiprobable increments are $0,\pm e_1,\ldots,\pm e_D$.
The covariance matrix of one increment is $2(2D+1)^{-1}I_D$, so
$G_{N,D}$ is its Gaussian local-limit prediction. Put
\[
 \rho=\frac ND,\qquad q=\rho^{-1}=\frac DN.
\]
The result is uniform in the two integer parameters; in particular,
it does not first fix the dimension.

\begin{theorem}[Dense expansion and Gaussian threshold]
\label{lcw:ulb:thm:dense-gaussian}
There are absolute constants $C$ and $\rho_0$ such that, for
$D\ge1$ and $N/D\ge\rho_0$,
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:ulb:eq:dense-uniform}
 \log\frac{R_{N,D}}{G_{N,D}}
 =-\frac{3D}{16N}+\frac{D^3}{48N^2}
 +O\left(\frac{D^2}{N^2}+\frac{D^4}{N^3}
               +\frac1N+e^{-N/(4D)}\right).
\end{equation}
The implied constant is absolute. Consequently, along every sequence
with $N/D\to\infty$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $R_{N,D}/G_{N,D}\to1$ if and only if $D^3/N^2\to0$;
\item if $D^3/N^2\to\xi\in(0,\infty)$, then
      $R_{N,D}/G_{N,D}\to e^{\xi/48}$;
\item if $D^3/N^2\to\infty$, then
      $\log(R_{N,D}/G_{N,D})\sim D^3/(48N^2)$.
\end{enumerate}
In particular, the critical scale is $N\asymp D^{3/2}$.
If $N/D^{3/2}\to s\in(0,\infty)$, the critical ratio is
$\exp(1/(48s^2))$. The same conclusions hold for even and odd $N$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We first convert the parity-resolved saddle into a saddle involving
$e^z I_0(2z)^D$. This conversion is justified below; it is not an
assumption that a second saddle can be discarded uniformly.

Write $N=2m+\epsilon$, with $\epsilon\in\{0,1\}$, and set
\[
 F(t)=I_0(2\sqrt t),\qquad
 H_0(t)=\cosh\sqrt t,\qquad
 H_1(t)=\frac{\sinh\sqrt t}{\sqrt t}.
\]
The exact parity identity is
\[
 [z^N]e^zI_0(2z)^D=[t^m]F(t)^DH_\epsilon(t).
\]
Theorem~\ref{lcw:ulb:thm:global} for this coefficient has relative
error $O(1/m)=O(1/N)$, with an absolute constant.

Define
\[
 B(z)=z\frac{d}{dz}\log I_0(2z),\quad
 A_+(z)=DB(z)+z,
\]
and let $\zeta>0$ solve $A_+(\zeta)=N$. Existence and uniqueness
follow from positivity of the tilted variance: $A_+'(z)>0$;
also $A_+(0)=0$ and $A_+(z)\to\infty$. Set
\[
 V_+=\zeta A_+'(\zeta).
\]
We claim the following uniformly valid positive-saddle formula:
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:ulb:eq:dense-positive-saddle}
 R_{N,D}=
 \frac{N!e^{\zeta}I_0(2\zeta)^D}
 {(2D+1)^N\zeta^N\sqrt{2\pi V_+}}
 \left(1+O\left(N^{-1}+e^{-N/(4D)}\right)\right).
\end{equation}

For completeness, put
\[
 Q_\epsilon(z)=1+(-1)^\epsilon e^{-2z}.
\]
Then $H_\epsilon(z^2)=e^zQ_\epsilon(z)/(2z^\epsilon)$.
If $\eta^2$ is the exact parity saddle, its equation is
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:ulb:eq:dense-exact-saddle}
 A_+(\eta)+\eta\frac{Q_\epsilon'(\eta)}{Q_\epsilon(\eta)}=N.
\end{equation}
The standard large-positive-argument expansion of $I_0$ and its
derivatives implies
\[
 B(z)=2z-\tfrac12+O(z^{-1}),\quad
 B'(z)=2+O(z^{-2}),\quad B''(z)=O(z^{-3}).
\]
Hence $\zeta\asymp\rho$, $A_+'(z)\asymp D$ near $\zeta$,
and, in fact, $\zeta\ge N/(2D+1)\ge\rho/3$ exactly, since
$I_1(2\zeta)/I_0(2\zeta)<1$ by the positive integral representation.
For $z\ge1$ the perturbing term in
\eqref{lcw:ulb:eq:dense-exact-saddle} is $O(ze^{-2z})$, and its derivative
is $O(ze^{-2z})$. The mean-value theorem, first on
$[\zeta-1,\zeta+1]$ and then between the two roots, gives
\[
 |\eta-\zeta|\le C\frac{\zeta e^{-2\zeta}}D.
\]
The positive exponent
$E(z)=D\log I_0(2z)+z-N\log z$ has $E'(\zeta)=0$ and
$E''(z)=O(D/\zeta)$ on this short interval. Its change is therefore
$O(\zeta e^{-4\zeta}/D)$, and $\log Q_\epsilon(\eta)=O(e^{-2\zeta})$.
If $V_t$ denotes the variance at the exact parity saddle, direct
differentiation gives
\[
 4V_t=\eta A_+'(\eta)+
 \eta\frac{d}{d\eta}\left(
       \eta\frac{Q_\epsilon'(\eta)}{Q_\epsilon(\eta)}\right).
\]
It follows that $4V_t/V_+=1+O(\zeta e^{-2\zeta})$.
The factor $1/2$ in $H_\epsilon$ consequently cancels the factor
$2$ from replacing $\sqrt{V_t}$ by $\sqrt{V_+}/2$.
Since $\zeta e^{-2\zeta}=O(e^{-\rho/4})$, the exact parity
saddle theorem proves \eqref{lcw:ulb:eq:dense-positive-saddle}.
This also explains the uniform treatment of the two parities.

It remains to expand this real saddle. Let
\[
 c=1+\frac1{2D}\in[1,3/2],\qquad
 w=2\zeta,\qquad v=cw,
\]
and define
\[
 \ell(w)=\log I_0(w)-w+\tfrac12\log(2\pi w),\qquad
 \ell_c(v)=\ell(v/c).
\]
The classical expansion \cite{dlmf1040}, for positive $w\to\infty$, is
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:ulb:eq:dense-bessel-log}
 \ell(w)=\frac1{8w}+\frac1{16w^2}+O(w^{-3}).
\end{equation}
The expansion may be differentiated to the fixed orders used here;
this follows either from the differentiated Bessel expansions or
from Laplace's method applied to
$I_0(w)=\pi^{-1}\int_0^\pi e^{w\cos\theta}\,d\theta$.
All resulting bounds for $\ell_c$ are uniform for $1\le c\le3/2$.
The saddle equation becomes
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:ulb:eq:dense-v-equation}
 \rho=v-\tfrac12+v\ell_c'(v)
      =v-\tfrac12-\frac{c}{8v}-\frac{c^2}{8v^2}+O(v^{-3}).
\end{equation}
Its derivative with respect to $v$ is $1+O(v^{-2})$, uniformly
in $c$. Substitution followed by the mean-value theorem therefore
gives the uniform inverse expansion
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:ulb:eq:dense-v-expansion}
 v=\rho+\tfrac12+\frac{c}{8\rho}
       +\frac{2c^2-c}{16\rho^2}+O(\rho^{-3}).
\end{equation}

Applying Stirling's formula with remainder $O(1/N)$ to
\eqref{lcw:ulb:eq:dense-positive-saddle}, and subtracting $\log G_{N,D}$,
yields
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:ulb:eq:dense-rate-representation}
 \log\frac{R_{N,D}}{G_{N,D}}
   =D\mathcal A(\rho,c)+\frac12\log\frac{N}{V_+}
       +O\left(N^{-1}+e^{-\rho/4}\right),
\end{equation}
where
\[
 \mathcal A(\rho,c)
   =(\rho+\tfrac12)\log\frac\rho v-\rho+v+\ell_c(v).
\]
There is no untracked $D$-dependent constant here: the factors
$(2D+1)^N$, the Gaussian normalization, and the Bessel prefactor
have all been retained exactly.

Using \eqref{lcw:ulb:eq:dense-v-expansion} in this last display gives
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:ulb:eq:dense-rate-series}
 \mathcal A(\rho,c)
 =\frac{c-1}{8\rho}
   +\frac{1}{\rho^2}\left(\frac1{48}+\frac{c^2-c}{16}\right)
   +O(\rho^{-3}).
\end{equation}
Furthermore, since $V_+=D v\,d\rho/dv$,
\[
 \frac12\log\frac{N}{V_+}
 =-\frac1{4\rho}
   +\frac1{\rho^2}\left(\frac1{16}-\frac c8\right)
   +O(\rho^{-3}).
\]
Because $D(c-1)=1/2$, the terms of order $\rho^{-1}$ combine
to $-3/(16\rho)$. The leading term of order $D\rho^{-2}$
is $D/(48\rho^2)$, and all other second-order terms are
$O(\rho^{-2})$. Multiplying the uniform remainder in
\eqref{lcw:ulb:eq:dense-rate-series} by $D$ proves
\eqref{lcw:ulb:eq:dense-uniform}.

Finally put $\chi=D/\rho^2=D^3/N^2$. Formula
\eqref{lcw:ulb:eq:dense-uniform} reads
\[
 \log(R_{N,D}/G_{N,D})
 =\frac\chi{48}-\frac3{16\rho}
    +O\left(\frac\chi\rho+\rho^{-2}+N^{-1}+e^{-\rho/4}\right).
\]
This gives the three asserted limits. For the ``only if'' direction
in the first assertion, any failure of $\chi\to0$ has a subsequence
on which $\chi$ converges to a positive finite value or tends to
infinity, or a further subsequence of one of these two forms.
The other two assertions then preclude a ratio tending to one.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Nonlazy walks and failure of every constant Gaussian bound]
\label{lcw:ulb:cor:nonlazy-dense}
For positive even $N$, let
\[
 R^0_{N,D}=\frac{N!}{(2D)^N}[z^N]I_0(2z)^D,\qquad
 G^0_{N,D}=\left(\frac{D}{2\pi N}\right)^{D/2}.
\]
Uniformly as $N/D\to\infty$,
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:ulb:eq:nonlazy-dense}
 \log\frac{R^0_{N,D}}{2G^0_{N,D}}
 =-\frac{D}{4N}+\frac{D^3}{48N^2}
  +O\left(\frac{D^2}{N^2}+\frac{D^4}{N^3}+\frac1N\right).
\end{equation}
The three conclusions of Theorem~\ref{lcw:ulb:thm:dense-gaussian} hold with
$R/G$ replaced by $R^0/(2G^0)$. In particular, there is no universal
finite constant $K$ for which
\[
 R^0_{N,D}\le K\left(\frac{D}{2\pi N}\right)^{D/2}
 \qquad\hbox{for every positive even $N$ and every $D$}.
\]
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The exact parity identity now has no idle factor:
$[z^{2m}]I_0(2z)^D=[t^m]F(t)^D$. Its variance in the $t$ variable
is exactly one fourth of the variance in $z$, yielding the lattice
factor $2$ in the denominator of \eqref{lcw:ulb:eq:nonlazy-dense}.
The saddle computation is otherwise exactly the preceding proof
with $c=1$. Thus the $\rho^{-1}$ term in $D\mathcal A$ vanishes,
while the prefactor contributes $-1/(4\rho)$ and
$D\mathcal A$ contributes $D/(48\rho^2)$. There is no parity
approximation and hence no exponentially small error to retain.
Choosing any even $N$ with $N\sim D^{4/3}$ gives
\[
 \log\frac{R^0_{N,D}}{2G^0_{N,D}}
       \sim\frac{D^{1/3}}{48}\longrightarrow\infty,
\]
which excludes every finite $K$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[The side of approach to the Gaussian value]
\label{lcw:ulb:cor:gaussian-sign}
If $D\to\infty$ and $N/D^2\to s\in(0,\infty)$, then
\[
 D\log\frac{R_{N,D}}{G_{N,D}}
     \longrightarrow\frac{1-9s}{48s^2}.
\]
For even $N$ in the same regime,
\[
 D\log\frac{R^0_{N,D}}{2G^0_{N,D}}
     \longrightarrow\frac{1-12s}{48s^2}.
\]
Thus the eventual signs switch at $s=1/9$ in the lazy model and
$s=1/12$ in the nonlazy model, away from the critical equality cases.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Multiply \eqref{lcw:ulb:eq:dense-uniform} or \eqref{lcw:ulb:eq:nonlazy-dense} by $D$.
Every remainder is $O(D^{-1})$ or exponentially smaller in this regime.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[The eventual Gaussian upper-bound thresholds]
\label{lcw:ulb:cor:eventual-threshold}
Let $T_0(D)$ be the least positive even integer $M$ such that
$R^0_{N,D}\le2G^0_{N,D}$ for every positive even $N\ge M$.
Let $T_1(D)$ be the least positive integer $M$ such that
$R_{N,D}\le G_{N,D}$ for every integer $N\ge M$.
These thresholds are finite, and
\[
 \frac{T_0(D)}{D^2}\longrightarrow\frac1{12},\qquad
 \frac{T_1(D)}{D^2}\longrightarrow\frac1{9}
 \qquad(D\to\infty).
\]
More precisely, for each $\delta>0$ and all sufficiently large $D$,
the nonlazy upper bound holds simultaneously at every even
$N\ge(1/12+\delta)D^2$, and the lazy upper bound holds at every
$N\ge(1/9+\delta)D^2$. Each model has a failing time arbitrarily
close to its corresponding lower scale $(1/12-\delta)D^2$ or
$(1/9-\delta)D^2$ when that scale is positive.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
For the nonlazy model multiply \eqref{lcw:ulb:eq:nonlazy-dense} by $\rho$.
For the lazy model do the same in \eqref{lcw:ulb:eq:dense-uniform}.
Writing $a=1/4$ or $a=3/16$, respectively, gives
\[
 \rho\log(\text{return}/\text{Gaussian})
 =-a+\frac{D}{48\rho}
  +O\left(\rho^{-1}+D\rho^{-2}+D^{-1}
              +\rho e^{-\rho/4}\right),
\]
where the last term is omitted in the nonlazy case and its Gaussian
includes the period factor. For every fixed $c>0$, the error is
$O_c(D^{-1})$ uniformly on the entire half-line $\rho\ge cD$.
Thus if $c>1/(48a)$, the expression is at most
$-a+1/(48c)+O_c(D^{-1})<0$ for all sufficiently large $D$,
simultaneously for every allowed time on that half-line.

If $0<c<1/(48a)$, choose an allowed integer $N_D$ nearest to $cD^2$.
Corollary~\ref{lcw:ulb:cor:gaussian-sign} makes its ratio strictly greater
than one for large $D$, so the eventual threshold exceeds $N_D$.
Together these statements squeeze the thresholds to the asserted
limits; parity rounding changes times by only $O(1)$.
For each fixed dimension, finiteness in the nonlazy case follows from
the eventual bound in \cite{ballsterbenz}; in the lazy case it follows
from the negative first fixed-dimension correction established below.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The first two fixed-dimension coefficients}
\label{lcw:ulb:subsec:dense-cumulant-check}

The coefficient of $D^3/N^2$ has an independent check that also
explains the cancellation of a prospective $D^2/N$ obstruction.
For fixed $D$, Fourier inversion gives
\[
 R_{N,D}=(2\pi)^{-D}\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^D}
 \left(\frac{1+2\sum_{j=1}^D\cos\theta_j}{2D+1}\right)^N
 \,d\theta.
\]
With $a=2D+1$ and $\theta_j=\sqrt{a/(2N)}\,x_j$, write
$S_r=\sum_{j=1}^D x_j^r$. Locally near $\theta=0$, the exponent through order $N^{-2}$ is
\[
 -\frac{S_2}{2}+\frac{A(x)}N+\frac{B(x)}{N^2}+O_D(N^{-3}
 (1+\|x\|^8)),
\]
where
\[
 A(x)=\frac{aS_4}{48}-\frac{S_2^2}{8},\qquad
 B(x)=-\frac{a^2S_6}{2880}+
       \frac{aS_2S_4}{96}-\frac{S_2^3}{24}.
\]
For fixed $D$, the absolute characteristic function is bounded by
some $q_D<1$ outside any sufficiently small fixed neighborhood of
zero: the idle step makes this walk aperiodic. On the local part,
a rescaled cutoff $\|x\|\le N^\delta$, with fixed sufficiently small
$\delta>0$, gives an integrable Gaussian Taylor remainder; the
remaining local tail is exponentially small in $N^{2\delta}$.
Thus integration against $D$ independent standard Gaussian variables
gives
\[
 \mathbb E A=-\frac{3D}{16},
 \qquad
 \mathbb E B+\frac12\mathbb E A^2
   =\frac{D(32D^2-21D-8)}{1536}.
\]
Thus, for fixed $D$,
\begin{align*}
 \frac{R_{N,D}}{G_{N,D}}
 &=1-\frac{3D}{16N}
   +\frac{D(32D^2-21D-8)}{1536N^2}+O_D(N^{-3}),\\
 \log\frac{R_{N,D}}{G_{N,D}}
 &=-\frac{3D}{16N}
   +\frac{D(4D^2-6D-1)}{192N^2}+O_D(N^{-3}).
\end{align*}
This calculation is a check of the coefficients, not the source of
the uniform growing-dimension error bound. The latter was proved
by the scalar parity saddle above. In particular, directly promoting
the fixed-$D$ error term $O_D(N^{-3})$ to a uniform statement would
not establish the dimension threshold.

\section{Exact computation and reproducible checks}
\label{lcw:ulb:sec:computation}

The proofs above establish the infinite-parameter assertions. The
accompanying programs provide independent finite checks, an exact
counterexample certificate, and numerical illustrations. Every large
dimension entry below is identified by its evaluation method.

\subsection{An integer coefficient recurrence}

For isotropic walks define
\[
 Q_{n,D}=(n!)^2[t^n]F(t)^D.
\]
These numbers are integers, with $Q_{0,D}=1$. The following recurrence
computes all coefficients through degree $m$ using $O(m^2)$ integer
arithmetic operations, without a loop over the dimension:
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:ulb:eq:miller}
 nQ_{n,D}=\sum_{k=1}^n\bigl((D+1)k-n\bigr)
                \binom nk^2 Q_{n-k,D}.
\end{equation}
To verify it, write $F(t)=\sum f_kt^k$, $F(t)^D=\sum g_nt^n$,
and compare coefficients in $F(F^D)'=DF'F^D$. This gives
$ng_n=\sum_{k=1}^n((D+1)k-n)f_kg_{n-k}$; multiplication by
$(n!)^2$ proves \eqref{lcw:ulb:eq:miller}. The closed-walk count is
then the positive finite sum
\begin{equation}\label{lcw:ulb:eq:exactsum}
 A_{N,D}(\lambda)=\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor N/2\rfloor}
    \binom N{2k}\binom{2k}{k}Q_{k,D}\lambda^{N-2k}.
\end{equation}
For rational $\lambda$, this is an exact rational computation.
The arithmetic-operation count does not bound bit complexity: the
integers can be large, and the recurrence itself includes signed terms.

The independent implementation multiplies truncated rational power
series directly. A third implementation enumerates endpoints of short
walks by dynamic programming, retaining separate positive and negative
directional weights. These methods check different parts of the
combinatorial identity. The finite counterexample also has its own
standard-library-only verifier, including the Machin lower bound for
$\pi$ and the exact positive integer comparison.

\subsection{How to reproduce and extend the calculations}

The archive contains the following entry points:
\begin{description}[style=nextline]
\item[\texttt{code/verify\_lyons.py}]
Verifies the exact finite counterexample with rational arithmetic.
It needs only the Python standard library and runs independently of
the numerical package.
\item[\texttt{code/verify\_endpoints.py}]
Checks the general endpoint coefficient identity against an independent
step-by-step dynamic program. It uses only exact rational arithmetic
and the Python standard library.
\item[\texttt{code/walks.py}]
Implements exact isotropic counts, rational anisotropic convolution,
the scalar parity saddle, cumulants, and arbitrary fixed-order
Edgeworth corrections. Numerical evaluations use logarithms to avoid
overflow; rational activities passed to the numerical routines should
first be converted to \texttt{mpmath} values at the chosen precision.
\item[\texttt{code/reproduce.py}]
Runs the complete saved verification grid and regenerates
\texttt{data/verification.json}, the three tables, and both figures.
\end{description}
From the archive root, the commands are
\begin{verbatim}
python code/verify_lyons.py
python code/verify_endpoints.py
python code/reproduce.py
latexmk -pdf article.tex
\end{verbatim}
Package dependencies are recorded
in \texttt{requirements.txt}; the PDF can be built directly from the
included tables and figures without rerunning the numerical work.

The finite exact certificates and the analytic inequalities have
different roles from floating-point diagnostics. The latter do not
perform outward rounding. A certified numerical interval for a large
count requires enclosing the saddle, its generator value, and its
variance, then applying \eqref{lcw:ulb:eq:explicit}.

\section{Further research questions and a development programme}
\label{lcw:ulb:sec:outlook}

The results leave several concrete problems. The first three are the
most direct continuations: they seek usable replacements for the
disproved Gaussian bound, a precise transition window, and effective
certification. Later items extend the probability laws or the class of
walks. Each question is proposed as a task still open within this
investigation; an exhaustive literature search remains necessary
before assigning historical novelty.

\subsection{A corrected universal Gaussian upper bound}

The original conjecture cannot be repaired by changing its constant.
The dense theorem suggests an exponential correction depending on
$D^3/N^2$. Determine the smallest explicit functions $U(D,N)$ for which
\[
 R^0_{N,D}\le 2G^0_{N,D}\,e^{U(D,N)}
\]
holds for every positive even $N$ and $D\ge1$, and for which
$U(D,N)\sim D^3/(48N^2)$ whenever that term dominates in the dense
range. A result restricted to $N\ge cD$ with an explicit $c$ would
already be useful. The positive scalar saddle and the $12/B$ local
bound provide a rigorous starting inequality. The remaining task is
to replace the implicitly defined saddle by an explicit expression
while retaining the sharp exponential scale.

Corollary~\ref{lcw:ulb:cor:eventual-threshold} already determines the leading
last-failure thresholds: $T_0(D)\sim D^2/12$ and $T_1(D)\sim D^2/9$.
What remains is an explicit dimension onset for the uniform
upper-bound region, lower-order terms in these thresholds, and a
classification of finite-dimension exceptions. Those questions demand
more information than the leading quadratic scale.

\subsection{The narrow window near the quadratic crossing}

For nonlazy walks the leading sign changes at $N/D^2=1/12$;
for the particular lazy walk it changes at $1/9$. Determine the next
terms in the crossing locations and the profile in windows such as
\[
 N=\frac{D^2}{12}+\alpha D+O(1)
\]
for even nonlazy lengths. On this scale the leading terms already
proved cancel, so a new term in the uniform scalar expansion and
an explicit first saddle correction are required. It would also be
valuable to prove uniqueness, or a controlled number, of crossings
of the ratio through one at each sufficiently large dimension.
The present sign corollary does not assert such uniqueness.

\subsection{Effective high-order bounds and a verified evaluator}

The $24/m$ envelope is explicit, whereas the constants $C_K$ in the
all-orders theorem are existential. Produce a recurrence for usable
constants and an onset for each $K$. A practical implementation should
enclose Bessel values and derivatives, locate the monotone saddle by
interval arithmetic, and bound the local integral together with its
tails. One can then compare the cost of using more terms with that of
evaluating an exact finite coefficient.

A related sharp problem is to determine
\[
 \sup B\left|\sqrt{2\pi B}\,\Prob(S=\E S)-1\right|
\]
over Bernoulli sums with integer mean, and the corresponding supremum
over the narrower Bessel--hyperbolic family. The proof gives the upper
bound $12$ in the first class. Numerical improvement alone would not
identify the extremal distributions or exclude singular parameter
limits.

\subsection{From rare collisions to Gaussian occupation fluctuations}

The sparse joint theorem assumes $m^2/D=O(1)$. Investigate the
intermediate sparse region
\[
 m/D\to0,\qquad m^2/D\to\infty.
\]
Repeated axes now have a diverging mean. Determine the range in which
a Poisson approximation remains accurate in total variation, and
the range in which centering and scaling yields a normal law. The
next structural scale is the appearance of a nonnegligible number
of triple-use axes, measured at first order by $m^3/D^2$.
The marked logarithm in \eqref{lcw:ulb:sp:eq:log} identifies the terms
that must be retained.

A successful theorem should control the joint law of $J$, the number
of double-use axes, and the number of triple-use axes, and should
match the proportional-dimension Gaussian occupation law in the
predecessor \cite{lcw}. This is a probability-law problem beyond the
already uniform counting formula.

\subsection{Anisotropic collision laws and effective dimension}

The counting theorem permits arbitrary $c_i$, but the sparse law was
proved only for $c_i=1$. For a sparse anisotropic tilt, the quantities
$\sum_i c_i$, $\sum_i c_i^2$, and $\sum_i c_i^3$ suggest the relevant
pair- and triple-collision scales. Formulate a condition excluding
domination by a single coordinate, then prove an anisotropic joint
Poisson law with explicit error. The natural candidate effective
dimension is
\[
 D_{\rm eff}=\frac{(\sum_i c_i)^2}{\sum_i c_i^2},
\]
when the denominator is nonzero. Determine how this parameter and
higher normalized power sums enter the first correction. The formula
is a proposed organizing parameter, not a theorem that one may
substitute it for $D$ everywhere.

\subsection{A moving idle activity in the dense regime}

The global theorem is uniform in $\lambda$, while the dense crossover
was calculated for $\lambda=1$ and $\lambda=0$. Classify the dense
asymptotics when the idle activity moves between these regimes or
grows with $D$. The parity suppression is controlled by the size of
$\lambda$ times the positive saddle, so its transition can occur
on a different scale from the Gaussian correction. A full theorem
should identify when both lattice arcs matter, when one is negligible,
and how the covariance normalization changes the first two uniform
coefficients. The exact $H_\epsilon$ representation remains available
throughout.

\subsection{Nonzero endpoints and other step sets}

Corollary~\ref{lcw:ulb:cor:endpoint} already gives the uniform coefficient
expansion for arbitrary prescribed endpoints, using Bessel factors of
orders $|x_i|$. Determine explicit Gaussian and large-deviation
simplifications of that saddle when the coordinate profile of the
endpoint moves with the dimension. In particular, characterize which
power sums or maximal-coordinate conditions determine the transition
between central and displaced regimes. Near ballistic endpoints the
residual budget $\lfloor(N-|x|_1)/2\rfloor$ may be small, and a
separate exact or rare-event approximation may be preferable. The
variance lower bound and the negative-zero representation themselves
are already proved for these endpoints.

More generally, characterize finite symmetric step sets whose
parity- or residue-reduced return generator has only negative real
zeros, or factors into functions with an equally useful local
probability representation. For a general graph or a step set with
diagonal moves, coordinate independence can disappear; the present
product argument does not automatically survive.

\subsection{A staged formalization in \repo}

The exact finite counterexample is the most accessible first target.
Formalize the multinomial recurrence, compute $C_{13,16}$ by a
verified evaluator, prove the rational Machin lower bound, and close
the strict integer inequality. This yields an independently checkable
formal theorem without first developing asymptotic analysis.

The analytic programme can then be separated into reusable results:
countable Bernoulli products and coefficient tilting; the uniform
characteristic-function bound; the explicit local inequality;
the Bessel product and variance estimate; and the finite-order
integrated expansion. The sparse coefficient-transfer lemma is another
short independent target. The dense crossing theorem would follow
only after a formal uniform remainder for the differentiated Bessel
expansion. This order gives useful intermediate theorems at each stage.

\subsection{Implications for spanning-tree entropy estimates}

The return-probability conjecture arose in the study of spanning-tree
entropy, where Felker and Lyons express the entropy through a series
of return probabilities \cite{felkerlyons}. Investigate tail bounds
that combine exact initial coefficients with the corrected dense
estimate. The finite counterexample by itself does not invalidate
results whose tails use a separately established large-time bound;
the threshold and the assumptions of each application must be checked.
A useful outcome would be dimension-uniform certified truncation
rules for this series.

\paragraph{Recommended order of work.}
First formalize the exact counterexample and build an interval evaluator
for the already proved saddle bound. Then derive a corrected explicit
Gaussian upper inequality and resolve the quadratic crossing window.
The marked sparse transition and anisotropic probability laws provide
the next coherent research programme beyond counting.
