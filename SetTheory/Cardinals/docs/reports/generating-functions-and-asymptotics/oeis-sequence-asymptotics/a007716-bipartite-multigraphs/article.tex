\begin{abstract}
\textbf{Part I.}
Let $a_n$ count nonnegative integer $n\times n$ matrices of total sum $n$, modulo independent row and column permutations. Equivalently, these are bipartite multigraphs with $n$ edges, no isolated vertices and distinguished shores. We prove a relative equivalent
$a_n\sim B_n^2\exp(W(n)^2/2)/n!$, where $B_n$ is the Bell number, and a complete fixed-order expansion with rational functions of $W(n)$ as coefficients. An exact invariant-partition formula controls all vertex automorphisms; the nonnegligible parallel-edge contribution is retained by a positive weighted count. We give the first two corrections, a finite coefficient algorithm, and all-orders smooth inverse models with rigorous integer-threshold brackets. The result strengthens the published logarithmic estimate for this sequence. No full classification of exponentially small sectors is claimed.

\smallskip
\textbf{Part II.}
Choose uniformly an isomorphism class of bipartite multigraphs with $n$ edges, distinguished shores and no isolated vertices. We prove that the probability of a nontrivial vertex automorphism is $2W(n)^3/n+O((1+W(n))^7/n^2)$. Outside probability $O((1+W(n))^6/n^2)$, every nontrivial vertex group is generated by one transposition of two leaves with a common neighbor. Disconnection has probability asymptotic to $W(n)^2/n$ and is usually caused by one isolated edge. For the connected counting sequence A007718 we prove an all-orders finite-shift expansion in A007716, transfer the previously established complete relative asymptotic expansion, and give rigorous smooth inverse models and integer-threshold brackets. The proof distinguishes vertex automorphisms from parallel-edge permutations and controls graphs without a giant component. All orders are fixed, and no effective onset or priority claim is made.
\end{abstract}

\part{Asymptotic expansions and inverse growth (Report~88)}\label{bpm:part:one}

\section{The sequence and main results}
Write $a_n$ for the number of nonnegative integer $n\times n$ matrices whose entries sum to $n$, up to separate row and column permutations. Deleting zero rows and columns identifies these objects with bipartite multigraphs with distinguished shores, no isolated vertices and $n$ edges. Their first terms are
\[
1,1,4,10,33,91,298,910,3017,9945,34207,\ldots .
\]
This is OEIS A007716~\cite{OEIS}. Transposition is not an additional identification.
Let $B_n$ denote the Bell number and set $w=W(n)$, so that $w\ee^w=n$.

\begin{theorem}\label{bpm:thm:leading}
As $n\to\infty$,
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:eq:leading}
 a_n\sim\frac{B_n^2}{n!}\exp(w^2/2)
 \sim\frac{\exp\{n(w-\log w-1+2/w)+w^2/2-2\}}
 {(w+1)\sqrt{2\pi n}}.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
The factor $\exp(w^2/2)$ diverges. Keeping only the identity permutation in Burnside's lemma would miss it.

\begin{theorem}\label{bpm:thm:all}
For every fixed integer $J\ge0$, there are rational functions $Q_j(w)$, with $Q_0=1$, and a finite exponent $M_J$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:eq:all}
 a_n=\frac{B_n^2}{n!}\ee^{w^2/2}
 \left\{\sum_{j=0}^J\frac{Q_j(w)}{n^j}
       +O_J\!\left(\frac{(1+w)^{M_J}}{n^{J+1}}\right)\right\}.
\end{equation}
Section~\ref{bpm:sec:profiles} gives a finite exact algorithm. The first correction is
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:eq:Q1}
 Q_1(w)=\frac{w^2(w^4+11w^3+22w^2+12w-6)}{12(w+1)^2}.
\end{equation}
At order one, the remainder in braces is $O((1+w)^{10}/n^2)$.
\end{theorem}
All orders are fixed as $n$ tends to infinity. No convergence of the infinite series is asserted. In particular, this is stronger than a logarithmic equivalent.

\begin{writenote}
the exponent $M_J$ is shown to exist but not computed for $J\ge2$, and the
order-one bound $(1+w)^{10}$ is asserted from three displayed error sizes
(end of Section~\ref{bpm:sec:checks}); both are recorded as
Questions~\ref{bpm:q:MJ} and~\ref{bpm:q:ten}. Part~II
(Theorem~\ref{bpm:sym:thm:sym} and Lemma~\ref{bpm:sym:lem:residual})
identifies the term $w^3$ in $Q_1=R_1+w^3$ as the leaf-transposition mass
$Y_{n-2}/Z_n$.
\end{writenote}

Pavlichin, Jiao and Weissman~\cite[Theorem 12 and Appendix C]{PML} explicitly identify this sequence and prove $\log a_n\sim n\log n$. Their displayed result is $\exp((1+o(1))n\log n)$, despite the accompanying phrase ``precise asymptotics.'' The incidence-matrix results of Cameron, Prellberg and Stark~\cite{CPS,CPS2} concern zero-one matrices and different labeling conventions. We discuss this distinction further in Section~\ref{bpm:sec:scope}.

\section{Exact weighted counts and invariant partitions}
Let $M_{k,l}(n)$ be the number of nonnegative $k\times l$ matrices of total $n$ with no zero row or column. Define
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:eq:Z}
 Z_n=\sum_{k,l\ge0}\frac{M_{k,l}(n)}{k!l!}
     =\sum_{[G]}\frac1{|\Aut(G)|},
\end{equation}
where automorphisms preserve the shores. Orbit--stabilizer proves the second equality. Adding isolated vertices gives
\[
 \binom{kl+n-1}{n}=\sum_{i\le k,j\le l}\binom ki\binom lj M_{i,j}(n).
\]
Summing nonnegative terms after division by $k!l!$ yields
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:eq:mass}
 Z_n=\ee^{-2}\sum_{k,l\ge0}\frac{\binom{kl+n-1}{n}}{k!l!}.
\end{equation}
For $n>0$, terms with $kl=0$ are zero.

\begin{writenote}
the labelled analogue $\sum_{k,l}M_{k,l}(n)$, without the factor
$1/(k!\,l!)$, is OEIS A120733 (nonnegative matrices of total sum $n$ with no
zero row or column); the collection report
\nolinkurl{generating-functions-and-asymptotics/oeis-sequence-asymptotics/a260700-parabolic-double-cosets}
names it, with Kotěšovec's asymptotic note on it, as a different sequence
from its own count. No result transfers between the labelled and the
unlabelled count.
\end{writenote}

A pair of set partitions of $[n]$ represents a multigraph with edge labels $[n]$: label $i$ joins its containing block in each partition. Removing edge labels is the simultaneous $S_n$ action. Thus, if $f(\sigma)$ counts the set partitions invariant under $\sigma$,
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:eq:Burnside}
 a_n=\frac1{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_n}f(\sigma)^2.
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}\label{bpm:lem:fixed}
If $\sigma$ has $c$ element cycles with lengths $\ell_1,\ldots,\ell_c$, then
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:eq:fixed}
 f(\sigma)=\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_c}\prod_{S\in\pi}
 \sum_{k\mid\gcd(\ell_i:i\in S)}k^{|S|-1}.
\end{equation}
Moreover, writing $d=n-c$,
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:eq:globalf}
 B_c\le f(\sigma)\le B_c\prod_i\ell_i\le2^d B_c,
 \qquad
 Z_n=\frac1{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_n}B_{c(\sigma)}^2.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
An orbit of $k$ blocks consumes a nonempty group $S$ of element cycles. Its length $k$ divides each $\ell_i$ in that group. Each cycle chooses a residue phase among the $k$ blocks, and a common cyclic shift has no effect, giving $k^{|S|-1}$ choices. The groups of consumed cycles partition $[c]$, proving~\eqref{bpm:eq:fixed}.
The $k=1$ terms give $B_c$. For any group, its divisor sum is at most $\prod_{i\in S}\ell_i$: if $g$ is their gcd, the sum is at most $g^{|S|}$, including the case $|S|=1$. Also $\ell\le2^{\ell-1}$.
Finally, Dobinski's identity $B_c=\ee^{-1}\sum_{k\ge0}k^c/k!$ and
$\sum_{\sigma\in S_n}x^{c(\sigma)}=\rise xn$ turn the last sum in~\eqref{bpm:eq:globalf} into~\eqref{bpm:eq:mass}.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{An elementary Bell ratio bound}
For all $j\ge1$,
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:eq:Bellratio}
 \frac{B_j}{B_{j-1}}\ge\frac{j}{16\log(j+1)}.
\end{equation}
Bell numbers are moments of a Poisson variable, so they are log-convex and $B_j/B_{j-1}\ge B_j^{1/j}$. For $j\ge2$, put $k=\lfloor j/\log(j+1)\rfloor$. Then $k\ge j/(2\log(j+1))$, $k\log k\le j$, and the corresponding term of Dobinski's sum gives
\[
 B_j^{1/j}\ge k\exp\{-(1+\log k!)/j\}\ge k\ee^{-1-1/j}.
\]
This implies~\eqref{bpm:eq:Bellratio}; $j=1$ is immediate.

Let $s(n,n-d)$ be the unsigned Stirling number of the first kind. Since it equals $e_d(1,\ldots,n-1)$,
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:eq:Stirlingbound}
 s(n,n-d)\le\binom{n-1}{d}\fall{n-1}{d}
             \le\frac{\fall nd^2}{d!}.
\end{equation}
Every $d$-subset product is at most $\fall{n-1}{d}$, which proves the first inequality. Combining~\eqref{bpm:eq:globalf}--\eqref{bpm:eq:Stirlingbound}, the contribution of $d>D$ in~\eqref{bpm:eq:Burnside}, relative to $B_n^2/n!$, is at most
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:eq:deficittail}
 \sum_{d>D}\frac{(4C^2\log^2(n+1))^d}{d!}.
\end{equation}
With $D=\lfloor A\log^2(n+1)\rfloor$ and a sufficiently large fixed $A$, this is $\exp(-c\log^2n)$.

\subsection*{A quantitative comparison with the weighted count}
Split off the nontrivial element cycles used by block orbits of length at least two. If $h$ cycles are selected, their extra weight is at most $B_h$ times their lengths' product. Their total length is at most $2d$. On $d\le D$, equation~\eqref{bpm:eq:Bellratio} gives $B_{c-h}/B_c\le\eta^h$, where $\eta=C'\log n/n$. Thus
\[
 1\le\frac{f(\sigma)}{B_c}
 \le\sum_{h=0}^d\frac{B_h}{h!}(2d\eta)^h
 \le\frac1{1-2d\eta}=1+O(\log^3n/n).
\]
Here $B_h\le h!$, for example by writing each partition block as an increasing permutation cycle. Squaring, summing positive terms, and using~\eqref{bpm:eq:deficittail} proves
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:eq:asymmetry}
 \frac{a_n}{Z_n}=1+O(\log^3n/n).
\end{equation}
This bounds the weighted effect of automorphisms, rather than relying only on high-probability asymmetry.

\section{The relative weighted saddle}
Put $x=n/w=\ee^w$. Let $K,L$ be independent with Dobinski law
\[
 \Pp(K=k)=\frac{k^n}{\ee B_n k!},\qquad k\ge1.
\]
Equation~\eqref{bpm:eq:mass} gives exactly
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:eq:Dobinski}
 Z_n=\frac{B_n^2}{n!}\E R_n(KL),\qquad
 R_n(z)=\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}(1+j/z).
\end{equation}
For $\varepsilon\le1/4$ with $n\varepsilon^2\gg\log n$,
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:eq:concentrate}
 \Pp(|K/x-1|>\varepsilon)\le\exp(-c n\varepsilon^2),
 \qquad \Pp(K<x/2)\le\exp(-c n).
\end{equation}
Indeed Stirling reduces the logarithmic mass to
$h(k)-\tfrac12\log(2\pi k)+O(1/k)$, where
$h(k)=n\log k-k\log k+k$. Its real maximum is at $x$, and
$h''\le-n/(4x^2)$ on $[x/2,2x]$. Comparing with the term nearest $x$ gives the bounds there, with only polynomial summation factors. Below $x/2$ concavity gives an exponential loss. Above $2x$, the exact successive ratio $(1+1/k)^n/(k+1)$ is at most $\ee^{-cw}$, giving a geometric tail.

Choose $\varepsilon=w^{-3}$. On the central rectangle,
\[
 \log R_n(KL)=\frac{n(n-1)}{2KL}+O\!\left(\frac{n^3}{(KL)^2}\right)
 =\frac{w^2}2+O(w^2\varepsilon+w^2/n+w^4/n)
 =\frac{w^2}2+o(1).
\]
Three estimates control the complement, including rare small products.
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $K,L\ge x/2$, then $\log R_n(KL)\le2w^2$. Equation~\eqref{bpm:eq:concentrate} bounds the noncentral contribution by $\exp(2w^2-cn/w^6)$.
\item If $\min(K,L)<x/2$ but $KL\ge n^{3/2}$, then $\log R_n(KL)\le\sqrt n/2$, giving $2\exp(\sqrt n/2-cn)$.
\item If $KL<n^{3/2}$, bound the unnormalized sum~\eqref{bpm:eq:mass} directly by
\[
 \binom{KL+n-1}{n}\le[\ee(\sqrt n+1)]^n.
\]
Since $\ee^{-2}\sum1/(k!l!)\le1$, this region has mass at most $\exp(\tfrac12n\log n+O(n))$. The single Dobinski term nearest $x$ gives
\[
 \log(B_n^2/n!)\ge n\log n-2n\log w-n+2n/w-O(\log n),
\]
so this region is negligible even compared with $B_n^2/n!$.
\end{enumerate}
We have proved $Z_n\sim(B_n^2/n!)\ee^{w^2/2}$. Together with~\eqref{bpm:eq:asymmetry} and the classical Bell equivalent below, this proves Theorem~\ref{bpm:thm:leading}. The last two tail estimates remain exponentially small after multiplication by any fixed power of $n$; we will use this fact again.

\section{Bell expansions and uniform finite shifts}\label{bpm:sec:Bell}
The Bell EGF is $\exp(\ee^z-1)$. At its saddle $w\ee^w=n$, the cumulants are $\kappa_j=\ee^w T_j(w)$, with $T_j$ the Touchard polynomial. In particular,
\[
 \kappa_2=n(w+1),\quad \kappa_3=n(w^2+3w+1),\quad
 \kappa_4=n(w^3+6w^2+7w+1).
\]
The classical saddle method~\cite{OR} gives, to every fixed order, rational functions of $w$ and controlled remainders. We record the precise form needed here:
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:eq:Bell}
 B_n=\frac{\exp\{n(w-1+1/w)-1\}}{\sqrt{w+1}}
 \left(1+\frac{\beta_1(w)}n+O\!\left(\frac{(1+w)^2}{n^2}\right)\right),
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:eq:beta}
 \beta_1(w)=-\frac{w^2(2w^2+7w+10)}{24(w+1)^3}.
\end{equation}
At order $J$, the remainder is $O_J((1+w)^{J+1}/n^{J+1})$.

\begin{writenote}
the order-one case of~\eqref{bpm:eq:Bell}, with $\beta_1=-P(r)$ and the
same error $O(r^2/n^2)$, $r=w$, is already proved in the collection report
\nolinkurl{generating-functions-and-asymptotics/oeis-sequence-asymptotics/a277364-bell-asymptotics}
(its \nolinkurl{A277364_asymptotics.tex}, Section ``The Bell saddle and its
first correction'', equation \texttt{eq:bellfinal}), whose pre-correction
\texttt{eq:precorrection} is the Gaussian correction displayed below and
whose remark on two normalizations is the $1/(12n)$ step below. The
paragraph below is a second route to that statement; the all-orders form,
its uniformity in $w$ and the shifts~\eqref{bpm:eq:Bellshift} are not in
that report. The all-orders uniformity is argued here in one paragraph and
is recorded as Question~\ref{bpm:q:bellsaddle}.
\end{writenote}

Here are direct uniformity details. On the circle $z=w\ee^{i\theta}$, the logarithmic modulus loss is at least $c n w\theta^2$ for $|\theta|\le c_0/w$. Beyond this interval, discarding the inner cosine gives a loss at least $c n/w^2$. For the central variable $X=\theta\sqrt{n(w+1)}$, put $\epsilon=\sqrt{w/n}$. The exact phase before subtracting its linear and quadratic terms is
\[
 \epsilon^{-2}\left[\exp\left\{w\left(
 \exp\frac{i\epsilon X}{\sqrt{w(w+1)}}-1\right)\right\}-1\right].
\]
Its remaining singularity at zero is removable, and its correction is $O(\epsilon|X|^3)$ uniformly for $w\ge1$. On the auxiliary circle $|\epsilon|=c_J/(1+|X|)^3$, it is bounded. Cauchy expansion through odd degree $2J+1$, followed by symmetric Gaussian integration with cutoff $|X|\le\epsilon^{-1/4}$, gives error $O_J(\epsilon^{2J+2})$. This proves the stated fixed-order remainder. The first Gaussian correction is
\[
 \frac1n\left\{\frac{w^3+6w^2+7w+1}{8(w+1)^2}
       -\frac{5(w^2+3w+1)^2}{24(w+1)^3}\right\}.
\]
Adding the factorial correction $1/(12n)$ gives~\eqref{bpm:eq:beta}.

Let $b(x)$ be the logarithm of the leading factor in~\eqref{bpm:eq:Bell}. Exactly,
\[
 b'(x)=w_x-\frac{w_x}{2x(1+w_x)^2},\qquad w_x=W(x),
\]
and $b''(x)=w_x/(x(1+w_x))+O(x^{-2})$, $b'''(x)=O(x^{-2})$.
Uniformly for $0\le s\le2D+2$,
\begin{align}\label{bpm:eq:Bellshift}
 \log\frac{B_{n-s}}{B_n}
 ={}&-sw+\frac1n\left\{\frac{sw}{2(w+1)^2}
                     +\frac{s^2w}{2(w+1)}\right\}\nonumber\\
 &+O\!\left(\frac{s^3+s(1+w)+(1+w)^2}{n^2}\right).
\end{align}
This follows by Taylor expansion of the explicit smooth Bell model, using its remainder at the two endpoints. No derivative of an uncontrolled remainder is taken. At every fixed order, the same argument gives coefficients polynomial in $s$, rational in $w$, with error $n^{-J-1}$ times a fixed polynomial in $s,w$.

\section{A finite marked profile expansion}\label{bpm:sec:profiles}
We now prove Theorem~\ref{bpm:thm:all}, including the effects of all automorphisms.
For a finite vector $b=(b_2,b_3,\ldots)$ with $h=\sum b_i$, define $E(b)$ on a labeled multiset of cycles of these lengths by
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:eq:E}
 E(b)=\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_h}\prod_{S\in\pi}
       \sum_{k\ge2,\ k\mid\gcd(\ell_i:i\in S)}k^{|S|-1},
 \qquad E(0)=1.
\end{equation}
It is a finite integer. A recurrence chooses the block containing the least labeled selected cycle and applies the same rule to its complement. If $m_i$ counts the cycles of length $i$ of $\sigma$, the exact formula is
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:eq:marked}
 f(\sigma)=\sum_{0\le b_i\le m_i, i\ge2}
 E(b)\prod_i\binom{m_i}{b_i}\,B_{c-h}.
\end{equation}
For $f(\sigma)^2$, take two independent selections $b,b'$. Their binomial factors multiply. This includes every overlap of the two selected sets: they need not be disjoint, and no additional matching factor is present.

\subsection*{Two positive cutoffs}
First restrict to $d=n-c\le D=A_J\log^2(n+1)$; equation~\eqref{bpm:eq:deficittail} controls the discarded part. Let $M$ be moved support. On this range $M\le2D$, and the normalized degree-$h$ sum in~\eqref{bpm:eq:marked} is at most $(\eta M)^h$, where $\eta=C'\log n/n$. Terms with $h+h'>J$ in its square have total at most
\[
 \sum_{j>J}(j+1)(\eta M)^j=O_J((\eta M)^{J+1}).
\]
Summing positive weighted terms gives relative error
$O_J(n^{-J-1}\log^{3J+3}n)$.

Next define the long-cycle defect
\[
 v=\sum_{i\ge3}(i-2)m_i.
\]
Under the probability law with weights $B_{c(\sigma)}^2/(n!Z_n)$, first sample positive $K,L$ with weights proportional to $\rise{KL}{n}/(K!L!)$, then sample an Ewens permutation with parameter $T=KL$. Its factorial moments are exactly
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:eq:Ewens}
 \E\prod_i\fall{m_i}{a_i}
 =\frac{\fall ns\,T^{\sum_i a_i}}
 {\prod_i i^{a_i}\,\rise{T+n-s}{s}},\qquad s=\sum_i ia_i\le n.
\end{equation}
The formula follows by marking the ordered cycles and summing over the remaining permutation.
The region $\min(K,L)<x/2$ is negligible by the weighted-mass tails. Otherwise $T\ge n^2/(4w^2)$. For $u\ge1$, expansion in the nonnegative quantities $u^{i-2}-1$ and~\eqref{bpm:eq:Ewens} give
\[
 \E(u^v\mid T)\le
 \exp\left\{\sum_{j\ge1}\frac{n^{j+2}}{(j+2)T^{j+1}}(u^j-1)\right\}.
\]
Take $u=n/(16w^4)$. For large $n$ it exceeds one, and
\[
 \sum_{j\ge1}\frac{n^{j+2}u^j}{(j+2)T^{j+1}}
 \le4w^2\sum_{j\ge1}(4w^2)^{-j}\le2.
\]
Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:eq:longtail}
 \Pp(v>J\mid T)\le\ee^2(16w^4/n)^{J+1}.
\end{equation}
On the retained deficit range $f^2/B_c^2$ is uniformly bounded. Thus discarding $v>J$ in the unweighted count has relative error $O_J(n^{-J-1}w^{4J+4})$, plus a negligible tail.

\subsection*{The exact retained summand}
Fix the finitely many long-cycle multiplicities $(m_i)_{i\ge3}$ with $v\le J$, and write
\[
 H=\sum_{i\ge3}m_i,\quad t=m_2,\quad d=t+v+H,\quad
 M=2t+v+2H.
\]
Choose $b,b'$ with $h+h'\le J$. Their contribution to $a_n/(B_n^2/n!)$ is
\begin{align}\label{bpm:eq:summand}
 &\frac{\fall nM}{2^t t!\prod_{i\ge3}i^{m_i}m_i!}
 \frac{B_{n-d-h}B_{n-d-h'}}{B_n^2}\,E(b)E(b')\nonumber\\
 &\hspace{12mm}\cdot\binom t{b_2}\binom t{b_2'}
 \prod_{i\ge3}\binom{m_i}{b_i}\binom{m_i}{b_i'}.
\end{align}
After extracting $(w/n)^{2d+h+h'}$ from the Bell factors and $n^M$ from the falling factorial, its explicit scale is
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:eq:scale}
 \frac{\lambda^t}{t!}\,
 n^{-v-h-h'}\frac{w^{2v+2H+h+h'}}{\prod_{i\ge3}i^{m_i}m_i!},
 \qquad\lambda=w^2/2,
\end{equation}
times the finite selection factors in~\eqref{bpm:eq:summand}.

For a requested order, retain $v+h+h'\le J$. The normalized falling factorial expands by
\[
 \log\frac{\fall nM}{n^M}
 =-\sum_{j\ge1}\frac{n^{-j}}j\sum_{a=0}^{M-1}a^j.
\]
Together with Section~\ref{bpm:sec:Bell}, every retained coefficient is polynomial in $t$ and rational in $w$, and every remainder is $n^{-J-1}$ times a fixed polynomial in $t,w$, uniformly for $t\le D$. Profiles inadmissible at a small integer $t$ have zero binomial selection factors; the finitely many small cases may equivalently be separated.

Extend each polynomial sum in $t$ to infinity. Since $D$ is a sufficiently large multiple of $\log^2n$, the polynomial-weighted Poisson tail is $\exp(-c\log^2n)$. Finally,
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:eq:Poisson}
 \ee^{-\lambda}\sum_{t\ge0}\frac{t^k\lambda^t}{t!}=T_k(\lambda).
\end{equation}
This produces $Q_0,\ldots,Q_J$ by finite rational operations. The same positive moments sum all error polynomials. Both cutoffs are already controlled, proving Theorem~\ref{bpm:thm:all}.

\section{The first corrections and exact checks}\label{bpm:sec:checks}
The weighted count has the same algorithm with no selected cycles. Its first correction can also be checked through
\[
 s(n,n-d)=\frac{n^{2d}}{2^d d!}
 \left\{1-\frac{d(2d+1)}{3n}
       +O\!\left(\frac{(d+1)^4}{n^2}\right)\right\},\qquad d\le D.
\]
The all-transposition term contributes $-d(2d-1)/n$; one 3-cycle contributes $4d(d-1)/(3n)$. Profiles of long defect $j\ge2$ are bounded by the geometric series in $8d^2/n$, proving the uniform error. Combining with~\eqref{bpm:eq:Bellshift} and taking Poisson moments gives
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:eq:R1}
 R_1(w)=\frac{w^4(w-2)}{12(w+1)}-\frac{w^2}{2(w+1)^2}.
\end{equation}
At extra order one, the only marked possibility is a single transposition, on either shore. Its weight is one and its selection factor is $t$. The extra Bell shift contributes $w/n$, so the Poisson mean gives $2\lambda w/n=w^3/n$. Thus $Q_1=R_1+w^3$, proving~\eqref{bpm:eq:Q1}.
The displayed fourth-degree errors average to $O((1+w)^{10}/n^2)$; the two cutoff errors at this order are respectively $O(w^6/n^2)$ and $O(w^8/n^2)$.

\section{Explicit inverse models and integer thresholds}\label{bpm:sec:inverse}
Put
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:eq:Phi}
 \Phi(x)=x(w-\log w-1+2/w)+\frac{w^2}2-2
          -\log(w+1)-\frac12\log(2\pi x),\qquad w=W(x).
\end{equation}
Its exact derivative is
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:eq:Phiderivative}
 \Phi'(x)=w-\log w+\frac1x
 \left(\frac{w^2}{w+1}-\frac{w}{(w+1)^2}-\frac12\right)
 \sim\log x.
\end{equation}
In particular the leading model is eventually increasing. If $\nu(y)$ is the eventual solution of $\Phi(\nu)=\log y$, Theorem~\ref{bpm:thm:leading} gives
\[
 \nu(a_n)=n+o(1/\log n).
\]
Nearest-integer rounding therefore recovers the index on the sequence range eventually. Also $a_{n+1}/a_n\sim n/W(n)^2$, so the actual sequence is eventually strictly increasing.

Combining the all-orders Bell expansion and Stirling with~\eqref{bpm:eq:all} gives rational functions $P_j(w)$ such that
\begingroup\renewcommand{\theHequation}{28a}% unique anchor for the tagged display
\begin{equation}\tag{28a}\label{bpm:eq:Pall}% number added 5 October 2026, batch 100
 a_n=\ee^{\Phi(n)}\left\{\sum_{j=0}^J\frac{P_j(W(n))}{n^j}
             +O_J(n^{-J-1}(1+W(n))^{M_J'})\right\},\qquad P_0=1.
\end{equation}\endgroup
Their finite rule multiplies the $Q$ series by the square of the Bell correction series and the reciprocal Stirling series. For example,
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:eq:P1}
 P_1(w)=\frac{w^7+12w^6+33w^5+32w^4-2w^3-19w^2-3w-1}
 {12(w+1)^3}.
\end{equation}
This equals $Q_1+2\beta_1-1/12$.
For fixed $J$ define the explicit smooth tail model
\[
 F_J(x)=\ee^{\Phi(x)}\sum_{j=0}^J P_j(W(x))x^{-j}.
\]
It is positive and strictly increasing eventually, with logarithmic derivative asymptotic to $\log x$. Write $x_J(y)$ for its tail inverse. For the discrete threshold $N(y)=\min\{n:a_n\ge y\}$, there is a constant $C_J$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:eq:ceil}
 \lceil x_J(y)-\varepsilon_J(y)\rceil\le N(y)
 \le\lceil x_J(y)+\varepsilon_J(y)\rceil,\qquad
 \varepsilon_J(y)=\frac{C_J x_J^{-J-1}(1+W(x_J))^{M_J'}}{\log x_J}
\end{equation}
for sufficiently large $y$, enlarging $C_J$ at endpoints if necessary. This follows by the mean-value theorem and monotone bracketing. In particular a single ceiling is valid when both endpoint ceilings agree, and range inversion has vanishing index error.

For a compositional rule, let $X=\nu(y)$ and formally put
$h(X)=\log(\sum_{j\ge0}P_j(W(X))X^{-j})$. Then the compatible inverse expansion is
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:eq:inverserule}
 X+\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{(-1)^k}{k!}
 \left(\frac1{\Phi'(X)}\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dX}\right)^{k-1}
 \frac{h(X)^k}{\Phi'(X)}.
\end{equation}
At every fixed order, only finitely many terms are needed. Taylor's theorem applies to the finite smooth model, and~\eqref{bpm:eq:ceil} transfers the error to thresholds. No exact interpolation of the discrete sequence or unconditional ceiling formula is imposed.

\section{A consequence for recurrence equations}
\begin{proposition}\label{bpm:prop:nonP}
The sequence $a_n$ is not P-recursive, even with complex polynomial
coefficients. Equivalently, its ordinary generating function
is not D-finite.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Theorem~\ref{bpm:thm:leading} gives
\[
 \log\frac{n!}{a_n}=2n\log\log n+O(n).
\]
Thus $E(z)=\sum_{n\ge0}(a_n/n!)z^n$ is entire. If $a_n$ were
P-recursive, substitution of $a_{n+j}=(n+j)![z^{n+j}]E(z)$ in its
polynomial recurrence would make the coefficients of $E$ P-recursive,
so $E$ would satisfy a nonzero linear differential equation with polynomial
coefficients.

Any entire solution of such an equation has a bound
$\max_{|z|=r}|E(z)|\le\exp(Cr^M)$ for some fixed $C,M>0$ and large $r$.
Indeed, outside a circle containing the zeros of the leading polynomial,
the companion first-order system has rational coefficients bounded by a
fixed power of $r$. Integrating along each radial segment and applying
Gronwall gives the stated bound uniformly in the direction; the initial
values on the fixed circle are bounded.
Cauchy's coefficient estimate at $r=(n/(CM))^{1/M}$ would then imply
\[
 \log\frac{n!}{a_n}\ge\frac nM\log n-O(n),
\]
contradicting the displayed coefficient growth. This proves the claim.
\end{proof}
The ODE criterion is classical; this is a direct consequence of the proved
relative asymptotic, with no separate priority assertion.

\section{More than two distinguished shores}\label{bpm:sec:shores}
Let $a_n^{(r)}$ count simultaneous $S_n$ orbits on ordered $r$-tuples
of set partitions of $[n]$, equivalently $r$-partite multihypergraphs
with $n$ hyperedges, distinguished shores and no isolated vertices.
\begin{proposition}\label{bpm:prop:shores}
For every fixed integer $r\ge3$,
\[
 a_n^{(r)}=\frac{B_n^r}{n!}
 \left\{1+O_r\!\left(\frac{\log^r n}{n^{r-2}}\right)\right\}.
\]
More precisely, the first relative excess is
$W(n)^r/(2n^{r-2})(1+o(1))$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Burnside gives $a_n^{(r)}=\sum_\sigma f(\sigma)^r/n!$.
Equations~\eqref{bpm:eq:globalf}, \eqref{bpm:eq:Bellratio} and
\eqref{bpm:eq:Stirlingbound} bound the normalized deficit-$d$ contribution by
\[
 \frac{(2C\log(n+1))^{rd}}{d!\,\fall nd^{r-2}}.
\]
The geometric mean of the largest $d$ factors of $n!$ is at least
$(n!)^{1/n}\ge n/\ee$, so $\fall nd\ge(n/\ee)^d$.
Summing $d\ge1$ gives $\exp(O_r(\log^r n/n^{r-2}))-1$.
For $d=1$, a transposition fixes $B_{n-1}+B_{n-2}$ set partitions,
so its contribution is
$\binom n2[(B_{n-1}+B_{n-2})/B_n]^r\sim W(n)^r/(2n^{r-2})$.
The bound just proved makes the $d\ge2$ contribution of smaller order.
\end{proof}
Thus two shores form an exceptional fixed-shore case: their correction
factor diverges, whereas for every fixed larger number of shores the
identity term is a relative equivalent.

\section{The model and the principal results}\label{bpm:sym:sec:model}
Let $\mathcal G_n$ be the finite set of isomorphism classes of bipartite multigraphs with $n$ edges, no isolated vertices and two distinguished shores. Isomorphisms preserve the shores; transposition is not an additional equivalence. Write $a_n=|\mathcal G_n|$, with $a_0=1$. Equivalently, $a_n$ counts nonnegative integer matrices of total sum $n$, up to separate row and column permutations, after zero rows and columns are removed. This is A007716~\cite{OEIS}.

Let $c^\circ_n$ count its connected members for $n\ge1$ and set $c^\circ_0=0$. Unique decomposition into connected components gives the formal identity
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:sym:eq:Euler}
 \mathcal A(z):=\sum_{n\ge0}a_nz^n=\prod_{m\ge1}(1-z^m)^{-c^\circ_m}.
\end{equation}
Thus $c^\circ_n$ agrees with A007718 at every positive index~\cite{OEIS718}; that OEIS entry separately assigns the conventional value $1$ at index zero. The first positive-index connected counts are
\[
1,3,6,17,40,125,354,1159,3774,13113,\ldots.
\]
All probability statements below use the uniform measure on $\mathcal G_n$, not a vertex-labeled or automorphism-weighted measure.

A \emph{leaf transposition} interchanges two degree-one vertices on the same shore that have a common neighbor and fixes every other vertex. Degree counts edge multiplicity. Let $\Lambda(G)$ be the number of these individual transpositions, and write $w=W(n)$ for the positive Lambert function, $w\ee^w=n$.

\begin{theorem}\label{bpm:sym:thm:sym}
For a uniformly chosen $G\in\mathcal G_n$,
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:sym:eq:sym}
 \Pp(\Aut(G)\ne1)=\frac{2w^3}{n}
       +O\!\left(\frac{(1+w)^7}{n^2}\right).
\end{equation}
Moreover,
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:sym:eq:exceptional}
 \Pp\bigl(\Aut(G)\text{ is neither trivial nor generated by one leaf transposition}\bigr)
 =O\!\left(\frac{(1+w)^6}{n^2}\right).
\end{equation}
Here $\Aut(G)$ always denotes the shore-preserving \emph{vertex} group.
\end{theorem}
In particular, conditional on having a nontrivial vertex automorphism, the group is a single leaf-transposition $C_2$ with probability $1-O((1+w)^3/n)$. The two possible shores occur equally often by transposing the entire graph.

\begin{theorem}\label{bpm:sym:thm:conn}
For each fixed integer $J\ge0$, define integers $\rho_j$ by the formal reciprocal
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:sym:eq:b}
 \sum_{j\ge0}\rho_jz^j=\frac1{\mathcal A(z)},\qquad
 (\rho_0,\rho_1,\rho_2,\rho_3,\rho_4)=(1,-1,-3,-3,-8).
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:sym:eq:shift}
 c^\circ_n=\sum_{j=0}^J \rho_j a_{n-j}+O_J(a_{n-J-1}).
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:sym:eq:disc}
 \Pp(G\text{ disconnected})
 =\frac{w^2}{n}
 +\frac{w^3(3w^3+5w^2+3w+2)}{(w+1)^2n^2}
 +O\!\left(\frac{(1+w)^{\xi_1}}{n^3}\right)
\end{equation}
for some fixed finite $\xi_1$. An isolated-edge component is present with exact probability $a_{n-1}/a_n$, and disconnection without such a component has probability $O(w^4/n^2)$.
\end{theorem}
The reciprocal in~\eqref{bpm:sym:eq:b} is purely formal. Neither it nor the product~\eqref{bpm:sym:eq:Euler} is asserted to converge for nonzero $z$.

\section{The exact leaf transposition mass}
Define
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:sym:eq:Y}
 Y_m=\frac12\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal G_m}\frac{|V(\Gamma)|+2}{|\Aut(\Gamma)|},
\end{equation}
where $|V(\Gamma)|$ counts all vertices. A marked neighbor may be an existing vertex of $\Gamma$ or a new isolated vertex before the two leaves are attached. The factor $1/2$ accounts for the unordered pair of added leaves. Sum over the two shores to obtain~\eqref{bpm:sym:eq:Y}.

There are useful exact alternatives. Introducing row and column vertex-count variables into~\eqref{bpm:eq:mass}, or differentiating the operation that adds isolated vertices, gives
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:sym:eq:Yforms}
 Y_m=\frac{\ee^{-2}}2\sum_{k,l\ge0}
 \frac{(k+l)\binom{kl+m-1}{m}}{k!l!}
 =\frac1{m!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_m}B_{c(\sigma)}B_{c(\sigma)+1}.
\end{equation}
The last equality uses Dobinski's identity and the rising-factorial cycle identity. It also holds at $m=0$, where $Y_0=1$.

\begin{lemma}\label{bpm:sym:lem:markexact}
For $n\ge2$, the marked Burnside term in which exactly one transposition is selected on exactly one shore is
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:sym:eq:markexact}
 Y_{n-2}=\frac1{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_n}
       2m_2(\sigma)B_{c(\sigma)}B_{c(\sigma)-1}
 =\sum_{G\in\mathcal G_n}\frac{\Lambda(G)}{|\Aut(G)|}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For a cycle type $\mu$, put $z_\mu=\prod_i i^{m_i}m_i!$. Deleting the selected 2-cycle gives a type $\nu$ of $n-2$ with $c(\mu)=c(\nu)+1$ and $2m_2(\mu)/z_\mu=1/z_\nu$. Summing proves the first equality from~\eqref{bpm:sym:eq:Yforms}.

To verify the group interpretation, fix an edge-labeled partition-pair representation of $G$. Its stabilizer in $S_n$ maps onto $\Aut(G)$. Its kernel has size
\[
 \Omega_G=\prod_{u,v}m_{uv}!,
\]
because it permutes only parallel edges. Every vertex automorphism lifts, and each fiber has exactly $\Omega_G$ elements. A leaf transposition has one forced 2-cycle on the two leaf edges. Its other edge cycles lie within parallel bundles, so they fix all remaining vertex blocks. In the marked expansion it therefore has exactly the prescribed single selected transposition on one shore. Conversely such a marking makes precisely two singleton vertex blocks exchange on one shore, while their edges lie in a fixed common block on the other shore. It gives one leaf transposition. There are $n!/(\Omega_G|\Aut(G)|)$ partition-pair realizations, and $\Omega_G\Lambda(G)$ marked lifts for each realization. Division by $n!$ yields $\Lambda(G)/|\Aut(G)|$.
\end{proof}
Parallel-edge permutations can be numerous. The kernel cancellation in this proof is essential; they are not the vertex automorphisms in Theorem~\ref{bpm:sym:thm:sym}.

\section{The residual and the symmetry probability}
\begin{lemma}\label{bpm:sym:lem:residual}
With $Y_{n-2}$ as above,
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:sym:eq:residual}
 0\le \mathcal R_n:=a_n-Z_n-Y_{n-2}
 =O\!\left(Z_n\frac{(1+w)^6}{n^2}\right),
 \qquad
 \frac{Y_{n-2}}{Z_n}=\frac{w^3}{n}
 +O\!\left(\frac{(1+w)^7}{n^2}\right).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
All marked terms are nonnegative. Remove the unmarked pair and the one-selected-transposition terms. On $d\le D$, the total contribution having at least two selected cycles across the two shores is, by~\eqref{bpm:sym:eq:markbound},
\[
 \sum_{j\ge2}(j+1)(Cdw/n)^j=O(w^6/n^2)
\]
relative to $B_c^2$. The remaining residual terms select a single cycle of length $i\ge3$. Their relative contribution is at most
\[
 2\eta\sum_{i\ge3}(\tau(i)-1)m_i
 \le2\eta\sum_{i\ge3}i m_i.
\]
On the complement of~\eqref{bpm:sym:eq:goodT}, $T\ge n^2/(4w^2)$; equation~\eqref{bpm:eq:Ewens} gives
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:sym:eq:longmoment}
 \E\left(\sum_{i\ge3}i m_i\,\middle|\,T\right)
 \le\sum_{i\ge3}\frac{n^i}{T^{i-1}}
 =O(w^4/n).
\end{equation}
The bad-$T$ part is negligible, since the sum is at most $n$. Thus one selected long cycle costs $O(w^5/n^2)$. The discarded $d>D$ tail is also negligible. This proves the first estimate.

For the second estimate first retain only permutations consisting of fixed points and $t$ transpositions, with $t\le D$. Put $\lambda=w^2/2$. Their contribution to $Y_{n-2}/(B_n^2/n!)$ is
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:sym:eq:pure}
 \sum_{t\le D}\frac{\fall n{2t}}{2^t t!}
       \frac{2t B_{n-t}B_{n-t-1}}{B_n^2}
 =\sum_{t\le D}\frac{\lambda^t}{t!}\frac{2tw}{n}
       \left\{1+O\!\left(\frac{(t+1)^2}{n}\right)\right\}.
\end{equation}
Indeed~\eqref{bpm:eq:Bellshift} and the Taylor expansion of the falling factorial show that the logarithm of the remaining factor is $O((t+1)^2/n)$ uniformly here. Its higher-order remainders are smaller on $t=O(w^2)$. Extending the Poisson sum past $D$ has superpolynomially small error after $A$ is chosen large. Since $\E t=\lambda$ and $\E[t(t+1)^2]=O((1+\lambda)^3)$, equation~\eqref{bpm:sym:eq:pure} divided by $\ee^\lambda$ is
\[
 \frac{w^3}{n}+O((1+w)^7/n^2).
\]

The terms with a long cycle contribute at most $2\eta\E[m_2\mathbf1_{\{\exists i\ge3:m_i>0\}}]$ relative to $Z_n$, apart from negligible tails. By the mixed factorial moments in~\eqref{bpm:eq:Ewens}, on the good-$T$ region this is bounded using
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:sym:eq:mixed}
 \E\left(m_2\sum_{i\ge3}m_i\,\middle|\,T\right)
 \le\frac{n^2}{2T}\sum_{i\ge3}\frac{n^i}{iT^{i-1}}
 =O(w^6/n).
\end{equation}
Terms whose total selected support exceeds $n$ have zero moment and may simply be included in the upper bound. The bad-$T$ part is negligible since the random variable is at most $n^2$. Thus the extra marked mass is $O((1+w)^7/n^2)$ relative to $Z_n$. Finally use the second line of~\eqref{bpm:sym:eq:inputs}; changing the normalization introduces an error of that same order.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{bpm:sym:thm:sym}]
Put $g=|\Aut(G)|$. The sign of a vertex permutation is a homomorphism to $\{1,-1\}$. If $\Lambda(G)>0$ it is onto, and every counted leaf transposition lies in its odd coset. Therefore $\Lambda(G)\le g/2$; the inequality is trivial if $\Lambda=0$. Consequently
\[
 \varrho(G):=\frac{g-1-\Lambda(G)}g\ge0.
\]
It vanishes for the identity group and for a group generated by one leaf transposition. For $g\ge3$, $\varrho(G)\ge1/2-1/g\ge1/6$. For $g=2,\Lambda=0$, it is $1/2$. These cases exhaust all other groups. Lemma~\ref{bpm:sym:lem:markexact} implies $\mathcal R_n=\sum_G \varrho(G)$, so the number of exceptional graphs is at most $6\mathcal R_n$.

For every nonexceptional graph,
\[
 \mathbf1_{\{\Aut(G)\ne1\}}=\frac{2\Lambda(G)}{|\Aut(G)|}.
\]
The two sides differ by at most one on an exceptional graph, since $2\Lambda/g\le1$. Lemma~\ref{bpm:sym:lem:residual} now gives
\[
 \Pp(\Aut(G)\ne1)=\frac{2Y_{n-2}}{a_n}
       +O((1+w)^6/n^2).
\]
Also $a_n/Z_n=1+Y_{n-2}/Z_n+\mathcal R_n/Z_n=1+O((1+w)^3/n)$. Substituting the second estimate in~\eqref{bpm:sym:eq:residual} proves~\eqref{bpm:sym:eq:sym} and the first proves~\eqref{bpm:sym:eq:exceptional}.
\end{proof}

\begin{writenote}
the identity $\mathcal R_n=\sum_G\varrho(G)$ is the combination of
$a_n=\sum_G1$, \eqref{bpm:eq:Z} and Lemma~\ref{bpm:sym:lem:markexact}:
$\sum_G(1-1/g-\Lambda(G)/g)=a_n-Z_n-Y_{n-2}$. Together with
Lemma~\ref{bpm:sym:lem:residual} it shows that Part~I's correction
$Q_1=R_1+w^3$ splits as the weighted count's $R_1$ plus exactly the
leaf-transposition mass, the residual entering only at order $n^{-2}$.
\end{writenote}

\section{Endpoint convolution and the giant component}\label{bpm:sym:sec:convolution}
The argument for connectivity needs more than $a_{n-1}/a_n\to0$. In particular one must control graphs whose components are all small.

\begin{lemma}\label{bpm:sym:lem:conv}
For each fixed positive integer $q$,
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:sym:eq:conv}
 \sum_{q\le k\le n/2}a_k a_{n-k}=O_q(a_{n-q}).
\end{equation}
There is a constant $\gamma>0$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:sym:eq:central}
 \sum_{n/4\le k\le n/2}a_k a_{n-k}=O(a_n\ee^{-\gamma n}).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Direct differentiation of the explicit $\Phi$ gives
\begin{align}\label{bpm:sym:eq:phider}
 \Phi'(x)&=\log x-2\log W(x)
 +\frac1x\left\{\frac{W(x)^2}{1+W(x)}
 -\frac{W(x)}{(1+W(x))^2}-\frac12\right\},\nonumber\\
 \Phi''(x)&\sim 1/x.
\end{align}
Choose a fixed integer $n_0$ beyond the onset of $\Phi''>0$ and beyond a two-sided constant comparison $a_k\asymp\ee^{\Phi(k)}$. No convexity of the actual sequence, or fixed numerical onset such as $4$, is assumed.

Set $\Psi_n(t)=\Phi(t)+\Phi(n-t)$. For $n_0\le t\le n/4$,
\[
 \Psi_n'(t)\le\Phi'(n/4)-\Phi'(3n/4)\longrightarrow-\log3.
\]
Thus the exponential terms on this interval decrease at least geometrically and their sum is bounded by a constant times their left endpoint. On $n/4\le t\le n/2$, $\Psi_n$ is decreasing. Moreover,
\[
 \Phi(n/4)+\Phi(3n/4)-\Phi(n)
 =-\Ent(1/4)n+o(n),
\]
where $\Ent(p)=-p\log p-(1-p)\log(1-p)>0$. This follows immediately from
$\Phi(x)=x\log x-2x\log\log x-x+o(x)$ at the fixed scaled arguments. The polynomial number of central terms is absorbed by a smaller positive $\gamma$, proving~\eqref{bpm:sym:eq:central}.

If $q\ge n_0$, the first interval starts at $q$ and is $O_q(a_{n-q})$. If $q<n_0$, keep the finitely many $k=q,\ldots,n_0-1$ separately and start the geometric argument at $n_0$. The fixed-shift ratios from~\eqref{bpm:eq:Pall} give
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:sym:eq:ratios}
 \frac{a_{n-j}}{a_n}\sim\left(\frac{w^2}{n}\right)^j
 \quad\text{for every fixed }j\ge0,
\end{equation}
so these finite contributions and the endpoint at $n_0$ are $O_q(a_{n-q})$. The central part is smaller still. This proves~\eqref{bpm:sym:eq:conv}.
\end{proof}

\begin{writenote}
since $\log x=W(x)+\log W(x)$, the first line of~\eqref{bpm:sym:eq:phider}
is Part~I's~\eqref{bpm:eq:Phiderivative}; the asymptotic $\Phi''(x)\sim1/x$
is new here.
\end{writenote}

Call a component with more than $n/2$ edges a \emph{giant}; it is unique if present. Let $e_n$ count graphs with no such component. A graph counted by $e_n$ has a union of components with total edge count in $[n/4,n/2]$. Indeed choose a component already in that interval; if there is none, all components have fewer than $n/4$ edges, and greedily accumulating them until first reaching $n/4$ produces a union smaller than $n/2$. Hence
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:sym:eq:nogiant}
 e_n\le\sum_{n/4\le k\le n/2}a_k a_{n-k}
       =O(a_n\ee^{-\gamma n}).
\end{equation}
The bound counts possible pairs of unions and may overcount graphs, which is harmless. For all other graphs the giant and the remaining graph determine one another uniquely, giving the exact identity
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:sym:eq:giant}
 a_n=\sum_{0\le j<n/2}a_j c^\circ_{n-j}+e_n.
\end{equation}

\begin{proof}[Proof of the finite-shift part of Theorem~\ref{bpm:sym:thm:conn}]
Since $0\le c^\circ_k\le a_k$, truncate~\eqref{bpm:sym:eq:giant} at fixed $J$. Equations~\eqref{bpm:sym:eq:conv} and~\eqref{bpm:sym:eq:nogiant} give
\[
 a_n=\sum_{j=0}^J a_j c^\circ_{n-j}+O_J(a_{n-J-1}).
\]
Apply this relation at $n-i$ to order $J-i$, multiply by $\rho_i$, and sum over $0\le i\le J$. The coefficient of $c^\circ_{n-k}$ is $\sum_{i=0}^k \rho_i a_{k-i}=\mathbf1_{\{k=0\}}$. Every error has size $O_J(a_{n-J-1})$. This proves~\eqref{bpm:sym:eq:shift}.
\end{proof}

\section{Disconnection and finite fragments}
Removal of one isolated-edge component is a bijection from graphs that have at least one such component to $\mathcal G_{n-1}$. Components of this type are indistinguishable, so no multiplicity factor appears. For a disconnected graph without an isolated-edge component, its smallest component has edge count between $2$ and $n/2$. Selecting it and its complement, and overcounting if necessary, gives a bound by~\eqref{bpm:sym:eq:conv} with $q=2$. Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:sym:eq:isolatededge}
 \Pp(G\text{ disconnected})=\frac{a_{n-1}}{a_n}
     +O\!\left(\frac{a_{n-2}}{a_n}\right),\qquad
 \Pp(\text{disconnected, no isolated edge})=O(w^4/n^2).
\end{equation}
Conditional on disconnection, the graph consists of a connected $(n-1)$-edge graph and a single isolated edge with probability $1-O(w^2/n)$. One can see this either from~\eqref{bpm:sym:eq:giant} at fragment size one or from $c^\circ_{n-1}/a_{n-1}=1-O(w^2/n)$.

For the second correction, write
\[
 \alpha_1(w)=\frac{w^2}{w+1}-\frac{w}{(w+1)^2}-\frac12.
\]
Taylor expansion of the finite smooth model in~\eqref{bpm:eq:Pall}, rather than differentiation of its error, yields
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:sym:eq:ratio2}
 \frac{a_{n-1}}{a_n}=\frac{w^2}{n}
 \left\{1+\frac{\delta(w)}n+O\!\left(\frac{(1+w)^{\xi_2}}{n^2}\right)\right\},
 \qquad \delta(w)=\frac{w(2-w^2)}{(w+1)^2}.
\end{equation}
Indeed the correction in the logarithmic ratio is
$-\alpha_1(w)+(w-1)/(2(w+1))=\delta(w)$, by~\eqref{bpm:sym:eq:phider}. The factor $1+P_1(w)/n+\cdots$ changes this ratio only at relative order $n^{-2}$ times a fixed polynomial in $w$. Taking $J=2$ in~\eqref{bpm:sym:eq:shift}, using $a_{n-2}/a_n=w^4/n^2+O(n^{-3}(1+w)^{\xi_3})$, and combining $w^2\delta(w)+3w^4$ proves~\eqref{bpm:sym:eq:disc}.

More generally, fix an isomorphism class $\Gamma\in\mathcal G_j$. For $n>2j$, the event that the complement of the giant is exactly $\Gamma$ has the exact probability
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:sym:eq:fragment}
 \Pp(\text{fragment}=\Gamma)=\frac{c^\circ_{n-j}}{a_n}
 \sim\left(\frac{w^2}{n}\right)^j.
\end{equation}
There are $a_j$ possible fragments of size $j$, each with the same probability. Conditional on that size, their distribution is exactly uniform. Equation~\eqref{bpm:sym:eq:shift} and the finite-shift expansion of~\eqref{bpm:eq:Pall} supply their complete fixed-order probability expansions.

\section{All orders and inverse growth for A007718}
\begin{theorem}\label{bpm:sym:thm:connectedall}
There are rational functions $P^\circ_j(w)$ with $P^\circ_0=1$ such that for each fixed $J\ge0$ and some finite $M^\circ_J$,
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:sym:eq:connectedall}
 c^\circ_n=\ee^{\Phi(n)}\left\{\sum_{j=0}^J\frac{P^\circ_j(w)}{n^j}
 +O_J\!\left(\frac{(1+w)^{M^\circ_J}}{n^{J+1}}\right)\right\}.
\end{equation}
A finite rule computes them from the coefficients $P_j$ in~\eqref{bpm:eq:Pall} and the integers $\rho_j$ in~\eqref{bpm:sym:eq:b}. In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:sym:eq:C1}
 P^\circ_1(w)=P_1(w)-w^2.
\end{equation}
Thus
\[
 c^\circ_n\sim\frac{B_n^2}{n!}\ee^{w^2/2}
 \sim\frac{\exp\{n(w-\log w-1+2/w)+w^2/2-2\}}
 {(w+1)\sqrt{2\pi n}}.
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Insert~\eqref{bpm:eq:Pall} at each of the finitely many shifts in~\eqref{bpm:sym:eq:shift}. Taylor-expand the explicit smooth functions at $n$. Derivatives use $W'(x)=W(x)/(x(1+W(x)))$, so all resulting coefficients are rational functions of $w$. The leading shift factor is $(w^2/n)^j$, which means that only shifts $j\le J$ are relevant through order $J$. The omitted convolution term is
\[
 O_J(a_{n-J-1})=\ee^{\Phi(n)}O_J(n^{-J-1}(1+w)^{2J+2}).
\]
All other errors are the same power of $n^{-1}$ times a fixed polynomial bound in $w$. No derivative of an asymptotic remainder is taken. The sole extra term at order one is $\rho_1w^2=-w^2$, proving~\eqref{bpm:sym:eq:C1}.
\end{proof}
Explicitly, put $F_J(x)=\ee^{\Phi(x)}\sum_{k=0}^J P_k(W(x))x^{-k}$ and expand
$\sum_{j=0}^J \rho_jF_J(x-j)/\ee^{\Phi(x)}$ through $x^{-J}$. This is the promised finite rule. For example the next coefficient obeys
\[
 P^\circ_2=P_2-w^2P_1-\frac{w^3(3w^3+5w^2+3w+2)}{(w+1)^2}.
\]

For inversion define the explicit connected model
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:sym:eq:model}
 F^\circ_J(x)=\ee^{\Phi(x)}\sum_{j=0}^J P^\circ_j(W(x))x^{-j}.
\end{equation}
It is positive and strictly increasing eventually, and
$(\log F^\circ_J)'(x)\sim\log x$. Let $x^\circ_J(y)$ be its tail inverse. The connected sequence itself is eventually increasing because~\eqref{bpm:sym:eq:connectedall} gives $c^\circ_{n+1}/c^\circ_n\sim n/W(n)^2\to\infty$.

\begin{corollary}\label{bpm:sym:cor:inverse}
For the integer threshold $N^\circ(y)=\min\{n\ge1:c^\circ_n\ge y\}$ there is a constant $\widetilde C_J>0$ such that, for all sufficiently large $y$,
\begin{equation}\label{bpm:sym:eq:bracket}
 \left\lceil x^\circ_J(y)-\varepsilon^\circ_J(y)\right\rceil
 \le N^\circ(y)\le
 \left\lceil x^\circ_J(y)+\varepsilon^\circ_J(y)\right\rceil,
 \quad
 \varepsilon^\circ_J(y)=\frac{\widetilde C_J x^\circ_J(y)^{-J-1}(1+W(x^\circ_J(y)))^{M^\circ_J}}
 {\log x^\circ_J(y)}.
\end{equation}
Also $x^\circ_J(c^\circ_n)=n+O_J(n^{-J-1}(1+w)^{M^\circ_J}/\log n)$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The relative error in~\eqref{bpm:sym:eq:connectedall} has the same size as the corresponding logarithmic error. Apply the mean-value theorem to $\log F^\circ_J$, whose derivative is asymptotic to $\log x$, and enlarge $\widetilde C_J$ to cover the two neighboring integer endpoints. Monotonicity then brackets the first index reaching $y$. The finitely many pre-tail values cannot affect the threshold for sufficiently large $y$.
\end{proof}
An unconditional single-ceiling formula would not follow near integer crossings. Equation~\eqref{bpm:sym:eq:bracket} supplies the needed uncertainty interval; if its endpoint ceilings coincide, that common integer is the exact threshold. On the sequence range, nearest-integer rounding of $x^\circ_J(c^\circ_n)$ eventually recovers $n$.

One may also invert compositionally. Let $X$ solve $\Phi(X)=\log y$, and let $h^\circ(X)$ be the formal logarithm of $\sum_{j\ge0}P^\circ_j(W(X))X^{-j}$. The compatible formal inverse rule is
\[
 X+\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{(-1)^k}{k!}
 \left(\frac1{\Phi'(X)}\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dX}\right)^{k-1}
 \frac{h^\circ(X)^k}{\Phi'(X)}.
\]
At each requested algebraic order only finitely many terms are used; Taylor expansion applies to the finite model~\eqref{bpm:sym:eq:model}, and~\eqref{bpm:sym:eq:bracket} controls the actual integer threshold.

\begin{writenote}
Corollary~\ref{bpm:sym:cor:inverse} and the inverse rule are Part~I's
\eqref{bpm:eq:ceil} and~\eqref{bpm:eq:inverserule} with $P_j$ replaced by
$P^\circ_j$; the corollary below is Part~I's
Proposition~\ref{bpm:prop:nonP} applied to $c^\circ_n$, by the same
argument. Both are printed as manuscript~02 gives them.
\end{writenote}

\begin{corollary}\label{bpm:sym:cor:nonP}
The positive-index sequence A007718 is not P-recursive, even over complex polynomial coefficients. Its ordinary generating function is not D-finite.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The leading equivalent implies
$\log(n!/c^\circ_n)=2n\log\log n+O(n)$, so $E^\circ(z)=\sum_{n\ge1}(c^\circ_n/n!)z^n$ is entire. P-recursiveness of $c^\circ_n$ would imply a polynomial-coefficient linear ODE for $E^\circ$. Every entire solution of such an ODE satisfies $\max_{|z|=r}|E^\circ(z)|\le\exp(Cr^M)$ for some constants $C,M$: outside a circle containing the zeros of the leading coefficient, write the companion first-order system and apply Gronwall along radial segments, since its rational matrix is bounded by a power of $r$. Cauchy's bound with $r=(n/(CM))^{1/M}$ would force $\log(n!/c^\circ_n)\ge(n/M)\log n-O(n)$, a contradiction.
\end{proof}

\section{Exact checks and source scope}\label{bpm:sym:sec:scope}
The direct checker constructs all pairs of set partitions through $n=6$, converts them to integer matrices, and canonicalizes by row and column permutations without merging the shores. It enumerates 44,169 partition pairs and 438 graph isomorphism classes in total, independently computing vertex automorphism orders, leaf transpositions, components and isolated edges. A second checker examines 226,342 edge permutations to verify every parallel-edge-kernel fiber, the leaf-lift counts, the unique-giant identity and every fragment-size count. A cycle-type checker verifies the marked mass, reciprocal coefficients and Euler transform on 2,714 cycle types through $n=20$, agreeing with the independent graph census at small sizes. Together the checkers test~\eqref{bpm:eq:Z}, \eqref{bpm:sym:eq:markexact}, the residual gap, the isolated-edge bijection, and the connected values. These are exact finite-identity and transcription checks, not proofs of asymptotic inequalities or effective onset.

The component method is classical in scope: Wright~\cite{Wright} and Bell, Bender, Cameron and Richmond~\cite{BBCR} analyze connectedness through counting sequences and component generating functions. Here we prove the needed endpoint and no-giant estimates directly for the present growth scale and retain the finite-shift remainder at every fixed order. The 2025 study by Cameron, del Valle and Roney-Dougal~\cite{Regular} considers regular bipartite multigraphs with fixed shore cardinality and increasing common degree. Its parameter regime and automorphism convention differ from this uniform variable-vertex-count ensemble. It is not a substitute for the kernel cancellation above.

This is an unrefereed mathematical companion to Part~I, with reproducible exact checks, not a Lean formalization. The preceding all-orders estimates are included as an explicit dependency. The literature search is bounded, and no exhaustive or first-in-literature priority assertion is made. No claim of effective constants, numerical asymptotic onset, or classification of all exponentially small corrections is intended.
