\begin{abstract}
A placement of $hw$ kings on a $2h\times2w$ board with no two attacking is a maximum-density placement: each $2\times2$ block holds exactly one king. This report prints three manuscripts on counting these placements. Part~I treats the cylinder with $2h$ open rows and $2w$ cyclic columns, $A(h,w)$: a uniform marked-star approximation with relative error $\Oh(h^{-1})$ when $w/h$ lies in a compact positive interval; a change of regime at $w/h=\log2$, with the free energy, the subcritical equivalent, every fixed order above the transition with explicit first and second corrections, and a three-term critical-window expansion; three limit laws for the geometry of a uniform placement; Lambert-$W$ inverse brackets along rational rays; and, for fixed height, squarefree denominators, the degree bound $\lfloor h/2\rfloor+1$ for every irreducible factor, attained at the heights $p-3$. Part~II treats the square cylinder $a_n=A(n,n)$, OEIS A137432: every fixed order $a_n=Cn^n(\sum_jc_jn^{-j})$ with $c_j\in\Q(e)$ (Part~I's supercritical theorem at $\lambda=1$), the exact $c_3$, a boundary law with rate $\Oh(n^{-1})$, and the ratio expansion $a_{n+1}/(na_n)=e(1+1/(2n)+\cdots)$, which proves the conjecture recorded in A137432 by Kot\v e\v sovec in 2011 that this ratio tends to $e$. Part~III treats the ordinary $2h\times2w$ board (A061593 at $h=2$, A061594 at $h=3$) with Part~I's Gram machinery: every irreducible factor of the reduced fixed-height denominator has degree $d\le\lfloor h/2\rfloor+1$ and exponent at most $h-2d+3$, so for even $h$ every top-degree factor is simple; an exponential-polynomial formula with degree bounds; Wilf's dominant term $(\alpha_hw+\beta_h)(h+1)^w$; and a Lambert-$W$ threshold bracket with an exponentially small real error. Parts~I and~III prove two of the assertions recorded as conjectures on p.~83 of Kot\v e\v sovec's book (Conjectures~1 and~4) and the upper half of a third (Conjecture~2, on both boards), without first-proof claims. The write adds a proof of the two-sided bound $2(h+1)^w\le A(h,w)\le2^h(h+1)^w$ with the cases of equality (the strict form of the upper bound for the square cylinder, as Part~II quotes it from the book, fails exactly at $n=1$), a proof that the dominant planar factor $1-(h+1)z$ has exponent exactly two at every height, and checks of the shared coefficients, the OEIS data and the inverses against the repository's transseries volume. The report is unrefereed, and nothing in it is formalized.
\end{abstract}

\emph{One model.} All three Parts encode a placement by a binary word $b\in\{0,1\}^h$ (the column offsets of the kings, constant along each block row) and, in each block column, a threshold $c\in\{0,\dots,h\}$ (the row offsets are $0$ above it and $1$ below). Two successive thresholds are compatible according to a zero-one matrix $M_b$ of size $h+1$, and $M_b=E_0E_1$ factors through the incidence matrix $C_b$ of the monochromatic components of the path coloured by $b$; the bipartite graph of $C_b$ is a caterpillar tree whose hubs are the runs of $b$. On the cylinder the count is a trace, $A(h,w)=\sum_b\tr(M_b^w)=\sum_b\tr((C_bC_b^{\mathsf T})^w)$ (Part~I, \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:trace} and \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:gram}; Part~II, \eqref{mdk:sq:eq:transfer} and \eqref{mdk:sq:eq:gram}). On the plane the transfer matrix is block upper triangular in the Boolean order of the words, with the same $M_b$ as diagonal blocks, and the count is a boundary pairing $\one^{\mathsf T}T^{w-1}\one$ (Part~III, \eqref{mdk:pl:eq:count}). Parts~I and~II derive the asymptotics from one long run of $b$ and the Schur-complement equation for the Perron eigenvalue of the caterpillar; Part~III uses the smaller Gram matrix to bound the algebraic degree of the poles and the Boolean rank to bound their order.

\section*{The A137432 conjecture and the leading constant}\phantomsection\label{mdk:sec:oeisconj}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{The A137432 conjecture and the leading constant}
The write read the entry A137432 \cite{oeisA137432} on 6~October 2026 (internal format and revision history). Its name is ``Number of ways to place n\textasciicircum2 nonattacking kings on a 2n X 2n cylindrical chessboard.'', offset~0 with $a(0)=1$ (prepended by Andrew Howroyd, 26~March 2023), and its author line reads ``Vaclav Kotesovec, Aug 31 2011''. Two formula lines concern this report:
\begin{itemize}
\item ``Conjecture: limit of a(n+1)/(n*a(n)) as n->infinity is e.'' The line is unsigned; the revision history shows it in revision~\#19, the entry's first version under its present name, made by Vaclav Kotesovec on 31~August 2011. \textbf{Proved} by Part~II, Corollary~\ref{mdk:sq:cor:inverse}: the object of the conjecture is literally Part~II's $a_n$, and \eqref{mdk:sq:eq:ratio} gives $a_{n+1}/(na_n)=e(1+1/(2n)+\Oh(n^{-2}))$. Only the order $M=0$ of Theorem~\ref{mdk:sq:thm:all} is needed for the limit: $a_{n+1}/(na_n)=(1+1/n)^{n+1}(1+\Oh(n^{-1}))\to e$. Part~I's Theorem~\ref{mdk:asp:thm:phases} at $\lambda_h=1$ gives the same leading equivalent by its own proof. Part~II says that its ratio expansion includes ``the previously recorded ratio-limit conjecture''.
\item ``a(n) \textasciitilde{} c * n\textasciicircum n, where c = 2*exp(1)*(exp(1) - 1)\textasciicircum2 / (exp(1) - 2)\textasciicircum2 = 31.1116835720\allowbreak 4905036826\allowbreak 4392279105\allowbreak 2352237386\allowbreak 275089... - Vaclav Kotesovec, Jul 29 2023, updated Mar 18 2024''. The entry gives no proof. Parts~I and~II prove it, with every fixed order, and both credit the constant to Kot\v e\v sovec (Part~II, \eqref{mdk:sq:eq:C}, prints 28 decimals, the entry 46; the write's 70-digit evaluation agrees with all of them).
\end{itemize}
Nothing was submitted to the OEIS.

\emph{The bounds of pp.~209--210.} Part~II, Section~\ref{mdk:sq:sec:sources}, reads there the lower bound $2(n+1)^n\le a_n$ and a strict upper bound $a_n<2^n(n+1)^n$, and notes that the strict bound ``needs the restriction $n\ge2$, since $a_1=4$ is equality''. Whether the book states the restriction elsewhere could not be checked.

\section*{The inverses and the transseries volume}\phantomsection\label{mdk:sec:inverses}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{The inverses and the transseries volume}
The repository's transseries volume \cite{TSvol} has a Part~0 apparatus for inverting rapidly growing functions. Its symbols are subscripted ``vol'' here where they meet this report's letters ($a_\vol$, $b_\vol$, $\kappa_\vol$, $d_\vol$, $v_\vol$, $X_\vol$, $L_\vol$). Statement by statement:
\begin{itemize}
\item Part~II, the leading inverse $N=L/W_0(L)$, $N\log N=L$ \eqref{mdk:sq:eq:Lambert}, with $L=\log(x/C)>0$: an \emph{instance} of the factorial core \file{p0:prop:factorial-core} with $\kappa_\vol=1$, $d_\vol=0$, $L_\vol=L$. The proposition gives $v_\vol=W_0(L)$ and $X_\vol=e^{v_\vol}=L/W_0(L)=N$, the unique solution with $\log N>0$.
\item Part~I, the leading inverse $N=(Y/p)/W_0(e^dY/p)$ of $pN\log N+bN=Y$ \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:Lambert}, with $d=b/p$: an \emph{instance} of the same proposition with $\kappa_\vol=p$, $d_\vol=b$ (Part~I's $b$; Part~I's $d$ is $d_\vol/\kappa_\vol$), $L_\vol=Y>0$. Then $v_\vol=W_0(e^dY/p)$ and $X_\vol=e^{v_\vol-d}=Y/(pv_\vol)=N$, using $v_\vol e^{v_\vol}=e^dY/p$; the side condition $p\log N+b>0$ holds for large $Y$. Part~II's inverse is the ray $p=q=1$ of this one ($b=0$, $\beta=0$, $D=C(1)$).
\item Part~III, the inverse $w_0(y)$ of $(\alpha x+\beta)n^x$ \eqref{mdk:pl:eq:W}: an \emph{instance}, after a change of variable, of the Lambert core \file{p0:thm:lambert-core}. With $X=x+\beta/\alpha$ and $b=\log n$ the equation $(\alpha x+\beta)n^x=y$ is $\log X+bX=\log(y/\alpha)+b\beta/\alpha$ for $X>0$, the core with $a_\vol=b=\log(h+1)$, $b_\vol=1>0$ and $L_\vol=\log(y/\alpha)+b\beta/\alpha$; its branch rule gives the unique positive solution $X=b^{-1}W_0(be^{L_\vol})$, which is \eqref{mdk:pl:eq:W} ($\alpha=\alpha_h\ge1>0$ by Theorem~\ref{mdk:pl:thm:leading}).
\item The one-step corrections: Part~I's \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:rootapprox} (perturbation $\beta\log t$) and \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:t1} (perturbation $\alpha_1/t$), Part~II's \eqref{mdk:sq:eq:first-inverse} (perturbation $c_1/t$) and Part~III's \eqref{mdk:pl:eq:firstcorrection} (perturbation $R$): each displayed correction is exactly the first-order term $X-\varrho(X)/\Phi_0'(X)$ of the perturbation-operator series \file{p0:eq:operator-series} (\file{p0:prop:operator-form}) about the corresponding core (for Part~I, $\Phi_0'(N)=p(\log N+1+d)$; for Part~II, $\Phi_0'(N)=1+\log N=D_N$). \emph{Analogues}, not instances of a theorem of the volume: the proposition is a formal series, and the error bounds are the Parts' own. The same holds for Part~II's fixed-order recursion for $t_M$ (Section~\ref{mdk:sq:sec:inverse}).
\item Part~III's threshold statements in Theorem~\ref{mdk:pl:thm:inverse}: an \emph{instance} of \file{p0:thm:staircase}. For $n_1$ larger than $\deg H_h$ and beyond the point where $f_h$ increases, the real extension $f_h$ restricted to $[n_1,\infty)$ is continuous, strictly increasing and equal to $P_h(w)$ at the integers, that is an admissible interpolation in the sense of \file{p0:def:three-inverses}, with inverse $x_h$; Part~III's identity $N_h(y)=\lceil x_h(y)\rceil$ is item~(1) of the theorem (for $y\ge P_h(n_1)$, the range of the restricted interpolation, where $N_h$ is the staircase started at $n_1$ because $P_h$ increases; \textup{corrected after the independent check of 7~October 2026, the range first read ``for $y$ above $P_h(n_1-1)$'', on part of which the restricted inverse is not defined}), \eqref{mdk:pl:eq:ceilbracket} follows from it by monotonicity of the ceiling, and the agreement case of the bracket is item~(2) with $x_\ast=w_0(y)$, $\eta=\varepsilon_h(y)$; \eqref{mdk:pl:eq:nosub} is item~(1) with the interpolation $g$.
\item Part~I's Theorem~\ref{mdk:asp:thm:inverse} and Part~II's bracket \eqref{mdk:sq:eq:bracket}: proved directly at the integers from the comparison function $g$ (resp.\ $g_M$) and the error $K_0/n$ (resp.\ $D_Mn^{-M-1}$), without an interpolation of the sequence; \emph{not} instances of \file{p0:thm:staircase}. When the two ceilings agree they determine the threshold, the same conclusion as item~(2) of that theorem, and the Parts' warnings that a single ceiling is not justified near a threshold describe its items~(2) and~(5).
\end{itemize}
None of the Parts claims novelty for the inversion mechanics; Part~III calls its inversion ``a standard inversion, not a priority claim''.

\section{Model and the phase diagram}\label{mdk:asp:sec:main}
The squares have coordinates $(r,s)$ with $0\le r<2h$ and $s\in\mathbb Z/(2w\mathbb Z)$. Two distinct kings attack when their row difference and circular column distance are both at most one. Rows do not wrap. We count placements on this labeled board, without dividing by rotations, reflections, or translations. Throughout $h,w$ are positive integers. A partition into $hw$ disjoint $2\times2$ blocks gives an upper bound of $hw$ kings; occupying every other row and column attains it. Thus $A(h,w)$ is exactly a maximum-density count.

The square diagonal $A(n,n)$ is \href{https://oeis.org/A137432}{OEIS A137432}. Fixed-height rectangular families already appear in Kot\v{e}\v{s}ovec's book, notably Section 2.6, pp.~169--196 \cite{kotesovec}, and in OEIS entries such as A194644, A194647, and A195656 \cite{oeisRect}. The orientation convention is important: $h$ always refers here to the open direction and $w$ to the wrapped direction. In general $A(h,w)\ne A(w,h)$.

Set
\[
L=\log2,\qquad \lambda_h=\frac wh,
\]
and define the explicitly evaluable marked-star sum
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:S}
S(h,w)=2(h+1)^w+\sum_{m=1}^{h-1}(h-m+3)2^{h-m-1}(m+1)^w.
\end{equation}
The terminology records a marked run in an exact binary encoding; $S$ itself is not an exact count of king placements.

\begin{theorem}[Uniform marked-star approximation]\label{mdk:asp:thm:star}
For every fixed $0<a\le b<\infty$, uniformly over integers $w$ with $a\le w/h\le b$,
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:uniform}
A(h,w)=S(h,w)\bigl(1+\Oh_{a,b}(h^{-1})\bigr).
\end{equation}
No assumption that the longest run exceeds $h/2$ is required.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Free energy and the two fixed regimes]\label{mdk:asp:thm:phases}
If $\lambda_h\to\lambda>0$, then
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:phi}
\lim_{h\to\infty}\frac1h\log\frac{A(h,w)}{h^w}=\Phi(\lambda),\qquad
\Phi(\lambda)=
\begin{cases}
L-\lambda+\lambda\log(\lambda/L),&0<\lambda<L,\\
0,&\lambda\ge L.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The maximizing run fraction is $s(\lambda)=\min(\lambda/L,1)$. The function $\Phi$ is continuously differentiable; its second derivative has left limit $1/L$ and right limit zero at $L$.

Uniformly on each compact subset of $0<\lambda_h<L$,
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:sub}
A(h,w)=h^{w+3/2}B(\lambda_h)e^{h\Phi(\lambda_h)}\bigl(1+\Oh(h^{-1})\bigr),\qquad
B(\lambda)=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi\lambda}}{L}\left(1-\frac\lambda L\right).
\end{equation}
Uniformly on each compact subset of $\lambda_h>L$, for every fixed integer $M\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:super}
A(h,w)=h^w C(\lambda_h)
\left(\sum_{j=0}^M\frac{c_j(\lambda_h)}{h^j}+\Oh_M(h^{-M-1})\right),
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:C}
F(q)=\frac{1-q}{1-2q},\qquad
C(\lambda)=2e^\lambda F(e^{-\lambda})^2,\qquad
c_0=1,\quad c_j(\lambda)\in\Q(\lambda,e^{-\lambda}).
\end{equation}
The notation for the coefficient field means that each $c_j$ is a rational function of the displayed variables, evaluated on the supercritical interval.
\end{theorem}
Section~\ref{mdk:asp:sec:coeff} gives $c_1,c_2$ explicitly and a finite algorithm for every fixed order. The quantifier ``fixed $M$'' does not assert convergence of the formal infinite series, uniformity for growing $M$, or a complete expansion in exponentially small sectors.

\begin{theorem}[Critical crossover through constant order]\label{mdk:asp:thm:critical}
Let
\[
t_h=\frac{w-Lh}{\sqrt h},\qquad I_j(t)=\int_0^\infty x^j e^{-tx-Lx^2/2}\,dx.
\]
For every fixed $T<\infty$, uniformly for $|t_h|\le T$,
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:critical}
\frac{A(h,w)}{h^w}=hG(t_h)+\sqrt h H(t_h)+J(t_h)+\Oh_T(h^{-1/2}).
\end{equation}
Writing $f_t(x)=e^{-tx-Lx^2/2}$, set
\begin{align}
a_t(x)&=t+Lx-\tfrac t2x^2-\tfrac L3x^3,\label{mdk:asp:eq:a}\\
b_t(x)&=L(-\tfrac12+x^2-\tfrac14x^4)+t(x-\tfrac13x^3).\label{mdk:asp:eq:b}
\end{align}
Then
\begin{align}
G(t)&=I_1(t),\label{mdk:asp:eq:G}\\
H(t)&=\int_0^\infty[3+xa_t(x)]f_t(x)\,dx
      =4I_0(t)-I_2(t)-\tfrac t6I_3(t),\label{mdk:asp:eq:H}\\
J(t)&=\frac{29}{12}+\int_0^\infty
 \left\{3a_t(x)+x\left[b_t(x)+\frac{a_t(x)^2}{2}\right]\right\}f_t(x)\,dx
 +4LG(t).\label{mdk:asp:eq:J}
\end{align}
The $29/12$ is an endpoint correction. The $4LG(t)$ is the first nontrivial spectral correction, which is absent from the marked-star sum.
\end{theorem}
All refined formulas use the exact parameters $\lambda_h$ or $t_h$, not a rounded nominal parameter. Section~\ref{mdk:asp:sec:floors} gives the explicit floor corrections. In particular,
\[
A(h,\lfloor Lh\rfloor)\sim\frac{h^{\lfloor Lh\rfloor+1}}{L}.
\]

\section{The exact geometric encoding}\label{mdk:asp:sec:encoding}
Index the $2\times2$ blocks by $(i,j)$, with $0\le i<h$ and $j$ modulo $w$. The unique king in block $(i,j)$ has position
\[
(2i+x_{ij},\,2j+y_{ij}),\qquad x_{ij},y_{ij}\in\{0,1\}.
\]
If $y_{ij}=1$ and $y_{i,j+1}=0$, the kings in these neighboring blocks attack: their column distance is one and their row distance is at most one. Hence the cyclic sequence $y_{ij}$ cannot descend, so it is constant in $j$:
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:word}
y_{ij}=b_i,\qquad b=(b_0,\ldots,b_{h-1})\in\{0,1\}^h.
\end{equation}
For $w=1$ constancy is automatic and involves no comparison of a king with itself. Similarly $x_{ij}=1,x_{i+1,j}=0$ would cause a vertical attack. Thus, for each $j$, the row offsets are nondecreasing in $i$, and there is a unique threshold $c_j\in\{0,\ldots,h\}$ with
\[
x_{ij}=0\quad(i<c_j),\qquad x_{ij}=1\quad(i\ge c_j).
\]
For two successive block-columns with thresholds $u=c_j$ and $v=c_{j+1}$, a down-right diagonal attack occurs exactly when
\[
u\le i<v-1,\qquad (b_i,b_{i+1})=(1,0).
\]
If $u<v$, excluding these attacks says that $b_u,\ldots,b_{v-1}$ is nondecreasing. The opposite diagonal direction similarly says, when $u>v$, that $b_v,\ldots,b_{u-1}$ is nonincreasing. If $u=v$, there is no extra condition. These conditions exclude every possible attack; nonneighboring blocks cannot contain attacking squares. When $w=2$, the two directions between block-columns meet the same pair, and both ordered conditions are imposed by the cyclic product below. When $w=1$, its single diagonal condition is automatic and the vertical rule already excludes the relevant attacks.

Define an $(h+1)\times(h+1)$ zero-one matrix $M_b$ by these allowed transitions. The list of thresholds is cyclic and labeled. Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:trace}
A(h,w)=\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^h}\tr(M_b^w),\qquad w\ge1.
\end{equation}
There is no factor $w$ and no division by $w$. The identity is not an empty-width convention: at $w=0$ its right side is $2^h(h+1)$.

\section{From a transfer matrix to a caterpillar}\label{mdk:asp:sec:tree}
Take the path with vertices $0,\ldots,h$ and edge colors $b_0,\ldots,b_{h-1}$. Let $E_c[u,v]$ be one if the entire interval from $u$ to $v$ has color $c$, including the empty interval. Then
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:factor}
M_b=E_0E_1.
\end{equation}
Indeed an intermediate vertex must be in the same color-0 component as $u$ and the same color-1 component as $v$. There is at most one such vertex, since two would require a nonempty interval of both colors. For $u<v$ it exists precisely when the interval is $0^*1^*$; for $u>v$, the increasing-order interval must be $1^*0^*$. An intermediate vertex outside the interval would again require a nonempty segment of both colors. This proves the factorization entry by entry.

Let $R_0,R_1$ be the indicator matrices of the color-0 and color-1 path components. We have $E_c=R_cR_c^{\mathsf T}$. Put
\[
C_b=R_0^{\mathsf T}R_1.
\]
Distinct components of different colors intersect in at most one path vertex, so $C_b$ is a zero-one matrix. Its bipartite incidence graph $T_b$ is connected: successive original path vertices give edges sharing the component of their connecting color. It has $h+1$ edges, one for each path vertex. If there are $n_0$ zero edges and $n_1$ one edges, its number of vertices is $(n_1+1)+(n_0+1)=h+2$. Thus it is a tree.

By cyclicity of trace (or the equality of nonzero spectra of rectangular products), for every positive $w$,
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:gram}
\tr(M_b^w)=\tr((C_bC_b^{\mathsf T})^w)
 =\frac12\tr(\mathcal A_b^{2w}),\qquad
\mathcal A_b=\begin{pmatrix}0&C_b\\C_b^{\mathsf T}&0\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
In particular the eigenvalues contributing to the trace are nonnegative squared adjacency eigenvalues. The zero eigenvalues are irrelevant at positive powers.

If $b$ has successive run lengths $r_1,\ldots,r_s$, $T_b$ is a caterpillar. For $s\ge2$ its spine consists of $s$ vertices, with pendant-leaf counts
\[
r_1,\ r_2-1,\ldots,r_{s-1}-1,\ r_s.
\]
For $s=1$ it is the star with $h+1$ leaves. This follows by contracting the alternating monochromatic components along the original path; each internal run loses one endpoint to each neighboring spine edge. In every case a run of length $m$ gives a hub of degree $m+1$. Deleting that hub removes $m+1$ of the tree's $h+1$ edges; hence its remaining branches contain $h-m$ edges in total.

\subsection{Bounds and the rooted Schur equation}
Write $\Lambda_b=\|\mathcal A_b\|^2$. If the longest run has length $m$, separate the caterpillar adjacency into pendant-star edges and spine-path edges. These have norms at most $\sqrt{m+1}$ and $2$, respectively. Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:norm}
\Lambda_b\le(\sqrt{m+1}+2)^2.
\end{equation}
Now mark a run of length $m$ and delete its hub. Apart from isolated pendant leaves, there are at most two rooted branches, with adjacency matrices $Q_\ell,Q_r$. Let $\beta$ bound their squared norms and let
\[
B_j=\langle e_\ell,Q_\ell^{2j}e_\ell\rangle+
     \langle e_r,Q_r^{2j}e_r\rangle,\qquad j\ge1,
\]
with absent branches contributing zero. The spectral theorem gives
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:moments}
0\le B_j\le B_1\beta^{j-1}.
\end{equation}
If $\Lambda=\Lambda_b>\beta$, the resolvent expansion in the Schur complement at $\sqrt\Lambda$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:schur}
\Lambda=m+1+\sum_{j\ge1}B_j\Lambda^{-j},\qquad
0\le\Lambda-(m+1)\le\frac{B_1}{\Lambda-\beta}.
\end{equation}
To see the constant term, each neighbor of the deleted hub contributes $1/\sqrt\Lambda$ to the first resolvent term; there are $m+1$ such neighbors. Odd rooted moments vanish because each branch is bipartite. Multiplication by $\sqrt\Lambda$ yields the displayed equation. The lower bound $\Lambda\ge m+1$ follows from the hub star.

If $k=h-m$, then also $\beta\le k$: the squared adjacency norm of any bipartite graph is the squared operator norm of its incidence matrix, at most its squared Frobenius norm, namely its number of edges. If the first two outward run lengths on each branch are $s_\ell,t_\ell$ and $s_r,t_r$, missing lengths being zero, rooted walk counts give
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:B12}
B_1=s_\ell+s_r,\qquad B_2=s_\ell^2+t_\ell+s_r^2+t_r.
\end{equation}
Interlacing after deleting the hub bounds every adjacency eigenvalue other than the Perron pair by $\sqrt\beta$ in absolute value. Thus
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:nonperron}
0\le\tr(M_b^w)-\Lambda_b^w\le(h+1)\beta^w.
\end{equation}
These estimates keep the non-Perron trace contribution separate from the Perron expansion.

\section{Marked runs and the uniform approximation}\label{mdk:asp:sec:uniform}
Mark a particular run of length $m$ in a word of length $h$. Set $k=h-m$ and let $\ell,r$ be the total lengths on its left and right, so $\ell+r=k$. Each boundary is encoded by its outward run composition. The inner bit is forced opposite to the central bit. Let
\[
\alpha_0=1,\qquad \alpha_j=2^{j-1}\ (j\ge1),\qquad
\sum_{j\ge0}\alpha_jq^j=F(q)=\frac{1-q}{1-2q}.
\]
There are two choices of the central bit. Therefore the exact number of marked words with this run length is
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:marked}
R_h(h)=2,\qquad R_h(h-k)=2\sum_{\ell=0}^k\alpha_\ell\alpha_{k-\ell}
=(k+3)2^{k-1}\quad(1\le k<h).
\end{equation}
This counts marked runs of exact length $m$, not just longest runs. A word with several eligible runs appears several times. The distinction will be dealt with before a one-hub approximation is used.

\begin{lemma}[Bounded average branch moment]\label{mdk:asp:lem:mean}
For the marked words of defect $k$, with $B_1=s_\ell+s_r$,
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:mean}
\mathbb E_k B_1=\frac{4k}{k+3}\quad(k\ge1),\qquad \mathbb E_0B_1=0.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The weighted series for the first run length on one outward composition is
$F(q)\sum_{s\ge1}s q^s=F(q)q/(1-q)^2$.
For the two boundaries, before the central-bit factor, the total series is
\[
2F(q)^2\frac q{(1-q)^2}=\frac{2q}{(1-2q)^2}.
\]
Its coefficient at $q^k$ is $k2^k$. The count coefficient is
$[q^k]F(q)^2=(k+3)2^{k-2}$. The central-bit factor cancels in their ratio.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{mdk:asp:thm:star}]
Fix $a\le w/h\le b$. All constants below may depend on $a,b$. Choose a small fixed $\delta>0$ with $L+a\log\delta<0$, allowing strict slack in this inequality.

\emph{Small longest runs.} For words whose longest run satisfies $m<\delta h$, the norm bound \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:norm}, the $2^h$ choices of word, and the at most $h+1$ nonzero trace terms give
\[
\sum_{\max\mathrm{run}<\delta h}\tr(M_b^w)\le h^w e^{-ch}
\]
for sufficiently large $h$. Strict slack absorbs the $\Oh(\sqrt h)$ norm correction and all polynomial factors. The portion of the marked-star sum with $m<\delta h$ has the same estimate: there are at most $h2^h$ marked runs and their weights are $(m+1)^w$. The baseline $S(h,w)\ge2(h+1)^w\ge2h^w$ makes these errors exponentially small relative to $S$.

\emph{Two macroscopic runs.} Choose $0<\epsilon<\delta/16$. For a marked run of length $m\ge\delta h$, states having a second run of length at least $\epsilon h$ are at most
\[
\operatorname{poly}(h)\,2^{h-m-\epsilon h}
\]
in number. Indeed specify the two disjoint run intervals and their bits, then allow all other positions freely. There are polynomially many choices of interval endpoints. This upper bound compared with $R_h(m)\ge2^{h-m-1}$ is exponentially small. If there is no space for the second run the count is zero.

For the actual king contribution, mark a longest run $m$. Uniformly for $m\ge\delta h$, \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:norm} yields
\[
\frac{\Lambda_b^w}{(m+1)^w}\le e^{C\sqrt h}.
\]
The trace dimension and possible choices of longest run contribute only polynomial factors. The entropy penalty $2^{-\epsilon h}$ dominates these factors and $e^{C\sqrt h}$. Summing over $m$ bounds all such bad words by $S e^{-c_\epsilon h}$; the same argument removes bad marked states from $S$. This explicitly includes ties and multiple large hubs.

\emph{One macroscopic run.} Every remaining word has a unique run $m\ge\delta h$, with all other runs shorter than $\epsilon h$. It corresponds to exactly one good marked state. On deleting its hub, \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:norm} applied to the remaining caterpillar branches gives
\[
\beta\le(\sqrt{\epsilon h+1}+2)^2\le2\epsilon h
\]
for large $h$. Since $\Lambda\ge m+1\ge\delta h$, the Schur bound gives
\[
0\le\Lambda-(m+1)\le C\frac{B_1}{h}.
\]
Moreover $B_1\le2\epsilon h$. Hence
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:relativeperturb}
0\le\frac{\Lambda^w}{(m+1)^w}-1\le C\frac{B_1}{h}.
\end{equation}
For precision, the logarithm of the ratio is bounded by $CB_1/h$, a uniformly bounded number, and $e^x-1\le C'x$ on such a bounded interval. The non-Perron bound \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:nonperron} is exponentially small relative to $(m+1)^w$ because $\beta/(m+1)\le2\epsilon/\delta<1$ and $w\ge ah$.

For each $m$, the sum of $B_1$ over good marked states is at most its sum over all marked states, which is less than $4R_h(m)$ by Lemma~\ref{mdk:asp:lem:mean}. Summing \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:relativeperturb} therefore produces an error $\Oh(S/h)$. Each good word contributes at least its star weight, so the same decomposition gives both inequalities in \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:uniform}. There is no remaining multiplicity or trace-dimension loss.
\end{proof}

\section{Evaluation below the transition}\label{mdk:asp:sec:sub}
The exponential rate of a marked-run summand with $s=m/h$ is
\[
f_\lambda(s)=(1-s)L+\lambda\log s,\qquad0<s\le1.
\]
Its maximum is attained at $s=\lambda/L$ if $\lambda<L$, and at $s=1$ otherwise. Polynomial multiplicities do not change the logarithm divided by $h$. Finite-sum upper bounds and a term near the maximizing integer give \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:phi}, using Theorem~\ref{mdk:asp:thm:star}. The same observation also explains why the transition occurs at $L$.

For a sharp subcritical equivalent, substitute $j=m+1$ in \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:S}:
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:Sj}
S(h,w)=2(h+1)^w+2^h\sum_{j=2}^h(h-j+4)j^w e^{-Lj}.
\end{equation}
When $w/h$ lies in a fixed compact subinterval of $(0,L)$, the saddle $j=w/L$ is a linear distance below $h$. Extending separately the positive sums of $j^w e^{-Lj}$ and $j^{w+1}e^{-Lj}$ to infinity changes each by an exponentially small relative error. Their resulting difference remains of order $h$ times the first sum, since $1-w/(Lh)$ stays bounded away from zero. Thus cancellation does not invalidate this operation. The $j=1$ and star-endpoint terms are also exponentially negligible.

For any integer $p\ge1$, the exact pole series is
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:polesum}
\sum_{j\ge1}j^p e^{-Lj}
=p!\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb Z}(L+2\pi i\ell)^{-p-1}
=\frac{\Gamma(p+1)}{L^{p+1}}\bigl(1+\Oh(\rho^p)\bigr)
\end{equation}
for a fixed $\rho<1$. One derivation differentiates the partial-fraction expansion of $(e^z-1)^{-1}$ $p$ times at $z=L$; after differentiation the pole series is absolutely convergent, including $p=1$. Alternatively it follows by Poisson summation applied to $x^pe^{-Lx}$ on the positive half-line. The nonzero poles are uniformly farther from $L$ than the pole at zero; summing their absolute values gives the displayed geometric estimate.

Applying \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:polesum} with $p=w,w+1$ yields
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:subgamma}
A(h,w)=\frac{2^h\Gamma(w+1)}{L^{w+1}}
\left(h+4-\frac{w+1}{L}\right)\bigl(1+\Oh(h^{-1})\bigr).
\end{equation}
For $S$ the error here is exponentially small; for $A$ the relative $\Oh(h^{-1})$ comes from the spectral approximation. Uniform Stirling expansion gives \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:sub}. The constants $4$ and $1/L$ inside the parentheses do not by themselves give the full first correction to $A$: spectral structure also contributes at that order.

\section{All fixed orders above the transition}\label{mdk:asp:sec:super}
Assume $a\le\lambda_h\le b$ with $a>L$. We give the uniform argument, not just a formal expansion at each fixed boundary.

\subsection{Localization and summable tails}
For every fixed small $\epsilon>0$, words whose longest run is at most $(1-\epsilon)h$ contribute $\Oh(h^w e^{-c_\epsilon h})$. For $m/h$ bounded away from zero this follows from \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:norm}, the bound $2h2^{h-m}$ on words with a marked longest run, and
\[
(1-m/h)L+\lambda_h\log(m/h)<0.
\]
The inequality is uniform away from $m/h=1$ because $\lambda_h\ge a>L$. For sufficiently small $m/h$, counting all $2^h$ words gives a strictly negative rate as in Section~\ref{mdk:asp:sec:uniform}.

For $m=h-k$ with $k\le\epsilon h<h/2$, the long run is unique. Put $d=1-k$, $D=h+d$. By \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:schur} and $B_1\le k$, $\beta\le k$,
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:tailperturb}
0\le\Lambda-D\le\frac{k}{h-2k+1}.
\end{equation}
The inequality $\log x\le x-1$ consequently gives
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:tail}
(\Lambda/h)^w\le C e^{-ak}\qquad(0\le k\le\epsilon h).
\end{equation}
The constant is uniform in $h,k,\lambda_h\in[a,b]$. Multiplicity is $\Oh((k+1)2^k)$, so this is summable with ratio at most $2e^{-a}<1$. Separately, by \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:nonperron}, the normalized non-Perron sum is bounded by a polynomial factor times
\[
\exp\{h[\epsilon L+a\log\epsilon]\},
\]
which is exponentially small for sufficiently small $\epsilon$. After that separate bound, no trace dimension factor is carried into the Perron coefficient expansion.

For any desired fixed algebraic order, truncate at $k\le A_M\log h$, where $A_M$ is sufficiently large. Equation~\eqref{mdk:asp:eq:tail} makes the omitted tail smaller than the requested power of $h^{-1}$.

\subsection{Uniform analytic remainder}
Put $u=1/h$ and $y=\Lambda/h$. The Schur equation becomes
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:implicit}
y=1+du+\sum_{j\ge1}B_j u^{j+1}y^{-j}.
\end{equation}
The bound $B_j\le k^j$ shows, by geometric-series estimates, that on a complex disk $|u|\le c/(k+1)$ the right side is a contraction in $|y-1|\le1/4$, for a fixed sufficiently small $c>0$. It maps the disk into itself and has derivative of absolute value less than one there. Its fixed point is analytic and agrees with the positive Perron solution on small positive $u$. In particular
\[
y=1+du+\Oh(ku^2),\qquad \frac{\log y}{u}-d=\Oh((k+1)^2|u|),
\]
where the apparent singularity at $u=0$ is removable; for $k=0$ the first remainder is exactly zero.

On the smaller disk $|u|\le c'/(k+1)^2$, the analytic function
\[
\mathcal H_\lambda(u)=\exp\left\{\lambda\left(\frac{\log y}{u}-d\right)\right\}
\]
is uniformly bounded for $\lambda\in[a,b]$. Cauchy estimates imply that its order-$j$ coefficient is $\Oh_j((k+1)^{2j})$, and that
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:cauchy}
\mathcal H_\lambda(1/h)=\sum_{j=0}^M P_j(\lambda;\ell,r)h^{-j}
 +\Oh_M((k+1)^{2M+2}h^{-M-1})
\end{equation}
provided $1/h\le c'/(2(k+1)^2)$. This holds uniformly for $k\le A_M\log h$ once $h$ is large. The coefficient notation includes the full outward compositions, not just their lengths. Multiplying by $e^{\lambda d}$ and summing the remainder against the marked multiplicities costs only a fixed moment of the geometric tail $(2e^{-a})^k$. Thus the total error is $\Oh_M(h^{-M-1})$ without a logarithmic loss. Coefficient tails can also be extended to all finite boundary compositions. This proves \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:super} with the leading sum
\[
2e^\lambda\sum_{\ell,r\ge0}\alpha_\ell\alpha_r e^{-\lambda(\ell+r)}
=2e^\lambda F(e^{-\lambda})^2.
\]

\subsection{A finite coefficient procedure}\label{mdk:asp:sec:coeff}
For a rooted branch with outward run lengths $s_1,\ldots,s_v$, define formal series recursively by
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:resolvent}
R_{v+1}(z)=1,\qquad
R_i(z)=\frac1{1-z(s_i-1+R_{i+1}(z))}.
\end{equation}
Eliminating pendant leaves in the rooted caterpillar proves this diagonal-resolvent identity. Padding missing run lengths by zero preserves $R=1$. The coefficient of $z^j$ involves only the first $j$ runs, with the right value when a branch terminates earlier. The branch sum satisfies
\[
B_j=[z^j](R_{\ell,1}(z)+R_{r,1}(z)-2).
\]
Formal substitution in \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:implicit}, followed by logarithm and exponentiation, computes each $P_j$ as a finite rational polynomial in $k$, $\lambda$, and finitely many outward run lengths.

Write $D_q=q\,d/dq$ and
\[
h_j(q)=\sum_{s\ge1}s^jq^s\quad(j\ge0),\qquad h_0=\frac q{1-q},\quad
h_1=\frac q{(1-q)^2},\quad h_2=\frac{q(1+q)}{(1-q)^3}.
\]
For a monomial in the first $v$ run lengths with last exponent $a_v>0$, its weighted sum over finite compositions is
\[
F(q)\prod_{i=1}^v h_{a_i}(q).
\]
The constant monomial has sum $F(q)$. Products combine independent left and right boundaries; powers of their total size $k$ are implemented by $D_q$. Since $h_{j+1}=D_qh_j$, all these sums are rational functions of $q$. This proves the claimed rationality at every fixed order, and specifies a finite coefficient algorithm rather than relying on interpolation.

For the first two corrections let $S_1=B_1$ and $T_1=B_2$. Expansion gives
\begin{align}
\Lambda&=h+d+S_1h^{-1}+(T_1-dS_1)h^{-2}+\Oh_k(h^{-3}),\label{mdk:asp:eq:lambdaexpand}\\
\frac{\Lambda^w}{h^w}
&=e^{\lambda_h d}\left[1+\frac{\lambda_h(S_1-d^2/2)}h\right.\notag\\[-0.3em]
&\hspace{4em}\left.+\frac{\lambda_h(T_1-2dS_1+d^3/3)
 +(\lambda_h^2/2)(S_1-d^2/2)^2}{h^2}+\Oh_k(h^{-3})\right].\label{mdk:asp:eq:perronexpand}
\end{align}
The fixed-$k$ notation in this display is made uniform upon summation by \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:cauchy}.

For a compact operator form put $\Delta=1-D_q$ and
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:weights}
\begin{split}
W&=F^2,\qquad W_S=2F^2h_1,\\
W_{SS}&=2F^2h_2+2F^2h_1^2,\qquad
W_T=2F^2(h_2+h_0h_1).
\end{split}
\end{equation}
These are the weighted sums of $1,S_1,S_1^2,T_1$ for two boundaries. Holding $\lambda$ fixed under differentiation and evaluating at $q=e^{-\lambda}$,
\begin{align}
c_1(\lambda)&=\frac{\lambda}{W}\left(W_S-\frac12\Delta^2W\right),\label{mdk:asp:eq:c1op}\\
c_2(\lambda)&=\frac1W\left\{\lambda\left(W_T-2\Delta W_S+\frac13\Delta^3W\right)
+\frac{\lambda^2}{2}\left(W_{SS}-\Delta^2W_S+\frac14\Delta^4W\right)\right\}.\label{mdk:asp:eq:c2op}
\end{align}
In explicit rational form,
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:c1}
c_1(\lambda)=-\frac{\lambda(4q^4-40q^3+45q^2-12q+1)}{2(1-q)^2(1-2q)^2},
\qquad q=e^{-\lambda},
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:c2}
c_2(\lambda)=\frac{\lambda P_6(q)}{3(1-q)^3(1-2q)^3}
+\frac{\lambda^2 P_8(q)}{8(1-q)^4(1-2q)^4},
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align*}
P_6(q)&=8q^6-380q^5+690q^4-401q^3+69q^2-5q+1,\\
P_8(q)&=16q^8-976q^7+2920q^6-2940q^5+977q^4+14q^3+2q^2-6q+1.
\end{align*}
At $\lambda=1$ these specialize to the square corrections
\[
c_1(1)=-13.572052159046386\ldots,\qquad
c_2(1)=576.331755295050712\ldots.
\]
The large size of these coefficients warns against assuming that a few terms give accurate finite-height numerics without a remainder estimate.

\section{The critical expansion}\label{mdk:asp:sec:criticalproof}
Fix $|t|\le T$ and write $w=Lh+t\sqrt h$, using only pairs for which $w$ is integral. For intermediate algebra the real exponent is also meaningful for the star sum, but it is not a king count unless $w$ is integral. In this section put
\[
\varepsilon_h=h^{-1/2},\qquad x=k\varepsilon_h,\qquad D=h+1-k.
\]

\subsection{Localization and spectral perturbation}
Choose a small fixed $\epsilon>0$, for example $1/4$. Words with no run longer than $(1-\epsilon)h$ are exponentially negligible after division by $h^w$. For $m/h=r$ away from zero their upper rate is
\[
(1-r)L+(w/h)\log r=L(1-r+\log r)+o(1),
\]
strictly negative on each compact subinterval bounded away from $r=1$. For $m\le h/4$, counting all words and using \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:norm} gives the negative upper rate $L+L\log(1/4)$. This covers the remaining endpoint. The $o(1)$ is uniform for bounded $t$.

Near the long run, $k\le\epsilon h$, non-Perron eigenvalues give an exponentially small aggregate contribution by \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:nonperron}: its rate is at most $\epsilon L+(w/h)\log\epsilon<0$. We may thus focus on the Perron eigenvalue of the unique long run. Here the stronger moment bound is
\[
0\le B_j\le B_1 k^{j-1}.
\]
Writing $S_1=B_1\le k$, the Schur equation gives
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:criticaldelta}
\Lambda-D=\frac{S_1}{h}+\Oh\left(\frac{S_1(k+1)}{h^2}\right).
\end{equation}
Indeed isolate $B_1/\Lambda$, bound the remaining sum by
$S_1k/[\Lambda(\Lambda-k)]$, and compare $1/\Lambda$ to $1/h$ using \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:tailperturb}. It follows that
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:criticalpower}
(\Lambda/D)^w=1+\frac{w}{h}\frac{S_1}{h}
+\Oh\left(\frac{S_1(k+1)}{h^2}\right).
\end{equation}
The exponent is uniformly bounded; its squared-error contribution is absorbed using $S_1^2\le kS_1$. A uniform second-moment assumption is unnecessary.

Let $N_0=2$ and $N_k=(k+3)2^{k-1}$ for $k\ge1$, and define the truncated base sum
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:Bbase}
\mathcal B_h=2(1+1/h)^w+\sum_{1\le k\le\epsilon h}N_k(D/h)^w.
\end{equation}
The choice of rounding at the upper limit is exponentially immaterial. Averaging \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:criticalpower} with \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:mean} gives
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:averagecritical}
\frac{A(h,w)}{h^w}=\mathcal B_h+
\frac{w/h}{h}\sum_{1\le k\le\epsilon h}N_k(D/h)^w\frac{4k}{k+3}
+\Oh_T(h^{-1/2}).
\end{equation}
For completeness, the error is bounded by
\[
\frac{C}{h^2}\sum_{k\ge1}(k+1)^2
\exp\left(\frac{Tk}{\sqrt h}-c\frac{k^2}{h}\right)=\Oh_T(h^{-1/2}),
\]
using the Gaussian estimate established next. The correction sum in \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:averagecritical} equals
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:4LG}
4LG(t)+\Oh_T(h^{-1/2}).
\end{equation}
The leading Riemann sum has this value because the exact average $4k/(k+3)$ cancels the multiplicity factor $k+3$. This is the full spectral contribution at constant order.

\subsection{A summably uniform Taylor expansion}
The inequality $\log(1-u)\le-u-u^2/2$, with $u=(k-1)/h$, yields
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:gaussian}
2^k(D/h)^w\le C_T e^{Tx-cx^2},\qquad1\le k\le\epsilon h.
\end{equation}
The replacement of $D$ by $\Lambda$ adds only a bounded factor by \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:criticaldelta}. Therefore the region $x>R_h=\sqrt{B\log h}$ has a tail smaller than any specified inverse power of $h$ if the fixed $B$ is sufficiently large. All needed polynomial moments are dominated uniformly for $|t|\le T$.

For $x\le R_h$, Taylor's theorem gives
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:logcritical}
kL+w\log(1+(1-k)/h)
=L-tx-\frac L2x^2+\varepsilon_h a_t(x)+\varepsilon_h^2b_t(x)
+\Oh_T(\varepsilon_h^3(1+x^5)).
\end{equation}
The $a_t,b_t$ are exactly \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:a}--\eqref{mdk:asp:eq:b}. Since $\varepsilon_h(1+R_h^3)\to0$, exponentiation yields
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:expcritical}
2^k(D/h)^w=2f_t(x)
\left[1+\varepsilon_ha_t(x)+\varepsilon_h^2\left(b_t(x)+\frac12a_t(x)^2\right)
+\Oh_T(\varepsilon_h^3(1+x^9))\right].
\end{equation}
The integrated error after multiplication by $k+3$ and summation is $\Oh_T(\varepsilon_h)$ without logarithmic loss: use the displayed polynomial times the Gaussian majorant, whose moments are uniformly finite, and choose the discarded tails stronger than the desired remainder.

To make the pointwise bookkeeping explicit, put $v_t=b_t+a_t^2/2$ and
\[
g_0=xf_t,\qquad g_1=(3+xa_t)f_t,\qquad g_2=(3a_t+xv_t)f_t.
\]
For $k\ge1$ the summand is
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:pointwise}
N_k(D/h)^w=\varepsilon_h^{-1}g_0(x)+g_1(x)+\varepsilon_hg_2(x)
+3\varepsilon_h^2v_t(x)f_t(x)
+\Oh_T(\varepsilon_h^3(k+3)(1+x^9)f_t(x)).
\end{equation}
The explicit fourth term has total $\Oh_T(\varepsilon_h)$ after summation. It cannot be hidden inside the smaller displayed pointwise remainder; it is absorbed only after summing.

\subsection{Endpoint terms and the constant coefficient}
Euler--Maclaurin with uniform Gaussian derivative bounds gives, for the functions needed here,
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:EM}
\sum_{k\ge1}g(k\varepsilon_h)
=\varepsilon_h^{-1}\int_0^\infty g(x)\,dx
-\frac{g(0)}2-\frac{\varepsilon_hg'(0)}{12}+\Oh_T(\varepsilon_h^3),
\end{equation}
or its lower-order version where sufficient. Here
\[
g_0(0)=0,\qquad g_0'(0)=1,\qquad g_1(0)=3.
\]
The exceptional $k=0$ term is $2(1+1/h)^w=4+\Oh_T(\varepsilon_h)$. Applying \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:EM} to \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:pointwise} therefore gives
\[
\mathcal B_h=hG(t)+\sqrt hH(t)
+\left(4-\frac1{12}-\frac32+\int_0^\infty g_2(x)\,dx\right)
+\Oh_T(h^{-1/2}).
\]
The endpoint combination is $4-1/12-3/2=29/12$. Adding \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:4LG} proves Theorem~\ref{mdk:asp:thm:critical}. Extending the formula $(k+3)2^{k-1}$ to $k=0$ would give a wrong constant.

\subsection{Evaluation and matching of the coefficient functions}
Completing the square gives
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:I0}
I_0(t)=\sqrt{\frac\pi{2L}}e^{t^2/(2L)}\erfc\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2L}}\right),
\qquad G(t)=\frac{1-tI_0(t)}{L}.
\end{equation}
Integration by parts gives
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:Irec}
LI_1+tI_0=1,\qquad LI_{j+1}+tI_j=jI_{j-1}\quad(j\ge1).
\end{equation}
Thus $G,H,J$ are explicit polynomial combinations of $t,L,I_0(t)$. The recurrence also reduces the integral expression for $H$ to \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:H}. At the center,
\begin{align}
G(0)&=\frac1L=1.4426950408889634\ldots,\notag\\
H(0)&=\left(4-\frac1L\right)\sqrt{\frac\pi{2L}}=3.8497251900678098\ldots,\label{mdk:asp:eq:center}\\
J(0)&=\frac{119}{12}-\frac8{3L}+\frac2{3L^2}=7.457059211633169\ldots.\notag
\end{align}
These are coefficient values, not an assertion that an integer pair has $w=Lh$.
\begin{writenote}[Independent check of the write, 7 October 2026]
The three closed forms agree with the integral definitions \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:G}--\eqref{mdk:asp:eq:J} at $t=0$ (80-digit quadrature). The printed $G(0)$ and $J(0)$ are truncations; the printed $H(0)$ is rounded in its last digit: $H(0)=3.849725190067809793\ldots$, so its truncation to the printed length is $3.8497251900678097\ldots$. The delivered digits are kept.
\end{writenote}

For $t\to+\infty$, rescaling $x=y/t$ in $G$ and expanding the Gaussian under its Laplace integral gives
\[
G(t)=t^{-2}-3Lt^{-4}+15L^2t^{-6}+\Oh(t^{-8}).
\]
For $t\to-\infty$, completing the square and localizing at $x=-t/L$ gives
\[
G(t)\sim\frac{-t}{L}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{L}}e^{t^2/(2L)}.
\]
The first matches $C(L+\delta)\sim\delta^{-2}$; the second matches the formal nearcritical limit of \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:sub}. These are matching statements about coefficient functions and neighboring fixed-regime formulas. They do not extend the proven compact-$t$ remainder to an arbitrary growing window.

\section{Three limiting geometries}\label{mdk:asp:sec:laws}
Choose a maximum-density placement uniformly. Its associated word has weight $\tr(M_b^w)$. Write $M_h$ for the longest run and $K=h-M_h$ for the defect. On the high-probability event of a unique macroscopic run, record its central bit and its two outward compositions. Let $\ell,r$ be their lengths, so $\ell+r=K$. Set both boundary lengths and their fractions to zero on the exceptional event, and set $\ell/K=0$ when $K=0$; these conventions have no effect on the critical or subcritical limits.

The proof of Theorem~\ref{mdk:asp:thm:star} gives more than a scalar estimate. On the good marked-state set, the sum of absolute differences between the true state weights and the star weights is $\Oh(S/h)$; the omitted mass is exponentially small. After normalization, the two probability measures differ in total variation by $\Oh(h^{-1})$. One may adjoin a single exceptional state to make the comparison a statement on a common space. The central bit is exactly fair and independent of the run-composition data, by word complementation, which preserves the tree spectrum.

\begin{theorem}[Supercritical finite defect]\label{mdk:asp:thm:law-super}
If $\lambda_h\to\lambda>L$, set $q=e^{-\lambda}$. The left and right outward run compositions converge jointly to independent finite compositions, a specified composition of total length $j$ having probability $q^j/F(q)$. Their central bit is independent and fair. The limiting defect satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:defectpgf}
\mathbb E[z^{K_\infty}]=\frac{F(qz)^2}{F(q)^2},\qquad |z|\le1.
\end{equation}
Uniformly on compact supercritical intervals, comparison to the law with the exact $q_h=e^{-\lambda_h}$ has total variation error $\Oh(h^{-1})$, and every fixed nonnegative integer defect moment has error $\Oh(h^{-1})$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The leading state weight is $2e^{\lambda_h}q_h^{\ell+r}$ before separating the two equally likely bits. Section~\ref{mdk:asp:sec:super} bounds the first-order error by a summable polynomial in $K$ times $(2e^{-a})^K$. The same remains true after multiplication by any fixed power of $K$. Normalizing gives the stated total variation and moment bounds, and summing the two independent size series gives \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:defectpgf}.
\end{proof}
Equivalently, on each side the number of runs is geometric on $\{0,1,\ldots\}$ with ratio $q/(1-q)$; conditional on that number, run lengths are independent with probabilities $(1-q)q^{s-1}$, $s\ge1$. Literal boundary bits are constrained by the central bit; independence concerns outward compositions or boundaries encoded relative to it.

\begin{theorem}[Critical square-root defect]\label{mdk:asp:thm:law-critical}
If $t_h\to t\in\R$, then $K/\sqrt h$ converges in distribution, and in every fixed nonnegative integer moment, to $X_t$ with density
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:density}
p_t(x)=\frac{x e^{-tx-Lx^2/2}}{G(t)},\qquad x>0.
\end{equation}
Jointly the boundary lengths satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:jointdensity}
(\ell/\sqrt h,r/\sqrt h)\ \Longrightarrow\ (U_t,V_t),\qquad
p(u,v)=\frac{e^{-t(u+v)-L(u+v)^2/2}}{G(t)},\quad u,v>0.
\end{equation}
Thus $\ell/K$ converges to a uniform variable on $[0,1]$, independent of $K/\sqrt h$ and the fair central bit. At $t=0$, $X_0$ is Rayleigh with density $Lxe^{-Lx^2/2}$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The leading marked weight is $(k+3)f_t(k/\sqrt h)$ and its total is asymptotic to $hG(t)$. Riemann sums give \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:density}. The Gaussian bounds from Section~\ref{mdk:asp:sec:criticalproof}, also valid with a fixed polynomial multiplier and a bounded spectral factor, give the asserted moment convergence for the true weights. For a given $k\ge2$, each interior split $1\le\ell\le k-1$ has multiplicity $2^{k-1}$; each endpoint split has twice this multiplicity. The endpoints have total mass $\Oh(h^{-1/2})$. The interior two-dimensional Riemann sum gives \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:jointdensity}. Transforming $(u,v)$ to $(x=u+v,z=u/(u+v))$ introduces Jacobian $x$ and factorizes the density as $p_t(x)\,dz$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Subcritical linear defect and Gaussian run]\label{mdk:asp:thm:law-sub}
If $\lambda_h\to\lambda\in(0,L)$, then
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:sub-law}
\frac{M_h}{h}\ \longrightarrow\ \frac\lambda L\quad\hbox{in probability},\qquad
\frac{M_h-w/L}{\sqrt h}\ \Longrightarrow\ N\left(0,\frac\lambda{L^2}\right).
\end{equation}
The fraction $\ell/(h-M_h)$ converges to a uniform variable on $[0,1]$, independent of the Gaussian limit and central bit. All other runs have maximum $\Oh(\log h)$ with probability tending to one.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The saddle for $j=m+1$ in \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:Sj} is $j=w/L$, and the smooth prefactor $h-j+4$ changes its location by only $\Oh(1)$. Taylor expansion of $w\log j-Lj$ at the saddle has quadratic term $-L^2(j-w/L)^2/(2w)$. Standard Gaussian localization follows directly from the strict concavity of this exponent on a fixed proportional neighborhood of the saddle, with exponentially small complementary tails. This proves \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:sub-law}; replacing $j$ by $m=j-1$ affects the scaled center by $o(1)$. The uniform-split calculation is the same as above, with $k$ now proportional to $h$, so endpoint mass tends to zero. Conditional on a boundary length, all its compositions are equally likely; their cuts are independent fair choices among the internal positions. A union bound gives probability at most $Ch2^{-v}$ for any noncentral run to have length at least $v$. Taking $v$ a sufficiently large multiple of $\log h$ proves the last assertion for the marked measure, and total variation transfer proves it for placements.
\end{proof}
The transition is between linear, square-root, and finite defect scales. A unique macroscopic run persists for every fixed positive aspect ratio; it does not disappear in the subcritical regime.

\section{Floors and exact parameter conventions}\label{mdk:asp:sec:floors}
For a fixed $0<\lambda<L$, write $w=\lfloor\lambda h\rfloor=\lambda h+d_h$ with $-1<d_h\le0$. Since $\Phi'(\lambda)=\log(\lambda/L)$,
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:subfloor}
\frac{A(h,w)}{h^w}=h^{3/2}B(\lambda)e^{h\Phi(\lambda)}
\left(\frac\lambda L\right)^{d_h}\bigl(1+\Oh(h^{-1})\bigr).
\end{equation}
Discarding the factor $(\lambda/L)^{d_h}$ is not generally an asymptotic equivalent.

For fixed $\lambda>L$, the cleanest all-order statement is to keep $\lambda_h$ in \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:super}. At the first two orders, Taylor expansion with $w=\lambda h+d_h$ gives
\begin{align}\label{mdk:asp:eq:superfloor}
\frac{A(h,w)}{h^w}
=C(\lambda)\biggl[1&+\frac{c_1+d_hC'/C}{h}\notag\\
&+\frac{c_2+d_hc_1'+d_h(C'/C)c_1+(d_h^2/2)C''/C}{h^2}
+\Oh(h^{-3})\biggr],
\end{align}
where every unmarked function and derivative on the right is evaluated at $\lambda$. Uniformity holds for bounded $d_h$, including floor sequences. In particular replacing $C(\lambda_h)$ by $C(\lambda)$ preserves the leading equivalent, but not all higher coefficients.

For a critical floor sequence $w=\lfloor Lh+t\sqrt h\rfloor$, put $d_h=w-Lh-t\sqrt h$. The actual parameter is $t_h=t+d_h/\sqrt h$. Theorem~\ref{mdk:asp:thm:critical} and Taylor expansion yield the floor-aware formula
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:criticalfloor}
\frac{A(h,w)}{h^w}
=hG(t)+\sqrt h\,[H(t)+d_hG'(t)]
+J(t)+d_hH'(t)+\frac{d_h^2}{2}G''(t)+\Oh_T(h^{-1/2}).
\end{equation}
All derivatives exist and are bounded on compact intervals by their Gaussian-integral representations. Even the coefficient of $\sqrt h$ oscillates with the floor. For $t=0$, the center values in \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:center} must therefore be combined with these derivative terms before making a next-order assertion about $\lfloor Lh\rfloor$.

\section{Lambert W inversion along rational rays}\label{mdk:asp:sec:inverse}
Fix positive integers $p,q$ and set
\[
\lambda=p/q,\qquad a_n=A(qn,pn),\qquad n\ge1.
\]
A rational $p/q$ cannot equal $\log2$, so the ray is in one of the two fixed regimes. Define
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:inversedata}
\begin{split}
\beta&=\begin{cases}3/2,&\lambda<L,\\0,&\lambda>L,\end{cases}
\qquad b=p\log q+q\Phi(\lambda),\\
D&=\begin{cases}q^{3/2}B(\lambda),&\lambda<L,\\C(\lambda),&\lambda>L.
\end{cases}
\end{split}
\end{equation}
The forward theorems give
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:logray}
\log(a_n/D)=pn\log n+bn+\beta\log n+\Oh(n^{-1}).
\end{equation}
This proves eventual strict increase: the main consecutive difference is $p\log n+\Oh(1)$ and the difference of two individually $\Oh(1/n)$ errors is still $\Oh(1/n)$.

For a sufficiently large target $y$, put $Y=\log(y/D)$ and $d=b/p$. Let $W_0$ be the positive real Lambert $W$ branch and set
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:Lambert}
N=\frac{Y/p}{W_0(e^dY/p)}.
\end{equation}
Then $pN\log N+bN=Y$: setting $z=e^dN$ reduces the equation to $z\log z=e^dY/p$. Let $t=t(y)$ be the unique sufficiently large solution of
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:inverseg}
g(t)=pt\log t+bt+\beta\log t=Y.
\end{equation}
Its derivative is $p(\log t+1+d)+\beta/t>0$ for large $t$, and
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:rootapprox}
t=N-\frac{\beta\log N}{p(\log N+1+d)}+\Oh\left(\frac1{N\log N}\right).
\end{equation}
Indeed the displayed displacement is $\Oh(1)$ and cancels $\beta\log N$ against the linear term of $pt\log t+bt$. The remaining residual is $\Oh(1/N)$, and division by a derivative of order $\log N$ proves the error. When $\beta=0$, $t=N$ exactly.

\begin{theorem}[Integer first-crossing bracket]\label{mdk:asp:thm:inverse}
For the fixed ray define $\nu(y)=\min\{n\ge1:a_n\ge y\}$. There are constants $K,y_0>0$, depending on $p,q$, such that for $y\ge y_0$, with
\[
\eta(y)=\frac{K}{t(y)(1+\log t(y))},
\]
we have
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:ceil}
\lceil t(y)-\eta(y)\rceil\le\nu(y)\le\lceil t(y)+\eta(y)\rceil.
\end{equation}
The bracket eventually contains at most two adjacent integer candidates.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
By \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:logray}, $|\log(a_n/D)-g(n)|\le K_0/n$ beyond a fixed index. On a unit neighborhood of $t$, $g'$ is bounded below by a positive multiple of $1+\log t$. For sufficiently large $K$, the changes of $g$ from $t$ to $t\pm\eta$ dominate the possible $K_0/n$ errors. More explicitly, the integer $\lceil t-\eta\rceil-1$ is strictly below the target and $\lceil t+\eta\rceil$ is at or above it, by this derivative bound; both are within a fixed neighborhood of $t$. Eventual monotonicity then bounds every other large integer. Finally $y$ eventually exceeds every value in the finite initial segment. This proves the first-crossing assertion, including equality targets. Since $2\eta\to0$, the two ceiling endpoints differ by at most one eventually.
\end{proof}
The constants and onset in this theorem are asymptotic existence constants. The statement alone is not a certified finite-input algorithm. Near an exact threshold, exact comparison of the remaining integer candidates is necessary; there is no justified unconditional single-ceiling rule. The uncertainty in \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:rootapprox} is a real interval before rounding, not a vanishing real error for the integer $\nu(y)$.

\subsection{Higher-order inverse above the transition}
For $\lambda>L$, let
\[
\ell_j(\lambda)=[u^j]\log\left(\sum_{r\ge0}c_r(\lambda)u^r\right).
\]
For each fixed $M$,
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:lograyM}
\log(a_n/D)=pn\log n+bn+
\sum_{j=1}^M\frac{\ell_j(\lambda)}{q^jn^j}+\Oh_M(n^{-M-1}).
\end{equation}
Let $t_M$ solve the truncated smooth equation at target $Y$. The same proof gives a ceiling bracket around $t_M$ of half-width
\[
\Oh_M\left(\frac{t_M^{-M-1}}{1+\log t_M}\right),
\]
with fixed sufficiently large implicit constants. For $M=1$, put $\alpha_1=c_1(\lambda)/q$. The exact root of the first-order truncated equation satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:t1}
t_1=N-\frac{\alpha_1}{pN(\log N+1+d)}
+\Oh\left(\frac{N^{-3}}{(\log N)^2}\right).
\end{equation}
Substitution leaves a residual $\Oh(N^{-3}/\log N)$ in that truncated equation. Its derivative is of order $\log N$, proving the displayed error. The discrete bracket has the larger half-width $\Oh(N^{-2}/\log N)$ because the forward logarithmic remainder is $\Oh(n^{-2})$.

Rational rays keep the dimensions integral without a changing fractional-part correction. These inverse assertions do not silently cover arbitrary sequences $w=\lfloor\lambda h\rfloor$, nor a nominal critical ray. Those require separate treatment of their lattice oscillations.

\section{Exact fixed height spectral structure}\label{mdk:asp:sec:structure}
This appendix concerns an exact family already studied by Kot\v{e}\v{s}ovec and Alekseyev. In particular, Kot\v{e}\v{s}ovec's Conjecture 2 on p.~83, dated 15 September 2011, explicitly proposes maximal irreducible degree $\lfloor h/2\rfloor+1$ for the planar and horizontally cylindrical families; the accompanying discussion records cylindrical checks through $h=20$ by Alekseyev \cite[pp.~82--83]{kotesovec}. The following argument proves the cylindrical upper bound and equality at infinitely many heights. It does not prove equality for every height or the planar conjecture, and does not establish whether these consequences were already proved elsewhere.
\begin{writenote}[6 October 2026]
The planar side is Part~III (Report~175, written later the same day with this Part's Gram machinery). It proves the upper bound $\lfloor h/2\rfloor+1$ of Conjecture~2 for the planar family (Theorem~\ref{mdk:pl:thm:main}) and Conjecture~4 (Corollary~\ref{mdk:pl:cor:parity}), and it records that Conjecture~3 as printed contradicts the exponents on the same page (Remark~\ref{mdk:pl:rem:source}). Equality at every height remains open on both boards (Section~\ref{mdk:sec:further}, item~\ref{mdk:q:degree}). The write did not read the book; the dates and page numbers here are this Part's.
\end{writenote}

For fixed $h\ge1$ write
\[
\mathcal F_h(z)=\sum_{w\ge1}A(h,w)z^w.
\]
The Gram representation \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:gram} gives a finite set $\mathcal R_h$ of distinct positive real algebraic integers and positive integer weights $a_h(\rho)$ with
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:spectralpowers}
A(h,w)=\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal R_h}a_h(\rho)\rho^w,\qquad
\mathcal F_h(z)=\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal R_h}a_h(\rho)\frac{\rho z}{1-\rho z}.
\end{equation}
Complementing the word swaps the two colors and transposes $C_b$ up to reorderings. Its nonzero singular-value squares and multiplicities are unchanged. No word equals its bitwise complement when $h\ge1$, so every weight $a_h(\rho)$ is a positive even integer.

Each $\rho$ produces a nonzero simple pole at $z=1/\rho$, and all other terms are analytic there. Thus no spectral parameter cancels, and the minimal denominator is squarefree over characteristic zero. This proves the assertion recorded explicitly as Kot\v{e}\v{s}ovec's Conjecture 1, dated 29 August 2011, on p.~83 \cite{kotesovec}; the source records Alekseyev's checks through $h=20$. We do not claim this is its first proof. The characteristic polynomial of each integer Gram matrix is integral, so algebraic conjugates occur with equal multiplicity. Summing over words shows that $a_h(\rho)$ is constant on each conjugacy class. Every conjugate is a nonnegative real Gram eigenvalue; nonzero conjugates are strictly positive. These observations explain the simple-factor and equal-coefficient patterns discussed on pp.~178--180 of the book; no new-priority claim is attached to that explanation.

If $b$ has $n_0$ zero edges and $n_1$ one edges, then $C_b$ has size $(n_1+1)\times(n_0+1)$. Either Gram matrix has the same nonzero spectrum. Choosing the smaller one proves
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:asp:eq:degree}
\deg_{\Q}(\rho)\le\min(n_0+1,n_1+1)\le\left\lfloor\frac{h+2}{2}\right\rfloor
=\left\lfloor\frac h2\right\rfloor+1.
\end{equation}
Hence every irreducible factor of the minimal fixed-height recurrence polynomial, equivalently the reversed minimal generating-function denominator, has degree at most this bound. It is stronger than the $h+1$ bound credited to Alekseyev on p.~178, and matches the upper-bound portion of the earlier explicit conjecture on p.~83. The comparison to both passages is essential.

The bound is attained for infinitely many heights. Take $h=p-3$, where $p\ge5$ is an odd prime, and choose an alternating word. Its tree is the path on $p-1$ vertices, whose largest squared adjacency eigenvalue is
\[
\rho=4\cos^2\left(\frac\pi p\right)=2+2\cos\left(\frac{2\pi}p\right).
\]
For a primitive $p$th root of unity $\zeta$, $[\Q(\zeta):\Q]=p-1$ and $\zeta$ satisfies $X^2-(\zeta+\zeta^{-1})X+1$ over the real subfield. Since $\zeta$ is nonreal, that quadratic extension has degree exactly two. Thus
\[
\deg_{\Q}(\rho)=\frac{p-1}{2}=\left\lfloor\frac{h+2}{2}\right\rfloor.
\]
The positive spectral weight prevents cancellation of this irreducible factor. Infinitely many primes give infinitely many equality heights.

For context, the fixed-height leading law is also transparent. Every $C_b$ has $h+1$ entries equal to one, so its squared norm is at most $h+1$. Equality forces rank one, since its squared singular values sum to $h+1$. A connected zero-one rank-one incidence graph is complete bipartite, and a complete bipartite tree is a star. Here that means $b$ is constant. The two constant words each give the eigenvalue $h+1$ once; every nonconstant word has smaller top eigenvalue. Therefore, for fixed $h$,
\[
A(h,w)\sim2(h+1)^w\qquad(w\to\infty),
\]
recovering the prior law on p.~192. This limit holds in a different parameter regime from Theorems~\ref{mdk:asp:thm:phases} and~\ref{mdk:asp:thm:critical}.
\begin{writenote}[6 October 2026]
The same two facts (the Gram trace and the $h+1$ unit entries of $C_b$) bound every word's contribution by $(h+1)^w$, which gives $2(h+1)^w\le A(h,w)\le2^h(h+1)^w$ for all $h,w\ge1$, with the cases of equality: Remark~\ref{mdk:sq:rem:book-bounds} in Part~II.
\end{writenote}

\section{Sources scope and further questions}\label{mdk:asp:sec:sources}
The principal exact-family source is Kot\v{e}\v{s}ovec's sixth edition \cite{kotesovec}, particularly pp.~82--83 and Section 2.6, pp.~169--196. Its p.~169 explicitly fixes the open dimension and lets the wrapped dimension grow; pp.~169--192 display exact formulas, recurrence and generating-function factorizations, and spectral power sums. Page 192 gives the fixed-height leading equivalent, and p.~193 tabulates leading and subleading roots. Page 178 credits Alekseyev's September 2011 factor-generation method and a degree bound, while the earlier p.~83 gives the sharper explicit conjecture discussed in Appendix~\ref{mdk:asp:sec:structure}. The linked Zealint discussion was not recovered in the bounded review \cite{zealint}; its unavailability is a gap in historical coverage, not evidence about its contents or about priority.

Matsuo supplies the binary-word and cyclic-height encoding and an $\Oh(n^4)$ multiprecision-operation algorithm in the square setting \cite{matsuo}. Wilf's earlier planar transfer-matrix analysis provides a related block-decomposition antecedent \cite{wilf}. Planar maximum-density asymptotics in Wilf and Larsen \cite{wilf,larsen}, and the later planar enumeration of Brown \cite{brown}, have different boundary conditions. They should not be identified with the cylindrical joint-limit theorem. Likewise a cylinder problem with exactly one king in each row and column is a different density constraint.

The aspect-ratio transition and joint-limit laws in this report were not located in the primary materials inspected. This is a bounded comparison, not an exhaustive priority certification. Exact fixed-height enumeration, the square leading constant, and broad transfer-matrix ideas are established antecedents. The structural appendix is framed as a consequence of the exact representation with explicit credit to the prior conjecture, not as a claim of a newly proposed bound. No external publication, OEIS edit, author contact, or public repository modification is part of this report.

Several questions remain beyond the theorems proved here:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
\item Determine the intermediate regimes as $\lambda_h\to0$. The present uniform theorem requires a positive lower bound on $w/h$, and its one-macroscopic-run argument does not by itself cover the transition to fixed or slowly growing width.
\item Extend the critical expansion with effective uniform remainders to growing $|t_h|$, and determine maximal matching ranges with the two fixed-regime expansions. The large-$|t|$ formulas for $G$ alone do not answer this.
\item Make the error constants and onsets effective, especially for the integer inverse brackets. This would turn the asymptotic brackets into certified finite-input threshold tools.
\item Derive subcritical corrections beyond the relative $\Oh(h^{-1})$ equivalent. The marked-star sum is explicit, but averaging the spectral perturbation with a long, extensive boundary needs additional coefficient analysis.
\item Resolve the exact maximal degree at every cylindrical height and locate any earlier complete proof of the 2011 conjecture. The infinitely many equality heights proved here do not settle the every-height assertion or its planar counterpart.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Main results and conventions}\label{mdk:sq:sec:main}
The board consists of squares $(r,s)$ with $0\le r,s<2n$. Columns are read modulo $2n$; rows are not identified. Two distinct kings attack when their row difference is at most one and their circular column distance is at most one. We count placements on this labeled board, without quotienting by rotations, reflections, or translations. Set $a_0=1$. For $n\ge1$ the sequence is \href{https://oeis.org/A137432}{OEIS A137432}; it begins
\[
4,\ 32,\ 344,\ 4460,\ 66532,\ 1118398,\ 20984924,\ldots.
\]
Partitioning the board into disjoint $2\times2$ blocks gives an upper bound of $n^2$ kings. Occupying every other row and every other column attains it. Thus the count in this report is exactly a maximum-density count.

\begin{theorem}[All fixed orders]\label{mdk:sq:thm:all}
Put
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:sq:eq:C}
C=\frac{2e(e-1)^2}{(e-2)^2}=31.1116835720490503682643922791\ldots.
\end{equation}
There is a sequence $c_0,c_1,\ldots\in\Q(e)$, with $c_0=1$, such that for every fixed integer $M\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:sq:eq:main}
a_n=Cn^n\left(\sum_{j=0}^M\frac{c_j}{n^j}+\Oh_M(n^{-M-1})\right)
\qquad(n\longrightarrow\infty).
\end{equation}
Every coefficient is computable by a finite rational-function calculation. The first three nonconstant coefficients are given in Section~\ref{mdk:sq:sec:coeff}.
\end{theorem}
The quantifier ``fixed $M$'' is essential. We assert neither convergence of the infinite asymptotic series nor a remainder bound uniform in growing $M$. No fitted coefficients enter the proof.

\begin{theorem}[Boundary law]\label{mdk:sq:thm:law}
Choose a placement uniformly from the $a_n$ possibilities and form the binary word described in Section~\ref{mdk:sq:sec:geometry}. Except on an event of exponentially small probability, this word has a unique run longer than $n/2$. Let its central bit and its left and right outward run compositions be the associated state. Write $K$ for the total length of the two boundaries; on the exceptional event define $K=n-$ the maximum run length.

Let $q=e^{-1}$ and $F(q)=(1-q)/(1-2q)$. The state converges in total variation, at rate $\Oh(n^{-1})$, to a uniform bit independent of two independent finite compositions. A specified composition of total length $l$ has probability $q^l/F(q)$. The exceptional state has limiting probability zero. Moreover, for every fixed integer $p\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:sq:eq:moments}
\mathbb E[K^p]=\mathbb E[K_\infty^p]+\Oh_p(n^{-1}),\qquad
\mathbb E[z^{K_\infty}]=\frac{F(qz)^2}{F(q)^2}\quad(|z|\le1).
\end{equation}
In particular $\mathbb E[K]=2e/((e-1)(e-2))+\Oh(n^{-1})$.
\end{theorem}
Independence refers to run compositions, or to binary boundaries encoded relative to the central bit. The literal inner boundary bit must be opposite to the central bit and is therefore not independent of it.

\begin{corollary}[Ratio and inverse]\label{mdk:sq:cor:inverse}
The sequence is eventually strictly increasing and
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:sq:eq:ratio}
\frac{a_{n+1}}{n a_n}
=e\left(1+\frac1{2n}+\frac{-c_1-1/24}{n^2}+\Oh(n^{-3})\right).
\end{equation}
Write $\ell_j=[u^j]\log(\sum_{r\ge0}c_ru^r)$, and
\[
g_M(t)=t\log t+\sum_{j=1}^M\ell_jt^{-j}.
\]
For sufficiently large $x$, let $t_M(x)$ be the unique sufficiently large root of $g_M(t)=\log(x/C)$. There is a constant $A_M>0$ such that, with
\[
\eta_M(x)=\frac{A_Mt_M(x)^{-M-1}}{1+\log t_M(x)},\qquad
\nu(x)=\min\{n\ge0:a_n\ge x\},
\]
we have the precise integer bracket
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:sq:eq:bracket}
\left\lceil t_M(x)-\eta_M(x)\right\rceil
\le\nu(x)\le
\left\lceil t_M(x)+\eta_M(x)\right\rceil.
\end{equation}
The bracket eventually contains at most two adjacent integers. Its constants and onset are asymptotic existence constants, not certified finite-input numerical bounds.
\end{corollary}

\section{From placements to a transfer matrix}\label{mdk:sq:sec:geometry}
Index the $n^2$ blocks by $(i,j)$, $0\le i,j<n$. The king in block $(i,j)$ has coordinates
\[
(2i+x_{ij},\ 2j+y_{ij}),\qquad x_{ij},y_{ij}\in\{0,1\}.
\]
Every block contains exactly one king. Consider neighboring blocks in the same two-row band. If $y_{ij}=1$ and $y_{i,j+1}=0$, their column distance is one and their row distance is at most one, so they attack. Consequently the cyclic sequence of column offsets cannot descend. It is constant:
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:sq:eq:b}
y_{ij}=b_i\qquad(0\le j<n),\qquad b_i\in\{0,1\}.
\end{equation}
This argument also covers the boundary between the last and first block columns. For $n=1$ constancy is automatic.

In a fixed block column, an adjacent pair with $x_{ij}=1$ and $x_{i+1,j}=0$ would similarly attack. Hence $x_{ij}$ is nondecreasing in $i$, and there is a unique height $c_j\in\{0,\ldots,n\}$ with
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:sq:eq:c}
x_{ij}=\begin{cases}0,&i<c_j,\\1,&i\ge c_j.\end{cases}
\end{equation}
Only conflicts between diagonally neighboring blocks remain. Put $u=c_j$ and $v=c_{j+1}$, with block-column indices cyclic. A down-right attack from $(i,j)$ to $(i+1,j+1)$ occurs exactly when
\[
u\le i<v-1,\qquad (b_i,b_{i+1})=(1,0).
\]
Thus, when $u<v$, the substring $b_u,\ldots,b_{v-1}$ must be nondecreasing. An attack in the opposite diagonal direction occurs exactly when
\[
v\le i<u-1,\qquad (b_i,b_{i+1})=(0,1),
\]
so when $u>v$, the substring $b_v,\ldots,b_{u-1}$ must be nonincreasing. Equal heights always work. These conditions are sufficient as well: every possibly attacking pair lies in the same block or in horizontally, vertically, or diagonally adjacent blocks, all of which have been checked. For small widths, two directions can meet the same neighboring block; the cyclic conditions check both directions.

For a binary word $b=(b_0,\ldots,b_{n-1})$ let $M_b$ be the $(n+1)\times(n+1)$ matrix indexed by $0,\ldots,n$, with
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:sq:eq:M}
M_b[u,v]=\begin{cases}
1,&u=v,\\
1,&u<v\text{ and }b_u\cdots b_{v-1}\text{ has the form }0^*1^*,\\
1,&u>v\text{ and }b_v\cdots b_{u-1}\text{ has the form }1^*0^*,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
Here a starred block may be empty. For fixed $b$, a permitted list $(c_0,\ldots,c_{n-1})$ contributes one to the product $\prod_jM_b[c_j,c_{j+1}]$, where $c_n=c_0$. Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:sq:eq:transfer}
a_n=\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^n}\tr(M_b^n).
\end{equation}
The trace sums over labeled cyclic lists with a distinguished position $0$. We do not divide by $n$ or by a rotational stabilizer. This is precisely the labeled-board convention.

\section{Component factorization and the caterpillar}\label{mdk:sq:sec:tree}
Color the edges of the path on vertices $0,\ldots,n$ by $b_0,\ldots,b_{n-1}$. Let $E_\epsilon[u,v]=1$ if every edge between $u$ and $v$ has color $\epsilon$, for $\epsilon=0,1$. Empty intervals qualify. Thus $E_\epsilon$ is the matrix of membership in a common monochromatic component, including isolated vertices.

\begin{lemma}\label{mdk:sq:lem:factor}
The exact matrix identity $M_b=E_0E_1$ holds.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
An intermediate vertex $w$ counted by $(E_0E_1)[u,v]$ must lie in the same $0$-component as $u$ and the same $1$-component as $v$. It cannot lie outside the interval with endpoints $u,v$: a nonempty subinterval would then have to consist entirely of both colors. There cannot be two possible vertices $w$, for the interval between them would have the same contradiction. For $u<v$, existence of $w$ says exactly that the interval has form $0^*1^*$; for $u>v$ its increasing-order form is $1^*0^*$. For $u=v$ the unique choice is $w=u$.
\end{proof}

Let $R$ and $S$ be the vertex-component incidence matrices for the $0$-components and $1$-components. Then
\[
E_0=RR^\mathsf T,\qquad E_1=SS^\mathsf T,\qquad C_b=R^\mathsf TS.
\]
Two opposite-color components intersect in at most one path vertex, so $C_b$ has entries zero or one. Form a bipartite graph $T_b$ on these components, giving one edge for each nonempty intersection. Its $n+1$ edges correspond exactly to the original path vertices. It is connected, since successive path vertices give edges sharing the component of their intervening color. There are $n+2$ components altogether: if there are $n_0$ zero edges and $n_1$ one edges, their component counts are $n+1-n_0$ and $n+1-n_1$. Thus $T_b$ is a connected graph with one fewer edge than vertices, hence a tree.

Cyclicity of matrix trace gives, for $n\ge1$,
\begin{align}
\tr(M_b^n)&=\tr\bigl((C_bC_b^\mathsf T)^n\bigr),\label{mdk:sq:eq:gram}\\
A(T_b)&=\begin{pmatrix}0&C_b\\C_b^\mathsf T&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\tr(M_b^n)=\frac12\tr\bigl(A(T_b)^{2n}\bigr).\label{mdk:sq:eq:tree-trace}
\end{align}
This proof does not require diagonalizing the nonsymmetric matrix $M_b$. The nonzero adjacency eigenvalues occur in pairs $\pm\sigma_j(C_b)$, and all eigenvalues contributing on the Gram side are nonnegative.

\begin{lemma}[Run description]\label{mdk:sq:lem:caterpillar}
If $b$ has positive run lengths $(r_1,\ldots,r_t)$, then $T_b$ is a caterpillar. For $t\ge2$ its spine has $t$ vertices, with pendant leaf counts
\[
r_1,\ r_2-1,\ldots,\ r_{t-1}-1,\ r_t.
\]
For $t=1$ it is a star with $n+1$ leaves. Every run of length $r_i$ corresponds to a vertex of degree $r_i+1$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The nonsingleton monochromatic components are the runs. Consecutive runs intersect at their shared endpoint, producing the spine edges. At an internal run, its $r_i-1$ interior path vertices give singleton components of the opposite color and hence leaves. An endpoint run has one additional free path endpoint, producing $r_i$ leaves. If there is only one run, every path vertex meets an opposite-color singleton, giving $n+1$ leaves. The degree statements follow immediately.
\end{proof}

A composition of $n$ is an ordered list of positive integers summing to $n$. Each composition is the run list of exactly two binary words. If $T_r$ denotes its tree, \eqref{mdk:sq:eq:transfer} and \eqref{mdk:sq:eq:tree-trace} give the useful exact identity
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:sq:eq:composition}
a_n=\sum_{r\text{ a composition of }n}\tr\bigl(A(T_r)^{2n}\bigr).
\end{equation}
The factor two from the initial bit has canceled the factor one-half in the tree trace. This is a sum over ordered compositions, not over unlabeled tree isomorphism classes.

\section{A dominant run and its spectral equation}\label{mdk:sq:sec:long}
Suppose the word has a run of length $m>n/2$. It is unique. Delete the corresponding hub from $T_b$ and write
\[
k=n-m=l+r,
\]
where $l,r$ are the total boundary lengths on its two sides. Each nonempty side has its nearest bit opposite to the central bit. Its outward run list is an arbitrary composition. If $\alpha_l$ is the number of compositions of $l$, with the empty composition counted once, then
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:sq:eq:F}
\alpha_0=1,\qquad\alpha_l=2^{l-1}\ (l\ge1),\qquad
F(q)=\sum_{l\ge0}\alpha_lq^l=\frac{1-q}{1-2q}.
\end{equation}
There are $2\alpha_l\alpha_r$ such binary words for fixed $l,r$. The order on the left boundary is outward from the hub, not left to right on the original path.

Let $A_L,A_R$ be the adjacency matrices of the nonempty rooted branches after deleting the hub, with roots $v_L,v_R$. Besides them, the deletion leaves isolated leaves. The total number of branch edges is $k$, since the original tree has $n+1$ edges and the removed hub has degree $m+1$. Empty branches contribute zero throughout.

\begin{lemma}[Schur complement]\label{mdk:sq:lem:schur}
Let $\Lambda$ be the square of the Perron adjacency eigenvalue of $T_b$. Then $\Lambda\ge m+1>k$, and
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:sq:eq:schur}
\Lambda=m+1+\sum_{j\ge1}B_j\Lambda^{-j},\qquad
B_j=(A_L^{2j})[v_L,v_L]+(A_R^{2j})[v_R,v_R],
\end{equation}
with $0\le B_j\le k^j$ for $j\ge1$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The hub and its neighbors induce a star of degree $m+1$, so the variational principle gives $\Lambda\ge m+1$. A bipartite graph with $K$ edges has squared adjacency norm at most $K$: the squared norm of its bipartite incidence matrix is at most its squared Frobenius norm, which is $K$. If the two branches have $k_L,k_R$ edges, then their norms are at most $\sqrt{k_L},\sqrt{k_R}$, strictly below $\sqrt\Lambda$.

Apply the Schur complement at $\lambda=\sqrt\Lambda$. Its diagonal resolvents have convergent Neumann series. A tree is bipartite, so diagonal odd powers vanish. Multiplication by $\lambda$ gives the zeroth contribution equal to the number of hub neighbors, namely $m+1$, followed by \eqref{mdk:sq:eq:schur}. Finally,
$B_j\le k_L^j+k_R^j\le(k_L+k_R)^j=k^j$.
\end{proof}

Write $s_L,s_R$ for the first outward run lengths, $t_L,t_R$ for the second, and $u_L,u_R$ for the third, using zero for absent runs. Direct rooted closed-walk counting, or the resolvent recurrence in Section~\ref{mdk:sq:sec:coeff}, yields
\begin{align}
B_1&=s_L+s_R=:S,\label{mdk:sq:eq:B1}\\
B_2&=s_L^2+t_L+s_R^2+t_R=:T,\label{mdk:sq:eq:B2}\\
B_3&=\sum_{a\in\{L,R\}}(s_a^3+2s_at_a+t_a^2+u_a).\label{mdk:sq:eq:B3}
\end{align}
For example, the root degree is $s_a$. Four-step returns either choose two successive root edges, giving $s_a^2$, or visit the next spine vertex and use one of its outward edges, giving $t_a$.

\begin{lemma}[Spectral remainder]\label{mdk:sq:lem:remainder}
For every such word,
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:sq:eq:spectral}
\tr(M_b^n)=\Lambda^n+R_b,\qquad 0\le R_b\le n k^n.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The adjacency matrix after hub deletion has norm at most $\sqrt{k}$. By symmetric eigenvalue interlacing, at most one eigenvalue of $A(T_b)$ exceeds $\sqrt{k}$. Bipartite symmetry gives the corresponding statement below $-\sqrt{k}$. The tree is connected, so its Perron pair is simple. All remaining eigenvalues have absolute value at most $\sqrt{k}$. Their even powers contribute nonnegative terms to one-half of the trace; there are at most $n$ remaining terms after division by two and the harmless coarse bound in \eqref{mdk:sq:eq:spectral} follows.
\end{proof}

With $d=1-k$, expansion of \eqref{mdk:sq:eq:schur} at fixed boundary data starts as
\begin{align}
\Lambda&=n+d+\frac S n+\frac{T-dS}{n^2}+\Oh_k(n^{-3}),\label{mdk:sq:eq:lambda-first}\\
\left(\frac\Lambda n\right)^n
&=e^d\left(1+\frac{P_1}{n}+\frac{P_2}{n^2}+\cdots\right),\label{mdk:sq:eq:P-formal}\\
P_1&=S-\frac{d^2}{2},\nonumber\\
P_2&=T-2dS+\frac{d^3}{3}+\frac12\left(S-\frac{d^2}{2}\right)^2.\nonumber
\end{align}
The pointwise expansion is only a starting point. The next section supplies the uniform bounds needed before summing over all boundaries.

\section{Uniform tails and all fixed order remainders}\label{mdk:sq:sec:uniform}
\subsection{Words with no almost full run}
Let $m$ be the maximum run length, without assuming $m>n/2$. Split the caterpillar adjacency matrix into pendant-star edges and spine-path edges. The first matrix is a disjoint union of stars with maximum degree at most $m+1$; the second has norm at most two. The triangle inequality gives
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:sq:eq:norm}
\Lambda\le(\sqrt{m+1}+2)^2=m+\Oh(\sqrt n).
\end{equation}
The one-run star also satisfies this bound. There are at most $2n\,2^{n-m}$ words with maximum run length $m$: choose a marked start of one such run, its bit, and arbitrary remaining bits. The overcount is harmless. Also \eqref{mdk:sq:eq:gram} implies
\[
\tr(M_b^n)\le(n+1)\Lambda^n.
\]
For $n/4\le m\le(1-\epsilon)n$, set $t=m/n$. Apart from polynomial factors and an $\exp(\Oh(\sqrt n))$ factor from \eqref{mdk:sq:eq:norm}, the normalized exponential rate is
\[
f(t)=(1-t)\log2+\log t.
\]
On $(0,1]$, $f'(t)=1/t-\log2>0$ and $f(1)=0$. Thus $f$ is uniformly negative on $[1/4,1-\epsilon]$. For $m\le n/4$, the crude count $2^n$ and \eqref{mdk:sq:eq:norm} instead give exponential base $2(1/4+o(1))<1$. Summing over $m$ proves that, for every fixed $\epsilon\in(0,1)$, some $c_\epsilon>0$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:sq:eq:bulk}
\sum_{b:\,\max\operatorname{run}(b)\le(1-\epsilon)n}\tr(M_b^n)
=\Oh\bigl(n^n e^{-c_\epsilon n}\bigr).
\end{equation}
If the first interval is empty, only the second estimate is needed, or one can use any smaller positive $\epsilon$.

\subsection{Almost star bounds and a logarithmic cutoff}
Fix $0<\epsilon<1/2$. For $k\le\epsilon n$, Lemma~\ref{mdk:sq:lem:schur} gives
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:sq:eq:lambda-tail}
0\le\Lambda-(n-k+1)
\le\sum_{j\ge1}(k/\Lambda)^j
=\frac{k}{\Lambda-k}
\le\frac{k}{n-2k+1}.
\end{equation}
Using $\log v\le v-1$ yields
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:sq:eq:perron-tail}
\left(\frac\Lambda n\right)^n
\le\exp\left(1-k+\frac{k}{n-2k+1}\right)
\le C_\epsilon e^{-k}.
\end{equation}
There are at most $2(k+1)2^k$ central-bit and boundary-composition states of total length $k$. Put $\rho=2/e<1$. Their Perron contribution after normalization by $n^n$ has tail bounded by
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:sq:eq:tail}
C_\epsilon\sum_{k>K}(k+1)\rho^k.
\end{equation}
The trace remainder can be treated separately. By \eqref{mdk:sq:eq:spectral}, its aggregate over $k\le\epsilon n$ is bounded by a constant times
\[
n\sum_{1\le k\le\epsilon n}(k+1)2^k(k/n)^n
=\Oh\bigl(n^3(\epsilon 2^\epsilon)^n\bigr).
\]
Indeed $t\mapsto\log t+t\log2$ is increasing for $t>0$. Since $\epsilon<1/2$, the base $\epsilon2^\epsilon$ is less than one. Any fixed power of $k$ can be inserted with only a polynomial change in the prefactor. This observation is also needed for the probability law.

The remaining words are exponentially small by \eqref{mdk:sq:eq:bulk}. A cutoff
\[
K=K_M(n)=A_M\log n
\]
with sufficiently large fixed $A_M$ consequently removes all omitted words at any specified algebraic order. We next show that the local Taylor remainder can be summed without a logarithmic loss.

\subsection{A uniform analytic disk}
Put $h=k+1$, $d=1-k$, $u=1/n$, and $y=\Lambda/n$. The exact implicit equation is
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:sq:eq:implicit}
y=1+du+\sum_{j\ge1}B_j u^{j+1}y^{-j}.
\end{equation}
Regard the boundary data as fixed and $u$ as complex. On
\[
|u|\le\frac1{16h},\qquad |y-1|\le\frac14,
\]
set $r=k|u|/|y|\le1/12$. The correction sum on the right of \eqref{mdk:sq:eq:implicit} has absolute value at most
\[
|u|\frac r{1-r}\le\frac{|u|}{11},
\]
and its derivative with respect to $y$ has absolute value at most
\[
\frac{|u|}{|y|}\frac r{(1-r)^2}\le\frac1{121h}.
\]
Together with $|du|\le1/16$, these inequalities show that the right side maps the closed $y$-disk strictly into itself and is a uniform contraction. Iterating from $y=1$ produces its unique analytic fixed point. On a possibly smaller absolute disk of the same scale,
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:sq:eq:y-estimate}
y=1+du+\Oh(k u^2),\qquad
\frac{\log y}{u}-d=\Oh(h^2|u|).
\end{equation}
For $k=0$ the first correction is exactly zero. The logarithm is its analytic branch near one, and its apparent pole at zero is removable.

For real $u=1/n$ in the logarithmic window, \eqref{mdk:sq:eq:lambda-tail} places the actual Perron solution inside this disk; uniqueness identifies it with the analytic solution. Now define
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:sq:eq:H}
H(u)=\exp\left(\frac{\log y(u)}u-d\right)=\sum_{j\ge0}P_ju^j.
\end{equation}
The distinction between the two radii is important. The solution $y$ is analytic on a disk of radius proportional to $h^{-1}$, but to bound $H$ by an absolute constant we use the smaller disk
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:sq:eq:small-radius}
|u|\le r_h:=\frac c{h^2}
\end{equation}
for a sufficiently small fixed $c>0$. Equation~\eqref{mdk:sq:eq:y-estimate} implies $|H(u)|\le C$ there, with absolute $C$. Cauchy's coefficient estimates give
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:sq:eq:cauchy}
|P_j|\le C_jh^{2j},\qquad
\left|H(1/n)-\sum_{j=0}^MP_jn^{-j}\right|
\le C_Mh^{2M+2}n^{-M-1},
\end{equation}
provided $1/n\le r_h/2$. To see the remainder bound directly, bound the coefficient of order $j$ by $Cr_h^{-j}$ and sum the geometric tail from $M+1$ onward. This condition holds uniformly for $k\le A_M\log n$ once $n$ is large enough.

\subsection{Summation and completion of the expansion proof}
For a state with boundary total $k$, equations~\eqref{mdk:sq:eq:H} and \eqref{mdk:sq:eq:cauchy} say
\[
\left(\frac\Lambda n\right)^n
=e^{1-k}\left(\sum_{j=0}^MP_jn^{-j}
+\Oh_M((k+1)^{2M+2}n^{-M-1})\right).
\]
Summing the error over the logarithmic window costs at most
\[
C_Mn^{-M-1}\sum_{k\ge0}(k+1)^{2M+3}\rho^k
=\Oh_M(n^{-M-1}).
\]
Every polynomial moment of $\rho^k$ is summable. The omitted Perron tail, bulk, and non-Perron part are smaller by the preceding bounds. The coefficient bound in \eqref{mdk:sq:eq:cauchy} allows the logarithmic cutoff to be removed from each coefficient with the same precision, after increasing $A_M$ if necessary.

Let $\comp$ be the set of all finite compositions, including the empty one, and write $|\alpha|$ for their total length. Thus the coefficients are the absolutely convergent weighted sums
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:sq:eq:c-sum}
Cc_j=2e\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in\comp}
q^{|\alpha|+|\beta|}P_j(\alpha,\beta),\qquad q=e^{-1}.
\end{equation}
The factor two is the central bit. Since $P_0=1$, the leading sum is $2eF(q)^2$, exactly \eqref{mdk:sq:eq:C}. Section~\ref{mdk:sq:sec:coeff} proves that each normalized coefficient lies in $\Q(e)$ and is finitely computable, completing Theorem~\ref{mdk:sq:thm:all}.

\section{Exact coefficients and a finite algorithm}\label{mdk:sq:sec:coeff}
\subsection{Rooted resolvents}
For a branch whose outward runs are $s_1,\ldots,s_d$, define formal series
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:sq:eq:G}
G_{d+1}(z)=1,\qquad
G_i(z)=\frac1{1-z(s_i-1+G_{i+1}(z))}\quad(i=d,\ldots,1).
\end{equation}
The normalized diagonal resolvent at the root is $G_1(z)$. To verify this, remove the root. For an internal branch root there are $s_i-1$ pendant leaves and one outward spine branch. Each leaf contributes one to the normalized self-energy, while the spine branch contributes $G_{i+1}$. At the terminal vertex there are $s_d$ actual leaves, which agrees with the extra terminal value $G_{d+1}=1$.

Thus
\[
B_j=[z^j](G_{L,1}(z)+G_{R,1}(z)-2),\qquad j\ge1.
\]
An absent side has $G_1=1$. Padding missing terminal runs by zero preserves this value: $1/(1-z(0-1+1))=1$. Repeated truncation of \eqref{mdk:sq:eq:G} shows that its coefficient of $z^j$ is a polynomial in only the first $j$ outward runs. Equations~\eqref{mdk:sq:eq:B1}--\eqref{mdk:sq:eq:B3} follow by expansion.

\subsection{Formal inversion and weighted summation}
For a requested fixed order $M$, the following finite procedure computes $c_0,\ldots,c_M$.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=0.25em]
\item Truncate \eqref{mdk:sq:eq:G} at $z^{M+1}$ on each side. Only the first $M$ outward run variables are needed. Extract $B_1,\ldots,B_M$.
\item Solve \eqref{mdk:sq:eq:implicit} recursively in the ring of formal power series modulo $u^{M+2}$, starting with $y=1+du$. The coefficient of $u^r$ on the right depends only on previously determined coefficients.
\item Form $\log y/u-d$ modulo $u^{M+1}$ and exponentiate. This produces the rational polynomials $P_0,\ldots,P_M$ in $k$ and the finitely many run variables.
\item Sum each monomial over the two finite compositions using the rules below, then substitute $q=e^{-1}$ and divide by $F(q)^2$.
\end{enumerate}
There is no requirement to enumerate infinitely many compositions numerically.

Put
\[
h_a(q)=\sum_{s\ge1}s^aq^s,\qquad D=q\frac{d}{dq}.
\]
Here $h_0=q/(1-q)$ and $h_a=D^ah_0$ are rational functions. If a monomial in the initial outward runs is $s_1^{a_1}\cdots s_d^{a_d}$ with $a_d>0$, then its sum over all finite compositions is
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:sq:eq:run-sum}
F(q)\prod_{i=1}^d h_{a_i}(q).
\end{equation}
Indeed the first $d$ positive lengths are chosen independently, and the remaining composition is arbitrary. Required but unweighted earlier lengths contribute $h_0$; compositions shorter than $d$ contribute zero. The constant monomial has sum $F(q)$. The two sides multiply, and multiplying a summand by $k^p$ is implemented by $D^p$ on the joint weight. This last statement refers to the total boundary length, not merely to the displayed runs. Every operation is rational over $\Q(q)$. Consequently \eqref{mdk:sq:eq:c-sum} gives $c_j\in\Q(q)=\Q(e)$ for all $j$.

\section{Boundary distribution with rates}\label{mdk:sq:sec:law}
Let $\Omega$ be the countable set of a specified central bit and two finite compositions, together with an exceptional symbol $\star$. For ordinary $\omega$ let $k(\omega)$ be its total composition length. If $k<n/2$, the corresponding word of length $n$ is uniquely determined by inserting a central run of length $n-k$. Define its unnormalized placement mass, divided by $n^n$, as
\[
w_n(\omega)=\frac{\tr(M_b^n)}{n^n}.
\]
For ordinary states with $k\ge n/2$, set $w_n(\omega)=0$. Let $w_n(\star)$ be the total normalized mass of words with no run longer than $n/2$. The limiting unnormalized masses are
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:sq:eq:weights}
w_\infty(\omega)=e^{1-k(\omega)}\quad(\omega\ne\star),\qquad
w_\infty(\star)=0.
\end{equation}
Their total is $C$.

\begin{proposition}[Weighted mass convergence]\label{mdk:sq:prop:weighted}
For every fixed integer $p\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:sq:eq:weighted}
\sum_{\omega\ne\star}(1+k(\omega))^p
|w_n(\omega)-w_\infty(\omega)|
+(n+1)^pw_n(\star)=\Oh_p(n^{-1}).
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
First remove the non-Perron contribution over $k\le\epsilon n$ by the exponentially small estimate following \eqref{mdk:sq:eq:tail}. Its weighted sum is still exponentially small. The bulk estimate \eqref{mdk:sq:eq:bulk} handles all other finite-$n$ words outside this range, even after multiplying by $(n+1)^p$. In particular it handles $\star$.

On $k\le A\log n$, \eqref{mdk:sq:eq:cauchy} with $M=0$ gives
\[
\left|\left(\frac\Lambda n\right)^n-e^{1-k}\right|
\le\frac{C e^{1-k}(k+1)^2}{n}.
\]
After inserting the number of states and the weight $(1+k)^p$, its sum is $\Oh_p(n^{-1})$. In $A\log n<k\le\epsilon n$, both Perron and limiting masses have weighted tails bounded by a constant times
\[
\sum_{k>A\log n}(k+1)^{p+1}\rho^k.
\]
Choose $A$ sufficiently large to make this $\Oh_p(n^{-2})$. Finally, limiting states with $k\ge\epsilon n$, including those absent at finite $n$, have exponentially small weighted tails. Combining these estimates proves \eqref{mdk:sq:eq:weighted}.
\end{proof}

The true total mass is $a_n/n^n=C+\Oh(n^{-1})$. Divide \eqref{mdk:sq:eq:weighted} by it and use the finite limiting weighted moments to account for normalization. For $p=0$, one-half the resulting $\ell^1$ bound proves the asserted total variation rate. For each fixed $p$, the ordinary-state statistic $K^p=k^p$ and the exceptional bound $K^p\le n^p$ prove \eqref{mdk:sq:eq:moments}.

The normalized limiting mass at one specified bit and composition pair is
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:sq:eq:factor-law}
\frac{e^{1-k}}C=\frac{q^k}{2F(q)^2}.
\end{equation}
This factorization proves the uniform-bit and independence assertions. For one boundary let $R$ be its number of runs and put $h=q/(1-q)<1$. Since a positive run has total weight $h$, we have
\[
\mathbb P(R=r)=(1-h)h^r\quad(r\ge0).
\]
Conditional on $R$, the run lengths are independent and have distribution
\[
\mathbb P(s=j)=(1-q)q^{j-1}\quad(j\ge1).
\]
The empty boundary occurs when $R=0$. The two-boundary probability generating function is $F(qz)^2/F(q)^2$, and differentiating at $z=1$ gives
\[
\mathbb E[K_\infty]=2q\frac{F'(q)}{F(q)}
=\frac{2q}{(1-q)(1-2q)}
=\frac{2e}{(e-1)(e-2)}.
\]
This completes Theorem~\ref{mdk:sq:thm:law} with its moment rates.

\section{Growth ratio and inverse thresholds}\label{mdk:sq:sec:inverse}
\subsection{Ratio expansion}
Write $A(n)=a_n/(Cn^n)$. Theorem~\ref{mdk:sq:thm:all} implies
\[
\frac{A(n+1)}{A(n)}=1-\frac{c_1}{n^2}+\Oh(n^{-3}).
\]
Also
\[
\left(1+\frac1n\right)^{n+1}
=e\left(1+\frac1{2n}-\frac1{24n^2}+\Oh(n^{-3})\right).
\]
Multiplying gives \eqref{mdk:sq:eq:ratio}, including the previously recorded ratio-limit conjecture. Since $a_{n+1}/a_n\sim en$, eventual strict increase follows. Higher ratio corrections are obtained by ordinary formal multiplication of the fixed-order expansion; the uniform remainder follows by choosing one more fixed order than needed.

\subsection{Continuous approximations to the threshold}
Taking logarithms at fixed order gives
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:sq:eq:log-error}
\log(a_n/C)=n\log n+\sum_{j=1}^M\ell_jn^{-j}
+\Oh_M(n^{-M-1}),
\end{equation}
where
\[
\ell_1=c_1,\quad\ell_2=c_2-\tfrac12c_1^2,\quad
\ell_3=c_3-c_1c_2+\tfrac13c_1^3.
\]
Put $L=\log(x/C)$ and let $W_0$ be the positive real branch of Lambert's $W$ function. The leading real inverse is
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:sq:eq:Lambert}
N=\frac{L}{W_0(L)},\qquad N\log N=L.
\end{equation}
For large $x$ this is unambiguous. If $D_N=1+\log N$, a Taylor expansion of $g_1(N+\Delta)=L$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:sq:eq:first-inverse}
t_1(x)=N-\frac{c_1}{ND_N}
+\Oh\left(\frac{N^{-3}}{D_N^2}\right).
\end{equation}
For example, the first correction cancels $c_1/N$, and its remaining residual is $\Oh(N^{-3}/D_N)$; division by the derivative, which is comparable to $D_N$, proves the displayed error.

For any fixed $M$, the coefficients $\ell_1,\ldots,\ell_M$ produce $t_M$ by solving a monotone scalar equation. Alternatively, substitute $t=N+\Delta$ into
\[
(N+\Delta)\log(N+\Delta)
+\sum_{j=1}^M\ell_j(N+\Delta)^{-j}=N\log N
\]
and determine successive coefficients of $\Delta$ in powers of $N^{-1}$, whose coefficients are rational functions of $D_N$. The nonzero leading derivative $D_N$ makes this a finite recursive operation at every fixed order. Thus the coefficient algorithm also supplies arbitrary fixed-order continuous inverse expansions.

\subsection{Proof of the rounded bracket}
For fixed $M$, equation~\eqref{mdk:sq:eq:log-error} supplies constants $D_M,n_M$ with
\[
|\log(a_n/C)-g_M(n)|\le D_Mn^{-M-1}\quad(n\ge n_M).
\]
Moreover
\[
g_M'(t)=1+\log t+\Oh_M(t^{-2})>0
\]
for large $t$. There is therefore a unique sufficiently large solution $t_M(x)$. In a fixed relative neighborhood of $t_M$, the mean value theorem turns the logarithmic error into a displacement bounded by a constant times
$t_M^{-M-1}/(1+\log t_M)$. Increase the constant $A_M$ so that every sufficiently large integer below $t_M-\eta_M$ has $a_n<x$, while every sufficiently large integer at or above $t_M+\eta_M$ has $a_n\ge x$. Outside that relative neighborhood, monotonicity of $g_M$ gives a much larger gap than the error, so the same conclusions hold. Finally, $x$ eventually exceeds every term in the finite initial segment.

The least permissible integer is consequently bounded by the two ceilings in \eqref{mdk:sq:eq:bracket}. This argument includes $x=a_n$. Since $2\eta_M\to0$, the two ceilings eventually differ by at most one. A single ceiling formula is valid only if they agree; otherwise exact comparison with the adjacent sequence values resolves the ambiguity.

Combining \eqref{mdk:sq:eq:first-inverse} with the $M=1$ bracket gives the continuous threshold approximation
\[
N-\frac{c_1}{N(1+\log N)}
+\Oh\left(\frac{N^{-2}}{\log N}\right).
\]
A shrinking real error here is not an additive shrinking error for the integer $\nu(x)$ without the rounding qualification. Nor does this report furnish numerical values of $A_M,D_M,n_M$ sufficient to certify a finite-input inverse algorithm. Those are separate issues from the proved asymptotic bracket.

\begin{remark}[\wtag, 6 October 2026: two-sided bounds]\label{mdk:sq:rem:book-bounds}
For all integers $h,w\ge1$, with $A(h,w)$ the cylinder count of Part~I (so that $a_n=A(n,n)$),
\[
2(h+1)^w\le A(h,w)\le2^h(h+1)^w .
\]
Equality holds in the upper bound if and only if $h=1$ or $w=1$, and in the lower bound if and only if $h=1$. In particular $2(n+1)^n\le a_n$ for every $n\ge1$, and $a_n<2^n(n+1)^n$ for every $n\ge2$, while $a_1=4=2^1(1+1)^1$.
\end{remark}
\begin{proof}
Fix $b\in\{0,1\}^h$ and $w\ge1$. By \eqref{mdk:sq:eq:gram} (Part~I, \eqref{mdk:asp:eq:gram}), $\tr(M_b^w)=\tr((C_bC_b^{\mathsf T})^w)=\sum_ix_i^w$, where $x_1,\dots,x_r\ge0$ are the eigenvalues of the positive semidefinite matrix $C_bC_b^{\mathsf T}$. Their sum is $\tr(C_bC_b^{\mathsf T})$, the number of entries equal to one of the zero-one matrix $C_b$, which is $h+1$: the edges of the tree $T_b$ correspond to the $h+1$ path vertices (Section~\ref{mdk:sq:sec:tree}; Part~I, Appendix~\ref{mdk:asp:sec:structure}). For nonnegative reals and $w\ge1$, $\sum_ix_i^w\le(\sum_ix_i)^w$, and for $w\ge2$ equality holds if and only if at most one $x_i$ is nonzero, because the expansion of $(\sum_ix_i)^w$ contains, besides $\sum_ix_i^w$, the nonnegative terms $wx_i^{w-1}x_j$ for $i\ne j$. Hence $\tr(M_b^w)\le(h+1)^w$. For $w=1$ equality holds for every $b$. For $w\ge2$ it holds if and only if $C_b$ has rank one ($C_b\ne0$), which by Part~I, Appendix~\ref{mdk:asp:sec:structure} (a connected zero-one rank-one incidence graph is complete bipartite, and a complete bipartite tree is a star), equivalently by Part~III, Lemma~\ref{mdk:pl:lem:dominant}, happens exactly when $b$ is constant. Summing over the $2^h$ words gives the upper bound, with equality if and only if $w=1$ or every word is constant, that is $h=1$. For the lower bound, the two constant words contribute $(h+1)^w$ each, and every other word contributes $\tr(M_b^w)\ge(\max_ix_i)^w>0$; nonconstant words exist exactly when $h\ge2$. Taking $h=w=n$ gives the statements about $a_n$.
\end{proof}

The proof treats every fixed expansion order, but it does not control $c_j$ as $j\to\infty$. The sizes of $c_1,c_2,c_3$ alone do not settle these questions.

\paragraph{Effective inverse constants.}

\paragraph{Rectangular cylinders and a possible change of regime.}
For a labeled $2h\times2w$ horizontal cylinder the maximum is $hw$ kings. The same block geometry gives the exact count
\[
a_{h,w}=\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^h}\tr(M_b^w),
\]
where $M_b$ has size $h+1$ and is defined by the same substring rule. This exact identity follows from the same encoding; the square asymptotic theorem does not automatically extend to all aspect ratios.

\section{Scope and main conclusions}\label{mdk:pl:sec:scope}
\subsection{The counting convention}
Throughout, $h,w$ are positive integers. Squares of the ordinary rectangular board are distinguished, kings are indistinguishable, and a placement is a subset of squares. There is no periodic boundary identification and no quotient by rotations, reflections, or translations. Nonattacking means that the Chebyshev distance between any two occupied squares exceeds one. Partitioning the board into $hw$ disjoint $2\times2$ cells proves that at most $hw$ kings fit; this maximum is attained by taking the even-coordinate square in every cell.

We study exactly these maximum placements, with $h$ fixed and $w$ varying:
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:pl:eq:gf}
 F_h(z)=\sum_{w\ge1}P_h(w)z^w.
\end{equation}
All denominator statements refer to the reduced scalar rational function over $\Q$, with denominator normalized to have constant coefficient one. They do not refer to the full characteristic polynomial of a transfer matrix. The distinction matters because scalar boundary cancellation actually occurs.

\begin{theorem}\label{mdk:pl:thm:main}
For every $h\ge1$, $F_h$ is rational over $\Q$. If an irreducible polynomial of degree $d$ occurs in its reduced denominator with exponent $e$, then
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:pl:eq:main}
 d\le\floor{h/2}+1,\qquad 1\le e\le h-2d+3.
\end{equation}
Every finite pole is positive real. The same upper bounds hold for every reduced entry-denominator of the transfer resolvent constructed in Section~\ref{mdk:pl:sec:transfer}, and for every fixed rational scalar boundary pairing of that resolvent.
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}\label{mdk:pl:cor:parity}
If $h=2r$, every occurring irreducible denominator factor of degree $r+1$ has exponent one. If $h=2r+1$, every occurring factor of degree $r+1$ has exponent at most two.
\end{corollary}

The even-height conclusion proves the assertion recorded as Conjecture 4 in Kot\v e\v sovec~\cite[p.~83]{kotesovec}. The degree bound establishes only the upper-bound direction of the maximum-degree assertion recorded there as Conjecture 2. It does not prove that a factor of degree $\floor{h/2}+1$ survives in the planar denominator at every height. Nor does it settle the general low-degree multiplicity assertion on that page.

\subsection{Attribution and historical limits}
The subset-and-height encoding is present in Knuth's February 1994 notes~\cite{knuth}. Wilf~\cite[Lemmas 2--3]{wilf} gives the binary-word block upper triangular transfer and the scalar sum-of-entries generating function. Brown's 2021 preprint and 2025 published paper~\cite{brown} give a subset-and-height recursion whose Corollary 1 explicitly covers rectangles. These constructions are prior work. The component-incidence Gram method is also reused from companion cylindrical analysis; no first-priority claim for that machinery is made.

The mathematical contribution developed here is the degree-sensitive all-pole estimate from the smaller Gram dimension and strict Boolean ranks. A bounded prior-work search did not recover Alekseyev's full 2011 factor-generation derivation, which is credited in Kot\v e\v sovec's discussion. Thus this report proves a published assertion without assuming that the assertion remains open today, and without claiming the first proof in the literature. Wilf's leading fixed-height asymptotic, partial fraction extraction, and Lambert $W$ inversion are explicitly treated as prior or standard consequences.

\section{Geometry and the full transfer}\label{mdk:pl:sec:transfer}
\subsection{One king in every cell}
Index physical rows and columns from zero. In cell row $i$ and cell column $j$, write the occupied square as
\[
 (2i+a_{i,j},\,2j+b_{i,j}),\qquad a_{i,j},b_{i,j}\in\{0,1\}.
\]
In a fixed pair of physical columns, kings in adjacent cell rows conflict exactly when the upper vertical offset is one and the lower offset is zero: their row distance is then one, while their column distance is at most one. Consequently, the vertical offsets are nondecreasing. They have a unique threshold $u\in\{0,\ldots,h\}$:
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:pl:eq:threshold}
 a_i(u)=\begin{cases}0,&i<u,\\1,&i\ge u.\end{cases}
\end{equation}
A column-pair state is therefore $(b,u)$, where $b\in\{0,1\}^h$ and $0\le u\le h$. Every such state is valid, so the state count is
\[
 N=(h+1)2^h.
\]

\subsection{The exact adjacency rule}
Let $(b,u)$ and $(c,v)$ be states in successive column pairs. The kings in the same cell row force $b_i\le c_i$ for all $i$: otherwise their horizontal distance is one and their vertical distance is at most one. Once $b\le c$ coordinatewise, only attacks between adjacent cell rows remain.

For an upper-left and lower-right pair, the vertical distance can be one exactly when $i\ge u$ and $i+1<v$, and the horizontal distance can be one exactly when $b_i=1$ and $c_{i+1}=0$. For a lower-left and upper-right pair the conditions are $i+1<u$, $i\ge v$, $b_{i+1}=1$, and $c_i=0$. It follows that the zero-one transfer is
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:pl:eq:transfer}
 T_{(b,u),(c,v)}=1
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \begin{cases}
 b\le c,\ \text{and}\\
 (b_i,c_{i+1})\ne (1,0)\ \ (u\le i\le v-2),&u<v,\\
 (b_{i+1},c_i)\ne (1,0)\ \ (v\le i\le u-2),&u>v,\\
 \text{no further condition},&u=v.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Empty index intervals impose no condition.

The preceding tests are sufficient as well as necessary. Kings separated by at least two cell rows or two cell columns have a physical coordinate distance at least three. There are no other possible attacks.

\begin{lemma}\label{mdk:pl:lem:count}
For $w\ge1$, with $\one$ the all-ones vector of length $N$,
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:pl:eq:count}
 P_h(w)=\one^{\mathsf T}T^{w-1}\one,
 \qquad
 F_h(z)=z\one^{\mathsf T}(I-zT)^{-1}\one.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
A valid placement is uniquely a sequence of $w$ valid column-pair states with each successive pair allowed by $T$. Both boundary states are unrestricted. Summing these paths gives the first identity, and summing the matrix geometric series gives the second. In particular $P_h(1)=(h+1)2^h$. No empty-board convention at $w=0$ is needed.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Diagonal blocks and Boolean rank}
Order binary words by nondecreasing Hamming weight $|b|$, breaking ties arbitrarily. The transfer is block upper triangular because $b\le c$, and distinct words of equal weight cannot have an allowed transition. The diagonal block belonging to $b$ has size $h+1$ and entries
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:pl:eq:M}
 (M_b)_{u,v}=\begin{cases}
 1,&u<v\text{ and }b_u,\ldots,b_{v-1}\text{ is nondecreasing},\\
 1,&u>v\text{ and }b_v,\ldots,b_{u-1}\text{ is nonincreasing},\\
 1,&u=v,\\
 0,&\text{otherwise}.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
The strict off-diagonal blocks are essential for the planar problem. Replacing the scalar boundary expression in~\eqref{mdk:pl:eq:count} by a sum of block traces would count a different, periodic model.

\section{The smaller Gram matrix}\label{mdk:pl:sec:gram}
\subsection{Monochromatic components}
For a fixed word $b$, consider the path on vertices $0,1,\ldots,h$ whose edge $i$ joins $i$ to $i+1$ and has color $b_i$. For $k\in\{0,1\}$ retain just the color-$k$ edges, and let $X_k$ be the vertex-versus-component incidence matrix of that forest. Every row of $X_k$ contains exactly one entry equal to one. Put
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:pl:eq:EC}
 E_k=X_kX_k^{\mathsf T},\qquad C=X_0^{\mathsf T}X_1.
\end{equation}
A zero component and a one component intersect in at most one vertex: if they shared two vertices, the unique connecting path would have to consist entirely of both colors. Thus $C$ is a zero-one matrix.

\begin{lemma}\label{mdk:pl:lem:gram}
If $q=|b|$, then
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:pl:eq:factorization}
 M_b=E_0E_1=X_0CX_1^{\mathsf T},\qquad
 C\text{ has size }(q+1)\times(h-q+1).
\end{equation}
There are rectangular integer matrices $U,V$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:pl:eq:UV}
 UV=M_b,\qquad VU=G_b,
\end{equation}
where $G_b$ is symmetric positive semidefinite of dimension
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:pl:eq:dimension}
 r_b=\min(q+1,h-q+1).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The $(u,v)$ entry of $E_0E_1$ counts vertices $t$ in the zero component of $u$ and the one component of $v$. There is at most one such pivot. When $u<v$, it exists exactly if the intervening edge sequence consists of zeros followed by ones. When $u>v$, reading from $v$ to $u$ gives ones followed by zeros. For $u=v$, the unique pivot is $u$. These are precisely~\eqref{mdk:pl:eq:M}.

A path forest with $h+1$ vertices and $h-q$ zero edges has $q+1$ components; the one-edge forest has $h-q+1$. Finally the two choices
\begin{align*}
 U=X_0,\quad V=CX_1^{\mathsf T}&\quad\Longrightarrow\quad VU=CC^{\mathsf T},\\
 U=X_0C,\quad V=X_1^{\mathsf T}&\quad\Longrightarrow\quad VU=C^{\mathsf T}C
\end{align*}
give the claimed factorization. Choose whichever Gram matrix is smaller.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Simple finite poles of each diagonal resolvent}
For rectangular $U,V$, the identity
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:pl:eq:resolventUV}
 (I-zUV)^{-1}=I+zU(I-zVU)^{-1}V
\end{equation}
follows by direct multiplication wherever the inverses exist, and therefore as a rational matrix identity. Applying it to Lemma~\ref{mdk:pl:lem:gram} gives the decisive local information.

\begin{lemma}\label{mdk:pl:lem:localpole}
Every finite pole of $(I-zM_b)^{-1}$ is simple at worst and is the reciprocal of a positive eigenvalue $\rho$ of $G_b$. If $d=[\Q(\rho):\Q]$, then
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:pl:eq:rankinterval}
 d\le\min(q+1,h-q+1),\qquad d-1\le q\le h-d+1.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The real symmetric matrix $G_b$ is diagonalizable and has nonnegative eigenvalues. Its resolvent consequently has only simple finite poles, even when an eigenvalue is repeated. Nonzero eigenvalues are positive. Identity~\eqref{mdk:pl:eq:resolventUV} transfers these properties to the finite poles of the diagonal-block resolvent. The minimal polynomial of $\rho$ divides the characteristic polynomial of the integer matrix $G_b$, whose degree is $r_b$. This proves~\eqref{mdk:pl:eq:rankinterval}.
\end{proof}

One must not substitute a determinant argument for the simple-pole argument: a repeated eigenvalue can give a repeated determinant factor without giving a higher-order resolvent pole. Nor is it necessary to claim that $M_b$ is diagonalizable at zero. A zero-eigenvalue Jordan block contributes a polynomial in $z$ to its resolvent and does not produce a finite pole.

\section{Strict Boolean chains control multiplicity}\label{mdk:pl:sec:chains}
Write
\[
 T=D+K,\qquad D=\diag_b(M_b),\qquad R(z)=(I-zD)^{-1}.
\]
A nonzero block $K_{b,c}$ requires $b<c$ coordinatewise, hence $|b|<|c|$. A product with more than $h$ such steps vanishes even with arbitrary block diagonal factors inserted. Factoring $I-zT=(I-zD)(I-zRK)$, or multiplying directly, gives the finite expansion
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:pl:eq:chainexpansion}
 (I-zT)^{-1}=R\sum_{k=0}^{h}(zKR)^k.
\end{equation}
Each block is a finite sum of products of the form
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:pl:eq:chainterm}
 R_{b_0}(z)\,zK_{b_0,b_1}\,R_{b_1}(z)\cdots
 zK_{b_{k-1},b_k}\,R_{b_k}(z),
 \qquad b_0<b_1<\cdots<b_k,
\end{equation}
where $R_b(z)=(I-zM_b)^{-1}$.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{mdk:pl:thm:main}]
Rationality follows from~\eqref{mdk:pl:eq:count}. Let $\zeta$ be a finite pole of an entry of the full resolvent. It is nonzero, since the matrix is analytic at zero. At least one diagonal resolvent in~\eqref{mdk:pl:eq:chainexpansion} must have a pole at $\zeta$. Thus $\rho=1/\zeta$ is a positive Gram eigenvalue. Taking a nonzero reciprocal preserves algebraic degree, so put $d=[\Q(\rho):\Q]$.

By Lemma~\ref{mdk:pl:lem:localpole}, a diagonal factor in~\eqref{mdk:pl:eq:chainterm} can be singular at $\zeta$ only at ranks
\[
 d-1,d,\ldots,h-d+1.
\]
A strict Boolean chain visits each rank at most once. There are $h-2d+3$ ranks in this interval, each giving at most a simple pole. Products add pole orders at worst, and finite sums have order at most the largest summand order. Therefore every entry has pole order at most $h-2d+3$. The interval is nonempty only if $d\le\floor{h/2}+1$.

Fixed rational boundary vectors and the factor $z$ in~\eqref{mdk:pl:eq:count} cannot increase these orders or create new poles. Over $\Q$, irreducible polynomials are separable. In a reduced rational function, the exponent of an irreducible denominator factor equals the pole order at each of its roots. This proves~\eqref{mdk:pl:eq:main} and the entrywise assertion.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Corollary~\ref{mdk:pl:cor:parity}]
For $h=2r$, $d=r+1$ permits only rank $r$, so the order is at most one and an occurring factor has exponent exactly one. For $h=2r+1$, $d=r+1$ permits ranks $r$ and $r+1$, so the order is at most two.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Why degree attainment is a separate question}
The diagonal block spectrum need not survive the planar scalar pairing. For $h=3$, some block Gram matrices have eigenvalue one, but the reduced planar denominator is
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:pl:eq:h3den}
 (1-4z)^2(1-3z)(1-4z+2z^2)^2.
\end{equation}
Thus the pole at $z=1$ cancels from $F_3$, even though it occurs in individual full-resolvent entries. This directly prevents an argument that simply transfers cylindrical positive trace weights to the planar boundary expression.

For degree one the general estimate gives $e\le h+1$. It is deliberately an all-entry bound and is not the conjectured stronger global bound for the scalar low-degree factors. Sharpening it requires information beyond the number of allowable ranks.

\begin{remark}[\wtag, 6 October 2026: the dominant linear factor]\label{mdk:pl:rem:linear-exponent}
For every $h\ge1$ the factor $1-(h+1)z$ occurs in the reduced denominator of $F_h$ with exponent exactly two. In particular, at $h=1$ and $h=2$ some degree-one factor has exponent two, so a bound of value $\lfloor(h+1)/2\rfloor=1$ on the exponents of degree-one factors would fail there; whether Conjecture~3 bounds exactly these exponents could not be checked without the page.

\begin{proof}

So the pole order at $1/n$ is exactly two, and, as in the proof of Theorem~\ref{mdk:pl:thm:main}, the exponent of the irreducible factor $1-nz$ in the reduced denominator equals this pole order.
\end{proof}

\section{Exact exponential polynomial formulas at fixed height}\label{mdk:pl:sec:exppoly}
All asymptotic statements from this point onward hold with $h$ fixed. Constants may depend on $h$. No estimate is uniform as $h$ grows with $w$, and no square-board asymptotic follows from these statements.

Let $S_h$ be the reciprocals of the actual finite poles of the reduced $F_h$. Every element $\rho\in S_h$ is positive. Write $d_\rho=[\Q(\rho):\Q]$ and let $e_\rho$ be its pole order.

\begin{theorem}\label{mdk:pl:thm:exp-poly}
There are a polynomial $H_h(z)$ and polynomials $p_\rho(x)\in\Q(\rho)[x]$ such that, for every integer $w\ge1$ exceeding $\deg H_h$,
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:pl:eq:exp-poly}
 P_h(w)=\sum_{\rho\in S_h}p_\rho(w)\rho^w,
 \qquad
 \deg p_\rho=e_\rho-1\le h-2d_\rho+2.
\end{equation}
If $H_h=0$, the identity holds for every $w\ge1$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Partial fractions over the real algebraic numbers give
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:pl:eq:partialfractions}
 F_h(z)=H_h(z)+\sum_{\rho\in S_h}\sum_{j=1}^{e_\rho}
 \frac{A_{\rho,j}}{(1-\rho z)^j}.
\end{equation}
The coefficients belong to $\Q(\rho)$, as can be seen by expanding the rational function locally at $1/\rho$. The top coefficient $A_{\rho,e_\rho}$ is nonzero at an actual pole. Since
\[
 [z^w](1-\rho z)^{-j}=\binom{w+j-1}{j-1}\rho^w,
\]
we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:pl:eq:polynomialp}
 p_\rho(w)=\sum_{j=1}^{e_\rho}A_{\rho,j}\binom{w+j-1}{j-1}.
\end{equation}
It has degree exactly $e_\rho-1$, and Theorem~\ref{mdk:pl:thm:main} gives the bound. The polynomial part affects only a finite prefix.
\end{proof}

The restriction to actual scalar poles accounts for all cancellations before the asymptotic formula is formed. The degree-sensitive polynomial bound is the direct new consequence of the pole theorem. The partial fraction method itself is standard.

\section{The positive linear dominant term}\label{mdk:pl:sec:dominant}
Wilf~\cite{wilf} proves a positive linear prefactor at the dominant base $h+1$. We give a direct proof in the present Gram formulation, both to connect the results and to make the inverse analysis self-contained.

\begin{lemma}\label{mdk:pl:lem:dominant}
Every nonzero eigenvalue of the full transfer is positive and at most $n=h+1$. Only the two constant words $0^h$ and $1^h$ contribute the eigenvalue $n$; each contributes it once. All remaining nonzero eigenvalues are strictly smaller than $n$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Each path vertex determines its pair of zero and one components, and two such pairs cannot coincide for different vertices. Hence $C_b$ has exactly $h+1$ entries equal to one and
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:pl:eq:trace}
 \tr G_b=\|C_b\|_F^2=h+1=n.
\end{equation}
Its nonnegative eigenvalues are each at most this trace. Equality requires $\rank C_b=1$.

For a nonconstant word, choose two consecutive edges at a color change. The three incident vertices give, up to row and column permutation, a $2\times2$ submatrix
\[
 \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}
\]
of $C_b$. The missing intersection cannot appear elsewhere because the components are intervals of the path. Thus $\rank C_b\ge2$ and its largest eigenvalue is strictly below $n$. For either constant word $M_b=J$, where $J$ is the $n\times n$ all-ones matrix; its only nonzero eigenvalue is $n$, simple. Block triangularity completes the proof.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Wilf's leading form]\label{mdk:pl:thm:leading}
There are rational constants $\alpha_h\ge1$ and $\beta_h$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:pl:eq:leading}
 P_h(w)=(\alpha_h w+\beta_h)(h+1)^w+R_h(w).
\end{equation}
If subdominant scalar poles exist and
\[
 \theta_h=\max(S_h\setminus\{h+1\}),
\]
then $0<\theta_h<h+1$ and
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:pl:eq:remainder}
 R_h(w)=O_h(w^h\theta_h^w).
\end{equation}
Otherwise $R_h(w)=0$ for all sufficiently large integer $w$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Only the two constant-word diagonal factors can be singular at $z=1/n$, so the full scalar pole has order at most two. Put $\Pi=J/n$; then
\[
 \lim_{z\to1/n}(1-nz)(I-zJ)^{-1}=\Pi.
\]
The direct off-diagonal block from $0^h$ to $1^h$ is also $J$. The corresponding contribution to $F_h$ in the Boolean-path expansion is
\[
 z^2\one^{\mathsf T}(I-zJ)^{-1}J(I-zJ)^{-1}\one.
\]
Multiplying by $(1-nz)^2$ and taking $z\uparrow1/n$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:pl:eq:positivecontribution}
 n^{-2}\one^{\mathsf T}\Pi J\Pi\one=1.
\end{equation}
All other double-pole path contributions have nonnegative limits: intermediate block resolvents are analytic and entrywise nonnegative at $1/n$, because their spectral radii are smaller than $n$; the endpoint residues are $\Pi$; and the connecting blocks are nonnegative. Terms with fewer than two singular factors have zero scaled limit. Consequently
\[
 A=\lim_{z\to1/n}(1-nz)^2F_h(z)\ge1.
\]
The dominant partial fractions are $A/(1-nz)^2+B/(1-nz)$, so $\alpha_h=A\ge1$ and $\beta_h=A+B$. These numbers are rational since $F_h\in\Q(z)$ and $1/n\in\Q$.

All remaining terms in~\eqref{mdk:pl:eq:exp-poly} have positive bases below $n$ and polynomial degree at most $h$, proving~\eqref{mdk:pl:eq:remainder}. The possible polynomial part only affects finitely many widths.
\end{proof}

For any $\theta_h<\eta<h+1$, the polynomial factor can be absorbed to give $R_h(w)=O_{h,\eta}(\eta^w)$, the familiar exponential-error form. Using the exact subdominant maximum retains the factor $w^h$.

\section{Lambert W and integer thresholds}\label{mdk:pl:sec:lambert}
Fix $h$ and abbreviate
\[
 n=h+1,\qquad b=\log n,\qquad \alpha=\alpha_h,\qquad\beta=\beta_h.
\]
Consider the dominant real function $g(x)=(\alpha x+\beta)n^x$. On its increasing positive branch, its inverse at $y>0$ is
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:pl:eq:W}
 w_0(y)=\frac1b W_0\!\left(\frac{by}{\alpha}
                  e^{b\beta/\alpha}\right)-\frac\beta\alpha,
\end{equation}
where $W_0$ is the principal real Lambert function. Indeed, setting $t=b(x+\beta/\alpha)$ transforms $g(x)=y$ into
$t e^t=(by/\alpha)e^{b\beta/\alpha}$. This is a standard inversion, not a priority claim.

\begin{theorem}\label{mdk:pl:thm:inverse}
Suppose subdominant scalar poles exist, and set $r=\theta_h/n\in(0,1)$. Extend the finite sum in~\eqref{mdk:pl:eq:exp-poly} to real $x$ using its positive bases, and denote it by $f_h(x)$. It is eventually strictly increasing. Its eventual real inverse $x_h(y)$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:pl:eq:realinverse}
 x_h(y)=w_0(y)+O_h\!\left(w_0(y)^{h-1}r^{w_0(y)}\right).
\end{equation}
For the integer threshold
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:pl:eq:thresholdN}
 N_h(y)=\min\{w\ge1:P_h(w)\ge y\},
\end{equation}
there exist $C_h,Y_h>0$ such that for $y\ge Y_h$, with
\[
 \varepsilon_h(y)=C_h w_0(y)^{h-1}r^{w_0(y)},
\]
one has
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:pl:eq:ceilbracket}
 \ceil{w_0(y)-\varepsilon_h(y)}\le N_h(y)
 \le\ceil{w_0(y)+\varepsilon_h(y)}.
\end{equation}
If there are no subdominant scalar poles, then for all sufficiently large $y$,
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:pl:eq:nosub}
 N_h(y)=\ceil{w_0(y)}.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The finite exponential-polynomial formula and its derivative give, for large real $x$,
\begin{align*}
 f_h(x)&=g(x)+O_h(x^h\theta_h^x),\\
 f_h'(x)&=g'(x)+O_h(x^h\theta_h^x),\\
 g'(x)&=n^x[\alpha+b(\alpha x+\beta)]\sim\alpha b x n^x.
\end{align*}
Hence $f_h'(x)>0$ eventually. Write $w_0=w_0(y)$. The dominant differences $g(w_0\pm1)-y$ have size comparable to $w_0n^{w_0}$, with opposite signs, whereas the remainders are smaller by an exponential factor. Thus $x_h(y)\in(w_0-1,w_0+1)$ for large $y$. On that interval the derivative is bounded below by a positive constant times $w_0n^{w_0}$. The mean value theorem applied between $w_0$ and $x_h(y)$ gives~\eqref{mdk:pl:eq:realinverse}.

Every valid width-$w$ arrangement has $n$ distinct extensions obtained by appending the word $1^h$ with any threshold. The transfer conditions allow all these choices. Therefore
\[
 P_h(w+1)\ge nP_h(w)>P_h(w),
\]
so the threshold exists and the sequence is strictly increasing. For sufficiently large $y$, the finitely many exceptional prefix values and the initial nonmonotone portion of the real extension are below the relevant threshold. The exact eventual integer formula then yields
\[
 N_h(y)=\ceil{x_h(y)}.
\]
Applying ceilings to the real inverse bounds proves~\eqref{mdk:pl:eq:ceilbracket}. With no subdominant poles, $f_h=g$ eventually, which gives~\eqref{mdk:pl:eq:nosub}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The first explicit subdominant correction}
Continue to assume that subdominant scalar poles exist. The exact finite formula also permits one more inversion step. In this subsection, define $R(x)=f_h(x)-g(x)$ using the real exponential-polynomial extension, rather than a bound for an unspecified remainder. Then
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:pl:eq:firstcorrection}
 x_h(y)=w_0(y)-\frac{R(w_0(y))}{g'(w_0(y))}
 +O_h\!\left(w_0(y)^{2h-2}r^{2w_0(y)}\right).
\end{equation}
To verify this, put $t=w_0(y)$ and $\delta=x_h(y)-t$. The preceding theorem gives $\delta=O_h(t^{h-1}r^t)$. Taylor's theorem on $[t-1,t+1]$ yields
\[
 0=g'(t)\delta+R(t)
   +O_h(tn^t\delta^2)+O_h(t^h\theta_h^t|\delta|).
\]
After division by $g'(t)\asymp_h tn^t$, both error terms are
$O_h(t^{2h-2}r^{2t})$, proving~\eqref{mdk:pl:eq:firstcorrection}. Thus the first exponentially small correction is explicitly determined by the subdominant sectors of the exact partial fraction formula. It remains a fixed-height real-inverse estimate and does not remove the rounding issue below.

The real interval before ceilings has exponentially small width $2\varepsilon_h(y)$. Once that width is below one, the integer bracket has width zero or one. Its endpoints agree whenever $w_0(y)$ is farther than $\varepsilon_h(y)$ from every integer. Near an integer boundary, the unconditional replacement $N_h(y)=\ceil{w_0(y)}$ is not justified.

The constants in this asymptotic statement are existence constants. A certified answer for a specified finite $y$ needs explicit remainder and derivative bounds, or exact evaluation at the remaining candidate widths. Big-$O$ notation alone is not a finite numerical certificate. For $h=1$, there is no subdominant term and, for $y\ge4$,
\[
 N_1(y)=\ceil{\frac{W_0(2y\log2)}{\log2}-1}.
\]

\subsection{The all-width recurrence certificate}
A fitted prefix alone does not prove a generating function. The certificate used here supplies a finite exact reason that the recurrence holds for all subsequent indices. Put
\[
 s_m=\one^{\mathsf T}T^m\one\quad(m\ge0),\qquad
 Q(z)=\sum_{i=0}^{D}q_i z^i,\quad q_0=1,
\]
and choose a recorded integer $L\ge D$. The residual at index $L+j$ is
\begin{equation}\label{mdk:pl:eq:CHresidual}
 \sum_{i=0}^{D}q_i s_{L+j-i}
 =\one^{\mathsf T}T^j\left(\sum_{i=0}^{D}q_i T^{L-i}\right)\one.
\end{equation}
For an $N$-state transfer, the certificate checks that~\eqref{mdk:pl:eq:CHresidual} vanishes exactly for $j=0,\ldots,N-1$. Cayley--Hamilton implies that the sequence of these residuals satisfies the characteristic-polynomial recurrence of $T$. The $N$ consecutive zeros therefore propagate forward to every $j\ge0$.

The numerator is obtained by convolving $Q$ with the coefficients of $\sum_{m\ge0}s_mz^m$ at indices below $L$, and then multiplying by $z$ to obtain $F_h$. A rational polynomial gcd check certifies that numerator and denominator are coprime. One must retain the distinction between $L$ and $D$: zero-eigenvalue transients may require $L>D$, and Cayley--Hamilton does not justify propagation backward across them.

\section{Remaining questions}\label{mdk:pl:sec:remaining}
The theorem leaves several concrete problems.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Maximum-degree attainment.} Does an irreducible factor of degree $\floor{h/2}+1$ survive the reduced planar boundary pairing for every $h$? The present argument proves only the upper bound.
\item \textbf{Lower-degree multiplicities.} The rank interval allows $h-2d+3$ singular visits. Which visits can actually combine into nonzero scalar highest-order residues, and what stronger bounds hold at small $d$?
\item \textbf{Cancellation criteria.} Can one characterize algebraically or combinatorially when a Gram eigenvalue disappears from $F_h$, as eigenvalue one does at $h=3$?
\item \textbf{Height-uniform analysis.} What control on residues, pole separation, and spectral gaps would permit $h$ to grow with $w$? Fixed-height exponential errors give no such control by themselves.
\item \textbf{Effective inversion.} Can explicit manageable remainder bounds turn the two-ceiling bracket into a practical certificate at a prescribed large $y$, avoiding full transfer evaluation?
\item \textbf{Historical overlap.} Recovering the complete 2011 Alekseyev factor-generation argument remains necessary before any stronger priority statement is defensible.
\end{enumerate}
The proof of the stated all-height inequalities is complete independently of these questions. The unresolved issues concern sharpness, refinement, uniformity, effectivity, and historical attribution.
