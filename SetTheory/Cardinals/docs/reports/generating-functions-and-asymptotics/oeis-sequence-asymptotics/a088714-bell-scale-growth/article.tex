\begin{abstract}
Let $A(x)=\sum_{n\ge0}a_nx^n$ be the unique formal power series satisfying
$A(x)=1+xA(x)^2A(xA(x))$. Its coefficients form OEIS A088714.
We prove
\[
 \log a_n=n\log n-n\log\log n-n
       +O\!\left(\frac{n\log\log n}{\log n}\right),
 \qquad
 a_n^{1/n}\sim\frac{n}{e\log n}.
\]
The proof is based on an exact triangular recurrence obtained from
composition iterates. A simple path through that recurrence supplies the
lower bound. The upper bound comes from a coefficientwise inequality for
shifted Poisson moments, proved here by a positivity argument involving
Stirling numbers. More explicitly, for every $n\ge1$,
\[
 \max_{1\le h\le n+1}h^{\,n+1-h}
 \ \le\ a_n\ \le\
 \inf_{\lambda>0}\left(1+\frac2\lambda\right)^n T_n'(\lambda),
\]
where $T_n$ is the $n$th Touchard polynomial.
Consequently the ordinary generating function has radius zero, whereas
the exponential generating function is entire. Exact coefficient
computations accompany the proof. They suggest a considerably sharper
Bell-number comparison, but that comparison and its multiplicative
constant are not claimed as established results.

\emph{Part~II} (added October 1, 2026) realizes A088714 and its companion
A088713 as the moment sequences of unique probability measures $\mu,\nu$
on $[0,\infty)$, proves strict positivity of all Hankel minors, exact
Stieltjes-transform equations, golden-ratio endpoint laws
$F(s)\sim s^{-(3-\sqrt5)/2}$ and $G(s)\sim s^{-(\sqrt5-1)/2}$ with leading
constant one, the ratio laws
$a_{n+1}/a_n\sim c_{n+1}/c_n\sim n/\log n$, and logarithmic far-tail laws.
It uses the theorem of Part~I and does not settle the finer conjecture.
\end{abstract}

\part{Bell-scale growth}

\section{The problem and the precise result}

The OEIS entry A088714 defines the sequence through the formal functional
equation \cite{oeis-a088714}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:def}
 A(x)=1+xA(x)^2A(xA(x)),\qquad A(0)=1.
\end{equation}
Its beginning is
\[
1,\ 1,\ 3,\ 13,\ 69,\ 419,\ 2809,\ 20353,\ 157199,\ 1281993,\ 10963825,\ldots.
\]
The index convention throughout is $a_0=1$, $a_1=1$, $a_2=3$.
All logarithms are natural. Formal composition is legitimate because the
series substituted into $A$ has zero constant term.

The composition in \eqref{eq:def} makes this a different problem from
ordinary algebraic generating-function asymptotics. In fact, the answer
rules out a positive radius of convergence. One must not assume that the
formal identity initially defines a holomorphic function near the origin.

\begin{theorem}[Main asymptotic]\label{thm:main}
For the coefficients of \eqref{eq:def},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mainlog}
 \log a_n=n\log n-n\log\log n-n
       +O\!\left(\frac{n\log\log n}{\log n}\right).
\end{equation}
Equivalently, at the level of $n$th-root asymptotics,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:root}
 \boxed{\displaystyle
 a_n^{1/n}=\frac{n}{e\log n}
       \left(1+O\!\left(\frac{\log\log n}{\log n}\right)\right),
 \qquad
 \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{e\log n}{n}\,a_n^{1/n}=1.}
\end{equation}
In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:expform}
 a_n=\left(\frac{n}{e\log n}\right)^n
        \exp\!\left(O\!\left(\frac{n\log\log n}{\log n}\right)\right).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

The $-n$ in \eqref{eq:mainlog} is important: it identifies the constant
$1/e$ in \eqref{eq:root}. The weaker assertion $\log a_n\sim n\log n$
would miss both the logarithmic denominator and this constant.

\paragraph{What the theorem does not say.}
Equation \eqref{eq:expform} is not the multiplicative equivalent
$a_n\sim(n/(e\log n))^n$. The error in its logarithm is $o(n)$, but it
need not tend to zero. Likewise, an $n$th-root equivalent does not by
itself imply an equivalent for $a_n/a_{n-1}$. (2026-10-01: Part~II proves
$a_{n+1}/a_n\sim n/\log n$ through the Stieltjes-moment representation
and log-convexity, Theorem~\ref{gml:thm:maintail}.) Section~\ref{sec:finer}
discusses a finer numerical pattern without confusing it with the theorem.

A useful feature of the argument is that it gives bounds for every index,
not merely an eventual limiting statement.

\begin{theorem}[Finite-index comparison]\label{thm:finite}
Define the Touchard polynomials by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:touchard}
 T_n(\lambda)=\sum_{j=0}^n\ST{n}{j}\lambda^j,
\end{equation}
where $\ST{n}{j}$ denotes a Stirling number of the second kind, and let
$T_n'(\lambda)$ mean differentiation with respect to $\lambda$.
For every integer $n\ge1$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:finite}
 \boxed{\displaystyle
 \max_{h\in\{1,\ldots,n+1\}}h^{\,n+1-h}
 \ \le\ a_n\ \le\
 \inf_{\lambda>0}\left(1+\frac2\lambda\right)^nT_n'(\lambda).}
\end{equation}
For every $r>0$ and $\lambda>0$ this implies the entirely explicit bound
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cauchyupper}
 a_n\le
 \left(1+\frac2\lambda\right)^n
 \frac{n!}{r^n}(e^r-1)\exp\!\bigl(\lambda(e^r-1)\bigr).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

The remainder of the proof is self-contained. The OEIS supplies the
sequence identification and related formal identities. Standard Bell
number and Lambert $W$ information is used for interpretation and
numerical comparison, not to assume the answer for A088714.

\section{Formal identities and the exact coefficient recurrence}

\subsection{Existence, uniqueness, and direct extraction}

In \eqref{eq:def}, the coefficient of $x^n$ on the right depends only on
$a_0,\ldots,a_{n-1}$. Starting with $a_0=1$ therefore determines the
coefficients successively and proves existence and uniqueness in
$\mathbb Z[[x]]$. The same observation proves their nonnegativity.
Expanding the outer occurrence of $A$ gives the exact identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:directrec}
 a_n=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}a_k\,[x^{n-1-k}]A(x)^{k+2},\qquad n\ge1.
\end{equation}
This recurrence is useful for independently checking computed
coefficients. It also explains the finer heuristic in
Section~\ref{sec:finer}, but it is not the most convenient recurrence for
our rigorous upper bound.

\subsection{Passage to composition iterates}

Put $B(x)=xA(x)$. Equation \eqref{eq:def} becomes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:B}
 B(x)=x+B(x)B(B(x)).
\end{equation}
Since $B(x)=x+O(x^2)$, it has a formal compositional inverse. Substitution
of that inverse into \eqref{eq:B} yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:inverse}
 B^{\langle-1\rangle}(x)=x-xB(x)=x-x^2A(x).
\end{equation}
This is the reversion identity also recorded in the OEIS entry
\cite{oeis-a088714}.

For each integer $k\ge0$, define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Fk}
 F_k(x)=\frac{B^{\circ k}(x)}x,
 \qquad F_0(x)=1,
 \qquad F_1(x)=A(x).
\end{equation}
Here $B^{\circ0}(x)=x$ and $B^{\circ k}$ denotes $k$-fold composition,
not an ordinary power. Composing \eqref{eq:B} with $B^{\circ(k-1)}$ and
dividing by $x$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hierarchy}
 F_k(x)=F_{k-1}(x)+xF_k(x)F_{k+1}(x),\qquad k\ge1.
\end{equation}
The first few equations are the infinite system displayed in the OEIS
entry; the derivation here fixes the indexing and proves the general
identity \cite{oeis-a088714}.

Write
\[
 F_k(x)=\sum_{n\ge0}c_{n,k}x^n.
\]
Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:boundary}
 c_{0,k}=1\quad(k\ge0),\qquad c_{n,0}=0\quad(n\ge1),
 \qquad a_n=c_{n,1}.
\end{equation}
Coefficient extraction in \eqref{eq:hierarchy} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:triangular}
 c_{n,k}-c_{n,k-1}
 =\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}c_{i,k}c_{n-1-i,k+1}.
\end{equation}
Equivalently,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:triangularsum}
 c_{n,k}=\sum_{j=1}^k\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}
                  c_{i,j}c_{n-1-i,j+1}.
\end{equation}
Every term on the right has first index less than $n$. Consequently this
is a genuine triangular recurrence despite the occurrence of $k+1$.
It proves positivity and monotonicity in $k$, and is the main structural
tool of the article.

For illustration, the first three coefficient polynomials are
\begin{align*}
 c_{1,k}&=k,\\
 c_{2,k}&=k^2+2k,\\
 c_{3,k}&=k^3+5k^2+7k.
\end{align*}
Evaluating at $k=1$ gives $1,3,13$ as required. These are not ordinary
Touchard polynomials; the Poisson polynomials introduced later are
comparison objects, not an exact formula for the sequence.

\subsection{Relation to the signed reversion sequence}

For completeness, set
\[
 g(x)=-B^{\langle-1\rangle}(-x)=x+x^2A(-x).
\]
The inverse identity implies
\[
 g(g(x))=(1+x)g(x).
\]
For $n\ge2$ its coefficient of $x^n$ is $(-1)^{n-2}a_{n-2}$.
Thus the signed series in A067145 has the same coefficient magnitudes,
with this explicit index shift \cite{oeis-a067145}. This identity is an
additional verification route, not an analytic-convergence argument.

\section{A lower bound from an increasing-index path}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:powerlower}
For every $n\ge0$ and every integer $k\ge1$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:powerlower}
 c_{n,k}\ge k^n.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The case $n=0$ is immediate. Suppose the statement holds for smaller
first indices. By \eqref{eq:triangularsum},
\begin{align*}
 c_{n,k}
 &\ge\sum_{j=1}^k\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}j^i(j+1)^{n-1-i}\\
 &=\sum_{j=1}^k\bigl((j+1)^n-j^n\bigr)
  =(k+1)^n-1\ge k^n.
\end{align*}
The inner sum is a difference of two $n$th powers, and the outer sum
then telescopes. This completes the induction.
\end{proof}

The $i=0$ summand of \eqref{eq:triangular} and positivity imply
\[
 c_{n,k}\ge c_{n-1,k+1}\qquad(n\ge1).
\]
Iterating this inequality $h-1$ times, for $1\le h\le n+1$, gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pathlower}
 a_n=c_{n,1}\ge c_{n-h+1,h}\ge h^{n-h+1}.
\end{equation}
This proves the lower half of Theorem~\ref{thm:finite}.

\subsection{Optimizing the lower bound}

Let $N=n+1$ and temporarily allow $h$ to be real. The function
\[
 \phi_N(h)=(N-h)\log h
\]
has derivatives
\[
 \phi_N'(h)=\frac Nh-1-\log h,
 \qquad
 \phi_N''(h)=-\frac N{h^2}-\frac1h<0.
\]
Its unique maximum is attained at
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lowerW}
 h_*\bigl(1+\log h_*\bigr)=N,
 \qquad
 h_* =\frac{N}{W(eN)}.
\end{equation}
The positive real branch of Lambert $W$, characterized by
$W(z)e^{W(z)}=z$, is intended \cite{dlmf-w}. The exact maximizing integer
is among $\lfloor h_*\rfloor$ and $\lceil h_*\rceil$.
If $w_*=W(eN)$, the continuous maximum is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lowerWvalue}
 \phi_N(h_*)=N\left(w_*-2+\frac1{w_*}\right).
\end{equation}
Rounding to a nearest integer changes this value by
$O((\log N)^2/N)$: on an interval of fixed length around $h_*$,
$|\phi_N''(h)|=O((\log N)^2/N)$, and $\phi_N'(h_*)=0$.
This provides an accurate, explicit lower bound even when the leading
logarithmic expression is numerically crude.

For the main theorem, a simpler choice suffices. Put
\[
 L=\log n,\qquad \ell=\log\log n,
 \qquad h=\left\lfloor\frac nL\right\rfloor.
\]
As $n\to\infty$,
\[
 \log h=L-\ell+O(L/n),\qquad h=n/L+O(1).
\]
Hence \eqref{eq:pathlower} yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lowerexpanded}
 \log a_n\ge n(L-\ell-1)+\frac{n\ell}{L}-O(L).
\end{equation}
In particular the lower bound has the same $n\log n$, $-n\log\log n$,
and $-n$ terms as the claimed answer.

\section{The Poisson-moment comparison inequality}\label{sec:poisson}

The upper bound requires a comparison family that respects the increase
from $k$ to $k+1$ in \eqref{eq:triangular}. Ordinary exponential bounds
are too small, while a rough factorial bound loses the logarithmic
factor. Shifted Poisson moments provide the needed intermediate scale.

Let $X$ have the Poisson distribution with mean $\lambda>0$, and define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:M}
 M_n(k)=\E(k+X)^n,\qquad T_n(\lambda)=M_n(0).
\end{equation}
Their exponential generating functions are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mgf}
 \sum_{n\ge0}M_n(k)\frac{z^n}{n!}
 =\exp\!\bigl(kz+\lambda(e^z-1)\bigr),
 \qquad
 \sum_{n\ge0}T_n(\lambda)\frac{z^n}{n!}
 =\exp\!\bigl(\lambda(e^z-1)\bigr).
\end{equation}
Expanding the last exponential proves \eqref{eq:touchard}; at
$\lambda=1$ these polynomials give the Bell numbers. Standard Bell
identities are recorded in \cite{dlmf-bell}; the identities needed below
also follow directly from \eqref{eq:mgf}.

For $n\ge1$ set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:DS}
 D_n(k)=M_n(k)-M_n(k-1),\qquad
 S_n(k)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}M_i(k)M_{n-1-i}(k+1).
\end{equation}
The central estimate is the following.

\begin{lemma}[Shifted Poisson convolution inequality]\label{lem:poisson}
For every $n\ge1$, $k\ge1$, and $\lambda>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:poissonineq}
 S_n(k)\le\left(1+\frac2\lambda\right)D_n(k).
\end{equation}
In fact, after multiplication by $\lambda$, the difference between the
right and left sides is a polynomial with nonnegative coefficients in
$\lambda$ and $k-1$.
\end{lemma}

\subsection{Reduction to the shift \texorpdfstring{$k=1$}{k=1}}

Write $u=k-1$. The binomial theorem gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Dtranslate}
 D_n(k)=\sum_{r=1}^n\binom nr u^{n-r}D_r(1).
\end{equation}
There is a matching identity for the ordinary convolution:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Stranslate}
 S_n(k)=\sum_{r=1}^n\binom nr u^{n-r}S_r(1).
\end{equation}
To verify it, expand both shifted moments in the definition of $S_n(k)$.
For fixed nonnegative integers $p,q$ with $p+q=r-1$, the coefficient
sum over the convolution index is
\[
 \sum_i\binom ip\binom{n-1-i}q=\binom n{p+q+1}=\binom nr.
\]
This identity counts a subset of $r$ elements of an $n$-element ordered
set by the location of its $(p+1)$st element. Thus it holds including
all boundary cases. Equations \eqref{eq:Dtranslate} and
\eqref{eq:Stranslate} reduce the lemma to $k=1$.

Differentiating \eqref{eq:mgf} with respect to $\lambda$ shows
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Dderivative}
 D_n(1)=T_n'(\lambda).
\end{equation}
Suppress the argument $\lambda$ temporarily, write $S_n=S_n(1)$, and set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:H}
 H_n=(\lambda+2)T_n'-\lambda S_n.
\end{equation}
We shall prove that $H_n$ has nonnegative coefficients for every $n\ge1$.

\subsection{A recurrence for the difference polynomial}

The moment identities imply
\begin{align}
 T_{n+1}&=\lambda(T_n+T_n'),\label{eq:Trec}\\
 M_{n+1}(k)&=(k+\lambda)M_n(k)+\lambda M_n'(k).\label{eq:Mrec}
\end{align}
Here and below a prime means differentiation in $\lambda$, not in $k$.
Applying \eqref{eq:Mrec} to both factors in $S_n$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Srec}
 S_{n+1}=\frac\lambda2 S_n'
          +\left(\lambda+\frac32\right)S_n
          +\frac12\bigl(M_n(1)+M_n(2)\bigr).
\end{equation}
Indeed, the two shifted convolution sums add up to
$2S_{n+1}-M_n(1)-M_n(2)$. Furthermore,
\[
 M_n(1)=T_n+T_n',\qquad
 M_n(2)=T_n+2T_n'+T_n''.
\]
Substitute these formulas and \eqref{eq:Trec} into \eqref{eq:H}. A direct
simplification gives the exact recurrence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Hrec}
 H_{n+1}=\frac\lambda2 H_n'+(\lambda+1)H_n+K_n,
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}\label{eq:K}
 K_n=\frac{\lambda(\lambda+1)}2T_n''-2\lambda T_n'+2T_n.
\end{equation}
The initial difference polynomials are
\begin{align*}
 H_1&=2,\\
 H_2&=2+2\lambda,\\
 H_3&=2+6\lambda+\lambda^2,\\
 H_4&=2+14\lambda+8\lambda^2.
\end{align*}
They all have nonnegative coefficients.

\subsection{Why the forcing polynomial is nonnegative}

From \eqref{eq:touchard}, the coefficient of $\lambda^j$ in $K_n$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Kcoeff}
 [\lambda^j]K_n
 =\frac{(j-1)(j-4)}2\ST nj
   +\frac{j(j+1)}2\ST n{j+1}.
\end{equation}
For $n\ge4$, this is visibly nonnegative except possibly when $j=2$ or
$j=3$. Those cases require only
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stirlingineq}
 \ST n2\le3\ST n3\quad(n\ge3),\qquad
 \ST n3\le6\ST n4\quad(n\ge4).
\end{equation}
Here is a common elementary proof. If a partition of $n$ elements into
$r$ blocks has $n>r$, it has a nonsingleton block. Choose such a block by
any fixed deterministic rule and split off a specified element of it.
Remember the unordered pair of blocks created. Merging that pair
recovers the original partition. This is an injection into partitions
with $r+1$ blocks together with a choice of two of those blocks. Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mergeineq}
 \ST nr\le\binom{r+1}{2}\ST n{r+1}\qquad(n>r).
\end{equation}
Taking $r=2,3$ proves \eqref{eq:stirlingineq}.
Thus $K_n$ has nonnegative coefficients for all $n\ge4$.

Starting from $H_4$, recurrence \eqref{eq:Hrec} preserves coefficientwise
nonnegativity: differentiation, multiplication by $\lambda/2$ or by
$\lambda+1$, and addition of $K_n$ all preserve it. Together with the
three earlier base cases, this proves $H_n\ge0$ coefficientwise for all
$n\ge1$. By \eqref{eq:H} and \eqref{eq:Dderivative},
\[
 \lambda S_n(1)\le(\lambda+2)D_n(1).
\]
Finally, \eqref{eq:Dtranslate}--\eqref{eq:Stranslate} extend the result to
all $k\ge1$, proving Lemma~\ref{lem:poisson}.

\section{The global upper bound and completion of the proof}

\subsection{Comparison with the triangular recurrence}

Fix any $\lambda>0$ and write
\[
 \gamma=1+\frac2\lambda.
\]
We claim that, simultaneously for all $n\ge0$ and $k\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:majorant}
 c_{n,k}\le\gamma^n M_n(k).
\end{equation}
For $n=0$, both sides equal $1$. Assume the bound for smaller first
indices. Equations \eqref{eq:triangularsum} and \eqref{eq:poissonineq}
imply, for $n\ge1$,
\begin{align}
 c_{n,k}
 &\le\gamma^{n-1}\sum_{j=1}^k S_n(j)\notag\\
 &\le\gamma^n\sum_{j=1}^k\bigl(M_n(j)-M_n(j-1)\bigr)\notag\\
 &=\gamma^n\bigl(M_n(k)-M_n(0)\bigr)
 \le\gamma^n M_n(k).\label{eq:compareproof}
\end{align}
The boundary $k=0$ is immediate. This proves the induction.

Importantly, the penultimate expression is sharper than the induction
claim. At $k=1$, \eqref{eq:Dderivative} yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:touchardupper}
 a_n\le\left(1+\frac2\lambda\right)^nT_n'(\lambda).
\end{equation}
Because $\lambda$ was arbitrary, this proves the upper half of
Theorem~\ref{thm:finite}.

Differentiation of \eqref{eq:mgf} gives
\[
 \sum_{n\ge0}T_n'(\lambda)\frac{r^n}{n!}
 =(e^r-1)\exp\!\bigl(\lambda(e^r-1)\bigr).
\]
All coefficients are nonnegative. Any one term is bounded by the whole
sum when $r>0$, and \eqref{eq:cauchyupper} follows. This argument is just
a positive-coefficient bound; it does not require any analyticity of
$A(x)$.

\subsection{An explicit parameter choice}

Let $L=\log n$ and $\ell=\log L$ as before. For all sufficiently large
$n$, take
\begin{equation}\label{eq:parameters}
 \lambda=L,\qquad r=L-2\ell>0.
\end{equation}
Then $e^r=n/L^2$. Taking logarithms in \eqref{eq:cauchyupper}, and using
$\log(e^r-1)\le r$, gives
\begin{align}
 \log a_n
 &\le \log n!-n\log r
       +n\log(1+2/L)
       +L(e^r-1)+r\notag\\
 &=\log n!-n\log(L-2\ell)
       +n\log(1+2/L)+\frac nL-2\ell.\label{eq:explicitupperlog}
\end{align}
The elementary estimates
\begin{align*}
 \log n!&=nL-n+O(L),\\
 \log(L-2\ell)&=\ell-\frac{2\ell}{L}
                         +O\!\left(\frac{\ell^2}{L^2}\right),\\
 \log(1+2/L)&=\frac2L+O(L^{-2})
\end{align*}
now show
\begin{equation}\label{eq:upperexpanded}
 \log a_n\le n(L-\ell-1)
     +\frac{2n\ell+3n}{L}
     +O\!\left(\frac{n\ell^2}{L^2}+L\right).
\end{equation}
For the first estimate, even Stirling's full formula is unnecessary:
bounding the sum $\sum_{j=1}^n\log j$ between its neighboring integrals
already gives $\log n!=n\log n-n+O(\log n)$.

The lower estimate \eqref{eq:lowerexpanded} and upper estimate
\eqref{eq:upperexpanded} together give \eqref{eq:mainlog}. Dividing by
$n$ and exponentiating proves \eqref{eq:root} and \eqref{eq:expform}.
This completes the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}.

\begin{remark}[Why a tunable comparison is necessary]
A bound of the form $a_n\le C^n n!$ cannot identify the factor
$(\log n)^{-n}$. Even a comparison with $C^n$ times a fixed Bell-type
sequence leaves an unwanted constant $C$ in the $n$th root. Here the
factor $1+2/\lambda$ can approach $1$, and the valid choice
$\lambda=\log n$ makes its total logarithmic cost only $O(n/\log n)$.
The dependence of the Poisson mean on $n$ is fully allowed because
\eqref{eq:touchardupper} holds for every positive $\lambda$ at every
index.
\end{remark}

\section{Consequences and comparison with Bell numbers}

\subsection{Superexponential but subfactorial growth}

The main theorem immediately implies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:superexp}
 \frac{a_n}{C^n}\longrightarrow\infty
 \quad\text{for every fixed }C>0.
\end{equation}
On the other hand,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:subfact}
 \left(\frac{a_n}{n!}\right)^{1/n}
 =\frac1{\log n}\left(1+O\!\left(\frac{\log\log n}{\log n}\right)\right)
 \longrightarrow0.
\end{equation}
Thus $a_n/(C^n n!)\to0$ for every fixed $C>0$.
These two assertions locate the growth more precisely than the phrase
``almost factorial'': there is a factor of approximately
$(\log n)^n$ between $n!$ and $a_n$ on the logarithmic scale.

For every fixed $s<1$ and $C>0$,
\[
 \frac{a_n}{C^n(n!)^s}\longrightarrow\infty,
\]
because the logarithm of this quotient has dominant term
$(1-s)n\log n$. In the convention that Gevrey order $s$ means a bound
by $CK^n(n!)^s$, the series has exact Gevrey order $1$, with zero
factorial exponential type. This statement specifies the convention,
as Gevrey indexing is not uniform across the literature.

\subsection{The two generating functions behave differently}

By the Cauchy--Hadamard formula,
\[
 \rad A=\frac1{\limsup a_n^{1/n}}=0.
\]
In particular, there is no holomorphic germ at zero whose Taylor series
has these coefficients and satisfies \eqref{eq:def}. The formal series
itself remains perfectly well-defined and unique.

In contrast, the exponential generating function
\begin{equation}\label{eq:EGF}
 \mathcal A(t)=\sum_{n\ge0}a_n\frac{t^n}{n!}
\end{equation}
is entire by \eqref{eq:subfact}. Summing the finite-index upper bound
also gives, for every $t\ge0$ and $\lambda>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:EGFupper}
 \mathcal A(t)\le
 1+(e^{\gamma t}-1)
       \exp\!\bigl(\lambda(e^{\gamma t}-1)\bigr),
 \qquad\gamma=1+2/\lambda.
\end{equation}

\begin{corollary}[Double-exponential scale]\label{cor:EGF}
Along the positive real axis,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:doubleexp}
 \lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\log\log\mathcal A(t)}t=1.
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
For a fixed $\lambda$, \eqref{eq:EGFupper} bounds the limit superior by
$1+2/\lambda$. Letting $\lambda\to\infty$ gives the upper bound $1$.
For the lower bound fix $0<\eps<1$ and take
$n=\lfloor e^{(1-\eps)t}\rfloor$. The single term $a_nt^n/n!$ and
Theorem~\ref{thm:main} give
\[
 \log\left(\frac{a_nt^n}{n!}\right)
 =n\bigl(\log t-\log\log n+o(1)\bigr)
 =n\bigl(-\log(1-\eps)+o(1)\bigr).
\]
The constant inside the last parentheses is positive. Taking a further
logarithm shows that the limit inferior in \eqref{eq:doubleexp} is at
least $1-\eps$. Let $\eps\downarrow0$.
\end{proof}
Since the coefficients of $\mathcal A$ are nonnegative, its maximum
modulus on $|z|=t$ equals $\mathcal A(t)$. Consequently its usual entire
function order is infinite. Equation \eqref{eq:doubleexp}, rather than
just ``infinite order,'' states the useful growth scale.

\subsection{Bell-number scale is not Bell-number equivalence}

Let $\mathsf B_n=T_n(1)$ denote the Bell numbers. A standard asymptotic,
with $w=W(n)$, is \cite{dlmf-bell}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bellsaddle}
 \mathsf B_n\sim
 \frac{\exp\!\bigl(n(w-1+1/w)-1\bigr)}{\sqrt{1+w}}.
\end{equation}
In particular $\mathsf B_n^{1/n}\sim n/(e\log n)$. Therefore our theorem
implies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bellroot}
 \left(\frac{a_n}{\mathsf B_n}\right)^{1/n}\longrightarrow1,
 \qquad
 a_n=\mathsf B_n\exp(o(n)).
\end{equation}
These are the senses in which the sequence has Bell-number-scale growth.
They do not assert that $a_n/\mathsf B_n$ is bounded or converges. Indeed,
the finite computations in the next section show a substantial and
growing discrepancy. The formula \eqref{eq:bellsaddle} is a comparison
formula for Bell numbers, not a formula silently transferred to A088714.

\section{Exact computation and numerical diagnostics}\label{sec:numerics}

\subsection{A triangular dynamic program}

To obtain $a_0,\ldots,a_N$, store $c_{0,k}=1$ for $0\le k\le N+1$.
For each $n=1,\ldots,N$, compute
\[
 c_{n,0}=0,\qquad
 c_{n,k}=c_{n,k-1}+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}c_{i,k}c_{n-1-i,k+1}
 \quad(1\le k\le N-n+1).
\]
Then output $a_n=c_{n,1}$. The triangular cutoff is sufficient: every
requested entry on the right was computed at an earlier first index.
The number of products is exactly
\[
 \sum_{n=1}^N n(N-n+1)=\frac{N(N+1)(N+2)}6.
\]
Thus the algorithm uses $O(N^3)$ arbitrary-precision arithmetic
operations and $O(N^2)$ integer cells. These are arithmetic counts, not
unit-cost bit-complexity claims; the integers grow with $N$.

The archive contains both a dependency-free Python implementation and
a faster C++ implementation using GMP. The supplied data were computed
with exact integer arithmetic through $n=1200$. The Python and C++
outputs agree through $n=400$. Independent truncated-polynomial checks
verify \eqref{eq:def} and \eqref{eq:inverse}; the supplied verification
script also checks the auxiliary polynomial recurrences and finite
bounds on explicitly reported finite ranges.

\subsection{What the diagnostics measure}

Define

 D_n^{\mathrm{num}}

The notation $D_n^{\mathrm{num}}$ is distinct from the Poisson moment

behavior not established by that theorem. (2026-10-01: the first-order

\section{A finer asymptotic: formal motivation and its missing step}\label{sec:finer}

\subsection{The leading endpoint balance}

Write $R_n=a_n/a_{n-1}$ and consider \eqref{eq:directrec}. Near its outer
endpoint put $k=n-1-m$. Then the corresponding summand is
\[
 a_{n-1-m}[x^m]A(x)^{n+1-m}.
\]
Since $a_1=1$, the first approximation to the coefficient is $n^m/m!$.
If neighboring ratios can be treated as locally constant, the endpoint
sum divided by $a_n$ is approximately
\[
 \frac1{R_n}\sum_{m\ge0}\frac{(n/R_n)^m}{m!}
 =\frac{e^{n/R_n}}{R_n}.
\]
Setting this expression equal to $1$ leads to
\[
 R_n\log R_n\approx n,
 \qquad R_n\approx\frac n{W(n)}.
\]
This explains the natural Lambert $W$ scale of the ratios. It is not a
proof: local ratio regularity and uniform control of the other summands
have been assumed in deriving the approximation.

\subsection{The first correction and a candidate normalization}

There is more information in the same calculation. From the first few
coefficients,
\[
 \log A(x)=x+\frac52x^2+O(x^3).
\]
Expanding the near-endpoint coefficient for $m$ of logarithmic size gives
the formal local approximation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:localcoeff}
 [x^m]A(x)^{n+1-m}
 =\frac{n^m}{m!}
  \left(1+\frac{3m(m-1)}{2n}+\cdots\right).
\end{equation}
The factor $3/2$ combines the quadratic term of $\log A$ with the
replacement of $n+1-m$ by $n$.

Suppose, for this calculation, that the ratio has a smooth expansion
near $R_n=n/w$, where $w=W(n)$. Put
\[
 t=n/R_n,\qquad d=\frac{w}{1+w}.
\]
The local variation of the ratios contributes
$m(m+1)d/(2n)$ to the bracket multiplying the Poisson kernel. Averaging
$m(m-1)$ and $m(m+1)$ against that kernel replaces them by $t^2$ and
$t^2+2t$, respectively. The opposite endpoint, where one inner factor
has almost all the degree, begins with $2a_{n-1}$ from $A(x)^2$.
At this formal order the equation becomes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:formalbalance}
 1\approx\frac{e^t}{R_n}
  \left(1+\frac{(3+d)t^2+2dt}{2n}\right)+\frac2{R_n}.
\end{equation}
Writing $R_n=n/w+\delta$ and expanding gives
\[
 \delta(1+w)\approx2+\frac{(3+d)w+2d}{2},
\]
and hence the candidate ratio approximation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ratiocandidate}
 R_n\approx\frac n{W(n)}+2+
                \frac{W(n)}{2(1+W(n))^2}.
\end{equation}
This is consistent with the exact data, but the approximation signs in
this subsection are intentional.

A normalization whose successive quotient has these same first terms
is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normalizer}
 \mathcal N_n=
 \frac{\exp\!\bigl(n(w-1+1/w)-1+w^2+3w\bigr)}{\sqrt{1+w}},
 \qquad w=W(n).
\end{equation}
Indeed, using $w'=w/(n(1+w))$ and Taylor expansion in the real variable
$n$ shows
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normratio}
 \frac{\mathcal N_n}{\mathcal N_{n-1}}
 =\frac n{W(n)}+2+
     \frac{W(n)}{2(1+W(n))^2}
      +O\!\left(\frac{W(n)^3}{n}\right).
\end{equation}
One way to check the differentiation is to note that
\[
 \frac d{dn}\bigl[n(w-1+1/w)\bigr]=w.
\]
The additional derivative from $w^2+3w-\tfrac12\log(1+w)$ supplies the
constant and rational correction in \eqref{eq:normratio}.

\begin{conjecture}[Finer Bell comparison]\label{conj:finer}
There exists a constant $C>0$ such that, with $w=W(n)$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:conjecture}
 a_n\sim C\,\mathsf B_n\exp(w^2+3w)
       \sim C\,\mathcal N_n.
\end{equation}
\end{conjecture}
The existence or numerical value of $C$ is not established in this
article. The data and the endpoint calculation motivate the displayed
form, rather than merely an arbitrary fit to an exponential correction.

\subsection{A precise sufficient condition for the conjecture}

The unresolved analytic step can be stated cleanly.
\begin{proposition}[Conditional implication]\label{prop:conditional}
Suppose that for some fixed $p\ge0$ the actual ratios satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{eq:neededratio}
 \frac{a_n}{a_{n-1}}
 =\frac n{W(n)}+2+
     \frac{W(n)}{2(1+W(n))^2}
       +O\!\left(\frac{W(n)^p}{n}\right).
\end{equation}
Then Conjecture~\ref{conj:finer} follows with some finite, strictly
positive constant $C$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Compare \eqref{eq:neededratio} with \eqref{eq:normratio}. Since both
ratios are asymptotic to $n/W(n)$, their quotient is
\[
 1+O\!\left(\frac{W(n)^{\max(p,3)+1}}{n^2}\right).
\]
The error is summable because $W(n)=O(\log n)$. Therefore the successive
differences of $\log(a_n/\mathcal N_n)$ are absolutely summable. This
logarithm converges to a finite real number, so $a_n/\mathcal N_n$ tends
to a finite positive constant. Formula \eqref{eq:bellsaddle} then gives
the Bell-number version.
\end{proof}

The main theorem does not supply \eqref{eq:neededratio}. In particular,
it does not justify replacing all nearby coefficient ratios by a smooth
function, nor does it control every non-endpoint contribution at a
summable error level. A proof of such uniform endpoint estimates would
be a genuine strengthening of the present result. This identifies the
remaining task without presenting a formal asymptotic calculation as a
completed proof.

\section{Conclusion}

OEIS A088714 has the precise $n$th-root growth
\[
 \boxed{a_n^{1/n}\sim\frac{n}{e\log n}.}
\]
The accompanying logarithmic error estimate is
\[
 \log a_n=n\log n-n\log\log n-n
       +O\!\left(\frac{n\log\log n}{\log n}\right).
\]
The mechanism is visible in the proof. Composition iterates turn the
functional equation into a positive triangular recurrence. Moving along
its increasing-index direction gives roughly $h^{n-h}$, whose optimum
occurs at $h$ of order $n/\log n$. Shifted Poisson moments furnish a
compatible upper comparison and recover the same leading scale,
including the factor $1/e$.

The result determines the growth at logarithmic precision $o(n)$ and
settles the convergence classification of both generating functions.
The finer proposed factor $\exp(W(n)^2+3W(n))$ remains explicitly
separate from the proved theorem.

\section{The sequences, the contribution, and its scope}
\subsection{Formal definitions and the earlier result}
Throughout, $a_0=c_0=1$, logarithms are natural, and a power series without
an explicitly specified analytic domain is a \emph{formal} power series.
The defining equations are
\begin{align}
 A(z)&=1+zA(z)^2A(zA(z)),\label{gml:eq:A}\\
 C(z)&=\frac1{1-zA(z)},\qquad A(z)=C(zA(z)).\label{gml:eq:C}
\end{align}
The second identity in \eqref{gml:eq:C} follows from \eqref{gml:eq:A}; it is also
recorded in the OEIS entries \cite{oeis-a088714,oeisC}. Coefficients are determined
successively because the right side of \eqref{gml:eq:A} at degree $n\ge1$
uses only coefficients of $A$ of degrees below $n$. Thus there is exactly
one solution with constant term $1$; its coefficients are positive integers.
The initial values are
\[
\begin{array}{c|rrrrrrrr}
n&0&1&2&3&4&5&6&7\\\hline
a_n&1&1&3&13&69&419&2809&20353\\
c_n&1&1&2&6&24&118&674&4308.
\end{array}
\]
The notation $C$ is used here for the generating function called $A$ in
OEIS A088713, whose defining identity is $C(z/C(z))=(1-z)^{-1}$.
For verification, put $B=zA$. Equation \eqref{gml:eq:A} gives
$B^{\langle-1\rangle}(z)=z-z^2A(z)=z/C(z)$. Composing $A=C\circ B$
with this inverse and substituting in \eqref{gml:eq:C} proves the identity for $C$.

Part~I of this report, \emph{Bell-Scale Growth of OEIS A088714},
proves (Theorem~\ref{thm:main})
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:oldgrowth}
 \log a_n=n\log n-n\log\log n-n+
 O\left(\frac{n\log\log n}{\log n}\right).
\end{equation}
Part~I explicitly separates this result from consecutive-coefficient
asymptotics and from a finer multiplicative Bell-number conjecture.
The golden-moment manuscript re-proves this bound, with the finite bounds
of Theorem~\ref{thm:finite}, in an appendix that is not reprinted here
(Section~\ref{gml:sec:source}). Part~I's proof uses no numerical
evidence, so the deductions below rest on written proofs only.

\subsection{Main results}
A normalized Stieltjes moment sequence is a sequence
$m_n=\int_{[0,\infty)}t^n\dd\sigma(t)$ for a probability measure $\sigma$
with all moments finite. It is \emph{determinate} when this measure is unique.
Define
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:exponents}
 \alpha=\frac{3-\sqrt5}{2},\qquad
 \beta=\frac{\sqrt5-1}{2},\qquad
 \alpha+\beta=1,\quad\beta^2+\beta=1.
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Moment and analytic structure]\label{gml:thm:mainmoment}
There are unique probability measures $\mu,\nu$ on $[0,\infty)$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:moments}
 a_n=\int t^n\dd\mu(t),\qquad c_n=\int t^n\dd\nu(t)\qquad(n\ge0).
\end{equation}
Both have all positive exponential moments finite. Their supports are
unbounded, and all minors of their infinite Hankel matrices with strictly
increasing row and column index lists are strictly positive. For
$(\LL m)_n=m_nm_{n+2}-m_{n+1}^2$, every $\LL^k a$ and $\LL^k c$, $k\ge1$,
is a Stieltjes moment sequence with strictly positive entries.

For $s>0$, set
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:FG}
 F(s)=\int\frac{\dd\mu(t)}{1+st},\qquad
 G(s)=\int\frac{\dd\nu(t)}{1+st}.
\end{equation}
These transforms satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:functions}
 F(s)=1-sF(s)^2F(sF(s)),\qquad G(s)=\frac1{1+sF(s)}.
\end{equation}
Moreover, $U(s)=sF(s)$ is an increasing homeomorphism of $(0,\infty)$ onto
itself and has inverse
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:Uinverse}
 U^{-1}(t)=t(1+U(t)).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Golden-ratio endpoint laws]\label{gml:thm:mainendpoint}
As $s\to\infty$,
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:transformpower}
 F(s)\sim s^{-\alpha},\qquad G(s)\sim s^{-\beta}.
\end{equation}
As $t\downarrow0$,
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:CDF}
 \mu([0,t])\sim\frac{\sin(\pi\alpha)}{\pi\alpha}t^\alpha,
 \qquad
 \nu([0,t])\sim\frac{\sin(\pi\beta)}{\pi\beta}t^\beta.
\end{equation}
Neither measure has an atom at zero. For $q>0$, its negative $q$th moment
is finite precisely when $q<\alpha$ for $\mu$, or $q<\beta$ for $\nu$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Consecutive ratios and the opposite tail]\label{gml:thm:maintail}
The two sequences satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:ratios}
 \frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\sim\frac{n}{\log n},\qquad
 \frac{c_{n+1}}{c_n}\sim\frac{n}{\log n}.
\end{equation}
Their representing measures satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:tails}
 \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\log\mu([x,\infty))}{x\log x}
 =\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\log\nu([x,\infty))}{x\log x}=-1.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

These statements concern entire infinite families of inequalities and
asymptotic regimes, rather than additional finite entries in an OEIS table.
The two ends of the same measures are quite different: an irrational power
law at zero and a logarithmically Poisson-scale tail at infinity.

\section{Two operator constructions that preserve positivity}\label{gml:sec:operators}
This section supplies the central existence mechanism. All operators here
are bounded: input measures have compact support. Unbounded support is
introduced only by taking weak limits in Section~\ref{gml:sec:limit}.

\subsection{The renewal transformation}
\begin{lemma}[A positive block operator]\label{gml:lem:renewal}
Let $\xi$ be a compactly supported probability measure on $[0,\infty)$,
and write $B(z)=\int(1-zt)^{-1}\dd\xi(t)$ near zero. Then
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:renewal}
 R(z)=\frac1{1-zB(z)}
\end{equation}
is the moment series of a compactly supported probability measure on
$[0,\infty)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
On $K=\C\oplus L^2(\xi)$ let $T$ be multiplication by $t$ and put
\[
 Q=\begin{pmatrix}1&\langle\sqrt t,\cdot\rangle\\
                     \sqrt t&T\end{pmatrix}.
\]
For $(u,v)\in K$, its quadratic form is
\[
 \langle (u,v),Q(u,v)\rangle
   =\int|u+\sqrt t\,v(t)|^2\dd\xi(t)\ge0.
\]
The mass-one assumption on $\xi$ is used in the coefficient of $|u|^2$.
Thus $Q$ is bounded, self-adjoint and positive. Its spectral measure in
the unit vector $e_0=(1,0)$ is a compactly supported probability measure
on $[0,\infty)$. A Schur complement, initially for sufficiently small $z$,
gives its moment series as
\begin{align*}
 \langle e_0,(I-zQ)^{-1}e_0\rangle
 &=\frac1{1-z-z^2\int t(1-zt)^{-1}\dd\xi(t)}\\
 &=\frac1{1-zB(z)}.
\end{align*}
The identity $B(z)=1+z\int t(1-zt)^{-1}\dd\xi(t)$ proves the last step.
\end{proof}

In coefficients, if $B=\sum b_nz^n$ and $R=\sum d_nz^n$, then
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:renewalrec}
 d_0=1,\qquad d_n=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}b_jd_{n-1-j}.
\end{equation}
Positivity of these coefficients alone would not prove the moment property;
the positive operator is the stronger ingredient.

\subsection{From a positive moment sequence to noncrossing moments}
Let $\NC(n)$ denote the partitions of $\{1,\ldots,n\}$ with no two distinct
blocks containing $i<j<k<\ell$ with $i,k$ in one block and $j,\ell$ in the
other. These are the noncrossing partitions. The empty partition has weight $1$.

\begin{lemma}[A positive full-Fock operator]\label{gml:lem:fock}
Suppose $d_n=\int t^n\dd\rho(t)$, where $\rho$ is a compactly supported
probability measure on $[0,\infty)$. Put
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:ncmoments}
 m_0=1,\qquad
 m_n=\sum_{\pi\in\NC(n)}\prod_{V\in\pi}d_{|V|}.
\end{equation}
Then $(m_n)$ is also a compactly supported Stieltjes moment sequence.
Writing $R(z)=\sum d_nz^n$, its moment series is the unique formal solution
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:ncseries}
 M(z)=R(zM(z)),\qquad M(0)=1.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Set $H=L^2(\rho)$ and let $T$ be multiplication by $t$. The full Fock space is
\[
 \Fock(H)=\C\Omega\oplus\bigoplus_{k\ge1}H^{\otimes k}.
\]
The vector $\Omega$ is the vacuum. Define maps to $H\otimes\Fock(H)$ by
\[
 D\Omega=0,\quad D(h_1\otimes\cdots\otimes h_k)
       =h_1\otimes(h_2\otimes\cdots\otimes h_k),\qquad
 J\psi=1\otimes\psi.
\]
The empty tail means $\Omega$. Both maps have norm $1$, and
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:Xpositive}
 X=(D+J)^*(T\otimes I)(D+J)\ge0.
\end{equation}
It is bounded. Expanding gives four operations: multiplication by $T$ on
the first tensor factor of a nonempty word; creation of a first factor
$t$; annihilation by the linear functional $\langle t,\cdot\rangle$; and
$d_1I$. The gauge operation vanishes on $\Omega$.

We verify the vacuum moments, not merely positivity. Expand $X^n$ into
words in these four operations and apply them from right to left.
A term contributing to the vacuum never attempts to remove a factor from
an empty word and ends at tensor length zero. Match each created tensor
factor with its eventual annihilation, using the last-in, first-out order
of first tensor factors. Gauges acting on that factor, together with its
creation and annihilation, form a block. Scalar operations form singleton
blocks. Operations acting on factors above it form nested blocks.
This correspondence is a bijection with noncrossing partitions; reading
such a partition from one end reconstructs the operator choices and the
stack of active factors.

A block of size $r\ge2$ has weight
$\langle t,T^{r-2}t\rangle=\int t^r\dd\rho(t)=d_r$; a singleton has
weight $d_1$. Nested closed pieces leave the lower tensor factors untouched
and contribute their own scalar weights. Thus
$\langle\Omega,X^n\Omega\rangle$ is exactly \eqref{gml:eq:ncmoments}.
The positive spectral measure of $X$ at $\Omega$ proves the assertion.

Finally, consider the block containing the first point of a noncrossing
partition. If its size is $r$, its weight is $d_r$, and there are $r$
independent intervening or final intervals, each carrying a noncrossing
partition. Consequently
\[
 M(z)=1+\sum_{r\ge1}d_r z^rM(z)^r=R(zM(z)).
\]
The degree-$n$ equation determines $m_n$ from earlier $m_j$, proving uniqueness.
\end{proof}

For example, $m_1=d_1$, $m_2=d_1^2+d_2$, and
$m_3=d_1^3+3d_1d_2+d_3$. In free-probability terminology, this is the
rate-one free compound Poisson construction with jump law $\rho$; the
operator proof above is sufficient without adopting that terminology.

\subsection{The nonlinear transform}
Combining the two lemmas gives the following general result.
\begin{theorem}[Moment-preserving nonlinear transform]\label{gml:thm:transform}
For a compact Stieltjes moment series $B$ with $B(0)=1$, the unique formal
solution $M=\T(B)$ of
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:T}
 M(z)=1+zM(z)^2B(zM(z)),\qquad M(0)=1,
\end{equation}
is again a compact Stieltjes moment series.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Apply Lemma~\ref{gml:lem:renewal} to obtain $R=(1-zB)^{-1}$, and then
Lemma~\ref{gml:lem:fock} to obtain $M=R(zM)$. The resulting identity is
$M=(1-zMB(zM))^{-1}$, which is equivalent to \eqref{gml:eq:T}.
\end{proof}
\begin{corollary}[Removal of compact support]
The same transformation preserves normalized Stieltjes moment sequences
with all moments finite, without a compact-support assumption.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Replace an input random variable $X\ge0$ by $\min(X,R)$. Its moments
converge to the input moments. Every output moment in the two lemmas is a
fixed polynomial in finitely many input moments, so the output moments
converge as well. The output first moment is $1$, and bounds on each next
moment give tightness and uniform integrability. A weak subsequential
limit therefore represents all output moments. No assertion of determinacy
is needed for this preservation statement.
\end{proof}
This is a preservation statement on an entire class of series. The
special feature of A088714 is that it is the coefficientwise attracting
fixed point of this transformation.

\section{The fixed point as a determinate moment law}\label{gml:sec:limit}
\subsection{Exact coefficient stabilization}
Start with $B_0(z)=1$, the moment series of $\delta_0$, and define
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:iterations}
 B_{r+1}=\T(B_r),\qquad C_r=(1-zB_r)^{-1}.
\end{equation}
Let $\mu_r,\nu_r$ be the compact probability measures supplied above for
$B_r,C_r$, respectively. All have nonnegative support.

\begin{lemma}[One additional correct coefficient per step]\label{gml:lem:stable}
If $B-\widetilde B$ is divisible by $z^N$, $N\ge1$, then
$\T(B)-\T(\widetilde B)$ is divisible by $z^{N+1}$. In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:stable}
 B_r\equiv A\pmod{z^{r+1}},\qquad C_r\equiv C\pmod{z^{r+2}}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For $M=\T(B)$, the coefficient $m_n$, $n\ge1$, depends only on
$b_0,\ldots,b_{n-1}$ and $m_0,\ldots,m_{n-1}$. Induction on $n\le N$
therefore proves agreement. Since $B_0$ and $A$ agree at degree zero,
iteration proves the first congruence. Equation \eqref{gml:eq:renewalrec}
proves the second.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Existence and uniqueness of the limiting measures}
The first moment of $\mu_r$ is $1$ for $r\ge1$. Hence
$\mu_r([R,\infty))\le1/R$; the measures are tight. Every subsequential
weak limit has all moments $a_n$. Indeed, for fixed $n$, the moments of
orders $n$ and $n+1$ eventually stabilize, and
\[
 \int_{[R,\infty)}t^n\dd\mu_r(t)\le\frac{a_{n+1}}R
\]
for sufficiently large $r$. Bounded continuous cutoffs, followed by
$R\to\infty$, justify passage of the $n$th moment to the limit.
Denote one such limit by $\mu$.

Bound \eqref{gml:eq:oldgrowth} and
$\log(n!)=n\log n-n+O(\log n)$ imply
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:entire}
 \left(\frac{a_n}{n!}\right)^{1/n}\longrightarrow0.
\end{equation}
Thus for every $v>0$, monotone convergence gives
\[
 \int e^{vt}\dd\mu(t)=\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{a_nv^n}{n!}<\infty.
\]
The same argument applies to any measure with these moments. Its
characteristic function is then the absolutely justified moment expansion
$\sum a_n(iu)^n/n!$. Equality of characteristic functions determines the
probability measure. Thus $\mu$ is unique, and the whole sequence
$\mu_r$ converges weakly to it.

For $\nu_r$, the first moment is again $1$, and its moments converge
coefficientwise to $c_n$. Tightness and uniform integrability give a
representing measure $\nu$. To prove uniqueness, observe first that the
moments of a nonnegative random variable satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:supermult}
 a_i a_j\le a_{i+j}.
\end{equation}
For example, take independent copies $X,Y$ with law $\mu$ and use
$\E[(X^i-Y^i)(X^j-Y^j)]\ge0$. Also $a_j\le a_{j+1}$: log-convexity
of moments follows from Cauchy--Schwarz, and the initial ratio is
$a_1/a_0=1$.
Expanding the geometric series in \eqref{gml:eq:C}, for $n\ge1$ we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:compositions}
 c_n=\sum_{k=1}^n\ 
 \sum_{\substack{s_1+\cdots+s_k=n\\s_i\ge1}}
       \prod_{i=1}^k a_{s_i-1}.
\end{equation}
Each product is at most $a_{n-k}\le a_{n-1}$, and there are
$2^{n-1}$ compositions of $n$. Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:crudeC}
 a_{n-1}\le c_n\le2^{n-1}a_{n-1}.
\end{equation}
It follows that $\sum c_nv^n/n!$ is entire, proving determinacy and all
positive exponential moments for $\nu$ as well. Consequently
$\nu_r\Rightarrow\nu$.

Finally, \eqref{gml:eq:oldgrowth} gives $a_n^{1/n}\to\infty$. The moments of
a probability measure supported in $[0,R]$ are at most $R^n$. Thus $\mu$
has unbounded support. Inequality \eqref{gml:eq:crudeC} proves the same for $\nu$.

\section{Strict Hankel positivity and iterated log-convexity}\label{gml:sec:hankel}
We provide the strict statements explicitly, since existence of a
representing measure alone only guarantees nonnegative minors in general.

\begin{lemma}[Generalized Vandermonde positivity]\label{gml:lem:vander}
If $0\le e_1<\cdots<e_k$ are integers and
$0<t_1<\cdots<t_k$, then $\det[t_j^{e_i}]_{i,j=1}^k>0$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
A nonzero linear combination of $k$ distinct nonnegative powers has at
most $k-1$ distinct positive zeros. This follows by induction: divide by
the smallest power, differentiate, and use Rolle's theorem. Consequently
the determinant cannot vanish in the connected chamber
$0<t_1<\cdots<t_k$, so its sign is constant. Letting the nodes approach
a common positive point identifies its sign with that of the Wronskian
\[
 t^{\sum e_i-k(k-1)/2}\prod_{i<j}(e_j-e_i)>0
\]
times a positive ordinary Vandermonde factor. This proves the sign.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Strict total positivity]\label{gml:thm:hankel}
Let $m_n$ be the moments of a probability measure $\sigma$ on $[0,\infty)$
with all moments finite and infinitely many positive support points. For
any strictly increasing nonnegative index lists $i_1<\cdots<i_k$ and
$j_1<\cdots<j_k$,
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:minor}
 \det[m_{i_p+j_q}]_{p,q=1}^k>0.
\end{equation}
In particular this holds for both $(a_n)$ and $(c_n)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Expanding two determinants and integrating termwise gives
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:andreief}
 \det[m_{i_p+j_q}]=\frac1{k!}
 \int\cdots\int
  \det[t_\ell^{i_p}]_{p,\ell}
  \det[t_\ell^{j_q}]_{q,\ell}
  \prod_{\ell=1}^k\dd\sigma(t_\ell).
\end{equation}
All terms are integrable since all moments are finite. The product is
unchanged by a permutation of the $t_\ell$, and is nonnegative everywhere:
on distinct positive points this follows from Lemma~\ref{gml:lem:vander};
on repeated points it vanishes, and at zero it follows by continuity.
Choose $k$ separated positive support points and disjoint small ordered
neighborhoods. Each neighborhood has positive mass, and the integrand is
strictly positive on their product. Thus the integral is strictly positive.
Both measures constructed above have unbounded support, so the hypothesis
holds for every $k$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Strict infinite log-convexity]\label{gml:thm:infiniteLC}
For $m=a$ or $m=c$, every $\LL^k m$, $k\ge1$, is a Stieltjes moment
sequence with an unbounded positive support representation. In particular,
$(\LL^k m)_n>0$ for every $n\ge0$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
If $m_n=\int t^n\dd\sigma(t)$, then
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:Lmeasure}
 m_nm_{n+2}-m_{n+1}^2
 =\frac12\int\!\int (xy)^n(x-y)^2\dd\sigma(x)\dd\sigma(y).
\end{equation}
Thus $\LL m$ is the moment sequence of the pushforward under
$(x,y)\mapsto xy$ of the finite positive measure
$\tfrac12(x-y)^2\dd\sigma(x)\dd\sigma(y)$. All moments remain finite.
If $\sigma$ has unbounded support and a positive support point $y_0$,
choose support points $x_j\to\infty$. Small product neighborhoods of
$(x_j,y_0)$ have positive weighted mass and yield arbitrarily large
positive product support points. Therefore the new measure also has
unbounded support and positive total mass. Repeat this argument.
The representing measures after the first iteration need not have mass
one, but identity \eqref{gml:eq:Lmeasure} does not require normalization.
\end{proof}
The general preservation phenomenon is known \cite{wangzhu}; its use here
is justified by the newly constructed representing measures. Ordinary
strict log-convexity, $a_na_{n+2}>a_{n+1}^2$, is only the first layer.

\subsection{An exact determinant bridge between the two sequences}
For a sequence $m$, write
$D_n^{(h)}(m)=\det[m_{i+j+h}]_{i,j=0}^{n-1}$, with $D_0^{(h)}=1$.
The following identities explain the repeated determinants in the numerical tables.
\begin{proposition}[Renewal Hankel identities]\label{gml:prop:detbridge}
For every $n\ge1$,
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:detbridge}
 D_n^{(1)}(c)=D_n^{(0)}(a),\qquad
 D_n^{(0)}(c)=D_{n-1}^{(1)}(a).
\end{equation}
These are algebraic consequences of $C=(1-zA)^{-1}$, independent of
moment positivity.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For a series $M=\sum m_nz^n$, its two bivariate Hankel kernels are
\[
 H_M(x,y)=\frac{xM(x)-yM(y)}{x-y},\qquad
 S_M(x,y)=\frac{M(x)-M(y)}{x-y}.
\]
They have coefficients $m_{i+j}$ and $m_{i+j+1}$, respectively.
A direct substitution gives
\[
 S_C(x,y)=C(x)C(y)H_A(x,y),\qquad
 H_C(x,y)=C(x)C(y)\bigl(1+xyS_A(x,y)\bigr).
\]
Multiplication of a bivariate kernel by $C(x)C(y)$ is congruence by a
lower-triangular Toeplitz matrix with diagonal entries $c_0=1$.
Its finite leading determinants are unchanged. The first kernel identity
proves the first formula; in the second identity the inside leading
matrix is the block diagonal matrix with blocks $1$ and the leading
$(n-1)\times(n-1)$ shifted Hankel matrix of $a$. This proves the second.
\end{proof}

\section{Exact functions behind the divergent series}\label{gml:sec:analytic}
\subsection{Passing the nonlinear identity to the limit}
Let
\[
 F_r(s)=\int\frac{\dd\mu_r(t)}{1+st},\qquad
 G_r(s)=\int\frac{\dd\nu_r(t)}{1+st}.
\]
For the compact measures, the formal identities become analytic identities
near $s=0$:
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:finiteanalytic}
 F_{r+1}(s)=1-sF_{r+1}(s)^2F_r(sF_{r+1}(s)),\qquad
 G_r(s)=\frac1{1+sF_r(s)}.
\end{equation}
They hold for every $s>0$. Indeed, both sides are real-analytic on this
connected interval, their composed arguments remain positive, and they
agree on an initial interval. This continuation argument concerns compact
approximants, not the divergent limiting power series.

Weak convergence gives $F_r(s)\to F(s)$ and $G_r(s)\to G(s)$ for each
$s>0$. It also permits the variable-argument passage in
\eqref{gml:eq:finiteanalytic}. For example, on any interval $s\ge\delta>0$,
\[
 |F_r'(s)|=\int\frac{t}{(1+st)^2}\dd\mu_r(t)\le\frac1{4\delta}.
\]
Since $sF_{r+1}(s)\to sF(s)>0$, this equicontinuity bound and pointwise
convergence imply
$F_r(sF_{r+1}(s))\to F(sF(s))$. Thus \eqref{gml:eq:functions} follows.

\subsection{The inverse equation and absence of an atom}
For every $s>0$, $0<F(s)<1$ because $\mu$ has positive mass away from zero.
Moreover
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:Uderivative}
 U'(s)=\int\frac{\dd\mu(t)}{(1+st)^2}>0,
 \qquad U(s)<s.
\end{equation}
The first identity in \eqref{gml:eq:functions} is equivalent to
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:Ucomposition}
 s=U(s)\bigl(1+U(U(s))\bigr).
\end{equation}
If $U$ were bounded, its right side would be bounded, which is impossible.
As $U(0+)=0$, strict monotonicity proves it is a homeomorphism onto
$(0,\infty)$, and \eqref{gml:eq:Uinverse} follows.

Also $F(s)\downarrow\mu(\{0\})$. A positive limit $p$ would give
$sF(s)^2F(sF(s))\ge sp^3$, contradicting \eqref{gml:eq:functions}.
Hence $F(s)\to0$. Since $U(s)\to\infty$, $G(s)=(1+U(s))^{-1}\to0$
as well. This proves the absence of atoms at zero for both measures.

\subsection{Asymptotic series with explicit remainders}
The identity for a finite geometric sum gives, for every integer $N\ge0$
and every $s>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:remainder}
 F(s)=\sum_{j=0}^N(-1)^ja_js^j
 +(-1)^{N+1}s^{N+1}\int\frac{t^{N+1}}{1+st}\dd\mu(t).
\end{equation}
In particular, the remainder has its displayed sign and absolute value
at most $a_{N+1}s^{N+1}$. The analogous statement holds for $G$ with $c_j$.
Thus the alternating formal series is a genuine Poincar\'e asymptotic
expansion as $s\downarrow0$, with finite-order upper and lower bounds,
even though its infinite sum has radius zero.

The entire functions
\[
 E_a(z)=\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{a_nz^n}{n!},\qquad
 E_c(z)=\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{c_nz^n}{n!}
\]
are the exponential moment transforms of $\mu,\nu$. Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:borel}
 F(s)=\int_0^\infty e^{-v}E_a(-sv)\dd v,
 \qquad G(s)=\int_0^\infty e^{-v}E_c(-sv)\dd v\qquad(s>0).
\end{equation}
To justify this, first use exponential integrability to identify
$E_a(-u)=\int e^{-ut}\dd\mu(t)$, and then use Tonelli's theorem in
\eqref{gml:eq:borel}. The integrand $E_a(-sv)$ lies between $0$ and $1$.
This is a Borel--Laplace representation on the negative real axis.
It means integrating the \emph{whole entire function}; exchanging its
infinite series with this integral is not justified and would recover
the divergent ordinary series.

These constructions select canonical transforms from the moment problem.
They do not assert uniqueness among every possible smooth, non-Stieltjes
solution of the nonlinear equation.

\section{Certified algebraic approximants and Fibonacci exponents}\label{gml:sec:approximants}
\subsection{Alternating pointwise bounds}
\begin{proposition}[Order reversal]\label{gml:prop:order}
Given the Stieltjes transform $H$ of a probability measure on $[0,\infty)$,
the equation
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:rootmap}
 y+sy^2H(sy)=1
\end{equation}
has exactly one solution in $(0,1)$ for each $s>0$. Increasing $H$
pointwise decreases this solution pointwise.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The left side is continuous, is $0$ at $y=0$, exceeds $1$ at $y=1$, and
is strictly increasing. For the last assertion, each function
$y^2/(1+sty)$ has derivative
$y(2+sty)/(1+sty)^2>0$ on $y>0$, so its average is increasing.
If $H\ge\widetilde H$, the left side for $H$ is at least that for
$\widetilde H$ at every $y$, and its unique crossing occurs no later.
\end{proof}

Since $F_0=1\ge F$, repeated order reversal in
\eqref{gml:eq:finiteanalytic} gives
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:brackets}
 F_1\le F_3\le F_5\le\cdots\le F
 \le\cdots\le F_4\le F_2\le F_0
 \qquad\text{on }(0,\infty).
\end{equation}
For nesting, start with $F_2\le F_0$ and apply the same reversal
successively. Section~\ref{gml:sec:limit} proves that both bounding sequences
converge to $F$. The convergence is uniform on compact subintervals of
$(0,\infty)$ by monotonicity and continuity.

Every $B_r$ is algebraic. Indeed, if $C_r$ satisfies a nonzero polynomial
relation $P(z,C_r(z))=0$, then $B_{r+1}=C_r(zB_{r+1})$ satisfies
$P(zB_{r+1},B_{r+1})=0$. The substitution $z^iy^j\mapsto z^iy^{i+j}$
is injective on polynomials, so this relation is nonzero. Start with
$B_0=1$ and use $C_r=(1-zB_r)^{-1}$.

The first two bounds are especially simple:
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:firstbounds}
 \frac{2}{1+\sqrt{1+4s}}=F_1(s)\le F(s)\le F_2(s),
 \qquad F_2(s)+sF_2(s)^3=1.
\end{equation}
The upper bound is the unique positive root of the cubic. To derive it,
write $v=F_1(sy)$, so that $v+syv^2=1$ and $1-y=sy^2v$; eliminating $v$
gives $1-y=sy^3$. Therefore \eqref{gml:eq:brackets} is also an effective route
to certified bounds using algebraic root isolation or nested interval
bisection, without truncating a divergent ordinary series at large $s$.

\subsection{The Fibonacci pattern}
Let $f_0=0$, $f_1=1$, and $f_{r+2}=f_{r+1}+f_r$ be the Fibonacci numbers.
\begin{theorem}[Endpoint exponents of every approximant]\label{gml:thm:fibonacci}
For each fixed $r\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:fibpower}
 F_r(s)\sim s^{-\alpha_r},\qquad
 \alpha_r=\frac{f_r}{f_{r+2}},\qquad s\to\infty.
\end{equation}
Every leading constant is $1$. The exponents alternate around $\alpha$
and converge to it:
\[
 0,\quad \frac12,\quad\frac13,\quad\frac25,\quad\frac38,
 \quad\frac5{13},\quad\frac8{21},\ldots\longrightarrow\alpha.
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Put $U_r(s)=sF_r(s)$. The finite identity yields
\[
 U_{r+1}^{-1}(t)=t(1+U_r(t)),\qquad U_0(t)=t.
\]
This inverse relation also proves inductively that every $U_r$ is an
increasing homeomorphism onto $(0,\infty)$. If $U_r(t)\sim t^{b_r}$,
then its successor's inverse is asymptotic to $t^{1+b_r}$, so
$U_{r+1}(s)\sim s^{1/(1+b_r)}$. With $b_0=1$, this gives
$b_r=f_{r+1}/f_{r+2}$, including the constant $1$. Since
$\alpha_r=1-b_r$, the formula follows. The elementary recurrence
$b\mapsto1/(1+b)$ has fixed point $\beta$ and reverses the order around
it, giving the stated alternating convergence.
\end{proof}

The brackets and this theorem alone determine
$\log F(s)/\log s\to-\alpha$. They do \emph{not} justify the full
constant-one equivalent by interchanging $r\to\infty$ with
$s\to\infty$. The next section supplies a separate rigidity argument
that closes precisely this gap.

\section{Why the golden-ratio law has leading constant one}\label{gml:sec:golden}
The derivation below avoids assuming regular variation or differentiable
asymptotics. Its conclusion is stronger than simply guessing a power in
the nonlinear equation.

\begin{lemma}[An asymptotic iteration rigidity principle]\label{gml:lem:rigidity}
Suppose $g:\R\to\R$ is increasing and continuous, $g(x)<x$,
$g(x)\to\infty$ as $x\to\infty$, and
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:gconstraint}
 g(g(x))+g(x)=x-\eps(x),\qquad \eps(x)\to0.
\end{equation}
Then $g(x)-\beta x\to0$, where $\beta^2+\beta=1$ and $\beta>0$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Put $d(x)=g(x)-\beta x$. Substitution in \eqref{gml:eq:gconstraint} gives
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:dcontraction}
 d(x)=-\beta d(g(x))-\beta\eps(x).
\end{equation}
Fix a sufficiently large real $X$. For $x>X$, iterate $g$ until it first
reaches a point at most $X$, and let $N=N(x)$ be that number of steps.
It is finite: otherwise the decreasing orbit has a limit at least $X$,
and continuity makes that limit a fixed point, contrary to $g(y)<y$.
By monotonicity, the exit point lies in $[g(X),X]$. Also $N(x)\to\infty$
as $x\to\infty$, because every fixed finite iterate of $g$ tends to infinity.

Let $M_X=\sup_{[g(X),X]}|d|$ and
$E_X=\sup_{y\ge X}|\eps(y)|$. Iterating \eqref{gml:eq:dcontraction} gives
\[
 |d(x)|\le\beta^{N(x)}M_X+
           \frac{\beta}{1-\beta}E_X.
\]
Take the limit superior as $x\to\infty$, then let $X\to\infty$.
The result is zero, proving the lemma.
\end{proof}

Apply this lemma to
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:gdef}
 g(x)=\log U(e^x).
\end{equation}
The properties of $U$ prove all its monotonicity, continuity and range
hypotheses. From \eqref{gml:eq:Ucomposition},
\[
 U(U(s))=\frac{s}{U(s)}-1
        =\frac{s}{U(s)}(1-F(s)).
\]
Taking logarithms at $s=e^x$ yields
\[
 g(g(x))+g(x)=x+\log(1-F(e^x)).
\]
Thus $\eps(x)=-\log(1-F(e^x))\to0$, since $F(e^x)\to0$.
Lemma~\ref{gml:lem:rigidity} now gives
\[
 \log\frac{U(s)}{s^\beta}\longrightarrow0.
\]
Exponentiating proves $U(s)\sim s^\beta$, hence
$F(s)=U(s)/s\sim s^{-\alpha}$. Finally
$G(s)=(1+U(s))^{-1}\sim s^{-\beta}$.
This proves \eqref{gml:eq:transformpower}, with no unspecified multiplicative
constant and no exchange of the two limits in the preceding section.

\section{Recovering the measures near zero}\label{gml:sec:tauberian}
For completeness we prove the precise special case of the Stieltjes
Tauberian theorem used here. General versions and their history are
available in \cite{langer}. The normalization of the transform matters.

\begin{lemma}[Power-law Stieltjes Tauberian lemma]\label{gml:lem:tauberian}
Let $\sigma$ be a probability measure on $[0,\infty)$ and
$H(s)=\int(1+st)^{-1}\dd\sigma(t)$. If
\[
 H(s)\sim K s^{-\gamma}\quad(s\to\infty),\qquad
 K>0,\quad0<\gamma<1,
\]
then
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:tauberian}
 \sigma([0,t])\sim
 K\frac{\sin(\pi\gamma)}{\pi\gamma}t^\gamma
 \qquad(t\downarrow0).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Let $\eta_s$ be the probability measure of the rescaled variable $y=st$,
weighted by $(1+st)^{-1}/H(s)$. Regard it as a measure on the compact
interval $[0,1]$ using the coordinate $w=y/(1+y)$, with $y=\infty$
corresponding to $w=1$. For $u>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:etas}
 \int\frac{1+y}{1+uy}\dd\eta_s(y)
   =\frac{H(us)}{H(s)}\longrightarrow u^{-\gamma}.
\end{equation}
The integrand is the bounded continuous function
$[1+(u-1)w]^{-1}$ on $[0,1]$.

Consider the probability measure on $(0,\infty)$ with density
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:etadensity}
 \frac{\sin(\pi\gamma)}\pi\frac{y^{\gamma-1}}{1+y}.
\end{equation}
Euler's beta integral
$\int_0^\infty y^{\gamma-1}(1+y)^{-1}\dd y=\pi/\sin(\pi\gamma)$
normalizes it. Substitution $v=uy$ shows that its integral in
\eqref{gml:eq:etas} is $u^{-\gamma}$. This probability measure is the unique
one on the compact $w$-interval with those integrals. Indeed, for
$|u-1|<1$ expand $[1+(u-1)w]^{-1}$ uniformly as a power series. The
integrals determine all moments of $w$; polynomials are dense in
continuous functions on $[0,1]$, so these moments determine the measure.

Every subsequential weak limit of $\eta_s$ therefore equals
\eqref{gml:eq:etadensity}. Compactness proves convergence of the whole family.
In particular, the limit has no mass at $y=1$ or at either compactification
endpoint. The bounded function $(1+y)\mathbf1_{\{y\le1\}}$ is continuous
except at $y=1$, so weak convergence gives
\[
 \frac{\sigma([0,1/s])}{H(s)}
 =\int_{y\le1}(1+y)\dd\eta_s(y)
 \longrightarrow\frac{\sin(\pi\gamma)}\pi
                   \int_0^1y^{\gamma-1}\dd y
 =\frac{\sin(\pi\gamma)}{\pi\gamma}.
\]
Using the assumed equivalent for $H(s)$ proves \eqref{gml:eq:tauberian}.
\end{proof}

Apply the lemma to $F$ with $(K,\gamma)=(1,\alpha)$ and to $G$ with
$(K,\gamma)=(1,\beta)$. This proves \eqref{gml:eq:CDF}.
In particular, arbitrarily small positive intervals adjacent to zero
have positive mass. The result is about \emph{distribution functions}:
it does not justify differentiating the equivalent or asserting a density.

\begin{corollary}[Sharp negative-moment thresholds]\label{gml:cor:negative}
For $q>0$,
\[
 \int t^{-q}\dd\mu(t)<\infty\ \Longleftrightarrow\ q<\alpha,
 \qquad
 \int t^{-q}\dd\nu(t)<\infty\ \Longleftrightarrow\ q<\beta.
\]
At the critical exponent $\gamma$ for either law $\sigma$,
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:critical}
 \int_{[\varepsilon,1]}t^{-\gamma}\dd\sigma(t)
 \sim\frac{\sin(\pi\gamma)}\pi\log\frac1\varepsilon.
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
For $0<x\le1$,
$x^{-q}=1+q\int_x^1t^{-q-1}\dd t$. Tonelli's theorem reduces finiteness
near zero to that of $\int_0^1t^{-q-1}\sigma([0,t])\dd t$.
The distribution-function equivalent makes this the elementary integral
of $t^{\gamma-q-1}$. The part on $[1,\infty)$ is bounded by the total
mass. At $q=\gamma$, integration by parts on $[\varepsilon,1]$ gives a
bounded endpoint term plus
$\gamma\int_\varepsilon^1t^{-\gamma-1}\sigma([0,t])\dd t$.
Its equivalent is the asserted logarithm. Possible endpoint atoms do not
alter it: the distribution-function equivalent makes their scaled
contribution bounded, hence negligible relative to the logarithm.
\end{proof}

\section{From global growth to consecutive-coefficient asymptotics}\label{gml:sec:ratios}
The earlier root equivalent by itself cannot control neighboring ratios.
The moment construction supplies the missing convexity.

\begin{lemma}[A convex-slope principle]\label{gml:lem:slopes}
Suppose $m_n>0$ is log-convex and
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:slopeshyp}
 \log m_n=n\log n-n\log\log n-n+o(n).
\end{equation}
Then $m_{n+1}/m_n\sim n/\log n$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Let $h(n)=\log m_n$, and interpolate linearly between integers. Log-convexity
makes this interpolation convex. Fix $0<\delta<1$. The forward slope
$h(n+1)-h(n)$ is bounded below by the secant from
$\lfloor(1-\delta)n\rfloor$ to $n$, and above by the secant from $n$
to $\lceil(1+\delta)n\rceil$. Inserting \eqref{gml:eq:slopeshyp}, accounting
for rounding by $o(1)$ in the quotients, gives
\begin{align*}
 \liminf_{n\to\infty}\left(h(n+1)-h(n)-\log n+\log\log n\right)
 &\ge -\frac{1-\delta}{\delta}\log(1-\delta)-1,\\
 \limsup_{n\to\infty}\left(h(n+1)-h(n)-\log n+\log\log n\right)
 &\le \frac{1+\delta}{\delta}\log(1+\delta)-1.
\end{align*}
Both right sides tend to zero as $\delta\downarrow0$. Hence the expression
inside parentheses tends to zero. Exponentiation proves the result.
\end{proof}

Strict log-convexity from Section~\ref{gml:sec:hankel} and
\eqref{gml:eq:oldgrowth} immediately prove the first equivalent in
\eqref{gml:eq:ratios}. It remains to transfer the sharp logarithmic growth to $c_n$;
the factor $2^{n-1}$ in \eqref{gml:eq:crudeC} is too large for this purpose.

\begin{proposition}[Sharper comparison for the companion sequence]\label{gml:prop:companion}
As $n\to\infty$,
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:Clog}
 \log c_n=\log a_{n-1}+O(\log n)
 =n\log n-n\log\log n-n+
 O\left(\frac{n\log\log n}{\log n}\right).
\end{equation}
Consequently $c_{n+1}/c_n\sim n/\log n$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Keeping track of the number of parts in \eqref{gml:eq:compositions}, and
using \eqref{gml:eq:supermult}, gives
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:binomialC}
 a_{n-1}\le c_n\le\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\binom{n-1}{j}a_j.
\end{equation}
Write $r_j=a_{j+1}/a_j$, which is increasing and satisfies
$r_j\sim j/\log j$. Put $m=\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor$.
The part $j\le m$ of the upper sum is at most $2^{n-1}a_m=o(a_{n-1})$
by \eqref{gml:eq:oldgrowth}. For $j>m$, monotonicity of $r_j$ gives
\[
 a_j\le a_{n-1}r_m^{-(n-1-j)}.
\]
Thus the rest of the sum is bounded by
\[
 a_{n-1}(1+1/r_m)^{n-1}
 \le a_{n-1}\exp((n-1)/r_m)
 =a_{n-1}\exp(O(\log n)).
\]
Together with the lower bound this proves the first equality in
\eqref{gml:eq:Clog}. The second follows from \eqref{gml:eq:oldgrowth} and
$\log r_{n-1}=O(\log n)$. Finally apply Lemma~\ref{gml:lem:slopes} to $c$,
whose log-convexity has already been proved.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[A quantitative, though slow, ratio estimate]
For $m=a$ or $m=c$,
\[
 \frac{m_{n+1}}{m_n}
 =\frac{n}{\log n}\left(1+
 O\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log\log n}{\log n}}\right)\right).
\]
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Let $h_0(t)=t\log t-t\log\log t-t$ and
$e_n=\log\log n/\log n$. On $[n/2,2n]$, the available logarithmic
estimate has error $O(ne_n)$, uniformly. For secants over length
$\delta n$, this contributes $O(e_n/\delta)$. Since
$h_0''(t)=O(1/n)$ there, the secant differs from $h_0'(n)$ by
$O(\delta)$; also $h_0'(n)=\log n-\log\log n+O(1/\log n)$.
Take $\delta=\sqrt{e_n}$ in the convex-slope bounds. Rounding endpoints
contributes only $O(\log n/(\delta n))$. Exponentiation gives the result.
\end{proof}
This is not a proof that $c_n\sim a_{n-1}$. The polynomial-factor
comparison is sufficient for the asserted logarithmic and ratio laws,
and no stronger equivalent is used below.

\section{The far tails from size-biased concentration}\label{gml:sec:tails}
We prove a general lemma and then apply it to both measures. This keeps the
lower-tail-bound argument separate from an insufficient Markov upper bound.

\begin{lemma}[A moment-growth tail principle]\label{gml:lem:tails}
Let $\sigma$ be a probability measure on $[0,\infty)$ with positive
moments $m_n$, unbounded support, and
\[
 \log m_n=n\log n-n\log\log n-n+o(n),\qquad
 r_n:=m_{n+1}/m_n\sim n/\log n.
\]
Then $\log\sigma([x,\infty))\sim-x\log x$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For the upper bound, Markov's inequality gives
$\sigma([x,\infty))\le m_n/x^n$. Choose
$n=\lfloor x\log x\rfloor$. Then
$\log n-\log\log n=\log x+o(1)$, and consequently
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:tailupper}
 \log\sigma([x,\infty))\le-(1+o(1))x\log x.
\end{equation}

For the reverse inequality define the probability measure
\[
 \dd\sigma_n(t)=\frac{t^n}{m_n}\dd\sigma(t).
\]
Its mean is $r_n$ and its relative variance is
\[
 \frac{\operatorname{Var}_{\sigma_n}(t)}{r_n^2}
   =\frac{r_{n+1}}{r_n}-1\longrightarrow0.
\]
The last limit follows directly from the ratio equivalent at $n$ and $n+1$.
Thus for each fixed $0<\eps<1$,
\begin{equation}\label{gml:eq:concentrate}
 \sigma_n([(1-\eps)r_n,(1+\eps)r_n])\longrightarrow1.
\end{equation}
Fix $\delta>0$ and choose $\eps>0$ so small that
$(1-\eps)(1+\delta)>1$. Now take
$n=\lfloor(1+\delta)x\log x\rfloor$.
Then $r_n\sim(1+\delta)x$, so the interval in \eqref{gml:eq:concentrate}
lies in $[x,\infty)$ for large $x$. On it, $t^n\le((1+\eps)r_n)^n$,
which implies
\[
 \sigma([x,\infty))\ge
 \frac{m_n}{((1+\eps)r_n)^n}
 \sigma_n([(1-\eps)r_n,(1+\eps)r_n]).
\]
Taking logarithms and using both hypotheses gives
\[
 \liminf_{x\to\infty}\frac{\log\sigma([x,\infty))}{x\log x}
 \ge-(1+\delta)(1+\log(1+\eps)).
\]
Let $\delta\downarrow0$ with admissible $\eps\downarrow0$, and combine
with \eqref{gml:eq:tailupper}. This proves the limit.
\end{proof}

Apply the lemma to $\mu$ using \eqref{gml:eq:oldgrowth} and to $\nu$ using
\eqref{gml:eq:Clog}, with their already proved ratio laws. This proves
Theorem~\ref{gml:thm:maintail}. Equivalently, for every fixed $\eps>0$,
for sufficiently large $x$ each tail lies between
$\exp(-(1+\eps)x\log x)$ and $\exp(-(1-\eps)x\log x)$.
Nothing here supplies a multiplicative tail equivalent or a density.

$N(N+1)(N+2)/6$ integer products and $O(N^2)$ integer storage cells.
These are arithmetic-operation counts, not unit-cost bit-complexity

\section{Further research questions and concrete next steps}\label{gml:sec:research}
The moment representation changes the available methods: it allows
orthogonal polynomials, resolvent bounds, probability tilting, and
Tauberian arguments to be used alongside formal reversion. The following
questions are not treated as established consequences of finite data.
They are follow-up tasks for this construction, not claims that every item
is a previously recognized open problem; existing general theorems may
resolve some of them once their hypotheses are checked.

\begin{question}[Regularity and the support of the two laws]
Are $\mu$ and $\nu$ absolutely continuous on $(0,\infty)$? Do their supports
fill all of $[0,\infty)$, or can gaps or positive atoms occur?
\end{question}
The present proof gives unbounded support, no atom at zero, and precise
mass near zero, but none of these excludes gaps or a singular component.
A concrete first step is to check applicable free-convolution regularity
theorems, and then continue the coupled Cauchy-transform identities
into the upper half-plane and analyze boundary values. Any proposed
density asymptotic must include a regularity argument; differentiating
\eqref{gml:eq:CDF} is not sufficient.

\begin{question}[A full asymptotic equivalent for the coefficients]
What is the multiplicative asymptotic equivalent of $a_n$? In particular,
does the finer Bell-number normalization proposed in Part~I
(Conjecture~\ref{conj:finer}) converge to a finite positive constant?
\end{question}
Part~I proposes a correction involving
$\exp(W(n)^2+3W(n))$ relative to Bell numbers. The ratio law proved here
is weaker: an error tending to zero in a single ratio can still accumulate
into an unbounded logarithmic error. A practical next step is to derive a
uniform expansion of the tilted-law mean beyond $n/\log n$, with a
summable remainder after the proposed normalization.

\begin{question}[Second-order golden-ratio asymptotics]
Can $F(s)=s^{-\alpha}(1+o(1))$ be sharpened to an explicit second-order
expansion, and does its correction exhibit oscillation under the inverse
iteration $s_{k+1}=s_k(1+s_{k-1})$?
\end{question}
The exact equation
$d(x)=-\beta d(g(x))-\beta\eps(x)$ is a suitable starting point.
Its negative contraction coefficient makes orbit-dependent alternating
corrections plausible, but the present proof does not establish a global
oscillatory profile or an optimal error rate.

\begin{question}[Effective convergence of the certified approximants]
How quickly do $F_{2r}(s)-F_{2r+1}(s)$ tend to zero, uniformly when $s$
varies over a growing interval? Can one obtain practical a priori
stopping bounds for numerical evaluation?
\end{question}
The proof gives monotone convergence on fixed compact intervals, but
not a useful global complexity estimate. The known finite-stage Fibonacci
exponents indicate why the limits $r\to\infty$ and $s\to\infty$ require
care. Interval evaluation with adaptive algebraic root isolation is a
concrete computational approach.

\begin{question}[Orthogonal polynomials and continued fractions]
What are the asymptotics of the Jacobi or Stieltjes continued-fraction
coefficients associated with $\mu$ and $\nu$?
\end{question}
Strict Hankel positivity guarantees nondegeneracy of every finite
orthogonalization step, while \eqref{gml:eq:detbridge} ties the two systems
together. Exact determinant tables can generate candidate recurrence
coefficients, but the main challenge is to prove their large-degree
behavior and connect it to the two endpoint laws.

\begin{question}[Sharper comparison of the companion sequence]
Does $c_n/a_{n-1}\to1$, and what is the first correction if it does?
\end{question}
Proposition~\ref{gml:prop:companion} supplies only a polynomial-factor bound.
The renewal recurrence suggests that endpoint terms should dominate its
convolutions. Turning that observation into a uniform bound on the entire
convolution, rather than on a fixed number of terms, would resolve the
question without assuming the full asymptotics of $a_n$.

\begin{question}[Other reversion fixed points and positivity-preserving maps]
Which variants of
$M=1+zM^pB(zM^q)$ preserve normalized Stieltjes moment sequences, and
which OEIS reversion sequences have fixed points of such maps?
\end{question}
The factorization in Section~\ref{gml:sec:operators} proves the case
$(p,q)=(2,1)$. It is not a proof for arbitrary $p,q$. Positive block
operators and noncrossing transforms offer testable sufficient mechanisms;
negative Hankel minors provide exact obstructions in unsuccessful cases.

\begin{question}[A modular formalization in ProveIt]
Can the proof be formalized with reusable lemmas for formal transforms,
positive kernels, compact moment limits, and iteration rigidity?
\end{question}
A feasible first stage is the formal coefficient stabilization and the
bivariate determinant identities, followed by the finite-remainder formula
and the convex-slope lemma. The operator-to-measure limit is a separate
analytic component. The scalar rigidity lemma is comparatively isolated
and could be formalized independently. No code included here is presented
as a completed Lean formalization.

\section{Conclusion}
A088714 and A088713 are not merely two rapidly growing coefficient lists.
They are the determinate moment sequences of a coupled pair of positive
measures. This representation proves every strict Hankel inequality,
provides monotone algebraic bounds for the associated transforms, and
makes their divergent ordinary generating functions meaningful on the
negative axis through explicit Stieltjes and Borel--Laplace formulas.

The nonlinear inverse relation $U^{-1}(t)=t(1+U(t))$ forces complementary
golden-ratio exponents, including their leading constants. Part~I's
Bell-scale coefficient bound, together with the new convexity, determines
consecutive ratios and the far-tail logarithmic law. The resulting picture
is rigorous at both endpoints while leaving the finer coefficient
normalization, measure regularity, and second-order behavior clearly open.

\section{Dependency and novelty audit}\label{gml:app:audit}
The main logical dependencies are as follows. The formal equation and
the two compact positive-operator constructions prove existence of a
limiting moment measure. The inherited growth bound supplies exponential
integrability and determinacy, and also rules out bounded support.
Moment positivity proves the strict Hankel and log-convexity statements.
Weak convergence of compact approximants proves the real functional
equation. Its inverse form, independently of any ratio asymptotics,
proves the golden-ratio transform law. The self-contained Tauberian lemma
then proves the small-$t$ distribution-function laws. Separately,
log-convexity plus the inherited growth bound proves the consecutive
ratios, and these prove the far tails by size-biasing.

This separation avoids several invalid shortcuts: no divergent ordinary
series is substituted at a positive real argument; no neighboring ratio
is deduced just from an $n$th-root equivalent; no infinite zero or
positivity statement is inferred from finite determinants; no cumulative
mass asymptotic is differentiated to invent a density; and no numerical
agreement is used to exchange the compact-approximation and large-argument
limits.
