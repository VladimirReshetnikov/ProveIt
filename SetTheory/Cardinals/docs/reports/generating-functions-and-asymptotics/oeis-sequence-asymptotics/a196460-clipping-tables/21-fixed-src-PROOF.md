# Zero-auxiliary clipping tables and A196460

4 October 2026. A separate mathematical continuation of the accepted fixed-table
zero-versus-one auxiliary-variable criterion. This is not a revision of
Reports 69 or 70 and is not a claim of priority or formal verification.

## Scope and main conclusions

Fix a positive clipping threshold K=n+1. A Boolean K by K table S accepts an
input (A,B) of positive integers according to (min(A,K),min(B,K)). Count tables
for which one integer-coefficient polynomial R(A,B), with no existential
auxiliary and no degree restriction, vanishes exactly on the accepted inputs.
Write that count as C_n. Then

    C_n = 1+a_n,
    a_n = sum_(r,c=0)^n binom(n,r) binom(n,c) 2^((n-r)(n-c))
        = sum_(j=0)^n binom(n,j) (1+2^j)^n.                 (1)

Thus a_n is OEIS A196460. For every fixed integer M>=0,

    a_n/2^(n^2) = sum_(k=0)^M P_k(n) 2^(-kn)
                  + O_M(n^(M+1) 2^(-(M+1)n)),             (2)

where the coefficient polynomials are explicit:

    P_k(x) = sum_(r=0)^k binom(x,r) binom(x,k-r) 2^(r(k-r)). (3)

The binomials in (3) are falling-factorial polynomials. The first terms are

    P_0=1,
    P_1=2x,
    P_2=3x^2-x,
    P_3=(13/3)x^3-5x^2+(2/3)x,
    P_4=(27/4)x^4-(33/2)x^3+(41/4)x^2-x/2.

There is also an all-orders inverse expansion at the sequence values. With
lambda=ln(2), s=sqrt(log_2(a_n)) and t=2^(-s),

    n = s + sum_(k=1)^M D_k(s)t^k + O_M(s^M t^(M+1))       (4)

for every fixed M>=1. A coefficient recurrence, the first three D_k, and a
proof without interpolating the discrete sequence appear below. The same
fixed-order expansions hold with a_n replaced by C_n. Their exact small-index
inverse conventions differ and are stated separately.

## 1. Why these are precisely the zero-auxiliary tables

The dependency is the theorem of ONE_WITNESS.md, Section 6, accepted in
AUDIT.md, Section 8. For completeness, its argument can be restated briefly.
If a polynomial vanishes on a cell with a tail coordinate, fixing the finite
coordinates leaves a polynomial vanishing on an infinite Cartesian grid.
Induction using the univariate finite-root theorem makes this restricted
polynomial identically zero. Every replacement of a tail coordinate by an
arbitrary positive integer must therefore remain accepted. In two dimensions:

* accepting (i,K), i<K, forces the entire row i;
* accepting (K,j), j<K, forces the entire column j;
* accepting (K,K) forces the entire table.

Conversely, if these closure conditions hold, multiply over accepted cells
the sums of squared deviations in their fixed, non-tail coordinates. A factor
vanishes exactly on the coordinate flat generated by its cell, and closure
ensures this flat accepts no unintended positive input. The empty product is
1. An all-tail factor is the empty sum 0, as required for the full table.
This proves zero-auxiliary representability. For every other table the
accepted one-positive-auxiliary construction gives exact minimum one.

The classification is for fixed finite clipping tables, one polynomial
equality, positive integer inputs, and table-dependent coefficients and
degree. It makes no assertion about global MRDP minimality or the auxiliary
count of a composed POWER construction.

## 2. Counting by boundary marks

If the corner (K,K) is accepted, exactly one table is possible: the full table.
Otherwise let R be the subset of the n finite row labels whose right-boundary
cell (i,K) is accepted, and let T be the subset of finite column labels whose
bottom-boundary cell (K,j) is accepted. These choices are uniquely read from
the table, even if some other interior rows or columns happen to be all ones.

Write r=|R| and c=|T|. Closure forces precisely all interior cells in one of
the selected rows or columns. The remaining (n-r)(n-c) interior cells are
independent Boolean choices. The boundary is already specified and the
corner rejected. Consequently there are

    binom(n,r) binom(n,c) 2^((n-r)(n-c))

tables with these boundary cardinalities. Summing and adding the full table
proves the first equality in (1). Reindexing j=n-r and summing over c yields
sum_c binom(n,c)(2^j)^(n-c)=(1+2^j)^n, proving the second.

In particular C_0,C_1,... begin

    2, 6, 48, 1194, 113856, 46857666, 83540629608.

There is no double counting of the full-interior table with rejected corner:
its boundary can have many different patterns, and those are different
clipping tables. Only the full table with accepted corner is the extra 1.

## 3. Labeled bipartite graphs and the existing relational model

Take two distinguished, labeled parts L and R, each of size n. A bipartite
graph here is a subset of L times R; it has n^2 independently available edges.
Let i(G) be the total number of isolated vertices in both parts. Then

    a_n = sum_G 2^(i(G)).                                  (5)

Indeed, count pairs (G,U), where U is an arbitrary subset of the isolated
vertices. Choosing r marked vertices on the left and c on the right forces
their incident edges absent and leaves all (n-r)(n-c) other edges free. This
is the double sum in (1). Equivalently, choose an active subset in each part
and then any graph on those active vertices. For a fixed ambient graph,
nonisolated vertices must be active and each isolated vertex independently
may be active or inactive, giving exactly 2^(i(G)) choices.

The direct clipping-table bijection takes G to be the graph of rejected
interior cells; accepted boundary row and column labels are the marked
isolated vertices. Thus a uniform n-by-n Bernoulli(1/2) bipartite graph obeys

    a_n/2^(n^2) = E[2^(i(G))].                              (6)

This is a useful probabilistic reading of the correction terms, not a claim
to invent an existing combinatorial interpretation. Svatos, Jung, Toth,
Wang and Kuzelka's 2023 paper explicitly lists A196460 against the relational
sentence

    (forall x forall y, U(x) or B(x,y))
    and (forall x forall y, B(x,y) or U_1(y)).

Here the independent unary predicates U and U_1 select the active row and
column labels, and B is forced true except possibly where both labels are
active. Setting edges to be the false entries of B recovers exactly the
active-subset graph model. This is an elementary interpretation of their
displayed sentence. Their table is Appendix A.1, Table 3, printed page 32
(PDF page index 31) of arXiv:2302.04606v1. The present clipping-table
identification uses the separately proved polynomial-closure criterion.

## 4. Exact regrouping and a uniform tail estimate

Divide the double sum by 2^(n^2). Its (r,c) summand is

    binom(n,r) binom(n,c) 2^(-n(r+c)+rc).

Grouping by k=r+c gives the exact finite identity

    a_n/2^(n^2) = sum_(k=0)^(2n) P_k(n)2^(-kn).             (7)

At integer n, binom(n,r)=0 for r>n. Thus the polynomial formula (3) agrees
with the valid finite sum, including k>n, and P_k(n)=0 for k>2n.
Each P_k has degree exactly k: its leading coefficient is the positive
number sum_(r=0)^k 2^(r(k-r))/(r!(k-r)!). Formula (7) alone does not justify
an asymptotic expansion; k grows to 2n. The following estimate does.

For all 0<=k<=2n, rc<=k^2/4 and Vandermonde's identity imply

    0 <= P_k(n)2^(-kn)
      <= B_(n,k) := binom(2n,k) 2^(-kn+k^2/4).             (8)

If 0<=k<=n-1, then

    B_(n,k+1)/B_(n,k)
      = ((2n-k)/(k+1)) 2^(-n+k/2+1/4)
      <= rho_n := 2n 2^(-n/2+1/4),

and rho_n tends to zero. For n>=M+1 and rho_n<1,

    sum_(k=M+1)^n B_(n,k)
      <= B_(n,M+1)/(1-rho_n)
      <= ((2n)^(M+1)/(M+1)!)
         2^(-(M+1)n+(M+1)^2/4)/(1-rho_n).                 (9)

For n<k<=2n, the function -nk+k^2/4 is decreasing on [n,2n],
so it is at most -3n^2/4. Hence

    sum_(k=n+1)^(2n) B_(n,k) <= 2^(2n-3n^2/4).             (10)

For every fixed M the bound (10) is
o(n^(M+1)2^(-(M+1)n)); its logarithm has a negative quadratic leading term.
Combining (7)--(10) proves (2). Moreover the remainder after M sectors is
nonnegative. The displayed bounds are explicit and need no probabilistic
independence approximation. This proves in particular the leading
equivalence credited to Kotesovec in OEIS.

The expansion means every fixed truncation has the stated error. No
convergence assertion is made for an infinite sum with noninteger x.

The order of the remainder is sharp. Apply (2) with M+1 and subtract its
first M+1 terms. Since P_(M+1) has positive leading coefficient alpha_(M+1),

    a_n/2^(n^2)-sum_(k=0)^M P_k(n)2^(-kn)
      ~ alpha_(M+1) n^(M+1)2^(-(M+1)n),
    alpha_k = sum_(r=0)^k 2^(r(k-r))/(r!(k-r)!).

There is also a direct factorial-moment interpretation of each sector.
In the random graph of (6), a specified r left and k-r right vertices are
all isolated with probability 2^(-nk+r(k-r)). Summing over the choices gives
E[binom(i(G),k)]=P_k(n)2^(-kn). Identity (7) is consequently the finite
binomial identity 2^(i(G))=sum_k binom(i(G),k), averaged over G.

## 5. How rare is zero auxiliary arity?

There are exactly 2^((n+1)^2) Boolean clipping tables. Therefore the zero-
auxiliary fraction is exactly

    F_n = C_n/2^((n+1)^2)
        = 2^(-2n-1) [a_n/2^(n^2) + 2^(-n^2)].             (11)

For fixed M, substitute (2) into (11), obtaining an all-orders expansion
with the same relative remainder. In particular

    F_n = 2^(-2n-1) [1+2n 2^(-n)+(3n^2-n)2^(-2n)
                    +O(n^3 2^(-3n))],
    F_n ~ (1/2)4^(-n) = 2^(1-2K).

Exactly the complementary fraction 1-F_n has minimum one positive
auxiliary in the stated representation class. The isolated extra full
table contributes precisely 2^(-n^2) inside the brackets of (11), which is
smaller than every fixed sector n^j2^(-kn) as n tends to infinity.

## 6. Logarithmic coefficients

In the formal ring Q[x][[z]], define L_k by

    log(1+sum_(k>=1) P_k(x)z^k) = sum_(k>=1) L_k(x)z^k.

Differentiating in z and comparing coefficients gives the constructive
recurrence

    L_k = P_k - (1/k) sum_(j=1)^(k-1) j L_j P_(k-j).        (12)

Induction gives degree(L_k)<=k. Explicitly,

    L_1=2x,
    L_2=x^2-x,
    L_3=x^3-3x^2+(2/3)x,
    L_4=(19/12)x^4-(15/2)x^3+(101/12)x^2-x/2.

For fixed M>=1, (2) and the Taylor remainder for log(1+u) at u=0 imply

    log_2(a_n)=n^2+(1/lambda) sum_(k=1)^M L_k(n)2^(-kn)
                    +O_M(n^(M+1)2^(-(M+1)n)).             (13)

For detail, (2) with M=0 bounds u=a_n/2^(n^2)-1 by O(n2^(-n)).
It tends to zero. The difference caused by the remainder in (2) has the
same order under the logarithm, whose derivative is bounded near 1.
The omitted Taylor powers are O(u^(M+1)), of the claimed order, and the
retained coefficients are exactly (12).

### Connected graphs explain the leading coefficients

Let b_k=k! alpha_k. It counts simple labeled graphs on k vertices equipped
with a specified proper coloring by two named colors: choose r vertices of
the first color, then choose any of the r(k-r) possible cross-color edges.
Let c_k count the connected such colored graphs, counting the two choices
for the color of an isolated singleton. A colored graph is uniquely a set
of connected colored components. Explicitly, summing products of c_j over
set partitions proves the formal identity

    sum_(k>=0) b_k z^k/k! = exp(sum_(k>=1) c_k z^k/k!).

Taking the top-degree coefficient in the formal definition of L_k therefore
shows that its coefficient of x^k is c_k/k!. No asymptotic theorem about
random graph connectivity is needed. The elementary rooted-component
recurrence is

    c_k = b_k-sum_(j=1)^(k-1) binom(k-1,j-1)c_j b_(k-j),

obtained by isolating the component containing label 1. Initially
c_1,c_2,c_3,c_4 are 2,2,6,38. Every c_k is positive: a star gives a
connected example for k>=2, and singletons give k=1.

## 7. Formal inversion and its sequence-point justification

Set lambda=ln(2)>0. Treat s and the formal variable t as independent during
coefficient extraction. Define D_k(s) recursively in the Laurent-polynomial
ring Q[lambda,lambda^(-1),s,s^(-1)]. Put

    delta_(m-1)(s,t)=sum_(j=1)^(m-1) D_j(s)t^j,

and define

    D_m(s) = -(1/(2s)) [t^m] {
       delta_(m-1)^2
       +(1/lambda) sum_(k=1)^m L_k(s+delta_(m-1))
                             t^k exp(-k lambda delta_(m-1)) }. (14)

Only finite Taylor coefficients are needed at each step. The coefficient
of the new D_m in the full equation is 2s, so the recurrence is unique.
It makes

    2s delta + delta^2
      +(1/lambda) sum_(k>=1)L_k(s+delta)t^k exp(-k lambda delta)

zero as a formal series. The first three coefficients are

    D_1(s) = -1/lambda,
    D_2(s) = -(s+1)/(2lambda) + 1/(2lambda^2 s),
    D_3(s) = -s^2/(2lambda) - 1/(3lambda)
             + 1/lambda^2 + 1/(lambda^2 s).                (15)

Induction in (14) gives D_m(s)=O_m(s^(m-1)) as s tends to infinity.
Indeed, a term of total t-degree m coming from L_k has s-degree at most m:
L_k has degree at most k, while a product of p coefficients D_j with
sum j=m-k has degree at most m-k-p. Polynomial differentiation only
lowers this bound; the exponential introduces powers of the fixed constant
lambda but no new positive s-degree. The delta^2 contribution has degree
at most m-2. Dividing by s proves the bound, including all negative powers.
More precisely, only the direct L_m(s) term can contribute s^m in (14):
every occurrence of a prior D_j lowers the maximum by at least one.
The connected-graph identity thus gives the exact leading coefficient

    D_m(s) ~ -c_m s^(m-1)/(2lambda m!).

To turn the formal calculation into (4), fix M and introduce only the finite
smooth auxiliary function

    H_M(x)=x^2+(1/lambda)sum_(k=1)^M L_k(x)2^(-kx).

This is a finite normal-form approximation, not an interpolation of a_n.
For the actual integer n, put s_n=sqrt(log_2(a_n)), t_n=2^(-s_n), and

    n_hat=s_n+sum_(k=1)^M D_k(s_n)t_n^k.

Equation (13) states

    H_M(n)-s_n^2=O_M(n^(M+1)2^(-(M+1)n)).                  (16)

Also s_n-n=O(2^(-n)), by (13) at first order and division by s_n+n.
Consequently n and n_hat both lie in [s_n-1,s_n+1] for large n, n is
asymptotic to s_n, and 2^(-n)/t_n tends to one. Finite Taylor expansion of
H_M(n_hat)-s_n^2 is precisely the expression annihilated through degree M
by (14). Its remainder is

    H_M(n_hat)-s_n^2=O_M(s_n^(M+1)t_n^(M+1)).              (17)

One direct way to track (17) is to use degree(D_j)<=j-1: every retained
residual coefficient of t-degree r has s-degree at most r. The finitely
many terms of degree r>=M+1 are bounded by s^(M+1)t^(M+1), since st tends
to zero. The analytic exponential Taylor remainders have the same bound
because delta_M=O(t) and the L_k have degree at most k. Negative powers
of s only improve the estimate for s>=1.

Uniformly on [s_n-1,s_n+1],

    H'_M(x)=2x+O_M(x^M2^(-x)) >= s_n

for large n. Subtract (16) and (17) and apply the mean-value theorem.
The resulting error bound is O_M(s_n^M t_n^(M+1)), proving (4).
No derivative of an unspecified interpolation, no analytic continuation of
the sequence, and no convergence of the infinite formal series is used.
Applying (4) one order further also proves the signed sharp remainder

    n-[s_n+sum_(k=1)^M D_k(s_n)t_n^k]
      ~ -c_(M+1) s_n^M t_n^(M+1)/(2lambda (M+1)!).

Thus every fixed inverse truncation eventually overestimates the exact
integer n by a positive error on the displayed exponentially small scale.

Replacing a_n by C_n changes log_2(a_n) by

    log_2(1+1/a_n)=O(2^(-n^2)),

and its square root by O(2^(-n^2)/n). These are smaller than every fixed
error scale in (13) and (4). Therefore all the same fixed-order coefficient
formulas hold at s=sqrt(log_2(C_n)) as n tends to infinity.

## 8. Exact integer inverses and the floor boundary

The double sum gives, for every n>=0,

    2^(n^2) <= a_n <= 2^(n^2+2n) < 2^((n+1)^2).           (18)

The lower bound is its r=c=0 term; for the upper bound replace every
2^((n-r)(n-c)) by 2^(n^2) and sum binomials to get 4^n.
In particular

    floor(sqrt(log_2(a_n)))=n                              (19)

at every sequence point, including a_0=1. Also a_n is strictly increasing:
the terms j<=n in the single sum for a_(n+1) each dominate twice their
counterpart for a_n, and an additional positive term occurs.

For any real y>=1, let m=floor(sqrt(log_2 y)). By (18), every index below m
qualifies for a_n<=y, every index above m fails, and the index m is decided
by one exact comparison. Thus

    N_a(y):=max{n>=0:a_n<=y}
           = m-1 if y<a_m, and m otherwise.               (20)

When m=0, y>=1=a_0 so only the second branch occurs. For y<1 the defining
set is empty. This exact inverse is more precise than an unqualified floor
of a truncated formal inverse.

To locate the comparison boundary, the forward version of (13) gives

    sqrt(log_2(a_m))
      = m + 2^(-m)/lambda
        + [(m-1)/(2lambda)-1/(2lambda^2 m)]2^(-2m)
        +O(m^2 2^(-3m)).                                 (21)

Thus N_a(y) differs from m precisely in a thin interval starting at the
perfect-square logarithmic boundary: m<=sqrt(log_2 y)<sqrt(log_2(a_m)).
An approximate boundary can classify y only when its distance from that
boundary exceeds the stated error. Inside the uncertainty interval, use
the exact comparison in (20). Taking a floor does not turn a real
asymptotic error automatically into an exact integer answer.

For C_n, (18) implies

    2^(n^2) < C_n < 2^((n+1)^2) for n>=1.

Hence (19) holds with C_n for n>=1. At n=0, however, C_0=2 and
floor(sqrt(log_2 C_0))=1. For y>=2 put m=floor(sqrt(log_2 y)), so m>=1.
Then C_(m-1)<=2^(m^2)<=y (equality is possible when m=1), while
C_(m+1)>y. Therefore (20) holds with C in place of a for all y>=2.
Below 2 no C-index qualifies. Formula (21) has the same fixed-order
expansion for C_m, with a beyond-all-fixed-orders change in its boundary.

## 9. Evidence, attribution, and limits

The fresh standard-library checker constructs rational sparse polynomial
and Laurent-polynomial expressions directly from these formulas. It does
not import or run either source packet, any upstream scientific code,
counter interpreter, physical simulator, or Lean. It verified:

* all 66,066 Boolean clipping tables with K=1,2,3,4;
* all 66,067 bipartite graphs with n=0,1,2,3,4, using the isolated-vertex weight;
* equality of the single sum, double sum, and sector regrouping for 0<=n<=32;
* 231 values of the coefficient polynomials P_0 through P_6;
* the displayed P and L coefficients and exact formal inverse cancellation
  through t^6, including the positive-degree bounds on D_1 through D_6;
* the elementary a_n and C_n brackets for the same 33 indices.

A separate fresh check verifies the leading-coefficient identities for
P_k, L_k, and D_k against b_k and c_k through k=6. The connected colored
counts obtained are 2,2,6,38,390,6062.

These checks corroborate the proof; they do not replace its infinite-grid,
uniform-tail, logarithmic-error, or mean-value arguments. The JSON evidence
also supplies coefficients through order six.

OEIS A196460 credits Paul D. Hanna, 2 October 2011, for the entry and
Vaclav Kotesovec, 25 June 2013, for a_n~2^(n^2). The retrieved primary entry
gives the exact sum, but no proof or article reference for that leading
equivalence. Its ordinary and exponential generating functions are formal:
(18) makes both power series have radius zero. We do not use them as
analytic functions.

The 2023 first-order model-counting paper supplies the already-existing
relational interpretation discussed in Section 3. A bounded web search
found no all-orders asymptotic or inverse treatment of A196460, but this is
not an exhaustive priority search. Bounded workspace text searches, the
retrieved ProveIt root and report catalogues, and search-engine queries
restricted to that public repository found no prior A196460 treatment.
Some deliberately unreadable local audit fixtures and unavailable web
pages limit that negative evidence. No claim of novelty follows.

The mathematical contribution of this packet is the explicitly proved
bridge from the accepted clipping-table classification to this known
sequence, together with a self-contained all-orders estimate and carefully
scoped inversion. Everything remains separate from Reports 69 and 70.
