\begin{abstract}
Let $p(n)$ count ordinary integer partitions whose allowed part sizes are
$1,2,5,14,42,\ldots$, the distinct Catalan numbers. This is OEIS A033552.
We develop a complete expansion in inverse powers of a logarithmic size
parameter, including a nonconstant periodic amplitude, an explicit first
correction, and an inverse expansion for the threshold at which $p(n)$ reaches
a prescribed value. The argument combines an all-orders coefficient saddle
estimate, a Barnes $G$ product identity, and an exponentially convergent
Fourier series involving $\Gamma(s)\zeta(1+s)$. The contour estimate controls
all noncentral arcs; the expansion is therefore more than a formal saddle
calculation. A further theorem gives a phase-dependent limiting distribution
for the largest allowed-part index in a uniformly chosen partition.
Exact computations through $n=10^6$ and independent high-precision checks
accompany the proofs. The inspected OEIS entry contains no asymptotic formula;
we do not claim that it records an explicit asymptotic conjecture, or that an
exhaustive literature-priority search has been completed. The sequence-specific
formulas and proofs below are the substantive output, not a claim that the
classical saddle or periodic-partition methods themselves are new.
\end{abstract}

\begin{resultbox}
\textbf{Principal formula.} Put $\alpha=\log 4$ and let $\mu$ be the large
solution of $n=4^\mu/\sqrt{\pi\mu}$. Then
\[
 p(n)=\e^{C+\Psi_\alpha(\mu)}\mu^{23/8}
 \exp\!\left(\frac\alpha2\mu^2-\frac{1+3\alpha}{2}\mu\right)
 \left(1+\frac{D_\alpha(\mu)}\mu+O(\mu^{-2})\right).
\]
Here $C$ is explicit and $\Psi_\alpha,D_\alpha$ are smooth, one-periodic
functions given below. Every subsequent inverse-power coefficient is
constructively determined. Replacing $\Psi_\alpha$ by a constant does
\emph{not} give an asymptotic equivalent.
\end{resultbox}

\part{All-orders asymptotics, periodic corrections and inverse growth}

\section{The problem, its status, and the contribution}
\label{ctp:sec:intro}

\subsection{The sequence and the normalization}
For $k\geq1$ define
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:catalan}
 c_k=\frac1{k+1}\binom{2k}{k},\qquad
 F(z)=\prod_{k\geq1}(1-z^{c_k})^{-1},\qquad
 p(n)=[z^n]F(z).
\end{equation}
Thus the allowed part sizes are $c_1=1,c_2=2,c_3=5,c_4=14,\ldots$.
The initial terms are
\[
 1,1,2,2,3,4,5,6,7,8,10,11,13,14,17,19,22,24,27,30,\ldots.
\]
The index starts at $n=0$. It is important not to include both $c_0$ and
$c_1$: doing so would create two colors of the unit part and a different
sequence. The definition and generating function agree with OEIS
A033552~\cite{oeis}.

The inspected entry supplies a generating function, a recursive computation,
and a reference to Pak's enumerative-complexity survey, but no asymptotic
formula. Pak explicitly discusses partitions into Catalan numbers among
restricted-partition counting problems that may be less tractable than some
classical examples~\cite[\S2.5]{pak}. That discussion concerns exact counting
complexity. An asymptotic expansion, however accurate to fixed order, does
not by itself settle that complexity question.

All logarithms in this article are natural. A periodic function is evaluated
on a real argument by periodic extension. All $O$-estimates concern the limit
of that argument, or of $n$, to positive infinity; their constants may depend
on a fixed truncation order but not on a fractional phase.

\subsection{What is established here, and what is not}
The principal contribution is a self-contained chain from the exact product
\eqref{ctp:eq:catalan} to the following results: an all-orders saddle expansion
with a contour proof; a completely normalized, phase-resolved explicit
expansion; a first correction containing derivatives of the periodic
amplitude; an inverse threshold expansion with error transport; and a
phase-dependent law for the largest part index. A general proposition
explains which coefficients depend only on the exponential and polynomial
growth of the allowed part sizes.

Periodicity in asymptotics of geometrically restricted partitions is a
classical phenomenon. Erd\H{o}s and Richmond discuss the de Bruijn--Mahler
phenomenon and its extensions~\cite{erdosrichmond}. Mellin analysis of
harmonic sums is developed systematically by Flajolet, Gourdon, and
Dumas~\cite{fgd}. We use that context, but prove the concrete estimates and
normalizations needed here rather than importing a vaguely matched theorem.
The explicit Catalan-part formulas below were derived for this report. A
limited search found no source giving this complete collection of formulas;
that observation is not a proof of publication priority.

\writenote{The phenomenon Erd\H{o}s and Richmond name goes back to de
Bruijn's treatment of Mahler's partition problem \cite{debruijn}, which
the delivered bibliography did not list: partitions into powers of a
fixed integer, whose logarithm carries a periodic function of a
logarithmic variable built from $\Gamma(s)\zeta(1+s)$ on the Mellin
lattice. The Catalan parts $c_k\sim4^k/(\sqrt\pi k^{3/2})$ are a
polynomially perturbed geometric sequence of that kind, and
\cref{ctp:lem:Fourier} is the corresponding Mellin computation; see also
the note after its proof.}

The manuscript contains conventional mathematical proofs and executable
checks. It is not a Lean formalization. The numerical checks corroborate,
but do not replace, the arguments. No computer search is used as a substitute
for a missing contour estimate or for a missing asymptotic remainder.

\section{Main asymptotic and inverse formulas}
\label{ctp:sec:main}

\subsection{Constants and the periodic amplitude}
Set
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:constants}
 \alpha=\log4,\qquad A=\frac{1+3\alpha}{2},\qquad
 \eta=\frac{23}{8},\qquad
 C=\frac14\log2+\frac34\log\pi+\log G(3/2),
\end{equation}
where $G$ is the Barnes function, normalized by $G(1)=1$ and
$G(z+1)=\Gamma(z)G(z)$~\cite{dlmfG}.
For the moment allow $\alpha>0$ to vary, and define
\begin{align}
 \kappa&=\frac{\pi^2}{12}-\frac{\gamma^2}{2}-\gamma_1,
       \label{ctp:eq:kappa}\\
 \Psi_\alpha(\theta)
 &=\frac\alpha{12}+\frac\kappa\alpha+
 \frac1\alpha\sum_{\ell\in\Z\setminus\{0\}}
 \Gamma\!\left(\frac{2\pi\ii\ell}{\alpha}\right)
 \zeta\!\left(1+\frac{2\pi\ii\ell}{\alpha}\right)
 \e^{2\pi\ii\ell\theta}.\label{ctp:eq:phase-fourier}
\end{align}
Here $\gamma$ is Euler's constant and $\gamma_1$ is the first Stieltjes
constant, with convention
$\zeta(1+s)=s^{-1}+\gamma-\gamma_1s+O(s^2)$.
The Fourier series and all of its derivatives converge absolutely.
Conjugate terms pair to make $\Psi_\alpha$ real.
A prime denotes differentiation with respect to $\theta$; a partial
$\alpha$-derivative holds $\theta$ fixed. The correction is
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:D}
\boxed{\displaystyle
 D_\alpha(\theta)=\frac16-\frac1{2\alpha}
 -\frac32\partial_\alpha\Psi_\alpha(\theta)
 +\frac{17}{8\alpha}\Psi_\alpha'(\theta)
 -\frac{\Psi_\alpha'(\theta)^2+\Psi_\alpha''(\theta)}{2\alpha^2}.}
\end{equation}
Only after taking the partial derivative do we set $\alpha=\log4$.

Define the large real size coordinate
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:mu}
 n=\frac{4^{\mu(n)}}{\sqrt{\pi\mu(n)}},\qquad
 \mu(n)=-\frac1{2\alpha}W_{-1}\!\left(-\frac{2\alpha}{\pi n^2}\right).
\end{equation}
The negative real branch $W_{-1}$ is intended. For sufficiently large $n$
this is the unique large solution and $\mu(n)\sim(\log n)/\alpha$.

\begin{theorem}[Explicit expansion, including the first correction]
\label{ctp:thm:explicit}
For the sequence \eqref{ctp:eq:catalan}, as $n\to\infty$ through integers,
\begin{align}
 \log p(n)={}&\frac\alpha2\mu^2-A\mu+\eta\log\mu+C+
 \Psi_\alpha(\mu)+\frac{D_\alpha(\mu)}\mu+O(\mu^{-2}).
 \label{ctp:eq:main-log}
\end{align}
Equivalently,
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:main-multiplicative}
 p(n)=\e^{C+\Psi_\alpha(\mu)}\mu^\eta
 \e^{\alpha\mu^2/2-A\mu}
 \left(1+\frac{D_\alpha(\mu)}\mu+O(\mu^{-2})\right).
\end{equation}
For every fixed $J\geq0$ there are smooth, one-periodic functions
$L_1,\ldots,L_J$, with $L_1=D_\alpha$, such that
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:all-orders-explicit}
 \log p(n)=\frac\alpha2\mu^2-A\mu+\eta\log\mu+C+
 \Psi_\alpha(\mu)+\sum_{j=1}^J\frac{L_j(\mu)}{\mu^j}
 +O_J(\mu^{-J-1}).
\end{equation}
Sections~\ref{ctp:sec:saddle}--\ref{ctp:sec:explicit-proof} prove this statement and
give a finite coefficient-construction procedure at each order.
\end{theorem}

The phrase ``all orders'' here means a remainder theorem for every fixed
truncation order. It does not assert convergence of the resulting infinite
inverse-power series, nor does it resolve exponentially smaller sectors.

\begin{corollary}[An unavoidable periodic factor]\label[corollary]{ctp:cor:oscillation}
There is no constant $B$ for which
\[
 p(n)\sim B\mu(n)^\eta\e^{\alpha\mu(n)^2/2-A\mu(n)}.
\]
More precisely, the limit points of the normalized sequence are exactly
\[
 \left[\exp\{C+\min_\theta\Psi_\alpha(\theta)\},\,
       \exp\{C+\max_\theta\Psi_\alpha(\theta)\}\right],
\]
an interval with distinct endpoints.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The gamma function has no zeros, and $\zeta(1+\ii t)\ne0$ for real
$t\ne0$~\cite{dlmfzeros}; hence the first Fourier coefficient in
\eqref{ctp:eq:phase-fourier} is nonzero. Thus $\Psi_\alpha$ is nonconstant.
For any fixed phase $\theta$, round
$4^{j+\theta}/\sqrt{\pi(j+\theta)}$ to the nearest integer $n_j$.
Implicit differentiation of \eqref{ctp:eq:mu} shows
$\mu(n_j)=j+\theta+o(1)$. Every phase therefore occurs as a subsequential
limit. Apply \eqref{ctp:eq:main-multiplicative} and continuity of $\Psi_\alpha$.
\end{proof}

The first Fourier harmonic has amplitude approximately
$0.00100279457386$. The modulation is small but does not tend to zero.
Its mean is approximately $0.64116608028709$. These are reproducible
floating-point evaluations, not interval-certified bounds.

\begin{corollary}[The first three logarithmic growth terms]
\label[corollary]{ctp:cor:logs}
With $X=\log n$,
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:three-logs}
 \log p(n)=\frac{X^2}{2\alpha}+\frac{X\log X}{2\alpha}
 +\left(\frac{\log(\pi/\alpha)-1}{2\alpha}-\frac32\right)X
 +O\bigl((\log X)^2\bigr).
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The defining equation is
$X=\alpha\mu-\tfrac12\log\mu-\tfrac12\log\pi$. Hence
\[
 \alpha\mu=X+\tfrac12\log X+\tfrac12\log(\pi/\alpha)
 +O((\log X)/X).
\]
Substitute this into \eqref{ctp:eq:main-log} and collect terms of orders
$X^2$, $X\log X$, and $X$.
\end{proof}
The positive sign of $X\log X$ is a concrete effect of the factor
$k^{-3/2}$ in the growth of $c_k$. Replacing $c_k$ by $4^k$ too early loses
that information.

\subsection{The inverse threshold}
For real $y>1$ define the integer staircase
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:threshold}
 N(y)=\min\{n\geq0:p(n)\geq y\}.
\end{equation}
Put
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:R-s}
 Y=\log y,\qquad R=\sqrt{2\alpha Y},\qquad s=R/\alpha.
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Inverse growth and its first correction]\label{ctp:thm:inverse}
As $y\to\infty$,
\begin{align}
 \log N(y)={}&R+A-\frac12\log s-\frac12\log\pi\notag\\
 &+\frac{\frac12(A^2-A)-\alpha\eta\log s
 -\alpha\{C+\Psi_\alpha(s+A/\alpha)\}}{R}
 +O\!\left(\frac{(\log R)^2}{R^2}\right).
 \label{ctp:eq:inverse}
\end{align}
In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:inverse-leading}
 N(y)\sim
 8\sqrt{\frac{e\alpha}{\pi}}\,
 \frac{\exp\!\left(\sqrt{2\alpha\log y}\right)}
 {(2\alpha\log y)^{1/4}}.
\end{equation}
The inverse has an expansion to arbitrary fixed order in $R^{-1}$,
with coefficients polynomial in $\log R$ and smooth periodic functions of
$s+A/\alpha$. Section~\ref{ctp:sec:inverse-proof} proves the passage from a
smooth asymptotic inverse to the integer threshold.
\end{theorem}

\section{An all-orders coefficient saddle estimate}
\label{ctp:sec:saddle}

\subsection{Positive cumulants and the saddle}
For $t>0$ write
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:H-K}
 H(t)=\log F(\e^{-t}),\qquad
 K_j(t)=(-1)^jH^{(j)}(t)
 =\sum_{k\geq1}c_k^j\sum_{\ell\geq1}\ell^{j-1}\e^{-\ell t c_k}
 \quad(j\geq1).
\end{equation}
Absolute convergence on each half-plane $\Rea t\geq t_0>0$ justifies
termwise differentiation. Since $K_1'(t)=-K_2(t)<0$, $K_1$ decreases
strictly from infinity to zero. Thus, for each $n>0$, there is a unique
$t_n>0$ satisfying
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:saddle}
 K_1(t_n)=n.
\end{equation}
The real logarithmic interpolation of the part sizes is
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:r}
 r(x)=\alpha x-\tfrac12\log\pi+
 \log\Gamma(x+\tfrac12)-\log\Gamma(x+2),\qquad x\geq1.
\end{equation}
It obeys $r(k)=\log c_k$. Since $r'(1)=1/2$ and
$r''(x)=\psi'(x+1/2)-\psi'(x+2)>0$, it is strictly increasing, with
$r'(x)\to\alpha$. Define
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:m}
 u_n=\log(1/t_n),\qquad m_n=r^{-1}(u_n).
\end{equation}
The parameters $m_n$ and $\mu(n)$ are not identical; their small difference
is responsible for several cancellations in the explicit formula.

\begin{lemma}[Size of the scaled cumulants]\label[lemma]{ctp:lem:cumulants}
Let $t=\e^{-r(m)}$ and $m\to\infty$. For each fixed integer $j\geq1$,
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:B}
 B_j(t):=t^jK_j(t)=(j-1)!\,m+O_j(1).
\end{equation}
In particular $nt_n\sim m_n$ and $m_n\sim(\log n)/\alpha$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Set $f_j(v)=v^j\sum_{\ell\geq1}\ell^{j-1}\e^{-\ell v}$.
For $0<v\leq1$, $f_j(v)=(j-1)!+O_j(v)$, by the elementary Laurent
expansion of the geometric sum and its derivatives. For $v\geq1$,
$f_j(v)$ decays exponentially up to a fixed polynomial factor.
The exact ratio
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:ratio}
 \frac{c_{k+1}}{c_k}=\frac{2(2k+1)}{k+2}\in[2,4)
\end{equation}
implies $\sum_{tc_k\leq1}tc_k=O(1)$ and a bounded sum of the
large-$v$ tails. There are exactly $\lfloor m\rfloor$ parts with
$tc_k\leq1$. Summing $f_j(tc_k)$ proves \eqref{ctp:eq:B}.
At the saddle, $nt=B_1=m+O(1)$, and
$\log n=r(m)+\log(m+O(1))=\alpha m+O(\log m)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[All-orders saddle expansion]\label{ctp:thm:saddle}
For every fixed $J\geq0$, with all cumulants evaluated at $t=t_n$,
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:saddle-expansion}
 p(n)=\frac{\exp(H(t)+nt)}{\sqrt{2\pi K_2(t)}}
 \left(1+\sum_{j=1}^J E_j(t)+O_J(m_n^{-J-1})\right).
\end{equation}
The correction $E_j$ is the finite sum
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:Ej}
 E_j=\sum_{\substack{k_3,\ldots,k_{2j+2}\geq0\\
                   \sum_{r=3}^{2j+2}(r-2)k_r=2j}}
 (-1)^{R/2}(R-1)!!
 \prod_{r=3}^{2j+2}\frac1{k_r!}
 \left(\frac{K_r}{r!K_2^{r/2}}\right)^{k_r},
 \qquad R=\sum_{r=3}^{2j+2}rk_r.
\end{equation}
Here $R$ is even, and $E_j=O_j(m_n^{-j})$. In particular,
\begin{align}
 E_1={}&\frac{K_4}{8K_2^2}-\frac{5K_3^2}{24K_2^3},\label{ctp:eq:E1}\\
 E_2={}&-\frac{K_6}{48K_2^3}+\frac{7K_3K_5}{48K_2^4}
 +\frac{35K_4^2}{384K_2^4}-\frac{35K_3^2K_4}{64K_2^5}
 +\frac{385K_3^4}{1152K_2^6}.\label{ctp:eq:E2}
\end{align}
\end{theorem}

\subsection{The noncentral arcs: why the coefficient estimate is valid}
The Cauchy coefficient integral is
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:cauchy}
 p(n)=\frac{\e^{H(t)+nt}}{2\pi}
 \int_{-\pi}^{\pi}
 \exp\{H(t-\ii\theta)-H(t)-\ii n\theta\}\dd\theta.
\end{equation}
Its normalized absolute value satisfies the exact identity
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:modulus}
 \left|\frac{F(\e^{-t+\ii\theta})}{F(\e^{-t})}\right|
 =\prod_{k\geq1}
 \left(1+\frac{4\e^{-tc_k}\sin^2(c_k\theta/2)}
 {(1-\e^{-tc_k})^2}\right)^{-1/2}.
\end{equation}
All factors on the right are at most one.
Fix a requested error order. In the estimates below, $B>0$, the number of
fixed factors $K$, and a small $\delta>0$ are chosen in that order and then
held fixed as $m\to\infty$.

For $|\theta|\leq tm^B$, use every part with $c_k\leq1/(tm^B)$.
There are $m-O(\log m)$ such parts. For them,
$|c_k\theta|\leq1$, and elementary sine and exponential estimates give
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:central-tail-bound}
 \left|\frac{F(\e^{-t+\ii\theta})}{F(\e^{-t})}\right|
 \leq\left(1+c(\theta/t)^2\right)^{-c'm}
\end{equation}
for fixed positive $c,c'$. Between
$t\log m/\sqrt m$ and $t$, integration of this bound gives
$O(t\exp\{-c''(\log m)^2\})$, after changing constants. Between $t$ and
$tm^B$, it gives $O(tm^B\e^{-c''m})$.
Both are smaller than $(t/\sqrt m)m^{-L}$ for every fixed $L$.

Next, for $tm^B\leq|\theta|\leq\delta$, select the first $K$ factors and
choose $\delta c_K\leq1$. Each selected factor in \eqref{ctp:eq:modulus} is
at most $C_Kt/|\theta|$. Hence
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:middle-bound}
 \int_{tm^B\leq|\theta|\leq\delta}
 \left|\frac{F(\e^{-t+\ii\theta})}{F(\e^{-t})}\right|\dd\theta
 =O_K\left(t\,m^{-B(K-1)}\right).
\end{equation}
Taking $K$ sufficiently large makes this negligible to any specified
inverse-power order relative to the main width $t/\sqrt m$.

Finally consider $\delta\leq|\theta|\leq\pi$. The unit-part factor is
$O_\delta(t)$. The functions $\sin(2\theta/2)$ and
$\sin(5\theta/2)$ do not vanish simultaneously on this compact set:
a simultaneous zero would require $\theta\in2\pi\Z$ because
$\gcd(2,5)=1$. Therefore at least one of the part-$2$ and part-$5$
factors is uniformly $O_\delta(t)$. Thus
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:outer-bound}
 \int_{\delta\leq|\theta|\leq\pi}
 \left|\frac{F(\e^{-t+\ii\theta})}{F(\e^{-t})}\right|\dd\theta
 =O_\delta(t^2).
\end{equation}
Since $t=\exp(-\alpha m+O(\log m))$, this is again negligible to every
inverse power of $m$ relative to $t/\sqrt m$.

These estimates eliminate possible competing root-of-unity arcs at the
precision claimed in \cref{ctp:thm:saddle}. They do not assert that all those
arcs vanish, or identify their exponentially smaller contributions.

\subsection{The central expansion and its remainder}
At the saddle the linear term in \eqref{ctp:eq:cauchy} cancels. With
$v=\theta\sqrt{K_2}$, the exponent becomes
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:central-exponent}
 -\frac{v^2}{2}+
 \sum_{r\geq3}\frac{\ii^rK_r}{r!K_2^{r/2}}v^r.
\end{equation}
For each fixed $r$, \cref{ctp:lem:cumulants} gives
$K_r/K_2^{r/2}=O_r(m^{-(r-2)/2})$. The central interval
$|\theta|\leq t\log m/\sqrt m$ corresponds to $|v|\leq C\log m$.
Taylor estimates for $H(t-\ii\theta)$ follow directly from its absolutely
convergent sums: the absolute value of a derivative along this vertical
segment is bounded by the corresponding positive derivative sum at $t$.

Expand the exponential in \eqref{ctp:eq:central-exponent}, assigning weight
$r-2$ to the term of degree $r$. Retain total weight through $2J+1$.
The integrated terms of odd weight vanish because their total degree in
$v$ is odd. The weight-$2j$ terms have Gaussian integral
$(-1)^{R/2}(R-1)!!$ after division by $\sqrt{2\pi}$, giving
\eqref{ctp:eq:Ej}.

For completeness, the remainder estimate does not require retaining a
power of $\log m$. On $|v|\leq C\log m$, the absolute correction to the
Gaussian exponent is $O(m^{-1/2}(|v|^3+1))$, which is at most
$v^2/4+o(1)$ outside a fixed bounded interval. A Taylor remainder through
weight $2J+1$ is bounded by
\[
 C_Jm^{-J-1}(1+|v|^{M_J})\e^{-v^2/4}
\]
for a fixed integer $M_J$, after including the Gaussian factor and
increasing the Taylor order of $H$ to $2J+4$ if necessary. Its integral is
$O_J(m^{-J-1})$. The omitted Gaussian tails are superpolynomially small.
Combining this with \eqref{ctp:eq:central-tail-bound}--\eqref{ctp:eq:outer-bound}
proves \cref{ctp:thm:saddle}.

The leading values of the correction polynomials are useful checks:
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:E-universal}
 E_1=-\frac1{12m}+O(m^{-2}),\qquad
 E_2=\frac1{288m^2}+O(m^{-3}),\qquad
 E_3=\frac{139}{51840m^3}+O(m^{-4}).
\end{equation}
They follow by substituting $B_r\sim(r-1)!m$ in \eqref{ctp:eq:Ej}.
At a finite $m$, using the exact cumulants is preferable to replacing each
$E_j$ by only its first universal term.

\subsection{A reusable sufficient hypothesis}
The same proof applies to an increasing sequence of positive integers
$a_1,a_2,\ldots$ when $a_1=1$, the ratios are eventually between two fixed
constants $1<q\leq Q<\infty$, and a finite subset of
$\{a_2,a_3,\ldots\}$ has greatest common divisor one. Replace $m$ by the
number of active parts $a_k\leq1/t$, up to an $O(1)$ difference. The finite
gcd condition is sufficient for the last compact arc estimate; it is not
claimed to be necessary. In particular, the proof as stated should not be
applied unchanged to pure powers of two, whose tail has a nontrivial gcd.

\section{The Catalan product and its moving periodic phase}
\label{ctp:sec:phase}

\subsection{The logarithm of a Catalan number and the Barnes product}
The standard gamma quotient in \eqref{ctp:eq:r} gives the complete expansion
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:r-asymptotic}
 r(x)=\alpha x-\beta\log x+c+\sum_{j\geq1}b_jx^{-j},
 \quad
 \beta=\frac32,\quad c=-\frac12\log\pi,
 \quad b_1=-\frac98,\quad b_2=\frac12.
\end{equation}
The meaning is asymptotic, to every fixed order with differentiated
remainders. A convenient exact coefficient formula is
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:bj}
 b_j=\frac{(-1)^{j+1}}{j(j+1)}
 \bigl(B_{j+1}(1/2)-B_{j+1}(2)\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $B_j(x)$ is a Bernoulli polynomial. For example,
$b_3=-21/64$. This follows by subtracting the shifted Stirling expansions
of the two log-gamma functions; it also gives an effective coefficient
source for every subsequent step.

For an integer $M\geq0$, put $S(M)=\sum_{k=1}^Mr(k)$. Repeated use of
$G(z+1)=\Gamma(z)G(z)$ gives the exact identity
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:S-exact}
 S(M)=\frac\alpha2M(M+1)-\frac M2\log\pi+
 \log G(M+3/2)-\log G(M+3)-\log G(3/2).
\end{equation}
The Barnes expansion~\cite{dlmfG}, or Euler--Maclaurin summation followed
by the exact identity to fix the constant, yields
\begin{align}
 S(M)={}&\frac\alpha2M^2-\frac32M\log M+
 \left(\frac\alpha2-\frac12\log\pi+\frac32\right)M
 -\frac{15}{8}\log M+K \notag\\
 &-\frac{19}{16M}+\frac{65}{128M^2}+O(M^{-3}),
 \label{ctp:eq:S-asymptotic}\\
 K={}&-\frac34\log(2\pi)-\log G(3/2).\label{ctp:eq:K}
\end{align}
Subtracting the expansion at $M-1$ from the expansion at $M$ recovers
\eqref{ctp:eq:r-asymptotic}; this is a useful local check, but the Barnes
identity is what determines the constant $K$.

\subsection{Separating the bulk from a localized lattice sum}
Let
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:g}
 g(v)=-\log(1-\e^{-\e^v})-(-v)_+,
 \qquad (-v)_+=\max\{-v,0\}.
\end{equation}
As $v\to-\infty$, $g(v)=O(\e^v)$; as $v\to+\infty$ it decays
superexponentially. It is continuous and piecewise analytic, with a jump
in its first derivative at zero. This mild kink will cancel against the
fractional-part polynomial.

For $m\geq1$, let $M=\lfloor m\rfloor$ and $\theta=m-M$. Define
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:Hm}
 \HH(m)=H(\e^{-r(m)}).
\end{equation}
Then the following identity is exact:
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:exact-decomposition}
 \HH(m)=Mr(m)-S(M)+\sum_{k\geq1}g(r(k)-r(m)).
\end{equation}
Indeed, the subtracted linear part $(-v)_+$ contributes $r(m)-r(k)$
precisely for $k\leq M$. If $m$ is an integer, the boundary term is zero,
so no convention ambiguity occurs.

Replacing $r(m+d)-r(m)$ by $\alpha d$ in the localized sum suggests
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:phase-lattice}
 \Psi_\alpha(\theta)=\frac\alpha2\theta(1-\theta)
       +\sum_{j\in\Z}g(\alpha(j-\theta)),\qquad 0\leq\theta\leq1.
\end{equation}
The sum converges absolutely, exponentially fast on the left and faster
on the right. The polynomial is forced by expansion of $Mr(m)-S(M)$;
it is not an adjustable normalization.

\begin{lemma}[Fourier representation of the phase]\label[lemma]{ctp:lem:Fourier}
The lattice formula \eqref{ctp:eq:phase-lattice} equals
\eqref{ctp:eq:phase-fourier}. In particular it extends to a smooth,
nonconstant one-periodic function, and termwise derivatives of its Fourier
series of every fixed order are legitimate.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For $\Rea s>0$, change variables $y=\e^v$ and expand
$-\log(1-\e^{-y})=\sum_{\ell\geq1}\e^{-\ell y}/\ell$ to obtain
\[
 \int_\R g(v)\e^{sv}\dd v
 =\Gamma(s)\zeta(1+s)-\frac1{s^2}.
\]
The integral involving $g$ extends analytically to $\Rea s>-1$.
The apparent singularity on the right at zero is removable: the product
of the Laurent expansions of $\Gamma(s)$ and $\zeta(1+s)$ has leading
term $s^{-2}$, no $s^{-1}$ term, and constant term $\kappa$.
Consequently the mean of the lattice sum is $\kappa/\alpha$.

For $\ell\ne0$, integrating its Fourier coefficient over $0\leq\theta\leq1$
and unfolding the lattice gives
\[
 \frac1\alpha\left\{
 \Gamma(s_\ell)\zeta(1+s_\ell)-\frac1{s_\ell^2}\right\},
 \qquad s_\ell=\frac{2\pi\ii\ell}{\alpha}.
\]
The coefficient of $\alpha\theta(1-\theta)/2$ is
$-\alpha/(4\pi^2\ell^2)$, which cancels the contribution of
$-1/(\alpha s_\ell^2)$. Its mean is $\alpha/12$.
This proves \eqref{ctp:eq:phase-fourier}.
The gamma factor has exponential decay on the vertical axis; the zeta
factor and its fixed-order derivatives have at most polynomial growth
there, as follows for example from Euler--Maclaurin continuation.
Hence every differentiated Fourier series converges absolutely.
Nonconstancy was proved in \cref{ctp:cor:oscillation}.
\end{proof}

\writenote{The same Mellin kernel and the same constant occur elsewhere
in ProveIt, for a different product. The Fabius-tree compilation
\emph{Geometric Uniform Convolutions and New Frontiers}
\cite{fabiusH}, in its section ``Mellin analysis and the hidden
log-periodic phase'', treats the Laplace product
$H_q(z)=\prod_{k\ge0}(1-\e^{-zq^k})/(zq^k)$, with kernel
$g(x)=\log\bigl((1-\e^{-x})/x\bigr)$, Mellin transform
$-\Gamma(1+s)\zeta(1+s)/s=-\Gamma(s)\zeta(1+s)$, and constant
$c_1=\pi^2/12-\gamma^2/2-\gamma_1$, which is the $\kappa$ of
\eqref{ctp:eq:kappa}. Neither text proves the other's theorem: the
products, the lattices and the normalizations differ, and the
coincidence is the de Bruijn--Mahler mechanism common to both.}

\subsection{The first two bulk orders, with a uniform remainder}
\begin{theorem}[Moving-lattice expansion]\label{ctp:thm:H}
Uniformly in the fractional part of $m$, as $m\to\infty$,
\begin{align}
 \HH(m)={}&\frac\alpha2m^2-
 \left(\frac32+\frac\alpha2\right)m+
 \frac{15}{8}\log m-\frac98-K+\Psi_\alpha(m)
 +\frac{P_1(m)}m+O(m^{-2}),\label{ctp:eq:H-expansion}\\
 P_1(\theta)={}&\frac{27}{16}-\frac32\partial_\alpha\Psi_\alpha(\theta).
 \label{ctp:eq:P1}
\end{align}
There is a complete extension with $\sum_{j=1}^J P_j(m)m^{-j}$ and
remainder $O_J(m^{-J-1})$. The $P_j$ are smooth and one-periodic. The
expansion may be differentiated any fixed number of times if sufficiently
many terms are retained.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
In \eqref{ctp:eq:exact-decomposition}, put $d=k-m$. For a bounded $d$,
\[
 r(m+d)-r(m)=\alpha d-\frac{\beta d}{m}+O((1+|d|)^2m^{-2}).
\]
Taylor expansion of the localized term therefore begins as
\[
 g(\alpha d)-\frac{\beta d}{m}g'(\alpha d)+O\bigl(m^{-2}
 (1+|d|)^L\e^{-c_0|d|}\bigr)
\]
for a fixed $L$; on the positive side an even stronger tail is available.
One-sided values at $d=0$ cause no difficulty because the multiplier $d$
vanishes. The missing terms with $k\leq0$ in the infinite lattice are
exponentially small.

Expansion of the exact bulk term using \eqref{ctp:eq:r-asymptotic} and
\eqref{ctp:eq:S-asymptotic} gives
\begin{align*}
 Mr(m)-S(M)={}&\frac\alpha2m^2-(\beta+\alpha/2)m+
 (\beta/2-b_1)\log m+b_1-K\\
 &+\frac\alpha2\theta(1-\theta)
 +\frac{27/16-(\beta/2)\theta(1-\theta)}m+O(m^{-2}).
\end{align*}
Combining this with the localized sum gives \eqref{ctp:eq:H-expansion}, since
\[
 \partial_\alpha\Psi_\alpha(\theta)
 =\tfrac12\theta(1-\theta)
  +\sum_{j\in\Z}(j-\theta)g'(\alpha(j-\theta)).
\]

Here is a uniform justification of the arbitrary-order procedure.
Split the lattice into $|d|\leq m^{1/3}$ and its complement.
In the first range, expansions of $r(m+d)-r(m)$ and Taylor expansions of
$g$ have remainders bounded by a fixed polynomial in $|d|$ times
$m^{-J-1}\e^{-c_0|d|}$. The signs of $d$ and of the two arguments of $g$
coincide, so Taylor expansion never crosses the kink. Summing preserves
$O_J(m^{-J-1})$. In the complementary range, monotonicity and the positive
lower bound for $r'$ give an exponentially small tail, including the
initial indices far to the left. The same reasoning applies after any
fixed number of derivatives, using more terms at the start.
At integral $m$, the jumps of the one-sided lattice derivatives cancel
exactly with those of the fractional-part bulk polynomials. This can be
seen either from the exact decomposition, by transferring the boundary
index between the two pieces, or coefficient by coefficient in that
identity. The resulting coefficient functions therefore match with all
fixed-order derivatives across the endpoints of a period.
\end{proof}

\subsection{A constructive rule for every coefficient}
\label{ctp:subsec:coefficient-rule}
The preceding proof can be made into a finite symbolic recipe. Write
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:Dj}
 r(m+d)-r(m)=\alpha d+\sum_{j\geq1}D_j(d)m^{-j},
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:Dj-formula}
 D_j(d)=\frac{\beta(-1)^jd^j}{j}
 +\sum_{h=1}^{j-1}b_h(-1)^{j-h}
       \binom{j-1}{h-1}d^{j-h}.
\end{equation}
For example, $D_1(d)=-\beta d$ and
$D_2(d)=\beta d^2/2-b_1d$. The localized contribution to the coefficient
of $m^{-j}$ is
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:Tj}
 T_j(\theta)=\sum_{d\in\Z-\theta}\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}
 \frac{g^{(\ell)}(\alpha d)}{\ell!}
 \sum_{\substack{r_1+\cdots+r_\ell=j\\r_\nu\geq1}}
       \prod_{\nu=1}^{\ell}D_{r_\nu}(d).
\end{equation}
Each coefficient is thus an absolutely convergent lattice sum of explicit
functions. At $d=0$, all $D_j(0)$ vanish, so arbitrary consistent one-sided
values of the derivatives give the same term, namely zero.

Expand $Mr(m)-S(M)$ with $M=m-\theta$, then add \eqref{ctp:eq:Tj}. This
produces $P_j(\theta)$. Bernoulli polynomials supply the bulk coefficients;
finite compositions of the $D_j$ supply the localized coefficients.
This is an all-orders construction, not merely a claim that unspecified
coefficients exist. Fourier transformation can be used afterward to
replace the localized sums by rapidly convergent Fourier series.

\section{Deriving the explicit coefficient expansion}
\label{ctp:sec:explicit-proof}

\subsection{The saddle coordinate and its correction}
Let $t=\e^{-r(m)}$, and define
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:Q}
 Q(m)=tK_1(t)=\frac{\HH'(m)}{r'(m)}.
\end{equation}
Differentiation of \eqref{ctp:eq:H-expansion}, followed by series division,
gives
\begin{align}
 Q(m)&=m+q_0(m)+\frac{q_1(m)}m+O(m^{-2}),\label{ctp:eq:Qexp}\\
 q_0&=-\frac12+\frac{\Psi_\alpha'}\alpha,
 &q_1&=\frac{P_1'}\alpha+\frac{\beta\Psi_\alpha'}{\alpha^2}.
 \label{ctp:eq:q01}
\end{align}
The phase functions on the right are evaluated at $m$.
The scaled variance satisfies the exact identity
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:B2Q}
 B_2=t^2K_2=Q+\frac{Q'}{r'},
\end{equation}
so that
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:B2exp}
 B_2=m+q_0+\frac{1+q_0'}\alpha+O(m^{-1}).
\end{equation}
More generally, the recurrence
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:B-recursion}
 B_{j+1}=jB_j+\frac{B_j'}{r'}
\end{equation}
generates every scaled cumulant from $Q$. This is useful both for the
proof and for the all-orders construction.

At the saddle, $n=\e^{r(m)}Q(m)$. Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:logn-m}
 \log n=\alpha m-\frac12\log m+c+
 \frac{b_1+q_0(m)}m+
 \frac{b_2+q_1(m)-q_0(m)^2/2}{m^2}+O(m^{-3}).
\end{equation}
The core coordinate \eqref{ctp:eq:mu} removes precisely the first three
terms. Write
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:m-reversion}
 m=\mu+\frac{a(\mu)}\mu+\frac{b(\mu)}{\mu^2}+O(\mu^{-3}).
\end{equation}
Taylor substitution into \eqref{ctp:eq:logn-m} gives the explicit functions
\begin{align}
 a&=-\frac{b_1+q_0}{\alpha},\label{ctp:eq:a}\\
 b&=\frac{(1/2-q_0')a-(b_2+q_1-q_0^2/2)}\alpha.
 \label{ctp:eq:b}
\end{align}
All functions here are evaluated at $\mu$.
The residual derivative in \eqref{ctp:eq:logn-m} is $\alpha+O(1/m)$,
which is bounded away from zero. Thus the formal coefficient comparison
also gives the asserted analytic error by the mean value theorem.
Repeating the argument proves an all-orders reversion with smooth periodic
coefficients.

\subsection{The normalization and the first correction}
Taking logarithms of \eqref{ctp:eq:saddle-expansion}, using
\eqref{ctp:eq:E-universal}, and writing $K_2=\e^{2r(m)}B_2$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:logp-saddle}
 \log p(n)=\HH(m)+Q(m)-r(m)-\frac12\log(2\pi B_2(m))
 -\frac1{12m}+O(m^{-2}).
\end{equation}
Let $d=b_1+q_0$. Substituting the preceding expansions into this equation
first produces the expression in the \emph{true} saddle coordinate:
\begin{align}
 \log p(n)={}&\frac\alpha2m^2-Am+\eta\log m+C+
 \Psi_\alpha(m)+d(m)+\frac{U(m)}m+O(m^{-2}),\label{ctp:eq:logp-m}\\
 U={}&P_1+q_1-b_1-\frac{q_0}{2}
 -\frac{1+q_0'}{2\alpha}-\frac1{12}.
 \label{ctp:eq:U}
\end{align}
The constant simplifies because
\[
 -K-c-\tfrac12\log(2\pi)
 =\tfrac14\log2+\tfrac34\log\pi+\log G(3/2)=C.
\]

Now substitute \eqref{ctp:eq:m-reversion}. At order one,
$\alpha a+d=0$, so the apparently additional phase derivative in $d$
\emph{cancels}. At order $1/\mu$, the coefficient is
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:D-unsimplified}
 \alpha b+(-A+\Psi_\alpha'+q_0')a+U.
\end{equation}
Using \eqref{ctp:eq:a}--\eqref{ctp:eq:b}, this simplifies first to
\[
 P_1-\frac7{12}-\frac1{2\alpha}
 +(1/2-b_1)q_0-\frac12q_0^2-\frac{q_0'}{2\alpha},
\]
and then, with $b_1=-9/8$ and \eqref{ctp:eq:P1}, exactly to
\eqref{ctp:eq:D}. In particular the $P_1'$ terms disappear. This explicit
cancellation is one reason to distinguish $m$ and $\mu$ throughout.
Equations~\eqref{ctp:eq:main-log} and \eqref{ctp:eq:main-multiplicative} follow.

\subsection{Completion of the all-orders proof}
For a fixed requested order $J$, use the following finite operations.
First compute sufficiently many $b_j$ from \eqref{ctp:eq:bj}, bulk coefficients
from \eqref{ctp:eq:S-exact}, and $P_j$ from \eqref{ctp:eq:Tj}. Next form $Q$ and
$B_2,B_3,\ldots$ by \eqref{ctp:eq:Q} and \eqref{ctp:eq:B-recursion}. Substitute
these in the finite Gaussian polynomials \eqref{ctp:eq:Ej} and take the
logarithm of the saddle prefactor and correction. Finally revert
\eqref{ctp:eq:logn-m} about $\mu$ and substitute.

The derivative-controlled estimates in \cref{ctp:thm:H} justify each finite
calculus operation. The saddle theorem supplies the coefficient-extraction
remainder. The reversion has derivative bounded away from zero, so it
preserves the desired error once one extra order has been retained before
substitution into the quadratic term. All coefficient operations are
addition, multiplication, differentiation, and division by series with
nonzero constant term in an algebra of smooth periodic functions. Thus
no new unbounded coefficient, such as $\log\mu$, is generated beyond the
single displayed $\eta\log\mu$. This proves
\eqref{ctp:eq:all-orders-explicit} and completes \cref{ctp:thm:explicit}.

A safe implementation rule is to retain two orders more than the final
requested order during the $m$-to-$\mu$ substitution. This is a bookkeeping
rule, not a new analytic assumption.

\section{Inverse growth and the integer staircase}
\label{ctp:sec:inverse-proof}

\subsection{Reversion of the quadratic logarithmic core}
Start with $Y=\log y$ and $s=\sqrt{2Y/\alpha}$. The leading quadratic
term suggests $\mu=s+A/\alpha+o(1)$. Set
\[
 \mu=s+\frac A\alpha+\frac{v(s)}s+
 O\!\left(\frac{(\log s)^2}{s^2}\right).
\]
Substitution into \eqref{ctp:eq:main-log}, at constant order after the
quadratic and linear terms cancel, yields
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:v-inverse}
 v(s)=\frac{A^2}{2\alpha^2}
 -\frac{\eta\log s+C+\Psi_\alpha(s+A/\alpha)}\alpha.
\end{equation}
Since $\log n=\alpha\mu-\tfrac12\log\mu-\tfrac12\log\pi$,
\eqref{ctp:eq:inverse} follows by Taylor expansion. The term
$D_\alpha(\mu)/\mu$ in $\log p$ first affects the next inverse order,
not the displayed $1/R$ correction.

Higher inverse terms are obtained by the same procedure. The leading
inverse derivative is of size $s$, so a residual term is removed by
division by $s$ and a bounded perturbation. Every differentiation of a
periodic coefficient leaves it in the same coefficient algebra, while
products of the already occurring $\log s$ terms produce polynomials in
$\log s$. The phase is always reducible by Taylor expansion to
$s+A/\alpha$. This proves the asserted form of the all-orders inverse.

\subsection{Why a continuous calculation describes the discrete threshold}
Adding a unit part proves that $p(n)$ is nondecreasing. Moreover,
$p(n)-p(n-1)$ counts partitions of $n$ without a unit part.
Every integer $n\geq4$ is a nonnegative combination of $2$ and $5$:
even $n$ use only $2$, and odd $n\geq5$ use one $5$ and then $2$.
Therefore $p(n)>p(n-1)$ for all $n\geq4$, and the threshold
\eqref{ctp:eq:threshold} is unambiguous for large $y$.

Let $f_J(x)$ denote the smooth right side of
\eqref{ctp:eq:all-orders-explicit}, with $n$ replaced by a large positive real
$x$. Its derivative with respect to $X=\log x$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:inverse-slope}
 \frac{\dd}{\dd X}f_J(\e^X)=\mu(\e^X)+O(1)>0.
\end{equation}
The asymptotic theorem says, at integers,
$\log p(n)=f_J(n)+O(\mu(n)^{-J-1})$ with a uniform constant.
If $f_J(x)=Y$, a change of $O(\mu^{-J-2})$ in $\log x$ moves $f_J(x)$
by the entire coefficient error. Bracketing the corresponding real
arguments by neighboring integers therefore gives
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:inverse-transport}
 \log N(y)-\log x
 =O(\mu(x)^{-J-2})+O(x^{-1}),
\end{equation}
provided $x$ is the inverse of the chosen smooth truncation. The bracket
uses only monotonicity, not an assumed canonical interpolation of $p$.

The $O(x^{-1})$ term comes from rounding an argument to an integer; it is
smaller than every fixed inverse power of $R$. The first term is the
analytic truncation uncertainty and is much larger at a fixed order.
Accordingly, the theorem gives an asymptotic formula for the threshold,
not an exact integer reconstruction algorithm for every $y$.
Equations~\eqref{ctp:eq:v-inverse} and \eqref{ctp:eq:inverse-transport} prove
\cref{ctp:thm:inverse}.

\subsection{A practical caution}
The expansion parameter is approximately $1/\log n$, not $1/n$.
Even at $n=10^6$, the effective number of active parts is only about
$11.36$. The leading inverse equivalent can therefore have a large
finite-argument error. The first correction helps substantially, but a
numerical application at moderate size should use the implicit saddle
formula and invert it numerically, preferably with a certified remainder
if an integer decision is required. Section~\ref{ctp:sec:computation} reports
actual errors rather than presenting an asymptotic equivalent as a
uniformly accurate approximation.

\section{The largest Catalan part in a random partition}
\label{ctp:sec:largest}
Choose uniformly among the $p(n)$ partitions of $n$. Let $J_n$ be the
largest index $k$ for which a part of size $c_k$ occurs. For $n>0$ this
is well-defined. It is natural to center $J_n$ at the saddle cutoff, not
at $\log_4 n$ alone.

\begin{theorem}[A phase-dependent largest-index law]\label{ctp:thm:largest}
Let $m=m_n$ be the true saddle coordinate, $M=\lfloor m\rfloor$, and
$\theta_n=m-M$. For every fixed integer $h$,
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:largest-law}
 \PP(J_n\leq M+h)=
 \prod_{j=h+1}^{\infty}\left(1-\exp\{-4^{j-\theta_n}\}\right)
 +O_h(m_n^{-1}).
\end{equation}
In particular, along any subsequence with $\theta_n\to\theta\in[0,1)$,
$J_n-M$ converges in distribution to the integer-valued law with cumulative
probabilities
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:largest-cdf}
 G_h(\theta)=\prod_{j=h+1}^{\infty}(1-\e^{-4^{j-\theta}}).
\end{equation}
The estimates are for fixed $h$; a growing-$h$ or moment theorem is not
being asserted.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Under the product probability measure at parameter $t$, let the numbers
of parts of sizes $c_k$ be independent geometric random variables:
\[
 \PP(Z_k=z)=(1-\e^{-tc_k})\e^{-tc_k z},\qquad z=0,1,2,\ldots.
\]
Conditioning on $\sum_k c_kZ_k=n$ gives the uniform distribution on
partitions of $n$, since every such partition has the same product
weight. This provides an exact probabilistic interpretation, not an
approximation.

Put $K=M+h$ and split the product at $K$. Let $H_\mathrm{pre}$ and
$H_\mathrm{tail}$ be its logarithms. At the full saddle, the removed tail
has scaled cumulants of every fixed order $O_h(1)$. To see this, write
$k=M+j$ and use
\[
 tc_{M+j}=\exp\{r(M+j)-r(m)\},
\]
which is bounded away from zero for each fixed starting $j=h+1$ and
then grows geometrically. The corresponding sums of the functions
$f_r$ from \cref{ctp:lem:cumulants} are bounded. Thus the removed mean is
$O_h(1/t)$, whereas the full standard deviation is $\asymp\sqrt m/t$.

Apply the same coefficient saddle argument to the prefix product. Its
saddle differs from $t$ by $O_h(t/m)$: the mean deficit is $O_h(1/t)$
and the derivative of the mean is of size $m/t^2$. Taylor's theorem
then shows that its saddle action differs from the prefix action
evaluated at $t$ by $O_h(1/m)$. The variance ratio is $1+O_h(1/m)$,
and the two first saddle correction factors are also $1+O_h(1/m)$.
The arc estimates remain uniform because the prefix still contains
$m-O_h(1)$ active parts and all the fixed small factors used in
Section~\ref{ctp:sec:saddle}. Consequently,
\[
 \frac{[z^n]\prod_{k\leq K}(1-z^{c_k})^{-1}}{p(n)}
 =\e^{-H_\mathrm{tail}(t)}\bigl(1+O_h(m^{-1})\bigr)
 =\prod_{k>K}(1-\e^{-tc_k})\bigl(1+O_h(m^{-1})\bigr).
\]
This ratio is exactly the desired conditional probability.

Finally, for each fixed $j$,
\[
 tc_{M+j}=4^{j-\theta_n}
 \left(1+O\left(\frac{1+|j|}{m}\right)\right).
\]
For $0\leq j-\theta_n\leq m/2$, the corresponding logarithmic
error is bounded by $C(1+|j|)/m$. The derivatives of the logarithmic
tail factors have a summable, superexponentially decaying majorant
throughout this range. The range $j-\theta_n>m/2$ contributes a
superexponentially small tail, and the finitely many negative indices
are controlled separately. Thus summation replaces the tail product
by \eqref{ctp:eq:largest-cdf} with error $O_h(m^{-1})$. This proves \eqref{ctp:eq:largest-law}.
For each phase, $G_h\to1$ as $h\to+\infty$ and $G_h\to0$ as
$h\to-\infty$, so it is the cumulative distribution of a proper
integer-valued law. Pointwise convergence at the integer cutoffs gives
the distributional assertion.
\end{proof}

\writenoteB{Part~II supplies what this theorem does not assert: the first
correction $G_h(1+C_h/m)$ (\cref{ctp:cl:cor:maxcorrection}, proved in both
batch-98 manuscripts), uniform two-sided tails and convergence of every
polynomial and exponential moment (\cref{ctp:cl:lem:maxtails,ctp:te:cor:maxmom}),
the first correction summed over every threshold
(\cref{ctp:cl:thm:maxweighted}) with $\E J_n$ and $\Var J_n$ to $O(m^{-2})$
\eqref{ctp:cl:eq:maxmean}--\eqref{ctp:cl:eq:maxvar}, and a growing-offset
estimate for $|h|\le m^{1/8}$ (\cref{ctp:te:thm:growing}).}

The local scale $4^{j-\theta}$ in this law is the same scale that produces
the Fourier amplitude in \eqref{ctp:eq:phase-fourier}. Both results describe
the bounded-width transition between parts that are typically available
in many copies and parts that are rarely used. The phase is not noise:
it records where the continuous cutoff falls between two consecutive
Catalan indices.

\section{A universality statement for polynomially perturbed geometric parts}
\label{ctp:sec:universality}
The preceding method separates universal local behavior from sequence-specific
normalization. The following proposition makes that separation precise.

\begin{proposition}[General exponential--polynomial part growth]
\label[proposition]{ctp:prop:general}
Suppose an increasing integer part sequence $a_k$ satisfies the sufficient
saddle hypotheses at the end of Section~\ref{ctp:sec:saddle}. Suppose its logarithm
has a smooth eventual interpolation with a termwise differentiable expansion
\[
 r(k)=\alpha k-\beta\log k+c+b_1/k+b_2/k^2+\cdots,\qquad\alpha>0.
\]
Define $K_{\aset}$ by
\begin{align*}
 \sum_{k=1}^M\log a_k={}&\frac\alpha2M^2-\beta M\log M
 +(\alpha/2+c+\beta)M\notag\\
 &+(b_1-\beta/2)\log M+K_{\aset}+O(M^{-1}).
\end{align*}
Let $p_{\aset}(n)$ be the corresponding partition number, and let $\mu$ be
the large solution of $n=\e^c\e^{\alpha\mu}\mu^{1-\beta}$. Then
\begin{align}
 \log p_{\aset}(n)={}&\frac\alpha2\mu^2+
 (1-\beta-3\alpha/2)\mu+
 (3\beta/2-b_1-1/2)\log\mu\notag\\
 &-K_{\aset}-c-\frac12\log(2\pi)+\Psi_\alpha(\mu)+O(\mu^{-1}).
 \label{ctp:eq:general}
\end{align}
There is an all-orders extension with smooth periodic inverse-power
coefficients. The function $\Psi_\alpha$ is exactly
\eqref{ctp:eq:phase-fourier}; it depends on $\alpha$, not on $\beta$ or the
finite initial segment.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The constant $K_{\aset}$ exists by Euler--Maclaurin summation of the
assumed expansion, or by subtracting enough explicitly summable terms.
The bulk and local argument gives
\[
 \HH(m)=\frac\alpha2m^2-(\beta+\alpha/2)m+
 (\beta/2-b_1)\log m+b_1-K_{\aset}+\Psi_\alpha(m)+O(m^{-1}).
\]
The local limit $r(m+d)-r(m)\to\alpha d$ is unchanged. Also
$Q=m+q_0+O(m^{-1})$, where $q_0=-1/2+\Psi_\alpha'/\alpha$,
independent of $\beta$. Therefore
\[
 \log n=\alpha m+(1-\beta)\log m+c+(b_1+q_0)/m+O(m^{-2}).
\]
Reversion about $\mu$ again gives
$m=\mu-(b_1+q_0)/(\alpha\mu)+O(\mu^{-2})$.
In the logarithm of the saddle estimate, the constant $b_1+q_0$ cancels
against the quadratic-core displacement just as before. The remaining
linear, logarithmic, and constant terms are exactly \eqref{ctp:eq:general}.
The proof of every higher order is the same finite construction with the
assumed coefficients $b_j$ in place of their Catalan values.
\end{proof}

This proposition explains why merely computing the exponential ratio of
part sizes is insufficient. The coefficient $\beta$ changes the linear
term and the size coordinate; $b_1$ changes the power of $\mu$; and the
whole initial sequence contributes to $K_{\aset}$. The local periodic
shape, on the other hand, is determined by the limiting logarithmic gap.

\subsection{What the supplied programs do}
The package contains an exact integer dynamic program for $p(0),\ldots,p(N)$,
a separate logarithmic-derivative recurrence check, arbitrary-precision
saddle evaluation, independent Fourier and real-lattice evaluations of the
phase, the explicit first-correction formula, and the inverse formula.
The numerical program implements $E_1$ and $E_2$ directly; a rational
multi-index generator lists the Gaussian polynomials through $E_4$ in the
recorded data. The all-orders theorem does not depend on a claim that an
unlimited symbolic implementation is already supplied.

Exact dynamic programming starts with $p(0)=1$ and $p(n)=0$ for $n>0$.
For each allowed part $c\leq N$, in increasing order, replace
$p(n)$ by $p(n)+p(n-c)$ for $n=c,c+1,\ldots,N$. The ascending update
allows repeated copies of $c$. An independent recurrence is
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:eq:exact-recursion}
 np(n)=\sum_{j=1}^n b(j)p(n-j),\qquad
 b(j)=\sum_{c_k\mid j}c_k,
\end{equation}
obtained from $zF'(z)/F(z)$. Agreement of these two algorithms is checked
through $n=300$. The first 20 terms agree with the inspected OEIS entry.
The stored small coefficient file is independently generated; it is not a
redistributed download of another author's table.

\section{Further research questions and concrete next steps}
\label{ctp:sec:future}
The following directions are deliberately separated from the proved
statements. Each would improve either the mathematical scope or the
reliability of computation.

\subsection{Certified errors and exact inverse decisions}
Replace the unspecified constants in the contour and moving-lattice
remainders by explicit, computable bounds. The proof already divides the
problem into positive sums, elementary arc inequalities, and Taylor
remainders. An interval implementation could enclose the saddle, truncate
both lattice tails with proven bounds, and return a rigorous interval for
$\log p(n)$. The decisive inverse question is then whether that interval
separates two neighboring integer thresholds. This is substantially
stronger than a fixed-order asymptotic equivalent.

\subsection{A symbolic compiler for periodic asymptotic coefficients}
Implement \eqref{ctp:eq:Tj}, \eqref{ctp:eq:B-recursion}, \eqref{ctp:eq:Ej}, and the
$m$-to-$\mu$ reversion in one coefficient algebra. A useful output would
include $L_2,L_3,L_4$ in a normalized basis of phase derivatives, together
with exact rational audits. The central design issue is to avoid treating
$\Psi_\alpha(m)$ as a constant under differentiation and to track the
partial $\alpha$-derivative separately from the phase derivative.

\subsection{Beyond-all-orders contributions}
The proof discards the compact outer arcs at a relative scale
$O(t\sqrt m)$, much smaller than every inverse power of $m$. Their actual
contributions may have an arithmetic organization at roots of unity.
Identify the dominant secondary arcs, their exponential actions, and how
they interact with the analytic tail of the Barnes expansion. A complete
transseries including such sectors would go beyond the all-orders
Poincar\'e expansion established here.

\subsection{Joint statistics near the largest part}
For finitely many indices $M+j$, the unconditioned geometric variables
suggest a phase-dependent local product limit. Prove a joint conditional
limit with an explicit first correction, rather than only the tail-absence
probability in \cref{ctp:thm:largest}. Extend the estimates uniformly in $h$
far enough to justify moment convergence and asymptotics for the expected
largest index. Neither extension follows automatically from the fixed-$h$
statement.

\writenoteB{Answered in Part~II: the joint conditional limit of the
multiplicities near the largest part with an explicit first correction, in
weighted $L^1$ for the whole upper process (\cref{ctp:cl:thm:edge}), with its
first covariances (\cref{ctp:cl:cor:cov}) and, for finite index sets, in
transform form jointly with the number of parts (\cref{ctp:te:thm:cutoff});
estimates uniform in $h$, moment convergence, and the expected largest index
to $O(m^{-2})$ (see the note after Theorem~\ref{ctp:thm:largest}). Higher
orders and the growing range beyond $m^{1/8}$ remain open
(\cref{ctp:cl:sec:future}).}

\subsection{The number of parts and mixed conditioning}
Introduce a marking variable for the total number of parts. Small allowed
parts and the bounded-width cutoff have very different fluctuation scales.
Determine the correct normalization and a joint limit with $J_n$.
A two-variable saddle may need a different treatment from a direct
Gaussian approximation because the unit-part multiplicity can remain
macroscopic on the scale $1/t$.

\writenoteB{Answered in Part~II: the normalization is $t_nN_n$, whose limit
is the non-Gaussian $W=\sum_k\mathbf E_k/c_k$, with every moment and a first
correction (\cref{ctp:te:thm:length}); jointly with $J_n$ the limit is
independent (\cref{ctp:cl:thm:joint}), and the first dependence is explicit
(\cref{ctp:te:thm:maxjoint,ctp:cl:thm:mixedcov}). As anticipated here, the
small parts are macroscopic on the scale $1/t$; the proofs mark the
generating function by the number of parts
(\cref{ctp:te:thm:operator,ctp:te:thm:length}) rather than use a
two-variable Gaussian. A lattice local limit for $N_n$ remains open
(\cref{ctp:cl:sec:future}).}

\subsection{Fuss--Catalan and other gamma-quotient part sequences}
Apply \cref{ctp:prop:general} to integer part sequences with a gamma-quotient
asymptotic, after checking the initial-part gcd condition and removing
any duplicated values. Calculate the normalization constant by Barnes
or multiple-gamma products when possible. The proposition predicts a
universal periodic shape for each limiting logarithmic gap, but the
power and the normalization require a new calculation in every family.

\subsection{Exact counting complexity}
The supplied dynamic program uses $O(n\log n)$ integer additions to
compute all coefficients through $n$, because there are $O(\log n)$
allowed parts up to $n$. That is not a polynomial-in-$\log n$ algorithm
for a single exact value. Determine whether Catalan-part partitions admit
a substantially faster exact counting method of the kind motivated by
Pak's discussion~\cite{pak}. The asymptotic formulas may provide useful
bounds or numerical guidance, but they do not settle this question.

\subsection{Formalization and a periodic coefficient algebra}
The finite coefficient identities, the rational Gaussian multi-index
formula, the gamma-quotient recurrence for Catalan numbers, and the
monotonicity argument for $N(y)$ are natural first formalization targets.
The analytic frontier is a reusable theorem for positive Euler products
with a controlled central arc and fixed-factor minor-arc suppression.
For ProveIt's transseries development, an algebra of smooth or analytic
periodic coefficient germs would encode the phase correctly. One should
not label the present contour proof formalized merely because some
underlying algebraic operations have Lean counterparts.

\section{Conclusion}
A033552 admits a concrete, computable all-orders asymptotic description
whose most visible unusual feature is a persistent periodic amplitude.
The dominant size coordinate is not simply $\log_4 n$: the Catalan
polynomial factor shifts the saddle and changes both the logarithmic
power and the inverse growth. Keeping that shift, the exact Barnes
constant, the local lattice correction, and the Gaussian coefficient
corrections in one calculation yields \eqref{ctp:eq:main-log} and its inverse.

The main analytic issues have explicit proofs: noncentral coefficient
arcs are negligible to every fixed inverse-logarithmic order; local
lattice Taylor expansions have uniform remainders; saddle reversion has
a derivative bounded away from zero; and integer threshold errors are
separated from analytic inverse errors. The largest-index theorem gives
a probabilistic consequence of the same local phase. What remains is not
a missing leading argument, but sharper certification, higher explicit
coefficients, secondary exponential sectors, wider statistics, and a
systematic formalization.

\section{Exact conditioning, saddle normalization and local estimates}
\label{ctp:cl:sec:setup}

\subsection{The model and the two statistics}
With $c_k$, $F$ and $p(n)$ as in \eqref{ctp:eq:catalan}, the unit part is
included once: $c_0=c_1=1$ does not give two kinds of part. For example
$p(5)=4$, from $5$, $2+2+1$, $2+1+1+1$ and $1+1+1+1+1$. Let $Z_k$ be the
multiplicity of $c_k$ under the uniform measure $\PP_n$ on the $p(n)$
partitions of~$n$, and
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:statistics}
 N_n=\sum_{k\ge1}Z_k,\qquad J_n=\max\{k:Z_k>0\}\quad(n>0).
\end{equation}
$N_n$ counts parts with multiplicity; $J_n$ is the largest \emph{index},
and the largest part size is $c_{J_n}$. ``Large'' refers to the size $c_k$,
or its index $k$, not to the number of copies.

There are about $\log_4n$ active sizes, and a typical active size carries
mass of order $n/\log n$, although the number of copies varies enormously
between small and large sizes. Three limits therefore coexist
(manuscript~06): at a fixed small size the multiplicity is of order $1/t_n$
with an exponential limit; a positive fraction of the active sizes has
Gaussian mass fluctuations, which fixing the total mass turns into a
bridge; and within a bounded number of indices of the largest used size
the multiplicities stay discrete with a phase-dependent geometric law.
Manuscript~10 puts the same point as two fluctuation scales: the saddle is
Gaussian in the \emph{total size}, but both statistics keep non-Gaussian
limits after conditioning on it, so a Gaussian approximation to either is
structurally wrong.

\subsection{Exact conditioning}
For $t>0$ let $\Q_t$ make the $Z_k$ independent with
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:geom}
 \Q_t(Z_k=z)=(1-\e^{-tc_k})\e^{-tc_kz},\qquad z\in\{0,1,2,\ldots\},
\end{equation}
counting failures before the first success, and put
$S=\sum_kc_kZ_k$. All moments of $S$ are finite, and almost surely only
finitely many $Z_k$ are nonzero, since $\sum_k\Q_t(Z_k>0)=\sum_k\e^{-tc_k}<\infty$.

\begin{lemma}[Conditioning and shifts]\label[lemma]{ctp:cl:lem:exact}
For every $t>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:condition}
 \PP_n=\Q_t(\,\cdot\mid S=n),\qquad
 \Q_t(S=n)=\frac{p(n)\e^{-tn}}{F(\e^{-t})}.
\end{equation}
For distinct indices $i_1,\ldots,i_d$ and nonnegative integers
$b_1,\ldots,b_d$,
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:survival}
 \PP_n(Z_{i_\ell}\ge b_\ell,\ 1\le\ell\le d)=\frac{p(n-v)}{p(n)},\qquad
 v=\sum_{\ell=1}^dc_{i_\ell}b_\ell,
\end{equation}
with $p(q)=0$ for $q<0$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Every multiplicity vector with weighted sum $n$ has $\Q_t$-probability
$\e^{-tn}/F(\e^{-t})$. For the second identity remove $b_\ell$ copies of
each specified part: this is a bijection with the partitions of $n-v$.
\end{proof}

The identity is exact, not an equivalence-of-ensembles assumption; it is
the one used in the proof of Theorem~\ref{ctp:thm:largest}. With
$H(t)=\log F(\e^{-t})$ and $K_r(t)=(-1)^rH^{(r)}(t)$ as in
\eqref{ctp:eq:H-K}, the $K_r$ are the cumulants of $S$ under $\Q_t$, the
saddle $t=t_n$ is the unique solution of $K_1(t_n)=\E_{\Q_{t_n}}S=n$
\eqref{ctp:eq:saddle}, and, with $r(x)$ as in \eqref{ctp:eq:r},
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:m}
 r(m)=\log(1/t),\qquad M=\lfloor m\rfloor,\qquad\theta=m-M\in[0,1),
 \qquad B_r=t^rK_r(t).
\end{equation}
This is Part~I's $m_n$ of \eqref{ctp:eq:m} and $\theta_n$ of
Theorem~\ref{ctp:thm:largest}; the phase is \emph{not} obtained by rounding
$\log_4n$. In interpolated notation $tc_m=1$, and exactly $M$ allowed sizes
are at most $1/t$. Since $r'(1)=1/2$ and
$r''(x)=\psi_1(x+\tfrac12)-\psi_1(x+2)>0$ (the trigamma function $\psi_1$
decreases), $0<r'(x)<\alpha$ for $x\ge1$, $r'(x)=\alpha-3/(2x)+O(x^{-2})$
and $r''(x)=O(x^{-2})$. With the ratio
\eqref{ctp:eq:ratio}, $c_{k+1}/c_k=2(2k+1)/(k+2)\in[2,4)$,
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:te:eq:ratio}
 r(m+d)-r(m)=\alpha d-\frac{3d}{2m}+O\!\left(\frac{1+|d|+d^2}{m^2}\right)
 \qquad(|d|\le m/2),
\end{equation}
by the differentiable Stirling expansion; its full expansion to each fixed
order is Part~I's \eqref{ctp:eq:Dj}.

\begin{lemma}[Active cumulants]\label[lemma]{ctp:cl:lem:cumulants}
For each fixed integer $r\ge1$,
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:cumulants}
 B_r=(r-1)!\,m+O_r(1).
\end{equation}
For a prefix $S_K=\sum_{k\le K}c_kZ_k$ write $B_{r,K}$ for its scaled
cumulants. If $K\le M$, then, uniformly in $K$,
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:prefixcumulants}
 B_{r,K}=(r-1)!\,K+O_r(1).
\end{equation}
Consequently
\begin{align}
 nt&=m+O(1),&\Var_{\Q_t}S&=\frac{m+O(1)}{t^2},\label{ctp:cl:eq:scale}\\
 m&=\frac{\log n+\tfrac12\log\log n+\tfrac12\log(\pi/\alpha)}{\alpha}
 +O\!\left(\frac{\log\log n}{\log n}\right),\label{ctp:cl:eq:mexplicit}
\end{align}
so that $m\sim\log n/\log4$ and $t\sim\log n/(n\log4)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For $f_r(v)=v^r\sum_{\ell\ge1}\ell^{r-1}\e^{-\ell v}=v^r\Li_{1-r}(\e^{-v})$,
$f_r(v)=(r-1)!+O_r(v)$ on $0<v\le1$, and $f_r$ decays exponentially times
a polynomial on $v\ge1$. There are exactly $M$ active terms $tc_k\le1$, and
by the ratio bound the sums of $tc_k$ over them and of the inactive
$f_r(tc_k)$ are uniformly bounded. This proves both cumulant estimates.
Stirling's expansion gives $r(m)=\alpha m-\tfrac32\log m-\tfrac12\log\pi+O(m^{-1})$;
combine it with $\log n=r(m)+\log(m+O(1))$ and invert once.
\end{proof}

\Cref{ctp:cl:lem:cumulants} restates Part~I's \cref{ctp:lem:cumulants}
(proved independently in both manuscripts, manuscript~10 with
$f_r(v)=v^r\Li_{1-r}(\e^{-v})$); the prefix form and \eqref{ctp:cl:eq:mexplicit}
are manuscript~06's additions. At the edge, for every fixed integer~$j$,
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:edgerate}
 v_j:=tc_{M+j}=u_j\left(1-\frac{3(j-\theta)}{2m}+O_j(m^{-2})\right),
 \qquad u_j=4^{j-\theta}.
\end{equation}
The coefficient $3/2$ reappears in every corrected edge law.

\subsection{Fourier bounds with uniform control}
Weak convergence of $S$ alone would not suffice: conditioning on one lattice
value needs a local estimate and control of all competing arcs. For the
full sum ($K=\infty$) or a prefix, the characteristic function has modulus
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:modulus}
 |\phi_K(y)|=\prod_{k\le K}\left(1+\frac{4\e^{-tc_k}\sin^2(c_ky/2)}
 {(1-\e^{-tc_k})^2}\right)^{-1/2},
\end{equation}
which for $K=\infty$ is Part~I's \eqref{ctp:eq:modulus}.

\begin{lemma}[Integrated Fourier control]\label[lemma]{ctp:cl:lem:fourier}
Fix $\varepsilon>0$. Uniformly for $K\ge\varepsilon m$,
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:atomupper}
 \sup_{\ell\in\Z}\Q_t(S_K=\ell)\le C_\varepsilon\,t/\sqrt m.
\end{equation}
If $K\le M$, put $\sigma_K^2=\Var S_K$. Then, uniformly for
$\varepsilon m\le K\le M$,
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:ullt}
 \sup_{\ell\in\Z}\left|\sigma_K\Q_t(S_K=\ell)
 -\phi\!\left(\frac{\ell-\E S_K}{\sigma_K}\right)\right|=o(1),
 \qquad\phi(x)=\frac{\e^{-x^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}.
\end{equation}
The full sum has the same local limit theorem.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Choose a fixed large $B$. For $|y|\le tm^B$ use all factors with $k\le K$
and $c_k\le1/(tm^B)$; at least $\varepsilon m-O(\log m)$ are available when
$0<\varepsilon\le1$. The elementary inequalities for $\sin$ and $1-\e^{-v}$
give $|\phi_K(y)|\le(1+c(y/t)^2)^{-c_\varepsilon m}$, and the integral
outside $|y|\le t\log m/\sqrt m$ within this range is smaller than
$tm^{-L}$ for every fixed $L$. For $tm^B\le|y|\le\delta$, choose a fixed
number $q$ of small part factors and then $\delta>0$ with $\delta c_q<1$;
their product is at most $C_q(t/|y|)^q$, with integral $O_q(tm^{-B(q-1)})$.
For $\delta\le|y|\le\pi$ the unit factor is $O_\delta(t)$, and the part-$2$
and part-$5$ factors cannot both resonate, since $\sin y$ and $\sin(5y/2)$
have no common zero on this compact set; one more $O_\delta(t)$ factor makes
this integral $O_\delta(t^2)$. All the fixed factors used are eventually in
every admissible prefix.

On the central interval
$\log\E\e^{\ii y(S_K-\E S_K)}=-\tfrac12\sigma_K^2y^2+\sum_{r\ge3}(\ii y)^r\kappa_{r,K}/r!$,
with normalized cumulants $O_r(m^{1-r/2})$. After $z=\sigma_Ky$ the integrand
converges in $L^1$ to $\e^{-z^2/2}$ by the bounds just proved, and Fourier
inversion gives \eqref{ctp:cl:eq:ullt}: the factor
$\e^{-\ii z(\ell-\E S_K)/\sigma_K}$ has modulus one, so the error is uniform
in $\ell$. Integrating the absolute value proves \eqref{ctp:cl:eq:atomupper};
for $\varepsilon>1$ retain the prefix $K=\lfloor m/2\rfloor$.
\end{proof}

The three arc ranges are those of Part~I, Section~\ref{ctp:sec:saddle},
which manuscript~10 adapts in the same way for the full sum
(\cref{ctp:te:thm:operator}); manuscript~06's addition is the uniformity
over prefixes that keep a fixed fraction of the active sizes, which
manuscript~10 also states for its tail bounds.

\begin{corollary}[Uniform coefficient ratios]\label[corollary]{ctp:cl:cor:ratio}
There is a constant $C$ such that for all large $n$ and every integer
$v\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:ratiobound}
 \frac{p(n-v)}{p(n)}\le C\e^{-tv}.
\end{equation}
For each fixed $A<\infty$, uniformly for $0\le tv\le A$,
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:ratiolimit}
 \frac{p(n-v)}{p(n)}=\e^{-tv}(1+o(1)).
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
By \cref{ctp:cl:lem:exact} the ratio is $\e^{-tv}\Q_t(S=n-v)/\Q_t(S=n)$.
The denominator is $t(2\pi m)^{-1/2}(1+o(1))$. Use
\eqref{ctp:cl:eq:atomupper} for the uniform bound and \eqref{ctp:cl:eq:ullt}
for bounded $tv$, since $v/\sigma=O(m^{-1/2})$.
\end{proof}

\emph{Second route (manuscript~10).} The coefficient operator gives
$\Q_t(S=n)=t(2\pi B_2)^{-1/2}(1+O(m^{-1}))$ (\cref{ctp:te:thm:operator});
combined with \eqref{ctp:cl:eq:atomupper} for the full sum this is
\eqref{ctp:cl:eq:ratiobound}, with $p(b)=0$ for $b<0$.

\subsection{The displacement expansion needed at the edge}
The following refinement records the term an uncentred normal approximation
would miss.

\begin{lemma}[A finite-tail likelihood expansion]\label[lemma]{ctp:cl:lem:edge-likelihood}
Fix $h\in\Z$, put $K=M+h$, and let
\[
 T=\sum_{k>K}c_kZ_k,\qquad \widehat T_{1,K}=t\E_{\Q_t}T,\qquad
 \widehat T_{2,K}=t^2\Var_{\Q_t}T,\qquad \delta_v=tT-\widehat T_{1,K}.
\]
For any upper multiplicity configuration $z$, its conditional likelihood
relative to its $\Q_t$-law is
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:likelihoodexp}
 R_v(z)=1+\frac1m\left\{\delta_v+\frac{\widehat T_{2,K}-\delta_v^2}2\right\}
 +\varepsilon_v(z),\qquad
 |\varepsilon_v(z)|\le\frac{C_h}{m^2}(1+|\delta_v|^4).
\end{equation}
In particular $\E_{\Q_t}|\varepsilon_v|=O_h(m^{-2})$. All constants can be
chosen uniformly in $\theta\in[0,1]$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The exact likelihood is $R_v(z)=\Q_t(S_K=n-T(z))/\Q_t(S=n)$. Every fixed
cumulant of the scaled tail $tT$ is bounded uniformly: its first rate
$tc_{K+1}$ is bounded below and later rates grow at least geometrically.
So the scaled prefix cumulants satisfy
$B_{2,K}=B_2-\widehat T_{2,K}=m+O_h(1)$ and $B_{3,K}=2m+O_h(1)$. With
$y=t(\ell-\E S_K)$, central Fourier expansion gives
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:displacement}
 \Q_t(S_K=\ell)=\frac{t}{\sqrt{2\pi B_{2,K}}}\left[1+E_{1,K}
 -\frac{y^2}{2B_{2,K}}-\frac{B_{3,K}\,y}{2B_{2,K}^2}
 +O_h\!\left(\frac{1+|y|^4}{m^2}\right)\right],
\end{equation}
where $E_{1,K}=B_{4,K}/(8B_{2,K}^2)-5B_{3,K}^2/(24B_{2,K}^3)=-1/(12m)+O_h(m^{-2})$
is the prefix analogue of Part~I's \eqref{ctp:eq:E1}. This is a polynomially
bounded \emph{absolute} error expansion, not a relative approximation at a
distant Gaussian tail. To see the remainder, set $x=y_{\rm Fourier}/t$,
expand the displacement factor through degree three and use
$|\e^{\ii a}-\sum_{q=0}^3(\ii a)^q/q!|\le|a|^4/4!$. The constant integral
has its usual fourth/sixth cumulant correction; the linear displacement
integral has leading term $-B_{3,K}y/(2B_{2,K}^2)$; the quadratic one gives
$-y^2/(2B_{2,K})$ with next error $O(y^2m^{-2})$; the cubic one is
$O(|y|^3m^{-2})$ by parity and the first odd cumulant; the fourth-order
remainder is $O(y^4m^{-2})$, and further centred expansion errors are
$O(m^{-2})$. The noncentral bounds of \cref{ctp:cl:lem:fourier}, at any
fixed precision, are uniform in the displacement, whose Fourier factor has
modulus one.

For the numerator $y=\widehat T_{1,K}-tT=-\delta_v$. The denominator is
$t(2\pi B_2)^{-1/2}(1-1/(12m)+O(m^{-2}))$. The common Edgeworth constant
cancels, $\sqrt{B_2/B_{2,K}}=1+\widehat T_{2,K}/(2m)+O_h(m^{-2})$, and the
linear term becomes $+\delta_v/m$. Bounded fourth moments of $tT$ give the
$L^1$ version.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Why the linear term matters]\label[remark]{ctp:cl:rem:linear}
The term $+\delta_v/m$ comes from the positive skewness of the geometric
variables. A purely Gaussian likelihood gives only
$(\widehat T_{2,K}-\delta_v^2)/(2m)$ and the wrong first correction. The
exact saddle normalization is also essential to the coefficient.
\end{remark}

\section{A conditioned Gaussian coefficient operator}
\label{ctp:cl:sec:operator}

This section is manuscript~10's. It extends Part~I's all-orders saddle
estimate \cref{ctp:thm:saddle}, whose coefficients $E_j$ it contains as the
unmarked case, to marked amplitudes. Its noncentral-arc analysis is that of
Part~I, Section~\ref{ctp:sec:saddle}, and of \cref{ctp:cl:lem:fourier}; in
manuscript~10 it yields the uniform bound
$\sup_a\Q_t(S=a)\le Ct/\sqrt m$, which is \eqref{ctp:cl:eq:atomupper} for the
full sum, and the same bound for every prefix keeping a fixed positive
fraction of the active sizes and the parts $1,2,5$.

\subsection{The finite operator}
For a marked coefficient write
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:te:eq:quotient}
 \mathcal R_n(\amp)=\frac{[z^n]\{F(z)\amp(-\log z;t)\}}{p(n)}.
\end{equation}
The notation is local on the coefficient contour: in each application the
full marked product is a single-valued power series in $z$. Set
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:te:eq:aell}
 \mathsf a_\ell=t^\ell\partial_\tau^\ell\amp(\tau;t)\big|_{\tau=t}.
\end{equation}
For $J\ge0$ and a jet $\mathsf a=(\mathsf a_0,\mathsf a_1,\ldots)$ define
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:te:eq:operator}
 \Omega_J[\mathsf a]=
 \sum_{\substack{\ell,k_3,\ldots,k_{2J+2}\ge0\\
 \ell+\sum_{r=3}^{2J+2}(r-2)k_r\le2J\\ \ell+R\ \mathrm{even}}}
 \frac{(-1)^{(\ell-R)/2}(\ell+R-1)!!\,\mathsf a_\ell}{\ell!\,B_2^{(\ell+R)/2}}
 \prod_{r=3}^{2J+2}\frac{B_r^{k_r}}{k_r!\,(r!)^{k_r}},
 \qquad R=\sum_{r=3}^{2J+2}rk_r.
\end{equation}
Here $(-1)!!=1$ and $\mathbf1$ is the constant jet $(1,0,0,\ldots)$. The
index set is finite. A term of weight $w=\ell+\sum(r-2)k_r$ has order
$m^{-w/2}$ when the jet is bounded.

\begin{theorem}[Conditioned Gaussian coefficient expansion]\label{ctp:te:thm:operator}
For each fixed $J$, the marked products used in Part~II satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:te:eq:operator-ratio}
 \mathcal R_n(\amp)=\frac{\Omega_J[\mathsf a]}{\Omega_J[\mathbf1]}
 +O_J(m^{-J-1}).
\end{equation}
The error is uniform on compact admissible sets of marking parameters and on
$0\le\theta\le1$ for fixed cutoff offsets. This applies to the
number-of-parts marks of \cref{ctp:cl:sec:number}, to finitely many
fixed-small-part marks, to finitely many cutoff marks
(\cref{ctp:te:thm:cutoff}) and to fixed-offset tail-deletion amplitudes,
including their products. For a tail deletion with $|h|\le m^{1/8}$ the
relative first-order remainder is instead bounded as in
\cref{ctp:te:thm:growing}.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Use $z=\e^{-(t+\ii y)}$ and $v=y/t$. At the saddle,
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:te:eq:gaussian-exp}
 H(t+\ii y)-H(t)+\ii ny=-\frac{B_2v^2}2+\sum_{r\ge3}\frac{(-\ii)^rB_rv^r}{r!},
 \qquad
 \amp(t+\ii y;t)=\sum_{\ell\ge0}\frac{\mathsf a_\ell(\ii v)^\ell}{\ell!}.
\end{equation}
Expand the second exponential and integrate against the centred Gaussian.
Odd powers vanish. A term with indices $(\ell,k_r)$ has Gaussian moment
$(\ell+R-1)!!B_2^{-(\ell+R)/2}$ and phase $\ii^\ell(-\ii)^R=(-1)^{(\ell-R)/2}$;
these are the terms of \eqref{ctp:te:eq:operator}.

The expansion also has an analytic remainder. In $|y|\le t/2$, derivatives
of $H$ have Cauchy bounds from the absolutely convergent geometric sums
\eqref{ctp:eq:H-K}; each fixed-order scaled derivative is $O(m)$. The
amplitude jets of the stated classes are bounded on a fixed complex
neighbourhood of $\tau/t=1$. On $|v|\le\log m/\sqrt m$, Taylor-expand to an
order above $2J+2$, integrate the remainder against a Gaussian majorant and
use parity: the first omitted even weight integrates to $O(m^{-J-1})$, and
higher remainders are bounded by taking a few more Taylor terms. The
Gaussian may be extended to the real line at a superpolynomially small
error.

Localization for the marks is proved, not inferred from local analyticity.
A number-of-parts mark replaces the geometric rate $tc_k$ by $t(c_k+s)$;
on a compact subset of $\Rea s>-1$ the total normalizing ratio is bounded
above and below. For a complex mark the angular phase has an additional
bounded multiple of $t$. In the central-tail estimate keep only the
$m-O(\log m)$ sizes with $c_k\ge m$ and $c_k\le1/(tm^B)$; for them, outside
the central window, $c_ky$ dominates the extra $O(t)$ phase, so the central
bound \eqref{ctp:eq:central-tail-bound} holds with changed constants. The
intermediate fixed-factor and outer $1,2,5$ estimates also survive the
$O(t)$ phase perturbation. Finitely many small-part marks behave the same
after their rates are shifted; finitely many cutoff marks can be omitted
from the modulus bound, the $m-O(1)$ remaining factors still localizing the
integral; a fixed-offset deletion retains $m-O(1)$ active sizes and all
fixed small factors. So the noncentral integrals of every listed class are
negligible to every inverse power. The unmarked denominator is the same
Gaussian integral with jet $\mathbf1$, with leading term one after
normalization; dividing the two expansions proves
\eqref{ctp:te:eq:operator-ratio}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The scope of the amplitude condition]\label[remark]{ctp:te:rem:scope}
Being analytic near $t$ alone does not imply \eqref{ctp:te:eq:operator-ratio}:
an arbitrary amplitude could be large on another arc. The theorem asserts the
estimate for the specified product amplitudes, with the noncentral bounds
proved above; it does not assume a general coefficient-transfer theorem for
locally analytic functions.
\end{remark}

\section{The sharp threshold for an independent upper process}
\label{ctp:cl:sec:threshold}

This section is manuscript~06's. For an integer $K\ge0$ let $\nu_{n,K}$ be
the law of $(Z_k)_{k>K}$ under $\PP_n$ and $\Q_{t,K}$ its independent
geometric law under $\Q_t$, on the countable space of finitely supported
upper multiplicity vectors, and
\[
 \Delta_n(K)=\TV(\nu_{n,K},\Q_{t,K}),\qquad
 \TV(P,Q)=\tfrac12\sum_z|P(z)-Q(z)|.
\]

\begin{theorem}[Complete cutoff criterion]\label{ctp:cl:thm:threshold}
Let $\Phi$ be the standard normal distribution function, and
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:d}
 d(a)=2\left[\Phi\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{-\log a}{1-a}}\right)
 -\Phi\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{-a\log a}{1-a}}\right)\right],\quad0<a<1,
 \qquad d(0)=1,\quad d(1)=0.
\end{equation}
For every sequence of nonnegative integer cutoffs $K=K_n$,
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:tvlimit}
 \Delta_n(K)-d\bigl(\min\{K/M,1\}\bigr)\longrightarrow0.
\end{equation}
In particular
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:iff}
 \Delta_n(K)\to0\iff(M-K)_+=o(M):
\end{equation}
an upper window is asymptotically independent exactly when it contains
$o(\log n)$ active sizes. If $K/M\to a\in(0,1)$ and
$T_K=\sum_{k>K}c_kZ_k$, then
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:criticalenergy}
 \frac{t(T_K-\E_{\Q_t}T_K)}{\sqrt m}\ \xrightarrow[\PP_n]{\ d\ }\ \Normal(0,a(1-a)),
\end{equation}
with convergence of every fixed moment; under $\Q_t$ the limiting variance
is $1-a$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Put $X=S_K$ and $T=S-X$. Independence under $\Q_t$ gives the exact
likelihood and total-variation identities
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:tv-exact}
 R_n(T)=\frac{\Q_t(X=n-T)}{\Q_t(S=n)},\qquad
 \Delta_n(K)=\tfrac12\E_{\Q_t}|R_n(T)-1|,
\end{equation}
the concrete Arratia--Tavar\'e reduction.

Suppose first $K/M\to a\in(0,1)$. Write $\sigma^2=\Var S$,
$\sigma_X^2=\Var X$, $\sigma_T^2=\Var T$ and $\widetilde W=(T-\E T)/\sigma_T$.
Then $\sigma_X^2/\sigma^2\to a$ and $\widetilde W\Rightarrow\Normal(0,1)$, for
instance by Lyapunov's condition: the third absolute centred moments sum to
$O((m-K)/t^3)$, while $\sigma_T^3\asymp(m-K)^{3/2}/t^3$; the bounded set of
inactive rates does not change this. The uniform local limit theorem and
$n=\E X+\E T$ give, uniformly in $T$,
\[
 R_n(T)=\frac\sigma{\sigma_X}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\sigma_T^2\widetilde W^2}{2\sigma_X^2}\right)+o(1).
\]
The leading term is bounded, so the limit of \eqref{ctp:cl:eq:tv-exact} is
$\frac12\E|a^{-1/2}\exp(-\frac{1-a}{2a}Z^2)-1|$ with $Z\sim\Normal(0,1)$.
Multiplying the standard normal density by the likelihood inside the
absolute value gives the density of $\Normal(0,a)$; the two densities cross
at $|z|=\sqrt{-a\log a/(1-a)}$, and integrating their difference between
the crossings gives \eqref{ctp:cl:eq:d}.

If $K/M\to1$ with $K\le M$, then $\sigma_T^2/\sigma^2\to0$, the likelihood
tends to one in probability and stays bounded, so $\Delta_n(K)\to0$. For
$K\ge M$ project the upper process after $M$ onto that after $K$: total
variation cannot increase. If $K/M\to0$, fix $0<\varepsilon<1$; eventually
$K\le\lfloor\varepsilon M\rfloor$, and projection gives
$\Delta_n(K)\ge\Delta_n(\lfloor\varepsilon M\rfloor)$, whose lower limit is
at least $d(\varepsilon)$; let $\varepsilon\downarrow0$. Since
$d(\varepsilon)\to1$, $\Delta_n(K)\to1$. This needs no local theorem for a
suffix of Catalan sizes. For an arbitrary cutoff sequence extract a
convergent subsequence of $\min(K/M,1)$; the three cases prove
\eqref{ctp:cl:eq:tvlimit} along it. Continuity of $d$ and the fact that its
only zero is at one give \eqref{ctp:cl:eq:iff}.

Finally the same bounded likelihood weights the canonical tail normal
limit, changing its variance from $1-a$ to $a(1-a)$. Uniform moment bounds
for the canonical normalized tail follow from its cumulants; the likelihood
is bounded by \cref{ctp:cl:lem:fourier}; applying these bounds at an order
above the moment considered proves moment convergence.
\end{proof}

\subsection{A sharp rate for growing windows}

\begin{theorem}[Rate inside the independent regime]\label{ctp:cl:thm:smalltail}
Suppose $L=M-K\to\infty$ and $L=o(M)$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:smalltail}
 \Delta_n(K)\sim\phi(1)\frac Lm,\qquad\phi(1)=\frac{\e^{-1/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}.
\end{equation}
No assumption such as $L\gg\sqrt m$ is needed.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $b=\sigma_T^2/\sigma^2\sim L/m$ and $\widetilde W=(T-\E T)/\sigma_T$.
Keeping the first odd term in the Fourier proof gives the uniform local
Edgeworth formula
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:firstedgeworth}
 \sigma_X\Q_t(X=\ell)=\phi(x)\left(1+\frac{\kappa_3(X)}{6\sigma_X^3}(x^3-3x)\right)
 +O(m^{-1}),\qquad x=\frac{\ell-\E X}{\sigma_X}.
\end{equation}
On the central interval the logarithmic characteristic remainder after the
cubic is $O(m^{-1}z^4)$; the cubic is imaginary and
$|\e^{\ii u}-1-\ii u|\le u^2/2$, so the integrated error is at most
$Cm^{-1}(z^4+z^6)\e^{-cz^2}$, and the three noncentral arc estimates are
smaller. This proves \eqref{ctp:cl:eq:firstedgeworth} uniformly in the
lattice target, for the prefix $X=S_K$ and for the full sum $X=S$. At
$n=\E S$ its odd term vanishes, so $\Q_t(S=n)=\phi(0)\sigma^{-1}(1+O(m^{-1}))$.

At $\ell=n-T$, $x=-\sqrt{b/(1-b)}\,\widetilde W$. Its Edgeworth term has
expected absolute size $O(m^{-1/2}\sqrt b)=O(\sqrt L/m)$, by bounded first
and third moments of $\widetilde W$, and the uniform remainder contributes
$O(m^{-1})$; both are $o(L/m)$. So
\[
 \Delta_n(K)=\tfrac12\E|g_b(\widetilde W)-1|+o(L/m),\qquad
 g_b(w)=(1-b)^{-1/2}\e^{-bw^2/(2(1-b))}.
\]
For $0\le b\le1/2$, $|g_b(w)-1-\frac b2(1-w^2)|\le Cb^2(1+w^4)$. Since
$\widetilde W\Rightarrow\Normal(0,1)$ with uniformly bounded higher moments,
$\E|1-\widetilde W^2|\to4\phi(1)$, and $\Delta_n(K)\sim b\phi(1)$.
\end{proof}

For example, observing the upper $M^{1/3}$ active indices gives distance
asymptotic to $\phi(1)M^{-2/3}$; observing half of them gives distance
tending to $d(1/2)=0.1660640749\ldots$ (\cref{ctp:cl:fig:tv}). These regimes cannot be told apart
by convergence of a single fixed edge coordinate.

\section{Corrected laws at the upper edge}
\label{ctp:cl:sec:edge}

\subsection{The corrected joint law of the upper process}
For $\theta\in[0,1]$ let $(Y_j^{(\theta)})_{j\in\Z}$ be independent with
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:idealgeom}
 \PP(Y_j^{(\theta)}=z)=(1-\e^{-u_j})\e^{-u_jz},\qquad u_j=4^{j-\theta}.
\end{equation}
For each fixed $h$ the restriction to $j>h$ has almost surely finitely many
nonzero coordinates; denote that upper law by $\Pi_{\theta,h}$. Define
\begin{align}
 \bar y_j&=\frac1{\e^{u_j}-1},& w_j&=\frac{\e^{u_j}}{(\e^{u_j}-1)^2},
 \label{ctp:cl:eq:muw}\\
 A_h&=\sum_{j>h}u_j\bar y_j,& V_h&=\sum_{j>h}u_j^2w_j,&
 U_h&=\sum_{j>h}(j-\theta)u_j\bar y_j,\label{ctp:cl:eq:AVB}\\
 T_h(z)&=\sum_{j>h}u_jz_j,& \delta_h(z)&=T_h(z)-A_h.\notag
\end{align}
Every sum converges, and all fixed moments of $T_h$ are bounded uniformly in
$\theta\in[0,1]$. (These are manuscript~10's $S_{1,h}$, $S_{2,h}$ and $U_h$;
$\bar y_j$ and $w_j$ are the mean and variance of $Y_j^{(\theta)}$.)

\begin{theorem}[Corrected joint edge law]\label{ctp:cl:thm:edge}
Fix $h\in\Z$. Let $\nu_{n,h}$ be the law of $(Z_{M+j})_{j>h}$ under $\PP_n$,
and
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:score}
 \mathcal P_{\theta,h}(z)=\delta_h(z)+\frac{V_h-\delta_h(z)^2}2
 +\frac32\sum_{j>h}(j-\theta)u_j(z_j-\bar y_j).
\end{equation}
Then, with $\theta=\theta_n$,
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:edgeexp}
 \int\left|\frac{\dd\nu_{n,h}}{\dd\Pi_{\theta,h}}(z)-1
 -\frac{\mathcal P_{\theta,h}(z)}m\right|\dd\Pi_{\theta,h}(z)=O_h(m^{-2}).
\end{equation}
For each fixed $q\ge0$ the same holds after multiplying the integrand by
$(1+T_h(z))^q$, with an $O_{h,q}(m^{-2})$ bound. In particular
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:edge-tv-sharp}
 \TV(\nu_{n,h},\Pi_{\theta,h})=\frac1{2m}\E_{\Pi_{\theta,h}}|\mathcal P_{\theta,h}|+O_h(m^{-2}),
\end{equation}
and the leading coefficient is positive at every phase: the approximation
rate $1/\log n$ is sharp.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
First keep the actual geometric rates $v_j=tc_{M+j}$; the conditional law
relative to that product is given by \cref{ctp:cl:lem:edge-likelihood}. We
transport the product from $v_j$ to $u_j$ in $L^1$. Put
$\eta_j=\log(v_j/u_j)$. The gamma-ratio expansion and its derivative bounds
give
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:eta}
 |\eta_j|\le C_h(1+|j|)/m,\qquad
 \left|\eta_j+\frac{3(j-\theta)}{2m}\right|\le C_h(1+j^2)/m^2,
\end{equation}
for all $j>h$ once $m$ is large: for positive $j-\theta$ use
$0\le x-\log(1+x)\le x^2/2$; the finitely many negative offsets use ordinary
Taylor expansion.

Interpolate logarithmically, $\lambda_j(s)=u_j\e^{s\eta_j}$, $0\le s\le1$;
all these rates are at least $c_h2^j$ for $j\ge0$. For a finite product with
density $p_s$ the score is
\[
 L_s=\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\log p_s=\sum_{j>h}\eta_j\lambda_j(s)
 \left(\frac1{\e^{\lambda_j(s)}-1}-Z_j\right),
\]
a sum of independent centred terms. Exponential decay of geometric moments
at large rates and \eqref{ctp:cl:eq:eta} give $\E_sL_s^2=O_h(m^{-2})$ and
$\E_s|L_s'|=O_h(m^{-2})$, uniformly in the finite truncation: all
controlling series are polynomial factors in $j$ and the rates times an
exponentially decaying function of the rate. Since $p_s''=p_s(L_s^2+L_s')$,
Taylor's integral remainder in $L^1$ yields
\[
 \Q_{t}\bigl((Z_{M+j})_{j>h}\in\cdot\bigr)=\Pi_{\theta,h}
 +\frac3{2m}\Bigl[\sum_{j>h}(j-\theta)u_j(Z_j-\bar y_j)\Bigr]\Pi_{\theta,h}
 +O_{L^1,h}(m^{-2}),
\]
and finite products converge to the infinite upper products in total
variation, so the uniform estimate passes to the limit.

The same argument works with polynomial energy weights: $u_j\le C_h4^j$
while $\lambda_j(s)\ge c_h2^j$, so the weighted geometric moments still have
summable bounds. Replacing the actual centred energy and its variance in the
quadratic conditioning score by $\delta_h,V_h$ changes that score times its
product measure by $O_h(m^{-1})$ in weighted $L^1$: differences of the
energy are sums of $(v_j-u_j)Z_j$, whose fixed polynomial moments are
$O_h(m^{-1})$, and the differences of mean and variance obey the same
bound; the factor $1/m$ makes this $O_h(m^{-2})$. Combining the two
expansions proves \eqref{ctp:cl:eq:edgeexp} and its weighted version.

The score has mean zero, since $\E\delta_h=0$ and $\E\delta_h^2=V_h$; the
reverse triangle inequality gives \eqref{ctp:cl:eq:edge-tv-sharp}. It is not
the zero variable: fixing all other coordinates at zero leaves a quadratic
polynomial in any one coordinate with nonzero coefficient $-u_j^2/2$, and
each such configuration has positive product probability.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The corrected approximation is signed]\label[remark]{ctp:cl:rem:signed}
$(1+\mathcal P_{\theta,h}/m)\Pi_{\theta,h}$ is a signed first-order
approximation of total mass one; it need not be nonnegative on every remote
configuration. The theorem is the $L^1$ bound \eqref{ctp:cl:eq:edgeexp}; it
asserts no global positivity of the truncated density, and the proof needs
no $L^2$ density ratio between the two infinite product measures.
\end{remark}

\begin{corollary}[First correlations of distinct large sizes]\label[corollary]{ctp:cl:cor:cov}
For distinct fixed integers $i,j$,
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:edgecov}
 \Cov_{\PP_n}(Z_{M+i},Z_{M+j})=-\frac{u_iu_jw_iw_j}m+O_{i,j}(m^{-2}).
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Choose $h<\min(i,j)$ in \cref{ctp:cl:thm:edge}. Under the product law the
centred coordinates are independent; in the expectation of
$(Z_{M+i}-\bar y_i)(Z_{M+j}-\bar y_j)$ all linear terms of the score vanish
and only the cross term of $-\delta_h^2/2$ survives, giving
$-u_iu_jw_iw_j/m$. The product of the two mean displacements is
$O(m^{-2})$, and the weighted remainder justifies the quadratic observable.
\end{proof}

So the asymptotically independent upper variables keep a specific negative
interaction at first order. \emph{Second route.} The cross term of
$-\frac12\mathcal D_I^2\mathcal G_I$ in manuscript~10's operator
\eqref{ctp:te:eq:cutoff-C} gives the same $-u_iu_jw_iw_j/m$; the dossier
checked this agreement by hand.

\subsection{The cutoff vector jointly with the number of parts}
Manuscript~10 states the edge law as a transform, for a finite set of
offsets, jointly with the number of parts. Its edge part is the
transform form of \cref{ctp:cl:thm:edge} (which is stronger: $L^1$, the
infinite upper process, polynomial weights); the joint statement with
$N_n$ is new. Let $I\subset\Z$ be finite and put, with $\LW$ the Laplace
transform of $W$ \eqref{ctp:te:eq:L},
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:te:eq:Q}
 \mathcal G_I(\mathbf u,\mathbf x)=\prod_{j\in I}
 \frac{1-\e^{-u_j}}{1-x_j\e^{-u_j}},\qquad
 \mathcal D_I=\sum_{j\in I}u_j\partial_{u_j}.
\end{equation}
In a derivative the $u_j$ are independent variables, set to $4^{j-\theta}$
afterwards. For $\mathbf x\in[0,1]^I$, and on a small complex neighbourhood
of that cube, the denominators stay away from zero. Define
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:te:eq:cutoff-C}
 \mathcal C_I\mathcal G=-\frac32\sum_{j\in I}(j-\theta)u_j\partial_{u_j}\mathcal G
 -\frac12(\mathcal D_I^2+\mathcal D_I)\mathcal G.
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Cutoff vector and its joint law with the number of parts]\label{ctp:te:thm:cutoff}
For fixed $I$ and compact admissible parameter sets,
\begin{align}
 \E\prod_{j\in I}x_j^{Z_{M+j}}&=\mathcal G_I+\frac{\mathcal C_I\mathcal G_I}m+O(m^{-2}),
 \label{ctp:te:eq:cutoff-law}\\
 \E\Bigl[\e^{-stN_n}\prod_{j\in I}x_j^{Z_{M+j}}\Bigr]&=\LW\mathcal G_I
 +\frac1m\Bigl\{\LW\,\mathcal C_I\mathcal G_I-\frac{s^2\LW''\mathcal G_I}2
 +s\LW'\mathcal D_I\mathcal G_I\Bigr\}+O(m^{-2}),\label{ctp:te:eq:joint-cutoff}
\end{align}
uniformly in $0\le\theta\le1$. Along any subsequence $\theta_n\to\theta$
the cutoff multiplicities tend to independent geometric variables with
parameters $\e^{-4^{j-\theta}}$, and this finite vector is independent of
$W$ in the joint limit.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The exact cutoff amplitude is a finite product of the factors of
\eqref{ctp:te:eq:Q} with $u_j$ replaced by $\tau c_{M+j}$. At $\tau=t$,
\eqref{ctp:cl:eq:edgerate} gives $tc_{M+j}=u_j(1-3(j-\theta)/(2m)+O_I(m^{-2}))$,
which produces the first term of \eqref{ctp:te:eq:cutoff-C}. At the limiting
arguments, the jets under $\tau=t(1+v)$ are $\mathsf a_0=\mathcal G_I$,
$\mathsf a_1=\mathcal D_I\mathcal G_I$ and
$\mathsf a_2=(\mathcal D_I^2-\mathcal D_I)\mathcal G_I$, and
\eqref{ctp:te:eq:first-op} produces the second term.

For the joint mark, the product of the length and cutoff amplitudes differs
from the exact joint amplitude by $O_I(t)$, with the same bound for scaled
derivatives: in the finitely many marked cutoff factors the extra mark
changes $\e^{-\tau c}$ to $\e^{-\tau c-st}$, and $tc$ is bounded above and
away from zero there. This is below every inverse power of $m$. The first
two jets of $\LW(s/(1+v))\mathcal G_I((1+v)\mathbf u)$ give
\[
 -\frac{\mathsf a_2+2\mathsf a_1}2=-\frac{s^2\LW''\mathcal G_I}2+s\LW'\mathcal D_I\mathcal G_I
 -\frac{\LW}2(\mathcal D_I^2+\mathcal D_I)\mathcal G_I;
\]
add the geometric-curvature correction to obtain \eqref{ctp:te:eq:joint-cutoff}.
The leading product transform proves the joint limit and independence; its
continuity at zero and at $\mathbf x=\mathbf1$ gives tightness. No
independence at finite $n$ is assumed.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Exact cylinder events.} For fixed nonnegative integers $r_j$ the
limiting probability of $\{Z_{M+j}=r_j,\ j\in I\}$ is
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:te:eq:cylinder}
 \pi_{\mathbf r}(\mathbf u)=\prod_{j\in I}(1-\e^{-u_j})\e^{-r_ju_j},
\end{equation}
with first correction $\mathcal C_I\pi_{\mathbf r}/m$, by coefficient
extraction in the marking variables or by the same amplitude argument
applied to $\prod_{j\in I}(1-z^{c_{M+j}})z^{r_jc_{M+j}}$. With
$T=\sum_{j\in I}(u_j/(\e^{u_j}-1)-r_ju_j)$ and
$V_2=\sum_{j\in I}u_j^2\e^{u_j}/(\e^{u_j}-1)^2$,
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:te:eq:cylinder-correction}
 \frac{\mathcal C_I\pi_{\mathbf r}}{\pi_{\mathbf r}}=-\frac32\sum_{j\in I}(j-\theta)
 \left(\frac{u_j}{\e^{u_j}-1}-r_ju_j\right)+\frac{V_2-T^2-2T}2.
\end{equation}
An independent finite-$n$ identity, used for verification, is
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:te:eq:inclusion-exclusion}
 \PP_n(Z_{M+j}=r_j\ (j\in I))=\sum_{A\subseteq I}(-1)^{|A|}
 \frac{p\bigl(n-\sum_{j\in I}(r_j+\ind_{\{j\in A\}})c_{M+j}\bigr)}{p(n)},
\end{equation}
obtained by removing the specified copies and excluding further copies of
the selected sizes; it needs no saddle approximation.

\subsection{The corrected largest-index law}
Define, as in Part~I's \eqref{ctp:eq:largest-cdf} and with the tail sums
\eqref{ctp:cl:eq:AVB},
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:G}
 G_h(\theta)=\prod_{j>h}(1-\e^{-4^{j-\theta}}),\qquad
 C_h(\theta)=-A_h+\frac{V_h-A_h^2}2-\frac32U_h,\qquad h\in\Z.
\end{equation}
$G_h$ tends to one as $h\to+\infty$ and to zero as $h\to-\infty$; it is the
distribution function of $\Jl_\theta=\max\{j:Y_j^{(\theta)}>0\}$. All
products and sums converge absolutely; for fixed $h$ they and all their
phase derivatives are bounded on $[0,1]$, and $G_h$ is bounded away from
zero there.

\begin{corollary}[First correction to the maximum]\label[corollary]{ctp:cl:cor:maxcorrection}
For every fixed $h\in\Z$, uniformly in the saddle phase,
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:maxcorrection}
 \PP_n(J_n-M\le h)=G_h(\theta)\left(1+\frac{C_h(\theta)}m\right)+O_h(m^{-2}).
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}[Proof (manuscript 06)]
The event is that all coordinates $j>h$ vanish. There $\delta_h=-A_h$, and
the last score term equals $-\frac32U_h$. Apply \cref{ctp:cl:thm:edge}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Second proof (manuscript 10)]
Set $K=M+h$. Removing all factors with index above $K$ gives the exact
amplitude
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:te:eq:tail-amp}
 \amp_K(\tau)=\prod_{k>K}(1-\e^{-\tau c_k}),
\end{equation}
with scaled tail cumulants
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:te:eq:Tr}
 \widehat T_{r,K}=t^r\sum_{k>K}c_k^r\Li_{1-r}(\e^{-tc_k}).
\end{equation}
At fixed $h$ these are bounded, and the first jets are
$\mathsf a_0=\amp_K(t)$, $\mathsf a_1=\amp_K(t)\widehat T_{1,K}$,
$\mathsf a_2=\amp_K(t)(\widehat T_{1,K}^2-\widehat T_{2,K})$. The first
conditioning correction is therefore
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:te:eq:tail-exact-first}
 \amp_K(t)\left\{\frac{\widehat T_{2,K}-\widehat T_{1,K}^2}{2B_2}
 -\frac{B_3\widehat T_{1,K}}{2B_2^2}\right\}.
\end{equation}
The local expansion of the Catalan sizes gives
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:te:eq:tail-phase}
 \amp_K(t)=G_h(\theta)\left(1-\frac{3U_h(\theta)}{2m}+O_h(m^{-2})\right),\qquad
 \widehat T_{1,K}=A_h+O_h(m^{-1}),\quad \widehat T_{2,K}=V_h+O_h(m^{-1}).
\end{equation}
To sum the size expansion, split $j-\theta$ at $m/2$: below it the
derivatives of $\log(1-\e^{-u_j})$ have a summable superexponential majorant
at the positive end and only finitely many negative $j$ occur; above it the
whole tail is superexponentially small. So the errors in
\eqref{ctp:te:eq:tail-phase} are uniform in the phase, and
\eqref{ctp:te:eq:tail-exact-first}--\eqref{ctp:te:eq:tail-phase} with
$B_2=m+O(1)$, $B_3=2m+O(1)$ give \eqref{ctp:cl:eq:maxcorrection}, in
manuscript~10's form $C_h=-\frac32U_h+\frac12(V_h-A_h^2-2A_h)$.
\end{proof}

The correction has two sources. The term $-\frac32U_h$ records the
polynomial perturbation of the geometric scale $4^k$ in the Catalan numbers;
the other three terms are the effect of conditioning on the exact total
size. Dropping either changes the first correction. In the alternative
description of manuscript~06, let $u_j=\exp(\alpha(j-\theta))$ with
variable $\alpha>0$; termwise differentiation gives
$\partial_\theta G_h=-\alpha A_hG_h$,
$\partial_\theta^2G_h=\alpha^2(A_h^2+A_h-V_h)G_h$ and
$\partial_\alpha G_h=U_hG_h$, so that
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:differentialcorrection}
 G_hC_h=\left(\frac1{2\alpha}\partial_\theta-\frac1{2\alpha^2}\partial_\theta^2
 -\frac32\partial_\alpha\right)G_h\Big|_{\alpha=\log4},
\end{equation}
the $\alpha$-derivative keeping $\theta,h$ fixed. This separates the
conditioning derivatives from the polynomial factor $k^{-3/2}$ in Catalan
growth, in the same way as $-\frac32\partial_\alpha\Psi_\alpha$ enters
Part~I's \eqref{ctp:eq:D}.

\begin{theorem}[The maximum jointly with the number of parts]\label{ctp:te:thm:maxjoint}
For fixed $h\in\Z$, compact-uniformly in $\Rea s>-1$,
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:te:eq:max-joint}
 \E\bigl[\e^{-stN_n}\ind_{\{J_n\le M+h\}}\bigr]=G_h(\theta)\Bigl[\LW(s)+\frac1m
 \Bigl\{C_h(\theta)\LW(s)-\frac{s^2\LW''(s)}2+A_h(\theta)\,s\LW'(s)\Bigr\}\Bigr]
 +O_{h,\mathcal K}(m^{-2}).
\end{equation}
In particular
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:te:eq:max-cov}
 \Cov\bigl(tN_n,\ind_{\{J_n\le M+h\}}\bigr)=\frac{S_1\,G_h(\theta)A_h(\theta)}m+O_h(m^{-2}),
\end{equation}
and the leading coefficient is strictly positive.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For the joint mark use the amplitude
$\amp_s(\tau;t)\prod_{k>K}(1-\e^{-\tau c_k-st})$, with $\amp_s$ the length
amplitude \eqref{ctp:te:eq:length-amplitude}. Its difference from
$\LW(st/\tau)\amp_K(\tau)$, together with fixed scaled derivatives, is
$O_h(tm)$ on the local neighbourhood. The product-jet calculation of
\cref{ctp:te:thm:cutoff} gives \eqref{ctp:te:eq:max-joint}, the cross term
being $s\LW'G_hA_h/m$. Differentiate at $s=0$ and subtract the product of
the marginal expectations to obtain \eqref{ctp:te:eq:max-cov}; positivity is
immediate from \eqref{ctp:cl:eq:G} and \eqref{ctp:cl:eq:AVB}.
\end{proof}

\section{Growing offsets, uniform tails and moments of the largest index}
\label{ctp:cl:sec:moments}

A fixed-threshold distributional limit does not imply a moment theorem, and
a fixed-$h$ estimate does not justify substituting $h=h(n)$. This section
supplies what Part~I's Theorem~\ref{ctp:thm:largest} withholds.

\subsection{A uniform growing-window statement}
The next result (manuscript~10) is formulated with the exact tail
quantities \eqref{ctp:te:eq:Tr} precisely to avoid an uncontrolled
replacement of them by their phase limits.

\begin{theorem}[Growing-offset estimate]\label{ctp:te:thm:growing}
There is a constant $C$ such that for all large $n$ and all integers
$|h|\le m^{1/8}$, with $K=M+h$,
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:te:eq:growing}
 \frac{\PP_n(J_n\le K)}{\amp_K(t)}=1+\frac{\widehat T_{2,K}-\widehat T_{1,K}^2}{2B_2}
 -\frac{B_3\widehat T_{1,K}}{2B_2^2}+O\!\left(\frac{(1+|h|)^4}{m^2}\right),
\end{equation}
the implicit constant being independent of $h$ and of the phase.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Put $H_-=1+\max(0,-h)$. The ratio bound on consecutive sizes gives
$\widehat T_{r,K}=O_r(H_-)$ for every fixed $r$, and the same geometric-sum
estimates apply to derivatives on $|\tau/t-1|\le1/4$. Direct
differentiation of $\log\amp_K$ and the exponential Bell recurrence give
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:te:eq:relative-jets}
 \frac{|t^\ell\amp_K^{(\ell)}(t)|}{\amp_K(t)}\le C_\ell H_-^\ell.
\end{equation}
On the central window the derivative remainder carries at most a factor
$\exp(CH_-|y|/t)$, bounded because $H_-\log m/\sqrt m\to0$ in the stated
range. Expand the relative amplitude and the phase as in
\eqref{ctp:te:eq:gaussian-exp}. The retained terms have even weights zero
and two; the first omitted even weight is four, and by
\eqref{ctp:te:eq:relative-jets} it integrates to $O(H_-^4/m^2)$. Higher
weights are bounded by continuing the Taylor expansion before estimating the
remainder; their extra factors are powers of $H_-/\sqrt m$ and $m^{-1/2}$,
uniformly small. The denominator correction changes the retained
nonconstant terms by $O(H_-^2/m^2)$.

Localization is uniform without dividing a crude full-product bound by a
tiny $\amp_K(t)$: the numerator divided by $\amp_K(t)F(\e^{-t})$ is exactly
the normalized prefix characteristic function. The prefix has at least
$m-O(m^{1/8})$ active sizes and every fixed small factor, so the three arc
estimates hold with uniform constants for the \emph{relative} integral.
With the central estimate and \eqref{ctp:te:eq:first-op} this proves
\eqref{ctp:te:eq:growing}.
\end{proof}

The exponent $1/8$ is deliberately conservative: the theorem gives a proved
window growing to infinity, not an optimal moderate-deviation range or a
uniform phase-polynomial formula for arbitrarily large $|h|$.

\begin{remark}[{[write]} Re-reading the central-window remainder]\label[remark]{ctp:te:rem:growing}
The dossier flagged this proof as compressed. The write checked that the
relative amplitude is analytic and zero-free on $|\tau/t-1|\le1/4$ with
$t(\log\amp_K)^{(\ell)}$-bounds $O_\ell(H_-)$: for $k>K$ the points
$\tau c_k$ lie in the sector $|\arg z|\le\arcsin\frac14$, where
$1-\e^{-z}$ vanishes only at $z=0$; the factors with $tc_k\le1$ (at most
$H_-+1$ of them) contribute $O(1)$ each to $t\,\dd\log\amp_K/\dd\tau$, and the
factors with $tc_k>1$ a geometrically convergent $O(1)$ in total. Bell's
formula then gives \eqref{ctp:te:eq:relative-jets}; on the central window
$|\log\amp_K(t+\ii y)-\log\amp_K(t)|\le CH_-|y|/t\le CH_-\log m/\sqrt m\to0$.
A term of weight $w$ carries at most $w$ amplitude derivatives, hence is
$O(H_-^wm^{-w/2})$; weight four is the stated error. The proof is complete
as stated. The same argument applies verbatim when the amplitude also
carries the number-of-parts mark of \cref{ctp:te:thm:length}, whose jets are
bounded; this is used in \cref{ctp:cl:thm:mixedcov}.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Uniform two-sided tails}

\begin{lemma}[Uniform two-sided tails]\label[lemma]{ctp:cl:lem:maxtails}
There are positive constants $C,c$ such that for all large $n$ and all
integers $r,h\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:maxtails}
 \PP_n(J_n-M>h)\le C\e^{-c2^h},\qquad
 \PP_n(J_n-M\le-r)\le C\e^{-cr^2}.
\end{equation}
The same bounds hold for $\Jl_\theta$, uniformly in $\theta\in[0,1]$.
Consequently, for every fixed $a>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:te:eq:UI}
 \sup_{n\ge n_0}\E\exp(a|J_n-M|)<\infty.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Right tail: the event that a size $c_k$ occurs has exact probability
$p(n-c_k)/p(n)$ (remove one copy), so the union bound,
\cref{ctp:cl:lem:exact} and \cref{ctp:cl:cor:ratio} give
$\PP_n(J_n>M+h)\le C\sum_{k>M+h}\e^{-tc_k}$. Since $tc_M>1/4$ and successive
sizes have ratio at least two, $tc_{M+h}\ge2^h/4$, and the sum is at most
$C\e^{-c2^h}$.

Left tail: first take $0\le r\le M/2$ and $K=M-r$. Independence before
conditioning gives
$\PP_n(J_n\le K)=\amp_K(t)\,\Q_t(S_K=n)/\Q_t(S=n)$; the event fixes the
upper process at zero. The uniform prefix atom bound, divided by the full
atom at its mean, is bounded by a constant, so
\[
 \PP_n(J_n\le K)\le C\prod_{k>K}(1-\e^{-tc_k})\le C\prod_{\ell=0}^{r-1}tc_{M-\ell}
 \le C\,2^{-r(r-1)/2},
\]
since $tc_{M-\ell}\le2^{-\ell}$. For $M/2<r<M$ bound the event by the one at
$r=\lfloor M/2\rfloor$ and reduce the constant in the Gaussian exponent; for
$r\ge M$ the event is empty because $J_n\ge1$. The product-law bounds follow
from the same elementary products and sums without conditioning. Summing
the two tails against $\e^{ah}$ proves \eqref{ctp:te:eq:UI}.
\end{proof}

Both manuscripts prove this lemma, by the same argument;
\eqref{ctp:te:eq:UI} is manuscript~10's statement of its consequence.

\begin{corollary}[All maximum moments and a periodic mean]\label[corollary]{ctp:te:cor:maxmom}
If $\theta_n\to\theta$, then $J_n-M\Rightarrow\Jl_\theta$, and all
polynomial and real exponential moments converge. Define
\begin{align}
 \bar\Jl(\theta)&=\E\Jl_\theta=\sum_{h\ge0}(1-G_h(\theta))-\sum_{h\le-1}G_h(\theta),
 \label{ctp:cl:eq:b}\\
 F_0(\theta)&=\bar\Jl(\theta)-\theta,\qquad
 \mathcal V_0(\theta)=\Var\Jl_\theta=\sum_{h\in\Z}(h-\bar\Jl(\theta))^2\,\mathfrak p_h(\theta),
 \notag
\end{align}
where $\mathfrak p_h=G_h-G_{h-1}$. Then $F_0$ and $\mathcal V_0$ are smooth
one-periodic functions and, along the full sequence,
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:te:eq:maxmean}
 \E J_n=m+F_0(\theta_n)+o(1),\qquad\Var J_n=\mathcal V_0(\theta_n)+o(1).
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
For each phase $G_h\to0$ as $h\to-\infty$ and $G_h\to1$ as $h\to+\infty$, so
it defines a proper integer law. \Cref{ctp:cl:cor:maxcorrection} gives
convergence at every integer cutoff, and \cref{ctp:cl:lem:maxtails} uniform
integrability for every polynomial and exponential function. Tail summation
proves \eqref{ctp:cl:eq:b}, and the variance formula follows from the point
probabilities. Uniform phase convergence on bounded $h$-ranges, followed by
the uniform tails, proves \eqref{ctp:te:eq:maxmean} on the full sequence.
Note that $G_h$ itself is \emph{not} one-periodic:
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:te:eq:phase-shift}
 G_h(\theta+1)=G_{h-1}(\theta),\qquad\Jl_{\theta+1}\stackrel d=\Jl_\theta+1,
\end{equation}
so $\bar\Jl(\theta+1)=\bar\Jl(\theta)+1$, while $F_0$ and $\mathcal V_0$
are periodic; it would be wrong to call $\bar\Jl$ periodic. Differentiated
lattice products have the same summable tails up to polynomial factors in
$|h|$, so termwise differentiation proves smoothness.
\end{proof}

This (manuscript~10's) corollary supplies the moment conclusion missing from
a fixed-$h$ statement and distinguishes a phase-dependent family of integer
laws from a continuous extreme-value approximation.
\Cref{ctp:cl:thm:maxweighted} below (manuscript~06) sharpens
\eqref{ctp:te:eq:maxmean} to an expansion with $O(m^{-2})$ remainder.

Every phase is realized by a subsequence: implicit differentiation of the
saddle equations gives $\dd m/\dd n=t/(r'(m)B_2)\sim1/(n\log4)$, so $m(n)$
is increasing, unbounded and has increments tending to zero, and rounding
the real inverse of $m=j+\theta$ realizes any limiting phase (compare the
proof of Part~I's \cref{ctp:cor:oscillation}). The phase family is a
feature of the integer sequence, not an artificial continuous parameter.

\subsection{The first correction summed over all thresholds}

\begin{theorem}[A weighted expansion for the maximum]\label{ctp:cl:thm:maxweighted}
Put $\mathfrak p_h(\theta)=G_h(\theta)-G_{h-1}(\theta)$ and
$\mathfrak r_h(\theta)=G_h(\theta)C_h(\theta)-G_{h-1}(\theta)C_{h-1}(\theta)$.
For every fixed $\lambda>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:weightedmax}
 \sum_{h\in\Z}\e^{\lambda|h|}\left|\PP_n(J_n-M=h)-\mathfrak p_h(\theta)
 -\frac{\mathfrak r_h(\theta)}m\right|=O_\lambda(m^{-2}).
\end{equation}
So the first correction is valid for every polynomially or exponentially
bounded function of the centred maximum.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $E^{\max}_h$ be the error in \eqref{ctp:cl:eq:maxcorrection}. We prove
$\sum_h\e^{\lambda|h|}|E^{\max}_h|=O_\lambda(m^{-2})$; successive differences
then prove the theorem.

\emph{Negative thresholds} $h=-r$, $0\le r\le M/2$. The prefix has at least
$M/2$ active sizes, so the Fourier constants are uniform, and its removed
scaled cumulants are $O_q(r+1)$. Repeating the displacement computation of
\cref{ctp:cl:lem:edge-likelihood}, now expanding the variance ratio for
$\widehat T_{2,K}/B_2\le1/2+o(1)$, gives at the all-zero configuration
\[
 R_v(0)=1+\frac{-\widehat T_{1,K}+(\widehat T_{2,K}-\widehat T_{1,K}^2)/2}m
 +O\!\left(\frac{1+r^4}{m^2}\right).
\]
The inverse-variance replacements contribute $O((r+1)/m^2)$ before
multiplication by the displacement, and all resulting terms fit the bound.
Write $\varrho_1(v)=v/(\e^v-1)$ and $\varrho_2(v)=v^2\e^v/(\e^v-1)^2$. For
$r\le M/2$ and $j>-r$ one has $m+j-\theta\ge m/2-O(1)$, so the proof of
\eqref{ctp:cl:eq:eta}, with the logarithmic expansion on
$(j-\theta)/m\ge-1/2-O(m^{-1})$, gives those bounds with a constant
independent of $r$. The logarithmic derivatives of $\varrho_1$ and
$\varrho_2$ vanish at zero to orders one and two; with \eqref{ctp:cl:eq:eta}
and the geometric rate bounds this gives, uniformly in this range,
\[
 |\widehat T_{1,K}-A_{-r}|+|\widehat T_{2,K}-V_{-r}|\le C/m.
\]
For the all-zero product itself, Taylor expansion of $\log(1-\e^{-v})$ gives
\[
 \log\frac{\amp_K(t)}{G_{-r}}=-\frac{3U_{-r}}{2m}+O\!\left(\frac{1+r^3}{m^2}\right),
 \qquad\left|\log\frac{\amp_K(t)}{G_{-r}}\right|\le C(1+r^2)/m,
\]
the cubic bound coming from summing the second-order rate errors over the
$r$ small rates. Since $G_{-r}\le C\e^{-cr^2}$, exponentiating and
multiplying the two expansions gives
\[
 |E^{\max}_{-r}|\le Cm^{-2}(1+r^4)G_{-r}\exp(C(1+r^2)/m)\le Cm^{-2}\e^{-c'r^2}.
\]
For $r>M/2$ the midpoint argument of \cref{ctp:cl:lem:maxtails} and the same
Gaussian bound for $G_{-r}C_{-r}$ give this with smaller exponent constants;
for $r\ge M$ the event is empty. All negative errors are summable with every
exponential weight.

\emph{Positive thresholds.} Use the weighted version of
\cref{ctp:cl:thm:edge} for the upper process $j>0$. For every $h\ge0$,
\[
 \int_{\{z_j=0\text{ for all }j>h\}}\mathcal P_{\theta,0}(z)\,\dd\Pi_{\theta,0}(z)
 =G_h(\theta)C_h(\theta):
\]
the free coordinates $1,\ldots,h$ have centred means, and their
contribution to the expected quadratic energy cancels the corresponding
variance term. So $E^{\max}_h$ is exactly the error of this marginal of the
upper-process expansion. Its signed remainder measure has total mass zero,
so the error on the event that all $j>h$ vanish is minus the error on the
complement. On the complement the energy $T_0$ is at least $4^{h-\theta}$,
so for fixed $\lambda$ the sum of the weights $\e^{\lambda h}$ over
thresholds below the largest occupied upper index is at most
$C_\lambda(1+T_0)^q$ for a fixed large $q$. The weighted $L^1$ bound of
\cref{ctp:cl:thm:edge} proves $\sum_{h\ge0}\e^{\lambda h}|E^{\max}_h|=O_\lambda(m^{-2})$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[{[write]} Re-reading the negative thresholds]\label[remark]{ctp:cl:rem:negative}
The dossier flagged the negative-threshold step as compressed (``repeating
the displacement computation'' uniformly for $r\le M/2$). The write checked

\subsection{The largest part size itself}
The largest size is $c_{J_n}$. Along any subsequence $\theta_n\to\theta$,

\section{The number of parts}
\label{ctp:cl:sec:number}

\subsection{The limiting variable}
Let $\mathbf E_1,\mathbf E_2,\ldots$ be independent exponential variables of
mean one, and
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:W}
 W=\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{\mathbf E_k}{c_k},\qquad S_r=\sum_{k\ge1}c_k^{-r},\qquad
 \omega_q=\E W^q.
\end{equation}
The series converges almost surely and in every $L^q$, since
$\sum_k1/c_k<\infty$ and Minkowski's inequality applies (equivalently,
the geometric lower bound on $c_k$ gives all moment bounds). Its Laplace
transform is
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:te:eq:L}
 \LW(s)=\E\e^{-sW}=\prod_{k\ge1}\frac{c_k}{c_k+s},\qquad\Rea s>-1,
\end{equation}
converging locally uniformly on this half-plane, whose boundary is forced by
the first exponential.

\begin{theorem}[Number-of-parts law, first correction and all moments]\label{ctp:te:thm:length}
As $n\to\infty$,
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:te:eq:length-first}
 \E\e^{-stN_n}=\LW(s)-\frac{s^2\LW''(s)}{2m}+O_{\mathcal K}(m^{-2})
\end{equation}
uniformly for $s$ in every compact $\mathcal K\subset\{\Rea s>-1\}$. In
particular $tN_n\Rightarrow W$, and for every fixed integer $q\ge0$
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:te:eq:moments}
 t^q\E N_n^q=\omega_q\left(1-\frac{q(q-1)}{2m}\right)+O_q(m^{-2}).
\end{equation}
For every real $a<1$, $\E\e^{atN_n}\to\E\e^{aW}$, so all fixed absolute
moments converge as well.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
With the marking variable $\e^{-st}$, the generating function marked by the
number of parts is $\prod_k(1-\e^{-st}z^{c_k})^{-1}$; its amplitude
relative to $F$ is
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:te:eq:length-amplitude}
 \amp_s(\tau;t)=\prod_{k\ge1}\frac{1-\e^{-\tau c_k}}{1-\e^{-\tau c_k-st}}.
\end{equation}
Uniformly on a small fixed complex neighbourhood of $\tau/t=1$ (depending
on $\mathcal K$, \cref{ctp:te:rem:localization}) and on $\mathcal K$,
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:te:eq:amplitude-approx}
 \amp_s(\tau;t)=\LW(st/\tau)\{1+O_{\mathcal K}(tm)\};
\end{equation}
an exponentially small error such as $tm$ is below every fixed inverse power
of~$m$. To prove it, put $g_0(x)=\log((1-\e^{-x})/x)$, with its analytic
branch in the relevant right half-plane. After factoring the linear terms
out of each factor, the remaining logarithm is
$\sum_k[g_0(\tau c_k)-g_0(\tau c_k+st)]$. For $tc_k\le1$ each difference is
$O_{\mathcal K}(t)$, and there are $O(m)$ terms; for $tc_k>1$ the bound
$g_0'(x)=O(1/|x|)$ in the chosen sector gives a summable $O_{\mathcal K}(1/c_k)$,
whose tail is $O_{\mathcal K}(t)$. Cauchy estimates give the same accuracy for
any fixed number of scaled $\tau$-derivatives. (The kernel $g_0$ is the
Laplace-product kernel of the Fabius-tree compilation cited after
Part~I's \cref{ctp:lem:Fourier}; it is not Part~I's $g$.)

So the jet may be replaced by
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:te:eq:length-jet}
 \mathsf a_\ell(s)=\frac{\dd^\ell}{\dd v^\ell}\LW\!\left(\frac s{1+v}\right)\Big|_{v=0},
 \qquad \mathsf a_0=\LW,\quad\mathsf a_1=-s\LW',\quad\mathsf a_2=2s\LW'+s^2\LW''.
\end{equation}
Apply \eqref{ctp:te:eq:first-op} and divide by $1+E_1$. With $B_2=m+O(1)$
and $B_3=2m+O(1)$ the two terms in $s\LW'$ cancel, and the remaining term is
\eqref{ctp:te:eq:length-first}. Laplace convergence gives the
distributional assertion; compact complex uniformity permits differentiation
at $s=0$ by Cauchy's formula, and since the $q$th derivative of
$s^2\LW''(s)$ at zero is $q(q-1)\LW^{(q)}(0)$ we obtain
\eqref{ctp:te:eq:moments}. Evaluation at $s=-a$, $a<1$, proves
exponential-moment convergence, and a slightly larger admissible exponential
moment gives uniform integrability for every fixed power.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{An explicit all-orders construction.} By the proof and
\cref{ctp:te:thm:operator}, for every fixed $J$,
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:te:eq:length-all}
 \E\e^{-stN_n}=\frac{\Omega_J[\mathsf a(s)]}{\Omega_J[\mathbf1]}+O_{J,\mathcal K}(m^{-J-1}),
\end{equation}
where \eqref{ctp:te:eq:length-jet} and \eqref{ctp:te:eq:operator} are finite
constructive rules at each order. For the $q$th raw moment the jet is
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:te:eq:moment-jet}
 \mathsf a_\ell=\omega_q(-1)^\ell(q)_\ell,\qquad(q)_\ell=q(q+1)\cdots(q+\ell-1),
\end{equation}
since differentiating the leading amplitude in the mark gives
$\omega_q(t/\tau)^q$. The exact $B_r$ keep the local phase without further
expansion; to display an ordinary inverse-$m$ expansion write
$B_r=(r-1)!\,m+\mathfrak b_r(\theta)+O_r(m^{-1})$ with $\mathfrak b_r$ as in
\eqref{ctp:te:eq:bzero}. That series converges absolutely with all phase
derivatives and defines a smooth one-periodic function; each higher
coefficient comes from Taylor-expanding \eqref{ctp:te:eq:ratio} inside
$f_r$ and summing polynomially weighted derivatives over the same lattice.
Remainder justification: terms with $k<m/2$ differ from the small-$v$
constant by an exponentially small summable error; terms with $k>3m/2$ have
a superexponentially small sum; in between, the differentiable Stirling
expansion of $r(m+d)-r(m)$ has remainder bounded by a fixed polynomial in
$d$ times the requested inverse power of $m$, and the derivatives of
$f_r(4^d)$ minus its small-$v$ constant decay exponentially at the negative
end and superexponentially at the positive end. These bounds permit summing
each remainder and termwise phase differentiation. So every fixed
inverse-power coefficient of \eqref{ctp:te:eq:length-all} is determined by
smooth one-periodic functions and derivatives of $\LW$: an all-orders
Poincar\'e expansion in $m^{-1}$ with a phase coefficient algebra, the same
algebra as Part~I's (\cref{ctp:te:rem:bperiodic}). It does not include the
exponentially small noncentral-arc sectors of a complete transseries.

\subsection{The first-order cancellation in the mean}

\begin{corollary}[Mean through its first nonzero conditioning term]\label[corollary]{ctp:te:cor:mean}
With $\omega_1=S_1$,
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:te:eq:mean-refined}
 t\E N_n=S_1\left(1+\frac{B_3-2B_2}{2B_2^2}\right)+O(m^{-3}).
\end{equation}
In particular $t\E N_n=S_1+O(m^{-2})$: there is no $m^{-1}$ term.
Moreover
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:S1}
 S_1=1+\frac{4\pi}{9\sqrt3}=1.806133050770763489\ldots,\qquad
 S_2=1.295732295098655075\ldots.
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
For $q=1$, \eqref{ctp:te:eq:moment-jet} is $\mathsf a_\ell=S_1(-1)^\ell\ell!$.
The weight-two terms of the operator give $S_1(B_3-2B_2)/(2B_2^2)$, already
$O(m^{-2})$ by \cref{ctp:cl:lem:cumulants}. The weight-four terms have zero
leading $m^{-2}$ coefficient: by the gamma benchmark
(\cref{ctp:te:prop:gamma}), on setting $B_r=(r-1)!\,m$ the full formal
moment quotient for $q=1$ is identically one, and replacing any leading
cumulant by its $O(1)$ correction in a weight-four term adds a factor
$O(m^{-1})$. The denominator correction changes the displayed $O(m^{-2})$
term only by $O(m^{-3})$. Equation~\eqref{ctp:te:eq:length-all} with $J=2$
proves \eqref{ctp:te:eq:mean-refined}.

For the constant, the beta integral gives
$1/c_k=(k+1)(2k+1)\int_0^1[x(1-x)]^k\dd x$. Summing from $k=0$ (temporarily
including $c_0=1$) with $\sum_{k\ge0}(k+1)(2k+1)y^k=(1+3y)/(1-y)^3$,
\[
 \sum_{k\ge0}\frac1{c_k}=\int_0^1\frac{1+3x(1-x)}{(1-x(1-x))^3}\dd x
 =16\int_0^1\frac{7-3y^2}{(3+y^2)^3}\dd y=2+\frac{4\pi}{9\sqrt3}.
\]
\emph{Route of manuscript 06:} an antiderivative of the last integrand is
$\frac{64y}{3(3+y^2)^2}+\frac{8y}{3(3+y^2)}+\frac8{3\sqrt3}\arctan(y/\sqrt3)$.
\emph{Route of manuscript 10:} the middle integral is elementary after
$x=(1+u)/2$. Subtract the $k=0$ term.
\end{proof}

$S_1$ is the reciprocal-Catalan constant, OEIS A121839 \cite{reciprocal},
whose entry sums from $k\ge1$ as here; it is not a new constant.
\Cref{ctp:te:cor:mean} proves the cancellation that manuscript~06 asked
about (\cref{ctp:cl:sec:future}) and that its recorded run shows
numerically: $m^2(t\E N_n-S_1)=0.578$ at $n=10^6$.

\begin{proposition}[Gamma benchmark for the universal coefficients]\label[proposition]{ctp:te:prop:gamma}
If $B_r$ is formally replaced by $(r-1)!\,m$, the moment quotient for
\eqref{ctp:te:eq:moment-jet} has the expansion of
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:te:eq:gamma-benchmark}
 \omega_q\frac{m^q\Gamma(m)}{\Gamma(m+q)}=\omega_q\prod_{j=0}^{q-1}(1+j/m)^{-1}
\end{equation}
for every integer $q\ge1$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
These are the cumulants of the local phase $-m\log\tau$ at $t$, with $nt=m$.
The inverse-Laplace Gaussian expansion for the unmarked model $\tau^{-m}$ has
exact coefficient-density factor $n^{m-1}/\Gamma(m)$; multiplication by
$\omega_q(t/\tau)^q$ changes the ratio to $\omega_qt^qn^q\Gamma(m)/\Gamma(m+q)$,
which is \eqref{ctp:te:eq:gamma-benchmark}. This exact model determines the
Gaussian formal coefficients; equivalently, expanding the finite product on
the right and the finite sums of \eqref{ctp:te:eq:operator} verifies the
identity to every prescribed order by formal Gaussian integration.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Cumulants, non-normality and lack of concentration}
Expanding $\log\E\e^{sW}=-\sum_k\log(1-s/c_k)$ at zero,
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:Wcumulants}
 \cum_r(W)=(r-1)!\,S_r\qquad(r\ge1).
\end{equation}
For a finite recurrence for all moments, put $h_0=1$ and
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:Wmoments}
 rh_r=\sum_{j=1}^rS_jh_{r-j};\qquad\text{then}\qquad\omega_r=\E W^r=r!\,h_r.
\end{equation}
$\Var W=S_2>0$ and $\cum_3(W)=2S_3>0$, so $W$ is not Gaussian; numerically
$2S_3/S_2^{3/2}\approx1.53685181130881994$. The number of parts therefore
satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:countmoments}
 \E N_n\sim S_1\,\frac{(\log4)\,n}{\log n},\qquad
 \Var N_n\sim S_2\left(\frac{(\log4)\,n}{\log n}\right)^2,
\end{equation}
that is $\Var N_n\sim S_2/t_n^2$. The limiting relative variance
$S_2/S_1^2$ is positive: the number of parts does not concentrate around its
mean on its natural scale, a Gaussian approximation would give the wrong
limit law, and replacing the count by its mean is not an accurate
description of a typical partition.

\begin{corollary}[{[write]} The first variance correction]\label[corollary]{ctp:cl:cor:var}
$t^2\Var N_n=S_2-\omega_2/m+O(m^{-2})$, where $\omega_2=S_2+S_1^2$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
By \eqref{ctp:te:eq:moments} with $q=2$, $t^2\E N_n^2=\omega_2(1-1/m)+O(m^{-2})$,
and by \cref{ctp:te:cor:mean} $(t\E N_n)^2=S_1^2+O(m^{-2})$; subtract.
\end{proof}

This is the field \texttt{variance\_first\_heuristic} of manuscript~06's
recorded run ($0.8943857364718$ at $n=10^6$, where the exact value is
$0.938223$, \cref{ctp:cl:tab:moments}), which that manuscript marked as an
exploratory, unproved refinement; its field \texttt{m\_times\_variance\_error},
$m(t^2\Var N_n-S_2)=-4.06$ at $n=10^6$, tends to $-\omega_2=-4.5578\ldots$ by
the corollary.

\subsection{Fixed small parts}
For fixed $d$ let $X_{k,n}=tc_kZ_k$, $1\le k\le d$. Their joint Laplace
transform has limit $\Lambda_d(\mathbf s)=\prod_{k=1}^d(1+s_k)^{-1}$ and
first correction
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:te:eq:smallparts}
 \E\exp\Bigl(-\sum_{k=1}^ds_kX_{k,n}\Bigr)=\Lambda_d(\mathbf s)
 -\frac1{2m}\sum_{i,j=1}^ds_is_j\partial_i\partial_j\Lambda_d(\mathbf s)+O(m^{-2}).
\end{equation}
To prove it, mark only these $d$ factors in \eqref{ctp:te:eq:length-amplitude};
their limiting arguments are $s_k/(1+v)$. With
$\mathcal D_{\mathbf s}=\sum_ks_k\partial_k$, $\mathsf a_1=-\mathcal D_{\mathbf s}\Lambda_d$ and
$\mathsf a_2=(\mathcal D_{\mathbf s}^2+\mathcal D_{\mathbf s})\Lambda_d$, and
$-(\mathsf a_2+2\mathsf a_1)/(2m)$ is the correction in
\eqref{ctp:te:eq:smallparts}. So the $X_{k,n}$ tend to independent mean-one
exponentials, while for $i\ne j$
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:te:eq:smallcov}
 \Cov(X_{i,n},X_{j,n})=-m^{-1}+O(m^{-2}).
\end{equation}
The independence is a limit statement; conditioning leaves a universal
negative first dependence among fixed small-part energies.

\subsection{Joint limit with the upper edge}

\begin{theorem}[Joint count and edge limit]\label{ctp:cl:thm:joint}
Along a subsequence with $\theta_n\to\theta\in[0,1]$, for every fixed
integer $h$,
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:joint}
 \bigl(t_nN_n,\ (Z_{M+j})_{j>h},\ J_n-M\bigr)\Rightarrow
 \bigl(W,\ (Y_j^{(\theta)})_{j>h},\ \Jl_\theta\bigr),
\end{equation}
the upper process on its countable space of finitely supported vectors and
the maximum defined by the full two-sided family. $W$ is independent of that
whole family. Every fixed mixed moment of $t_nN_n$ and $J_n-M$ converges:
$\E[(t_nN_n)^p(J_n-M)^q]\to\omega_p\E\Jl_\theta^q$. Moreover, without a
phase subsequence,
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:mgf}
 \lim_{n\to\infty}\E_{\PP_n}\e^{st_nN_n}=\E\e^{sW}=\prod_{k\ge1}(1-s/c_k)^{-1}
 \qquad\text{for every real }s<1.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Proof (manuscript 06)]
Fix finitely many small indices $i_1,\ldots,i_a$ and edge offsets
$j_1,\ldots,j_b$. For nonnegative real thresholds $x_\ell$ prescribe
$Z_{i_\ell}\ge\lceil x_\ell/t\rceil$, and for edge coordinates arbitrary
fixed integer thresholds $d_\ell\ge0$. The shift $v$ of
\eqref{ctp:cl:eq:survival} satisfies
$tv\to\sum_\ell c_{i_\ell}x_\ell+\sum_\ell4^{j_\ell-\theta}d_\ell$, so by
\eqref{ctp:cl:eq:ratiolimit} the joint survival probability tends to the
exponential of minus this sum, which factorizes into the survival functions
of independent $\mathbf E_{i_\ell}/c_{i_\ell}$ and $Y^{(\theta)}_{j_\ell}$.
Finite inclusion--exclusion gives the joint finite-coordinate limit.

For uniform control before summing all small multiplicities,
\eqref{ctp:cl:eq:survival} and \eqref{ctp:cl:eq:ratiobound} give
$\PP_n(Z_k\ge z)\le C\e^{-tc_kz}$, hence
$\E_{\PP_n}Z_k^r\le C\Gamma(r+1)/(tc_k)^r$ for every real $r\ge1$, and,
uniformly in large $n$,
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:counttail}
 \Bigl\|t\sum_{k>K}Z_k\Bigr\|_{L^r(\PP_n)}\le(C\Gamma(r+1))^{1/r}\sum_{k>K}\frac1{c_k}
 \longrightarrow0\qquad(K\to\infty).
\end{equation}
With the finite-coordinate limit this proves $tN_n\Rightarrow W$ with all
positive moments. For the upper process, truncate at a fixed large positive
offset: a nonzero coordinate beyond it is uniformly unlikely by
\cref{ctp:cl:lem:maxtails}, and geometric tails give tightness on the
remaining finite set. For the maximum also truncate at a fixed large
negative offset; the left tail makes losing the maximum arbitrarily
unlikely. These truncations, the finite joint survival limit and
\eqref{ctp:cl:eq:counttail} prove \eqref{ctp:cl:eq:joint} and the
independence; uniform higher moments from \eqref{ctp:cl:eq:counttail} and
\cref{ctp:cl:lem:maxtails}, with H\"older's inequality, give every mixed
moment.

For the moment generating function choose $0<s<s'<1$ and $y=\e^{s't}$. The
power series $\prod_k(1-z^{c_k})/(1-yz^{c_k})$ has nonnegative coefficients,
and convolution with \eqref{ctp:cl:eq:ratiobound} gives
\[
 \E_{\PP_n}\e^{s'tN_n}\le C\prod_{k\ge1}\frac{1-\e^{-tc_k}}{1-\e^{-t(c_k-s')}}
 \le C\prod_{k\ge1}\frac{c_k}{c_k-s'}<\infty,
\]
since $(1-\e^{-x})/x$ decreases on $x>0$. This exponential uniform
integrability and the distributional convergence give \eqref{ctp:cl:eq:mgf}
for $0<s<1$; for $s\le0$ bounded convergence suffices. The product converges
because $\sum_k1/c_k<\infty$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Second proof of the $(t_nN_n,J_n-M)$ marginal (manuscript 10)]
\Cref{ctp:te:thm:maxjoint} gives the joint Laplace transforms with every
integer cutoff, and marginal tightness follows from
\cref{ctp:te:thm:length,ctp:cl:lem:maxtails}; these determine the joint law
and its product form. For moments choose $0<a<1/2$ and $b>0$; by
Cauchy--Schwarz and the exponential bounds,
$\sup_n\E\e^{atN_n+b|J_n-M|}\le(\sup_n\E\e^{2atN_n})^{1/2}(\sup_n\E\e^{2b|J_n-M|})^{1/2}<\infty$,
which is the needed mixed uniform integrability.
\end{proof}

The independence is a limit assertion. At finite $n$ many small parts
compete for mass with large parts, and the exact covariance need not vanish
(\cref{ctp:cl:tab:moments}): its first order is the next theorem.

\begin{theorem}[{[write]} The first mixed covariance]\label{ctp:cl:thm:mixedcov}
Let $\Xi(\theta)=\sum_{h\in\Z}G_h(\theta)A_h(\theta)$, a smooth one-periodic
positive function. Along the full sequence,
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:mixedcov}
 m\Cov\bigl(t_nN_n,J_n\bigr)=-S_1\,\Xi(\theta_n)+O\!\left(\frac{(\log m)^{5/2}}m\right).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Since $J_n-M=\sum_{h\ge0}\ind_{\{J_n>M+h\}}-\sum_{h\le-1}\ind_{\{J_n\le M+h\}}$
and $M$ is deterministic,
$\Cov(tN_n,J_n)=-\sum_{h\in\Z}\Cov(tN_n,\ind_{\{J_n\le M+h\}})$; the series
converges absolutely by the tails below.

\emph{Step 1: a per-threshold covariance uniform in $|h|\le m^{1/8}$.} Mark
the tail-deleted product with the number-of-parts mark. Its amplitude is
$\amp_s(\tau;t)\prod_{k>K}(1-\e^{-\tau c_k-st})$, which equals
$\LW(st/\tau)\amp_K(\tau)$ up to a relative error
$O(tm)+O(\sum_{k>K}c_k^{-1})$, exponentially small in $m$ for
$|h|\le m^{1/8}$ (since $c_{M-r}\ge4^{-r}c_M$), with the same bound for
scaled derivatives. Relative to $\amp_K(t)$ its jets are products of the bounded
jets \eqref{ctp:te:eq:length-jet} with the relative jets
\eqref{ctp:te:eq:relative-jets}, so they are $O(H_-^\ell)$, and the proof of
\cref{ctp:te:thm:growing} (\cref{ctp:te:rem:growing}) gives, uniformly for
$|h|\le m^{1/8}$, $s$ on the circle $|s|=\tfrac12$ and the phase,
\[
 \frac{\E[\e^{-stN_n}\ind_{\{J_n\le K\}}]}{\amp_K(t)}=\LW-\frac{X(s)}{2B_2}
 -\frac{B_3Y(s)}{2B_2^2}+O\!\left(\frac{(1+|h|)^4}{m^2}\right),
\]
with the product jets
$X=2s\LW'+s^2\LW''-2s\LW'\widehat T_{1,K}+\LW(\widehat T_{1,K}^2-\widehat T_{2,K})$
and $Y=-s\LW'+\LW\,\widehat T_{1,K}$. The left side and the main terms are
analytic in $|s|<1$, so Cauchy's formula transfers the bound to the
derivative at $s=0$. With $\LW'(0)=-S_1$ this gives
\[
 \frac{\E[tN_n\ind_{\{J_n\le K\}}]}{\amp_K(t)}=S_1\Bigl[1+\frac{\widehat T_{2,K}-\widehat T_{1,K}^2}{2B_2}
 -\frac{B_3\widehat T_{1,K}}{2B_2^2}\Bigr]+\frac{S_1\widehat T_{1,K}}{B_2}
 +S_1\frac{B_3-2B_2}{2B_2^2}+O\!\left(\frac{(1+|h|)^4}{m^2}\right).
\]
Subtract $\E[tN_n]\,\PP_n(J_n\le K)/\amp_K(t)$, using
\eqref{ctp:te:eq:mean-refined} and \eqref{ctp:te:eq:growing}: the bracket
and the last displayed main term cancel up to
$O(m^{-2})\cdot O((1+|h|)^2/m)$, so
\[
 \Cov\bigl(tN_n,\ind_{\{J_n\le K\}}\bigr)=\amp_K(t)\Bigl[\frac{S_1\widehat T_{1,K}}{B_2}
 +O\!\left(\frac{(1+|h|)^4}{m^2}\right)\Bigr].
\]
At fixed $h$ this is \eqref{ctp:te:eq:max-cov}.

\emph{Step 2: replacing exact tail quantities by phase functions.} For
$h=-r$ with $0\le r\le M/2$, the comparison bounds in the proof of
\cref{ctp:cl:thm:maxweighted} (\cref{ctp:cl:rem:negative}) give
$|\log(\amp_K(t)/G_h)|\le C(1+h^2)/m$ and
$|\widehat T_{1,K}-A_h|\le C/m$; for $h\ge0$ the same bounds hold with
$C/m$, by \eqref{ctp:cl:eq:eta} and the geometric growth of the rates. With
$A_h=O(1+|h|)$ and $1/B_2=1/m+O(m^{-2})$, for $h^2\le m$,
\[
 \Bigl|\Cov\bigl(tN_n,\ind_{\{J_n\le M+h\}}\bigr)-\frac{S_1G_hA_h}m\Bigr|
 \le C\,G_h\frac{(1+|h|)^4}{m^2}.
\]

\emph{Step 3: summation.} Let $H=\lceil C_0\sqrt{\log m}\,\rceil$, so that
$H\le m^{1/8}$ and $H^2\le m$ for large $m$. For $|h|>H$ use
$|\Cov(tN_n,\ind_E)|\le\|tN_n-\E tN_n\|_2\,\PP_n(E)^{1/2}$ with $E$ or its
complement: by \cref{ctp:te:thm:length} and \cref{ctp:cl:lem:maxtails} this
is at most $C\e^{-c2^h}$ for $h>H$ and $C\e^{-ch^2}$ for $h<-H$, and the sum
over $|h|>H$ is $O(m^{-3})$ once $C_0$ is large. The same holds for
$\sum_{|h|>H}G_hA_h$, since $A_h\le C\e^{-c2^h}$ for $h\ge0$ and
$G_{-r}\le4^{-r(r-1)/2}$. Over $|h|\le H$ the errors of Step~2 sum to
$Cm^{-2}(\sum_{0\le h\le H}(1+h)^4+\sum_{r\ge1}\e^{-cr^2}(1+r)^4)=O(H^5/m^2)$.
Altogether
$m\Cov(tN_n,J_n)=-S_1\sum_hG_hA_h+O((\log m)^{5/2}/m)$.

Finally $\Xi>0$ termwise; it converges with all phase derivatives by the
bounds just used, and it is one-periodic because \eqref{ctp:te:eq:phase-shift}
and $A_h(\theta+1)=A_{h-1}(\theta)$ shift the summation index.
\end{proof}

\section{The law of \texorpdfstring{$W$}{W}: spectral expansion, natural boundary and inverse tail}
\label{ctp:cl:sec:spectral}

The law of $W$ can be evaluated without simulation, and much more than a
leading tail equivalent holds. Manuscript~06 gives the exponential
expansion with a one-sided coefficient bound; manuscript~10 gives two-sided
bounds, an explicit remainder and the analytic consequences. Define
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:te:eq:residue}
 \sd_j=\prod_{\substack{k\ge1\\k\ne j}}\frac{c_k}{c_k-c_j}
 =\prod_{k\ne j}\Bigl(1-\frac{c_j}{c_k}\Bigr)^{-1},\qquad
 \varpi=\prod_{\ell\ge1}(1-2^{-\ell})>0.
\end{equation}
For each $j$ the product converges, and $\operatorname{sign}(\sd_j)=(-1)^{j-1}$.

\begin{lemma}[Two-sided bounds on the spectral coefficients]\label[lemma]{ctp:te:lem:d}
For every $j\ge1$,
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:te:eq:d-bound}
 4^{-j(j-1)/2}\le|\sd_j|\le\varpi^{-2}2^{-j(j-1)/2}.
\end{equation}
In particular $\sum_j|\sd_j|c_j^r<\infty$ for every fixed $r\ge0$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For $k<j$ write the absolute factor as $(c_k/c_j)/(1-c_k/c_j)$. The bounds
$4^{-(j-k)}\le c_k/c_j\le2^{-(j-k)}$ make its product at least
$4^{-j(j-1)/2}$ and at most $\varpi^{-1}2^{-j(j-1)/2}$. For $k>j$ each
factor exceeds one, and their product is at most
$\prod_{\ell\ge1}(1-2^{-\ell})^{-1}=\varpi^{-1}$. The last assertion follows
from $c_j\le4^j$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Exact survival expansion]\label{ctp:te:thm:spectral}
For every real $x\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:Wdensity}
 \PP(W>x)=\sum_{j\ge1}\sd_j\e^{-c_jx},\qquad
 f_W(x)=\sum_{j\ge1}c_j\sd_j\e^{-c_jx}.
\end{equation}
The series and each fixed derivative converge absolutely and uniformly on
$x\ge0$. For $J\ge1$,
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:te:eq:spectral-rem}
 \Bigl|\PP(W>x)-\sum_{j=1}^J\sd_j\e^{-c_jx}\Bigr|
 \le\frac{\varpi^{-2}2^{-J(J+1)/2}}{1-2^{-(J+1)}}\e^{-c_{J+1}x}.
\end{equation}
The law has a strictly positive smooth density on $x>0$, and
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:te:eq:flat}
 \sum_{j\ge1}\sd_j=1,\qquad\sum_{j\ge1}\sd_jc_j^r=0\quad(r=1,2,\ldots).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For $W_N=\sum_{k\le N}\mathbf E_k/c_k$, partial fractions of the Laplace
transform $\prod_{k\le N}c_k/(s+c_k)$ give
$\PP(W_N>x)=\sum_{j\le N}\sd_j^{(N)}\e^{-c_jx}$ with
$\sd_j^{(N)}=\prod_{k\le N,k\ne j}c_k/(c_k-c_j)$. The proof of
\cref{ctp:te:lem:d} bounds these uniformly in $N$ (manuscript~06's route:
$|\sd^{(N)}_j|\le C2^{-j(j-1)/2}$ from $c_{\ell+1}/c_\ell\ge2$). For fixed $j$,
$\sd_j^{(N)}\to\sd_j$, and $W_N\to W$ almost surely; $W=\mathbf E_1+\sum_{k\ge2}\mathbf E_k/c_k$
has a continuous law, so dominated convergence proves \eqref{ctp:cl:eq:Wdensity},
and the last assertion of \cref{ctp:te:lem:d} gives the derivative bounds
(since $c_k\le4^{k-1}$). For \eqref{ctp:te:eq:spectral-rem} factor out
$\e^{-c_{J+1}x}$ and bound the coefficient tail by a geometric series:
consecutive majorants have ratios at most $2^{-(J+1)}$.

Flatness at zero: for every positive integer $R$, $W\ge W_R$, and
integration over the simplex gives
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:flatness}
 \PP(W\le x)\le\PP(W_R\le x)\le\frac{x^R}{R!}\prod_{k=1}^Rc_k\qquad(x\ge0).
\end{equation}
With the smoothness already proved and $R$ arbitrary, all right derivatives
of the distribution function vanish at zero; evaluating the uniform
derivative series at zero gives \eqref{ctp:te:eq:flat}.

Positivity: with $V=\sum_{k\ge2}\mathbf E_k/c_k$, convolution with
$\mathbf E_1$ gives $f_W(x)=\e^{-x}\E[\e^V\ind_{\{V<x\}}]$ for $x>0$. For
each $x>0$, $\PP(V<x)>0$: choose a far tail with expectation below $x/4$,
use Markov's inequality for it, and require each of the finitely many
preceding variables to be small.
\end{proof}

In particular $\PP(W>x)=\sd_1\e^{-x}+O(\e^{-2x})$ with
$\sd_1=\prod_{k\ge2}(1-1/c_k)^{-1}$, and the first coefficients are
\begin{align*}
 \sd_1&=2.78827476752297890, &\sd_2&=-2.08690959322975775,\\
 \sd_3&=0.31102280892305314, &\sd_4&=-0.01252727293482829,\\
 \sd_5&=0.00013971276281143.
\end{align*}
The alternating signs are essential: the expansion is not a mixture of
positive exponential laws. Near zero it cancels severely, exactly as
\eqref{ctp:te:eq:flat} quantifies, and the distribution function is flat
there: every derivative vanishes at $x=0$ \eqref{ctp:cl:eq:flatness}. At
large $x$ the expansion is very effective, the rates growing geometrically.
These are properties of the familiar infinite exponential convolution;
their role here is to make the Catalan limit law explicit.

\subsection{Smoothness without analytic continuation}
The change of variable $z=\e^{-x}$ turns \eqref{ctp:cl:eq:Wdensity} into
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:te:eq:T}
 \mathcal T(z)=\sum_{j\ge1}\sd_jz^{c_j},\qquad\PP(W>x)=\mathcal T(\e^{-x}).
\end{equation}
$\mathcal T$ is not the partition generating function $F(z)$ and not the
Laplace transform $\LW(s)$: three different analytic objects.

\begin{theorem}[Natural boundary and non-D-finiteness]\label{ctp:te:thm:boundary}
$\mathcal T$ has radius of convergence exactly one and is smooth on the
closed unit disk: all its fixed holomorphic derivatives extend continuously
to it. Nevertheless every point of $|z|=1$ is singular for analytic
continuation, so $\mathcal T$ is not D-finite, and the analytic function
$\PP(W>x)=\mathcal T(\e^{-x})$ on $\Rea x>0$ has the imaginary axis as a
natural boundary. The Laplace transform $\LW(s)$ is meromorphic with simple
poles at $-c_j$, $j\ge1$, and is also not D-finite.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The upper bound of \eqref{ctp:te:eq:d-bound} gives uniform convergence on
$|z|\le1$ of every differentiated series. The two-sided bound gives
$\lim_j|\sd_j|^{1/c_j}=1$, because $j^2/c_j\to0$; so the radius is exactly
one. We invoke one classical result: a power series of finite positive
radius supported on exponents $\lambda_j$ with $\lambda_{j+1}/\lambda_j\ge1+\delta$
for some $\delta>0$ has its circle of convergence as a natural boundary,
Hadamard's gap theorem \cite[\S13, pp.~116--117]{hadamard}. Its hypotheses
hold with $\lambda_j=c_j$ and $\delta=1$, by \eqref{ctp:eq:ratio}. A germ
satisfying a linear differential equation with polynomial coefficients
continues analytically along paths avoiding the finitely many zeros of the
leading coefficient, so it cannot have a whole circle as natural boundary;
$\mathcal T$ is not D-finite. Since $x\mapsto\e^{-x}$ is locally biholomorphic,
a continuation across an imaginary-axis point would continue $\mathcal T$
across the corresponding unit-circle point. Finally $\prod_j(1+s/c_j)$
converges locally uniformly on the whole $s$-plane ($\sum1/c_j<\infty$), with
exactly the distinct simple zeros $-c_j$; its reciprocal is $\LW$, and
infinitely many finite poles again contradict a polynomial-coefficient
linear differential equation.
\end{proof}

Even the singularity at $z=1$ has a direct reason: $\mathcal T(1)=1$ and all
its positive-order derivatives there vanish by \eqref{ctp:te:eq:flat}, so a
continuation near~$1$ would be locally the constant one, contradicting
$\mathcal T(0)=0$. The gap theorem strengthens this to \emph{every} boundary
point. This is a sequence-specific consequence of a classical theorem, not a
new gap theorem, and no conclusion about differential \emph{algebraicity}
is drawn from non-D-finiteness.

\subsection{A convergent inverse upper-tail expansion}
For $0<\epsilon<1$ let $\xi_\epsilon$ be the unique solution of
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:te:eq:quantile}
 \PP(W>\xi_\epsilon)=\epsilon;
\end{equation}
existence and uniqueness follow from \cref{ctp:te:thm:spectral}, and
$\xi_\epsilon\to\infty$ as $\epsilon\downarrow0$. Put
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:te:eq:V}
 \mathcal T_1(z)=\frac{\mathcal T(z)}{\sd_1z}=1+\frac{\sd_2}{\sd_1}z
 +\frac{\sd_3}{\sd_1}z^4+\frac{\sd_4}{\sd_1}z^{13}+\cdots;
\end{equation}
the sparsity of the exponents is inherited from the Catalan rates.

\begin{theorem}[Inverse tail, all orders and first four coefficients]\label{ctp:te:thm:inverse}
For small positive $\epsilon$ the following expansion converges:
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:te:eq:inverse-all}
 \xi_\epsilon=\log\frac{\sd_1}\epsilon-\sum_{r\ge1}\frac1r\Bigl(\frac\epsilon{\sd_1}\Bigr)^r
 [z^r]\,\mathcal T_1(z)^{-r}.
\end{equation}
In particular
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:te:eq:inverse-four}
 \xi_\epsilon=\log\frac{\sd_1}\epsilon+\frac{\sd_2}{\sd_1^2}\epsilon
 -\frac{3\sd_2^2}{2\sd_1^4}\epsilon^2+\frac{10\sd_2^3}{3\sd_1^6}\epsilon^3
 +\Bigl(\frac{\sd_3}{\sd_1^5}-\frac{35\sd_2^4}{4\sd_1^8}\Bigr)\epsilon^4+O(\epsilon^5).
\end{equation}
At any fixed order the coefficients use only finitely many $\sd_j$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Put $y=\epsilon/\sd_1$ and $z=\e^{-\xi_\epsilon}$; the equation is
$y=z\mathcal T_1(z)$ with $\mathcal T_1(0)=1$. The analytic inverse theorem
gives a convergent inverse germ $z=z(y)$, so
$\xi_\epsilon=\log(1/y)+\log\mathcal T_1(z(y))$ with the logarithm vanishing at
$y=0$. The residue substitution $y=z\mathcal T_1(z)$, or one integration by
parts in it, gives for $r\ge1$
\[
 [y^r]\log\mathcal T_1(z(y))=\frac1r[z^{r-1}]\frac{\mathcal T_1'(z)}{\mathcal T_1(z)^{r+1}}
 =-\frac1r[z^r]\mathcal T_1(z)^{-r},
\]
which proves \eqref{ctp:te:eq:inverse-all} with convergence, not merely
formal equality. Through order four replace $\mathcal T_1$ by
$1+az+bz^4+O(z^{13})$ with $a=\sd_2/\sd_1$, $b=\sd_3/\sd_1$; the
coefficients of $y,y^2,y^3,y^4$ are $a$, $-3a^2/2$, $10a^3/3$ and $b-35a^4/4$.
\end{proof}

The first new rate after $1$ and $2$ is $5$, so it first changes the inverse
expansion at order $\epsilon^4$; before that every correction comes from
reverting the first two exponentials, an example of sparse forward scales
creating denser inverse scales. The natural boundary is no obstacle to the
inverse germ at zero, since $\mathcal T'(0)=\sd_1\ne0$. The maximal
convergence radius of \eqref{ctp:te:eq:inverse-all} is not identified;
complex critical values of $\mathcal T$ may limit it before any boundary
value does (\cref{ctp:cl:sec:future}).

\paragraph{What this says about finite partitions.} For fixed
$0<\epsilon<1$, convergence in distribution and strict positivity of $f_W$
give convergence of the $(1-\epsilon)$-quantile of $tN_n$ (left-continuous
inverse) to $\xi_\epsilon$; the lattice spacing $t$ tends to zero. This is a
fixed-$\epsilon$ statement. Applying \eqref{ctp:te:eq:inverse-four}
afterwards is a sequential regime, first $n\to\infty$ and then
$\epsilon\downarrow0$; it proves no uniform formula for
$\epsilon=\epsilon(n)\to0$ and reconstructs no exact integer quantile by
rounding a truncation. That needs further tail and local-limit estimates
(\cref{ctp:cl:sec:future}).

\section{A logarithmic mass profile and a Gaussian bridge}
\label{ctp:cl:sec:bridge}

This section is manuscript~06's. The total mass is spread over many active
sizes, although the number of parts is dominated by the smallest sizes.
Define the cumulative mass profile
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:profile}
 \mathcal F_n(x)=\frac1n\sum_{k\le\lfloor xM\rfloor}c_kZ_k,\qquad0\le x\le1.
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Mass profile and finite-dimensional fluctuations]\label{ctp:cl:thm:bridge}
Under $\PP_n$,
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:shape}
 \sup_{0\le x\le1}|\mathcal F_n(x)-x|\to0\quad\text{in probability}.
\end{equation}
For every $0<x_1<\cdots<x_d<1$,
\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:bridgevector}
 \bigl(\sqrt m(\mathcal F_n(x_i)-x_i)\bigr)_{i=1}^d\Rightarrow(\mathbb B(x_i))_{i=1}^d,
\end{equation}
the right side centred Gaussian with $\Cov(\mathbb B(x),\mathbb B(y))=\min(x,y)-xy$.
Every fixed joint polynomial moment converges as well. This is a
finite-dimensional Brownian-bridge theorem; a functional central limit
theorem is not needed for \eqref{ctp:cl:eq:shape}.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Put $K_i=\lfloor x_iM\rfloor$ and, under $\Q_t$,
$R_i=(t/\sqrt m)\sum_{k>K_i}(c_kZ_k-\E_{\Q_t}c_kZ_k)$. The disjoint index
bands between the cutoffs, and the final tail, are independent; their scaled
second cumulants tend to their index-length fractions and their higher
normalized cumulants vanish. So $(R_1,\ldots,R_d)$ converges under $\Q_t$ to
a centred Gaussian vector $\mathbf G$ with covariance
$C_{ij}=1-\max(x_i,x_j)$.

The whole vector is measurable with respect to the tail after the earliest
cutoff $K_1$. By the uniform prefix local theorem its conditioning
likelihood is $x_1^{-1/2}\exp(-R_1^2/(2x_1))+o(1)$ uniformly over that tail,
and the likelihoods are uniformly bounded. On the conditioning event
$S=n=\E_{\Q_t}S$, $(t/\sqrt m)\sum_{k\le K_i}(c_kZ_k-\E_{\Q_t}c_kZ_k)=-R_i$
exactly. The limit law is therefore that of $-\mathbf G$ weighted by
$x_1^{-1/2}\exp(-\mathbf G_1^2/(2x_1))$, which integrates to one since
$C_{11}=1-x_1$. Completing the square changes the covariance to
\[
 C_{ij}-\frac{C_{i1}C_{1j}}{x_1+C_{11}}=1-\max(x_i,x_j)-(1-x_i)(1-x_j)=\min(x_i,x_j)-x_ix_j.
\]
No local theorem for an arithmetically restricted suffix is used. By
\cref{ctp:cl:lem:cumulants}, $t\E S_{K_i}=K_i+O(1)$ and $nt=m+O(1)$, so
replacing canonical centring by $x_i$, and the normalization by that of
\eqref{ctp:cl:eq:bridgevector}, changes the vector by $o(1)$; uniform higher
canonical moments and the bounded likelihood give convergence of joint
moments.

Pointwise convergence of the profile follows. At zero it vanishes
identically. At one, the scaled upper mass $t(S-S_M)$ is tight by the
fixed-upper-process theorem and its moment bounds, so
$1-\mathcal F_n(1)=O_{\PP_n}(m^{-1})$. A finite grid and monotonicity of
$\mathcal F_n$ prove \eqref{ctp:cl:eq:shape}.
\end{proof}

For example, the first half of the active indices carries asymptotically
half the total mass, with variance $1/4$ after the $\sqrt m$ normalization.
This differs from the total part count, which has a nondegenerate limit
after scaling by $t$: the distinction is whether multiplicities are weighted
by $c_k$.

\section{What is universal and what is Catalan-specific}
\label{ctp:cl:sec:universal}

The mechanism extends beyond one OEIS sequence, but its arithmetic
hypotheses must be stated. The following proposition (manuscript~06)
complements Part~I's \cref{ctp:prop:general}, which treats the counting
asymptotics under the hypotheses of Section~\ref{ctp:sec:saddle}; it does
not re-prove it.

\begin{proposition}[A sufficient lacunary class]\label[proposition]{ctp:cl:prop:universal}
Let $a_1<a_2<\cdots$ be positive integers such that
\begin{enumerate}
\item $a_1=1$, and some finite subset of $\{a_2,a_3,\ldots\}$ has greatest
common divisor one;
\item for all large $k$, $1<q_-\le a_{k+1}/a_k\le q_+<\infty$.
\end{enumerate}
For partitions with these allowed sizes let $t$ solve the weighted-mean
equation and $M=\#\{k:ta_k\le1\}$. Then
\cref{ctp:cl:thm:threshold,ctp:cl:thm:smalltail} hold with the same function
$d(a)$ and constant $\phi(1)$, with $M$ in place of $m$. The total-count
limit is $tN_n\Rightarrow\sum_{k\ge1}\mathbf E_k/a_k$ with all moments, and
the normalized index mass profile and its finite-dimensional bridge have the
same form.

Suppose in addition an eventual smooth interpolation
$\log a_k=r(k)$, $r(x)=\alpha x-\beta\log x+c+O(x^{-1})$ with $\alpha>0$, the
remainder differentiable three times with bounds $O(x^{-1-\ell})$. Set
$m=r^{-1}(\log(1/t))$ and $M=\lfloor m\rfloor$. Then the fixed-edge laws and
their first correction have exactly the forms above, with
$u_j=\e^{\alpha(j-\theta)}$ and $\frac32$ replaced by $\beta$, and the joint
small-part/edge independence holds.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The ratio bounds give scaled mean and variance $M+O(1)$ and other scaled
cumulants $(r-1)!\,M+O_r(1)$. The central and intermediate Fourier estimates
use a positive fraction of the active sizes and finitely many fixed factors.
On the compact outer arcs the unit factor gives one $O(t)$ factor; by the
finite gcd hypothesis the sines of the other selected sizes have no common
zero there, so one gives a second. The proofs of the local theorem, the
cutoff transition and the bridge therefore apply without change. The ratio
bounds give $\sum_k1/a_k<\infty$, so the shift identity and moment
domination prove the count limit. Under the interpolation hypothesis
$ta_{M+j}=\e^{\alpha(j-\theta)}(1-\beta(j-\theta)/m+O_j(m^{-2}))$, and the
$L^1$ product interpolation gives exactly the coefficient $\beta$ in the edge
score; the same survival identities prove the joint independence. The scalar
cumulant constants depending on the initial sizes cancel at first order.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[{[write]} Re-reading ``apply without change'']\label[remark]{ctp:cl:rem:universal}
The dossier flagged this proof as compressed. The write checked each
transfer. (1)~Outer arcs: a common zero of $\sin(a_ky/2)$ over the finite
gcd-one set $F$ needs $ya_k\in2\pi\Z$ for all $k\in F$, hence $y\in2\pi\Z$, so
on $\delta\le|y|\le\pi$ one factor of $F$ is $O_\delta(t)$, and with the unit
factor the arc integral is $O(t^2)=o(t/\sqrt M)$ exponentially, as
$t\le Cq_-^{-M}$. (2)~The likelihood expansion \eqref{ctp:cl:eq:likelihoodexp}
uses only $B_2=m+O(1)$, $B_3=2m+O(1)$ and $E_1=-1/(12m)+O(m^{-2})$, which
hold for every such sequence because $B_r=(r-1)!\,M+O_r(1)$ and $m-M\in[0,1)$;
for large $n$ every initial $a_k$ is at most $1/t$ and $r$ is eventually
increasing, so $M=\lfloor m\rfloor$ is the number of active sizes. (3)~The
transport bound \eqref{ctp:cl:eq:eta} needs, for $d=j-\theta\ge-m/2$,
$\eta_j=-\beta\log(1+d/m)+\varepsilon(m+d)-\varepsilon(m)$ with
$\varepsilon(x)=O(1/x)$, $\varepsilon'(x)=O(x^{-2})$; both bounds of
\eqref{ctp:cl:eq:eta} follow, with $\frac32$ replaced by $\beta$, using one
derivative of the remainder. (4)~The tails of \cref{ctp:cl:lem:maxtails}
hold with $2^h$ and $2^{-r(r-1)/2}$ replaced by $q_-^h$ and
$q_-^{-r(r-1)/2}$. With these readings the proof is complete. Its scope is
the first part's statistics and the first-order edge laws; the
higher-order statements of \cref{ctp:cl:sec:moments,ctp:cl:sec:spectral}
are not asserted for the class.
\end{remark}

The finite gcd condition is sufficient, not necessary. Pure powers of a
fixed integer fail this condition; their resonant arcs need a separate
argument, and the proposition does not justify applying the present
outer-arc proof to them. For Catalan numbers the witnesses $1,2,5$ make the
arithmetic check immediate (Part~I, Section~\ref{ctp:sec:saddle}). The
division between universal and specific data is now clear: the Gaussian
transition $d$ depends on the fraction of active variances retained; the
growth rate~$4$ sets the edge lattice, and the Catalan exponent $\frac32$
its first drift term; the entire small-size sequence determines the
exponential-sum law~$W$.

Both programs use arbitrary-precision integers for the finite Euler products
needed up to $n$ (manuscript~10 for every $0\le n\le10^6$). When a part of
size $c$ is added, the ascending update of the arrays $(p_s,A_s,B_s)$, which
sum $1,N,N^2$ over the partitions of $s$, is
\[

the entries at $s-c$ already include the new part type, which allows
repeated copies. Then $A_n/p_n=\E N_n$ and $B_n/p_n=\E N_n^2$. Prefix
snapshots give the exact law of $J_n$ and the numerators for $N_nJ_n$ and the

\begin{equation}\label{ctp:cl:eq:numerictv}

exceeds~$n$; omitting it would understate the distance. The integer arrays

\section{Further questions and research}
\label{ctp:cl:sec:future}

The following are directions, not theorems claimed by Part~II; neither
manuscript asserts that all are previously unposed. Each item names its
source. Questions one manuscript asked and the other (or the write) answered
are recorded as answered and not repeated as open.

\paragraph{Answered within Part II.}
(a)~Manuscript~06 asked for ``the possible cancellation of the first $1/m$
correction in $\E(tN_n)$'': proved by manuscript~10
(\cref{ctp:te:cor:mean}); as a consequence of \eqref{ctp:te:eq:mean-refined}
and \cref{ctp:te:rem:bperiodic}, [write]
$m^2(t\E N_n-S_1)=\frac{S_1}2\bigl(\mathfrak b_3(\theta)-2\mathfrak b_2(\theta)\bigr)+O(m^{-1})$,
which is $0.6058$ at the phase of $n=10^6$ against the recorded
$0.578$. (b)~Its ``$m\Cov(tN_n,J_n-M)$'': the limit is
$-S_1\Xi(\theta)$ ([write], \cref{ctp:cl:thm:mixedcov}). (c)~All five
fields of its \texttt{exploratory\_unproved\_refinements} are now accounted
for: the mean error as in~(a), the two variance fields by
\cref{ctp:cl:cor:var}, the two covariance fields by
\cref{ctp:cl:thm:mixedcov}. (d)~Manuscript~10's ``all-orders largest-index
moments'' at first order: \eqref{ctp:cl:eq:maxmean}--\eqref{ctp:cl:eq:maxvar}
(manuscript~06). (e)~Its ``expanding cutoff field'' for the upper window in
total variation: \cref{ctp:cl:thm:threshold,ctp:cl:thm:smalltail}
(manuscript~06). (f)~Its universality question in part:
\cref{ctp:cl:prop:universal}.

\subsection{Uniform higher orders across the cutoff transition (06)}
The regime $M-K\to\infty$, $M-K=o(M)$ has the universal leading constant
$\phi(1)$, while a fixed upper cutoff has the phase-sensitive coefficient of
\eqref{ctp:cl:eq:edge-tv-sharp}. Construct a uniform expansion connecting
them and determine the next coefficient throughout
$1\le M-K\le(1-\varepsilon)M$. The absolute value in the total-variation norm
introduces moving zeros of the density correction, so extending the local
Edgeworth expansion is not the whole problem.

\subsection{Higher corrected joint laws and the expanding cutoff field (06, 10)}
The proof of \cref{ctp:cl:thm:edge} is constructive: further displacement
integrals and gamma-ratio coefficients should give
$\dd\nu_{n,h}/\dd\Pi_{\theta,h}=1+\mathcal P_1/m+\mathcal P_2/m^2+\cdots$ with
weighted $L^1$ remainders at every fixed order, ideally by a finite symbolic
recurrence in centred tail energies and phase derivatives with automatic
checks $\E\mathcal P_r=0$ (manuscript~06). Manuscript~10 asks how fast the
finite index set of \cref{ctp:te:thm:cutoff} may grow while the joint law
stays close in total variation to a product of geometric laws, and for an
explicit correction operator for the whole transition field. For the upper
window this is answered by \cref{ctp:cl:thm:threshold,ctp:cl:thm:smalltail};
quantitative independence of the transition field from the low-index
length variables, in total variation rather than for transforms, remains
open.

\subsection{Functional bridge and a simultaneous three-scale theorem (06)}
\Cref{ctp:cl:thm:bridge} is finite-dimensional with a uniform law of large
numbers. A functional limit needs tightness near both index endpoints; one
route reserves finitely many small arithmetic witness factors for the
conditioning local limit and controls the remaining increments by moments.
A simultaneous theorem for the count limit, the whole bridge and the
two-sided edge process on one product space would make the three-scale
description precise; the topology and normalization at the lower endpoint
must be specified first.

\subsection{Local limits for the number of parts, and finite-size quantiles (06, 10)}
Weak convergence of $tN_n$ with moments does not imply a lattice local
limit. A natural target (06) is $\PP_n(N_n=\ell)=t\,f_W(t\ell)+o(t)$ in a
stated range, with a version conditioned on a largest-index threshold; this
is a two-variable coefficient problem whose Fourier analysis must handle the
joint lattice generated by $(c_k,1)$. Manuscript~10 notes that the first
\emph{formal} density correction suggested by \eqref{ctp:te:eq:length-first}
is $-\tfrac12(x^2f_W(x))''/m$; compact Laplace control does not prove this
density statement. A sufficiently uniform local theorem would transport the
correction to finite-$n$ quantiles and give admissible simultaneous ranges
$\epsilon=\epsilon(n)\downarrow0$, turning the sequential inversion of
\cref{ctp:te:thm:inverse} into a finite-size inverse theorem.

\subsection{The next mixed coefficient (06; [write])}
\Cref{ctp:cl:thm:mixedcov} has remainder $O((\log m)^{5/2}/m)$. Determine the
next coefficient of $m\Cov(tN_n,J_n)$ (at $n=10^6$ the exact value $-1.4368$
differs from the limit $-1.3206$ by about $1.3/m$), and whether the logarithm
can be removed: the write's summation uses only polynomial-in-$h$ errors up
to $|h|\asymp\sqrt{\log m}$. A joint version of
\cref{ctp:cl:thm:maxweighted} with the number-of-parts mark, as manuscript~06
proposed (``a marked coefficient expansion with a uniform remainder in the
marking parameter, followed by differentiated and weighted versions of the
maximum-law expansion''), would settle both. \emph{Dated note
(5~October 2026):} the independent check recorded in
\cref{ctp:cl:rem:mixedcov} observed that the same proof already reduces
the logarithmic factor to $\log\log m$, and that the numerics at
$m\le13.4$ show no logarithmic growth; removing it altogether remains
open.

\subsection{Distinct sizes and near-edge holes (06)}
Let $D_n=\#\{k:Z_k>0\}$. Manuscript~06 proposes, without proof, the edge
description of $D_n-M$ as occupied sites above the cutoff minus missing
sites below it,
$-\sum_{j\le0}\ind_{\{Y_j^{(\theta)}=0\}}+\sum_{j\ge1}\ind_{\{Y_j^{(\theta)}>0\}}$;
both limiting sums are almost surely finite. A theorem with moments needs a
uniform small-rate bound on missing-site probabilities under conditioning, a
different estimate from the large-multiplicity survival bound used for $N_n$.

\subsection{Universality with minimal hypotheses (06, 10)}
Remove or weaken the finite gcd hypothesis of \cref{ctp:cl:prop:universal}:
pure integer powers have strong root-of-unity resonances but much of the
same geometry, and a general theorem should distinguish a genuinely
competing lattice contribution from a resonance negligible after integration
(06). For $a_k\sim A\rho^kk^{-\beta}$ the local calculation predicts a
length limit $\sum\mathbf E_k/a_k$, a cutoff scale $\rho^{j-\theta}$ and a
curvature coefficient involving $\beta$ (10); \cref{ctp:cl:prop:universal}
proves this at first order for its class, and a general theorem with minimal
growth and lattice-smoothing hypotheses, separating the full-sequence
dependence of the length law from the local-ratio dependence of the cutoff
law, remains open. Fuss--Catalan and other gamma-quotient sequences are test
cases whose noncentral-arc hypotheses must be checked, not inferred from
their exponential growth; compare Part~I, question~\ref{ctp:sec:future}.6.

\subsection{The optimal transition for a receding maximum cutoff (10)}
\Cref{ctp:te:thm:growing} uses $|h|\le m^{1/8}$; its proof points to the
ratio $|h|/\sqrt m$, the deleted tail having scaled mean of order $|h|$.
Determine the optimal perturbative range, then the nonperturbative regime
$h\asymp\sqrt m$, where the prefix saddle should move on the width scale of
the full saddle. For $h$ proportional to $m$ a different large-deviation rate
function is ``likely'' the right object (manuscript~10's expectation,
unproved); the present Gaussian tail bound is not sharp enough to identify it.

\subsection{All-orders largest-index moments (10)}
First order is answered (item (d) above). Derive and normalize explicit
higher inverse-$m$ corrections for the mean, variance and higher cumulants
of $J_n$ by summing cutoff corrections with a proved summable remainder; the
growing-window estimate is a starting point, but the exact tail amplitudes
must be expanded uniformly in that window. A useful computational goal is a
phase-function library with certified extrema and Fourier tails.

\subsection{Small deviations of the limit law (10)}
The survival expansion is excellent for large $x$, but cancellation makes it
inefficient for $\PP(W\le x)$ as $x\downarrow0$. Determine the complete
logarithmic small-deviation expansion, with its phase modulation and its
inverse. The product $\LW(s)$ for large positive $s$ suggests a logarithmic
saddle related to the partition-counting saddle of Part~I, but the sign and
boundary factors differ (manuscript~10's suggestion, unproved).

\subsection{The convergence radius of the inverse tail (10)}
\Cref{ctp:te:thm:inverse} proves a positive radius without identifying it.
Locate the nearest complex critical values of $\mathcal T$ and decide whether
they, rather than the natural boundary, control the inverse germ; this would
give sharp coefficient growth and a principled truncation rule. Neither
non-D-finiteness nor the natural boundary answers it.

\subsection{Exponential sectors and arithmetic secondary arcs (10)}
The operator expansion keeps every algebraic order in $m^{-1}$ but discards
the marking correction $O(tm)$ and the noncentral arcs. Determine their
leading contributions and whether the roots of unity organize them into an
exponentially improved expansion; such sectors can matter numerically before
the inverse-$m$ series is accurate (\cref{ctp:te:tab:laplace}). This is the
statistics counterpart of Part~I, question~\ref{ctp:sec:future}.3; any claim
of a complete transseries must account for them.

\subsection{Certified computation, sampling and formalization (06, 10)}
Make the constants in the contour, moving-window and $L^1$ remainders and in
the tail bounds explicit and computable, and implement the positive geometric
tails with interval arithmetic. This would give certified total-variation
tolerances for sampling only the large parts and certified evaluations of the
periodic mean and variance functions (06), and finite-$n$ probability and
integer-quantile decisions (10). An exact sampler for the whole partition
must still handle the total-size constraint efficiently; an asymptotic law
does not settle that algorithmic problem. A feasible formalization order is
the finite Gaussian multi-index operator and the exact counting identities
first, then the analytic localization and conditioning steps (10); compare
Part~I, questions~\ref{ctp:sec:future}.1 and~\ref{ctp:sec:future}.8.

\section{Conclusion of Part II}
\label{ctp:cl:sec:conclusion}

Catalan-part partitions have two sharply different endpoint scales. The
number of parts is governed by an infinite convolution of exponentials, with
a sparse exact exponential representation, a natural boundary and a
convergent inverse-tail expansion; the largest index has a phase-dependent
integer law. The common size constraint disappears at leading order between
the endpoints, yet leaves explicit order-$1/\log n$ dependence, both inside
the upper edge (\cref{ctp:cl:cor:cov}) and between the endpoints
(\cref{ctp:te:thm:maxjoint,ctp:cl:thm:mixedcov}). For the whole upper
process the conditioning can be neglected exactly when the observed window
contains $o(\log n)$ active sizes, with the explicit curve $d(a)$ otherwise;
uniform tails and the all-threshold expansion close the gap between a
fixed-cutoff law and moment asymptotics; and in the bulk the mass fluctuates
as a Brownian bridge in the normalized index. The Gaussian coefficient
operator exposes the constraint without Gaussianizing the statistics
themselves.
