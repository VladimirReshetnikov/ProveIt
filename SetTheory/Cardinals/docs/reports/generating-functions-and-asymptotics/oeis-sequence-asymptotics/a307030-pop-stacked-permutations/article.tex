\begin{abstract}
Let $p_n$ be the number of permutations of $[n]$ in the image of one pass of pop-stack sorting (OEIS A307030), with $p_0=1$, and let $p_{n,k}$ count those with $k$ maximal ascending runs (OEIS A309993). This report prints three manuscripts on these numbers. Part~I gives an explicit exponential generating function $P=N/D$, a quotient of entire functions with no square-root branch; it proves that every pole is simple, with an explicit residue, that there are infinitely many positive poles, one for each solution of an explicit phase equation, and hence that $P$ is not D-finite and $(p_n)$ is not P-recursive; it shows that $P$ is nevertheless differentially algebraic, gives a Riccati equation that computes $p_0,\dots,p_N$ in $O(N^2)$ arithmetic operations, isolates the dominant pole $\rho$ by a positive-operator argument, gives the amplitude $C$ in closed form and certifies both to about fifty digits. These are the numerical predictions of Claesson, Gu\dh mundsson and Pantone (2019), now proved. Part~II proves the leading asymptotic $p_n=C\,n!\rho^{-n}(1+O(q^n))$ by a self-contained spectral and analytic Fredholm argument, without the explicit formula, with an elementary bracket for $\rho$ and an all-orders expansion of the continuous inverse. Part~III gives the bivariate generating function by runs, the mean and variance of the run count with exponentially small errors, a central limit theorem and a uniform lattice local limit theorem, and an alternate proof of the fixed-run denominator conjecture (Conjecture~2) of Claesson, Gu\dh mundsson and Pantone; a proof posted by Shivam Patel on 20 August 2026 came first, and Part~III credits it. The write records, with an interval-arithmetic proof, that the list of positive poles printed by Claesson, Gu\dh mundsson and Pantone omits the thirteenth pole $\rho_{12}=5.33887671271979\ldots$ (their repository lists it), confirms that each of their fifteen printed positive values is a correct truncation, places the inverses relative to the repository's transseries volume, and checks Patel's argument. Effective error constants and onsets, the ordering of the nonreal poles, a minimal differential equation, bit complexity and several finer probabilistic statements remain open. The report is unrefereed, and nothing in it is formalized.
\end{abstract}

\emph{One model.} All three Parts start from the same integral model: a run is a \emph{state}, either a singleton $x\in(0,1)$ or an interval $(a,b)$ with $0<a<b<1$; consecutive runs are joined by the overlap kernel $\one\{\lo(s)<\hi(t),\ \lo(t)<\hi(s)\}$; a singleton has weight $z$ and an interval $z^2e^{z(b-a)}$; and the exponential generating function is $1$ plus a bilinear pairing with the resolvent of the resulting compact operator (Part~I, \eqref{psp:egf:eq:resolvent}; Part~II, \eqref{psp:op:eq:resolvent}; Part~III, \eqref{psp:run:eq:resolvent}, where every run also carries a factor $u$). Each Part proves this normalization itself. Parts~I and~III then reduce the resolvent to a two-point boundary problem and solve it in closed form; Part~II does not solve it, and argues spectrally throughout. All three isolate the dominant pole by the same Perron argument and the same strict comparison on the circle $|z|=\rho$.

\section*{The predictions of Claesson, Gu\dh mundsson and Pantone, and their status}\phantomsection\label{psp:sec:cgp}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{The predictions of Claesson, Gu\dh mundsson and Pantone}
Claesson, Gu\dh mundsson and Pantone \cite{cgp} (arXiv:1908.08910, version~1 of 23~August 2019; the write read it and the 12-page author PDF, whose pagination and numbers are the same) computed $p_n$ for $n\le1000$ and analysed the sequence experimentally. They wrote $a_n$ for $p_n$ and $\mu$ for the dominant singularity. Their statements, and where this report stands on each:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{An open enumeration problem} (Section~1, p.~2): ``finding a generating function or a closed form solution to their enumeration remains an open problem.'' Part~I's Theorem~\ref{psp:egf:thm:main} gives the exponential generating function in closed form.
\item \emph{Counting cost} (Section~2, p.~5): their algorithm computes all counts up to $N$ ``in $O(N^4)$ time using $O(N^3)$ memory'', and they add that this ``assumes that all arithmetic operations are $O(1)$''. Part~I's recurrence \eqref{psp:egf:eq:recurrence} uses $O(N^2)$ arithmetic operations and $O(N)$ stored numbers in the same model; Part~I claims no bit complexity and no optimality.
\item \emph{Automated fitting} (Section~3.1, pp.~7--8): from 1000 terms they found no rational, algebraic, D-finite or D-algebraic fit for the ordinary generating function or for transforms of it including the exponential generating function, and derived rigorous size bounds for the rational, algebraic and D-finite cases (a D-finite equation would need $(k+2)(d+1)>1000$). Part~I's non-D-finiteness makes the D-finite bound moot, and its D-algebraic annihilator \eqref{psp:egf:eq:resultant} is consistent with their bounded search (Part~I says so; the write did not compute where the annihilator falls in their search space).
\item \emph{Conjecture~2} (Section~3.2, p.~9): for every $k$ the ordinary generating function of the permutations with $k$ runs is $F_k(x)=N_k(x)/\prod_{i=1}^k(1-ix)^{k-i+1}$, ``where $N_k(x)$ is a polynomial of degree $k(k+1)/2$, the same degree as the conjectured denominator.'' \textbf{Proved}: first by Shivam Patel (below), then by Part~III's Theorem~\ref{psp:run:thm:fixed}, an alternate proof that also gives the leading coefficient.
\item \emph{Positive real singularities} (Section~3.3, p.~10): fifteen values, the first nine to 75 decimals and the rest to 56, 47, 36, 18, 11 and 6 decimals, the last two $5.55979961612$ and $5.659669$. ``Each of these singularities is predicted to have critical exponent $-1$, making them simple poles.'' \textbf{Proved}, and more: every pole of $P$ is simple (Part~I, Section~\ref{psp:egf:sec:riccati}), and the positive poles are exactly the solutions $\rho_0<\rho_1<\cdots$ of the phase equation \eqref{psp:egf:eq:realpoles}. The write's interval computation shows that each printed value is a correct truncation of some $\rho_k$, and that the list is $\rho_0,\dots,\rho_{11},\rho_{13},\rho_{14},\rho_{15}$: it omits $\rho_{12}=5.33887671271979\ldots$ (Remark~\ref{psp:egf:rem:cgp-poles}). The authors' repository file \cite{repo}, added three days before the arXiv posting, lists $\rho_{12}$; the omission is in the printed list only.
\item \emph{Infinitely many singularities and non-D-finiteness} (Section~3.3, p.~10): ``These results suggest that the exponential generating function may posses an infinite number of singularities. If true, this would imply the non-D-finiteness of both the ordinary and exponential generating functions.'' \textbf{Proved} (Part~I, Section~\ref{psp:egf:sec:positive}, including the ordinary series).
\item \emph{Complex pairs} (Section~3.3, p.~10): three pairs, ``also simple poles'', near $0.42794\pm3.60126i$, $1.80793\pm4.04623i$, $2.50840\pm4.24168i$, printed to 73 decimals. Every pole is simple (Part~I). Newton's method on Part~I's $D$ started from the printed digits converges, in 220-digit arithmetic, to zeros of $D$ with $Q\ne0$, hence (by Part~I's pole-set theorem) to poles. The printed digits are correct to 62--66 decimal places, after which each printed string drops two consecutive digits; the repository's values agree with these zeros to at least 108 decimal places (Remark~\ref{psp:egf:rem:cgp-poles}). This is a numerical observation, not an interval certificate, and Part~I proves nothing about the positions of nonreal poles beyond the equation $D=0$.
\item \emph{The constants} (Section~3.3, pp.~10--11): $\mu\approx1.11343904173672704376\ldots$ (75 decimals printed), $\mu^{-1}\approx0.89811831857468696951\ldots$ (34 decimals printed) and $C\approx0.69568854907063576799\ldots$ (58 decimals printed), with $a_n\sim C\cdot n!\cdot(0.898118\ldots)^n$. \textbf{Proved} with an exponentially small relative error (Part~I, Theorem~\ref{psp:egf:thm:leading}; Part~II, Theorem~\ref{psp:op:thm:main}); $C$ has the closed form \eqref{psp:egf:eq:leading}. Part~I's certificate \eqref{psp:egf:eq:brackets} proves 56 decimals of $\rho$ and 48 of $C$; the write's interval check proves all 75 printed decimals of $\rho$, and its 220-digit evaluation of the closed form agrees with all 58 printed decimals of $C$ and all 34 of $\mu^{-1}$ (not interval-certified beyond Part~I's 48 decimals for $C$).
\end{itemize}
Asinowski, Banderier and Hackl \cite{abh} (DMTCS 22:2, 2021; read by the write) report these predictions in their Section~2.4 (p.~18), where they say that ``an exact asymptotic analysis of the growth behaviour of the sequence remains a challenge'', give the lower bound of their Theorem~12, and quote the conjectured equivalent $p_n\sim(0.695688\ldots)(0.898118\ldots)^nn!$. Their conclusion (p.~37) lists among open challenges ``the existence of closed-form formulas'' and says that ``the optimal cost of the computation of pop-stacked permutations is still open''. Part~I settles one instance of the first (a closed form for the exponential generating function of the total count) and improves the operation count for the second without settling optimality (Section~\ref{psp:sec:further}, item~\ref{psp:q:complexity}).

\emph{Patel's proof of Conjecture~2, and priority.} Part~III, Section~\ref{psp:run:sec:fixed}, credits a public proof of Conjecture~2 by Shivam Patel on MathDB \cite{patel}, posted 20~August 2026, and calls its own proof an alternate one; it makes no first-resolution claim. At intake (6~October 2026, earlier the same day) the page returned HTTP~403. At the write it was reachable, and the write read it in full. The page shows the conjecture with the credit to Claesson, Gu\dh mundsson and Pantone, a site-generated ``Progress summary'' marked ``Claimed solved'' that says the posted proof ``has not been independently verified'', and one solution by Shivam Patel, whose page metadata give posting and editing times 2026-08-20 14:02:52 and 14:04:08 UTC, as Part~III records. The argument encodes a pop-stacked permutation with $k$ runs as a surjective word on $[k]$ in which the occurrence intervals of every two adjacent letters overlap; it applies inclusion--exclusion over the two disjoint ways each adjacency can fail, which turns each term into a count of words respecting a poset $P$ on $[k]$ (a forest of oriented path edges); it classifies such words by the order of first occurrences, which gives $x^k\sum_\sigma\prod_{j=1}^k(1-m_jx)^{-1}$ over linear extensions $\sigma$, with $m_j$ the number of maximal elements of the first $j$ letters; since $m_j\le j$, every denominator divides $\prod_i(1-ix)^{k-i+1}$; and it evaluates $\lim_{x\to\infty}F_k(x)=-1$ by reading $\sum_\sigma\prod_j1/m_j$ as the total probability of removing a uniformly random maximal element step by step, so that the numerator has exact degree $k(k+1)/2$ and the stated leading coefficient. The write checked each step and found the argument correct and complete for the denominator, the degree and the leading coefficient, in agreement with Part~III's assessment. Patel also proves $N_k(1)=2(-1)^{k(k+1)/2-1}\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}j!$ for $k\ge2$ (only the two consistently oriented full chains have a pole of order $k$ at $x=1$); the write checked this against the published $N_1,\dots,N_5$ ($1$, $2$, $-4$, $-24$, $576$). Priority, as far as the write can see: Patel's proof (20~August 2026) precedes Part~III (3~October 2026); MathDB's status for the problem is ``Claimed solved'' (``CLAIMED SOLVED - unverified'' in the page data), with the note that the posted proof ``has not been independently verified''; no peer-reviewed proof is known to the write. Part~III's proof is different (a degree filtration of the bivariate formula) and stands on its own.

\section*{The inverses and the transseries volume}\phantomsection\label{psp:sec:inverses}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{The inverses and the transseries volume}
The repository's transseries volume \cite{TSvol} has a Part~0 apparatus for inverting rapidly growing functions. Its symbols are subscripted ``vol'' here where they meet this report's letters ($\kappa_\vol$, $d_\vol$, $v_\vol$, $X_\vol$, $L_\vol$, $h_\vol$, $f_\vol$, $R_\vol$). Statement by statement (Part~III has no inverse):
\begin{itemize}
\item Parts~I and~II, the initializer $x_0=L/W(L/(e\rho))$ (Part~II, \eqref{psp:op:eq:xzero}; Part~I, Section~\ref{psp:egf:sec:inverse}, with $L_y$ for $L=\log y$), the root of $x_0\log(x_0/(e\rho))=L$: an \emph{instance} of the factorial core \file{p0:prop:factorial-core} with $\kappa_\vol=1$, $d_\vol=-(1+\log\rho)$ and target $L_\vol=L>0$. That proposition gives $v_\vol=W_0(Le^{-1-\log\rho})=W_0(L/(e\rho))$, which is Part~I's $u$, and $X_\vol=e^{v_\vol+1+\log\rho}=e\rho e^{v_\vol}$; since $v_\vol e^{v_\vol}=L/(e\rho)$, $X_\vol=L/v_\vol=x_0$, and $\kappa_\vol\log X_\vol+d_\vol=v_\vol>0$, the side condition. Its slope formula gives $\Phi_0'(x_0)=v_\vol+1$, which is Part~I's $u+1$ and Part~II's $u=\log(x_0/\rho)$ (so Part~II's $u$ is Part~I's $u$ plus one, the difference the intake found numerically).
\item Part~I, the one-step correction \eqref{psp:egf:eq:inverseinit}: exactly the first-order term $X-\varrho(X)/\Phi_0'(X)$ of the perturbation-operator series \file{p0:eq:operator-series} (\file{p0:prop:operator-form}) about the factorial core $\Phi_0(x)=x\log(x/(e\rho))$, with $X=x_0$, $\Phi_0'(x_0)=u+1$ and $\varrho(x)=\frac12\log x+\log(C\sqrt{2\pi})+O(1/x)$, the difference $\mathcal H(x)-\Phi_0(x)$ by Stirling's formula. The error bound $O(1/(x_0u))$ is Part~I's own argument; the proposition is a formal series. An \emph{analogue}, not an instance of a theorem of the volume. The same holds for Part~II's \eqref{psp:op:eq:firstinverse}, which is the same formula.
\item Part~II, the recursion \eqref{psp:op:eq:inverserecurrence} for the coefficients $d_m$: an \emph{instance} of \file{p0:thm:core-reversion}, with the volume's quadratic part $h_\vol=0$. Take for $R_\vol$ the field $\QQ(u,b)$ of rational functions in two indeterminates (a commutative $\QQ$-algebra), $\Lambda=u$, the volume's $t$ equal to Part~II's $t$, and the unknown $E=d-d_0$ (the volume's second variable). \section{The explicit generating function}\label{psp:egf:sec:main}
A pop-stack pass reverses each maximal decreasing run and concatenates
the resulting increasing blocks. A permutation in its image is
\emph{pop-stacked}. The first values, including the empty permutation, are
\[
1,1,1,3,11,49,263,1653,11877,95991,862047,8516221.
\]
The established characterization~\cite{abbhl,abh} says that consecutive
maximal ascending runs, with endpoints $(a,b)$ and $(c,d)$, satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{psp:egf:eq:overlap}
 a<d,\qquad c<b.
\end{equation}
A singleton has equal endpoints. Thus a singleton may sit strictly inside
the range of an adjacent run, but two singleton runs cannot be adjacent.
The second inequality enforces the descent between maximal runs; the
first is the extra image condition.

Put
\[
 Q(z)=4e^z-1,\qquad E(z)=e^{z/2},\qquad B(z)=2E(z)+1,
\]
and define the entire functions
\begin{align}
 H(z)&=\sum_{k\geq0}\frac{(-1)^kz^{2k}Q(z)^k}{16^k(2k)!},\label{psp:egf:eq:H}\\
 J_0(z)&=\frac z4\sum_{k\geq0}\frac{(-1)^kz^{2k}Q(z)^k}{16^k(2k+1)!}.
 \label{psp:egf:eq:J}
\end{align}
Factorial denominators give locally uniform convergence. Set
\begin{equation}\label{psp:egf:eq:ND}
 N(z)=e^{-z}Q(z)\bigl(H(z)+B(z)J_0(z)\bigr),\qquad
 D(z)=B(z)H(z)-Q(z)J_0(z).
\end{equation}
\begin{theorem}\label{psp:egf:thm:main}
The exponential generating function of pop-stacked permutations is
\begin{equation}\label{psp:egf:eq:entire}
 \boxed{P(z)=\frac{N(z)}{D(z)}}.
\end{equation}
This denotes a meromorphic function on $\CC$, with removable common zeros
filled in. Near zero, write $r=\sqrt{4e^z-1}$ with $r(0)=\sqrt3$; then
\begin{equation}\label{psp:egf:eq:trig}
 P(z)=e^{-z}r\,
 \frac{(2E+1)\sin(zr/4)+r\cos(zr/4)}
      {(2E+1)\cos(zr/4)-r\sin(zr/4)}.
\end{equation}
The global expression \eqref{psp:egf:eq:entire} uses no square-root branch.
The genuine pole set is exactly
\begin{equation}\label{psp:egf:eq:poleset}
 \{\zeta\in\CC:D(\zeta)=0,\ Q(\zeta)\ne0\}.
\end{equation}
Every member is simple, and
\begin{equation}\label{psp:egf:eq:residue}
 \Res(P,\zeta)=-\frac{2e^{-2\zeta}(4e^\zeta-1)}{\zeta+2}.
\end{equation}
There are infinitely many positive poles. The EGF is not D-finite, and
the counts are not P-recursive, although the EGF is differentially
algebraic over $\mathbb Q(z)$.
\end{theorem}
The proof occupies Sections~\ref{psp:egf:sec:model}--\ref{psp:egf:sec:algebraic}.
The exact recurrence and arithmetic complexity appear in
Section~\ref{psp:egf:sec:recurrence}; the leading asymptotic and constants appear
in Sections~\ref{psp:egf:sec:dominance}--\ref{psp:egf:sec:certificate}.

Theorem~\ref{psp:egf:thm:main} proves the predictions of Claesson, Gu\dh mundsson and Pantone \cite[Section~3.3]{cgp} that the poles are simple, that there are infinitely many singularities, and that neither the exponential nor the ordinary generating function is D-finite (front matter, ``The predictions of Claesson, Gu\dh mundsson and Pantone''). \section{Mixed run states and exact normalization}\label{psp:egf:sec:model}
Let
\[
 I=(0,1),\qquad \Delta=\{(a,b):0<a<b<1\},\qquad
 X=I\sqcup\Delta,\qquad \mu=\dd x\sqcup\dd a\dd b.
\]
A state $x\in I$ is a singleton with $\lo(x)=\hi(x)=x$; a state
$(a,b)\in\Delta$ has $\lo=a,\hi=b$. Define
\begin{align}
 K(s,t)&=\one\{\lo(s)<\hi(t),\ \lo(t)<\hi(s)\},\label{psp:egf:eq:K}\\
 w_z(x)&=z,\qquad w_z(a,b)=z^2e^{z(b-a)},\label{psp:egf:eq:weight}\\
 (A_zf)(s)&=\int_X K(s,t)w_z(t)f(t)\dd\mu(t).
 \label{psp:egf:eq:operator}
\end{align}
Work, for example, on $L^2(X,\mu)$. The kernel is bounded, $\mu(X)=3/2$,
and the weights are uniformly bounded on compact $z$-sets. Thus $A_z$
is compact, entire in operator norm, and $A_z=O(|z|)$ near zero.

Here is the factorial normalization. Represent $n$ distinct labels by
independent coordinates in $(0,1)$. Each of the $n!$ rank orders has
volume $1/n!$. A run of length $\ell\geq2$ with extreme coordinates
$a<b$ has ordered interior-coordinate volume
$(b-a)^{\ell-2}/(\ell-2)!$. Its summed weight is
\[
 \sum_{\ell\geq2}z^\ell\frac{(b-a)^{\ell-2}}{(\ell-2)!}
 =z^2e^{z(b-a)}.
\]
A singleton contributes $z\dd x$. The product of kernels enforces exactly
the adjacent-run conditions \eqref{psp:egf:eq:overlap}; there is neither an
extra run-order factor nor an extra factorial. For instance,
\[
 \int_Xw_z\dd\mu=z+z^2\int_0^1(1-t)e^{zt}\dd t=e^z-1,
\]
the EGF of the unique increasing permutation of each positive size.

Summing over the number of runs, with absolute convergence for small
$|z|$, now proves
\begin{equation}\label{psp:egf:eq:resolvent}
 h=(I-A_z)^{-1}\one,\qquad
 P(z)=1+\int_Xw_z h\dd\mu.
\end{equation}
The pairing is bilinear; no complex conjugate is inserted.
\begin{writenote}
The same normalization and resolvent are proved in Part~II, Section~\ref{psp:op:sec:integral} (with $J$ for $K$ and $D$ for $\Delta$), and in Part~III, Section~\ref{psp:run:sec:model} (every run weight multiplied by $u$, measure $\nu$); \eqref{psp:egf:eq:resolvent} is \eqref{psp:op:eq:resolvent} and the case $u=1$ of \eqref{psp:run:eq:resolvent}. The overlap characterization \eqref{psp:egf:eq:overlap} is proved in Part~II, Lemma~\ref{psp:op:lem:characterization}.
\end{writenote}

\section{Cumulative boundary problem}\label{psp:egf:sec:bvp}
For small $z$, define the endpoint cumulatives
\begin{align*}
 U(x)&=\int_{\hi(s)\leq x}w_z(s)h(s)\dd\mu(s),&
 V(x)&=\int_{\lo(s)<x}w_z(s)h(s)\dd\mu(s),\\
 T&=\int_Xw_z h\dd\mu=P-1.
\end{align*}
Endpoint equality sets have measure zero. Hence
\begin{equation}\label{psp:egf:eq:ends}
 U(0)=V(0)=0,\qquad U(1)=V(1)=T.
\end{equation}
Complementing the two nonoverlap regions in \eqref{psp:egf:eq:K} gives
\begin{equation}\label{psp:egf:eq:hUV}
 h(x)=1+V(x)-U(x),\qquad h(a,b)=1+V(b)-U(a).
\end{equation}
These identities first yield continuous representatives and then the
smoothness used below. Introduce
\[
 I_0(x)=\int_0^x e^{z(x-a)}U(a)\dd a,\qquad
 J_1(x)=\int_x^1 e^{z(b-x)}V(b)\dd b.
\]
Direct differentiation of the cumulatives gives
\begin{align}
 U'&=ze^{zx}(1+V)-zU-z^2I_0, & I_0'&=U+zI_0,\label{psp:egf:eq:firstU}\\
 V'&=zV+ze^{z(1-x)}(1-U)+z^2J_1, & J_1'&=-V-zJ_1.
 \label{psp:egf:eq:firstV}
\end{align}
For example, differentiating the upper endpoint of the interval part of
$U$ gives $z^2\int_0^xe^{z(x-a)}(1+V(x)-U(a))\dd a$; combining this with
the singleton term $z(1+V(x)-U(x))$ gives \eqref{psp:egf:eq:firstU}.
Differentiating again and substituting eliminates the integral terms:
\begin{equation}\label{psp:egf:eq:secondUV}
 U''=ze^{zx}V',\qquad V''=-ze^{z(1-x)}U'.
\end{equation}
Writing $u=U'$ and $v=V'$ yields
\begin{equation}\label{psp:egf:eq:uv}
 u'=ze^{zx}v,\qquad v'=-ze^{z(1-x)}u,
 \qquad u(0)=z,\quad v(1)=z.
\end{equation}
The final boundary condition uses $U(1)=V(1)$ in \eqref{psp:egf:eq:firstV}.
For $z\ne0$, elimination gives the constant-coefficient problem
\begin{equation}\label{psp:egf:eq:ode}
 u''-zu'+z^2e^zu=0,\qquad u(0)=z,\quad u'(1)=z^2e^z.
\end{equation}
At zero the homogeneous scalar problem is $u''=0,u(0)=u'(1)=0$;
its only solution is zero. Analytic fundamental matrices therefore give
local uniqueness for \eqref{psp:egf:eq:ode}. The first-order problem
\eqref{psp:egf:eq:uv} is likewise locally unique, including $z=0$.

\begin{lemma}[Sufficiency of the boundary problem]\label{psp:egf:lem:sufficiency}
The locally unique solution of \eqref{psp:egf:eq:uv}, integrated from zero,
reconstructs precisely the resolvent in \eqref{psp:egf:eq:resolvent}.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The reflected pair $(v(1-x),u(1-x))$ solves the same equations and boundary
data. Uniqueness gives $u(x)=v(1-x)$. Therefore the reconstructed
$U(x)=\int_0^xu$ and $V(x)=\int_0^xv$ satisfy $U(1)=V(1)$.
Define $I_0,J_1$ from these functions and put
\begin{align*}
 F_1&=u-ze^{zx}(1+V)+zU+z^2I_0,\\
 F_2&=v-zV-ze^{z(1-x)}(1-U)-z^2J_1.
\end{align*}
Calculation using \eqref{psp:egf:eq:uv} gives $F_1'=zF_1$ and $F_2'=-zF_2$.
Their boundary values are $F_1(0)=0$ and
$F_2(1)=z(U(1)-V(1))=0$. Both vanish. Consequently the endpoint
cumulatives of the $h$ defined by \eqref{psp:egf:eq:hUV} have derivatives
$U',V'$ and initial values zero; they are exactly $U,V$.
Complementing nonoverlap regions gives $h=1+A_zh$. The Neumann inverse
is unique near zero, completing the sufficiency proof.
\end{proof}
This step retains the endpoint condition that could otherwise be lost
by differentiating. No global uniqueness of the unreduced boundary
problem is asserted at exceptional complex parameters.

\section{Explicit solution and the canceled factor}\label{psp:egf:sec:cancellation}
Choose $r=\sqrt{4e^z-1}$ locally at zero and put $w=zr/2$. Solving
\eqref{psp:egf:eq:ode} gives
\begin{align}
 u(x)&=e^{zx/2}\bigl(z\cos(wx)+A\sin(wx)\bigr),\label{psp:egf:eq:usolution}\\
 \frac Az&=\frac{2E-\cos w+r\sin w}{r\cos w+\sin w}.
 \label{psp:egf:eq:A}
\end{align}
Integrate the differential equation and use $P=1+\int_0^1u$ to obtain
\begin{equation}\label{psp:egf:eq:integrated}
 P=\frac{zu(1)-z^2/2+Aw}{z^2E^2}.
\end{equation}
Substitution, with $\sin^2w+\cos^2w=1$, simplifies this to
\begin{equation}\label{psp:egf:eq:fullangle}
 P=e^{-z}r\frac{2E-\cos w+r\sin w}{r\cos w+\sin w}.
\end{equation}
All identities with apparent division by $z$ are first obtained for
small nonzero $z$ and then continued to zero.

To display the cancellation, set $c=\cos(w/2)$, $s=\sin(w/2)$ and
\[
 D_+=(2E+1)c-rs,\quad D_-=(2E-1)c+rs,\quad L=rc+(2E+1)s.
\]
The identity $r^2=(2E-1)(2E+1)$ gives, by multiplication,
\begin{align}
 r(r\cos w+\sin w)&=D_+D_-,\label{psp:egf:eq:factor1}\\
 r(2E-\cos w+r\sin w)&=D_-L.\label{psp:egf:eq:factor2}
\end{align}
As $D_-(0)=1$, the common factor cancels near zero. Substituting into
\eqref{psp:egf:eq:fullangle} proves \eqref{psp:egf:eq:trig}. In particular, zeros of the
full boundary determinant coming solely from $D_-$ are not poles of the
scalar counting EGF.

Locally $H=\cos(zr/4)$ and $J_0=\sin(zr/4)/r$, so \eqref{psp:egf:eq:trig}
is exactly \eqref{psp:egf:eq:entire}. Both $N,D$ are entire and $N(0)=D(0)=3$.
This proves the branch-free meromorphic continuation and $P(0)=1$.
It does not require a square root or inverse trigonometric function to
be defined globally.

\section{Positive poles and non D finiteness}\label{psp:egf:sec:positive}
For real $z\geq0$ put $r=\sqrt{4e^z-1}>0$ and
\begin{equation}\label{psp:egf:eq:phase}
 \alpha(z)=\frac12\arccos\!\left(\frac1{2e^{z/2}}\right),\qquad
 \phi(z)=\frac{zr}{4}+\alpha(z).
\end{equation}
The real inverse cosine takes values in $[0,\pi]$.
Since $\tan\alpha=r/(2E+1)$, equation \eqref{psp:egf:eq:trig} becomes
\begin{equation}\label{psp:egf:eq:tan}
 P(z)=e^{-z}r\tan\phi(z).
\end{equation}
Differentiation simplifies to
\begin{equation}\label{psp:egf:eq:phiprime}
 \phi'(z)=\frac{e^z(z+2)}{2r}>0.
\end{equation}
Moreover $\phi(0)=\pi/6$ and $\phi(z)\to\infty$. Thus for each
integer $k\geq0$ there is a unique positive $\rho_k$ satisfying
\begin{equation}\label{psp:egf:eq:realpoles}
 \phi(\rho_k)=\frac\pi2+k\pi,
\end{equation}
or equivalently
\[
 \frac{\rho_k\sqrt{4e^{\rho_k}-1}}2+
 \arccos\!\left(\frac1{2e^{\rho_k/2}}\right)=(2k+1)\pi.
\]
The derivative and prefactor in \eqref{psp:egf:eq:tan} are nonzero there, so all
these points are genuine simple poles. This describes positive poles
only, without ordering them among complex poles by modulus.

An analytic D-finite germ satisfies a nonzero linear differential
equation with polynomial coefficients. At every point where its leading
coefficient is nonzero, the standard local linear-ODE continuation
theorem excludes singularities. Its finite-plane singularities can thus
occur at only finitely many points. The infinitely many distinct poles
above prove that $P$ is not D-finite. If $(p_n)$ were P-recursive, then
$(p_n/n!)$ would be P-recursive and its ordinary generating function $P$
would be D-finite, a contradiction. The ordinary series
$\sum p_nz^n$, understood formally, is not D-finite either.

\section{Riccati equation and every complex pole}\label{psp:egf:sec:riccati}
On a local branch in \eqref{psp:egf:eq:tan}, let $a=e^{-z}r$. Then
\[
 \frac{a'}a=\frac{1-2e^z}{Q},\qquad
 a\phi'=\frac{z+2}{2},\qquad
 \frac{\phi'}a=\frac{e^{2z}(z+2)}{2Q}.
\]
Differentiating $P=a\tan\phi$ yields the exact identity
\begin{equation}\label{psp:egf:eq:riccati}
 2Q P'=2(1-2e^z)P+(z+2)Q+(z+2)e^{2z}P^2,
 \qquad P(0)=1.
\end{equation}
It holds globally by meromorphic continuation. Since $2Q(0)=6$, it is
also a locally unique analytic initial-value equation at zero.

\subsection{Removal of the degenerate parameters}
All zeros of $Q$ are $z_k=-\log4+2\pi ik$, and they are simple. At such
a point $E=(-1)^k/2$. If $k$ is even, $B=2$, $D=2$ and $P(z_k)=0$.
If $k$ is odd, locally
\[
 B=1-\sqrt{1+Q}=-Q/2+O(Q^2),\quad H=1+O(Q),\quad
 J_0=z/4+O(Q).
\]
Consequently
\[
 D=-Q(z+2)/4+O(Q^2),\qquad N=4Q+O(Q^2).
\]
As $z_k\ne-2$, the singularity is removable and
$P(z_k)=-16/(z_k+2)$. Thus no zero of $Q$ is a pole.

The other potentially degenerate coefficient for a Riccati pole balance
is $z=-2$. Put $s=\sqrt{1-4/e^2}$, so $0<s<1$. Direct evaluation gives
\[
 D(-2)=(1+2/e)\cosh(s/2)-s\sinh(s/2)>0,
\]
because $1+2/e>1>s\tanh(s/2)$. Hence $-2$ is not a pole.

\subsection{Simplicity residues and the exact pole set}
At a genuine pole $\zeta$, the preceding argument gives
$Q(\zeta)\ne0$ and $\zeta\ne-2$. If its order is $m$, the most singular
orders in \eqref{psp:egf:eq:riccati} are $m+1$ from $P'$ and $2m$ from $P^2$;
the other terms are of lower order. Thus $m+1=2m$ and $m=1$.
Writing $P=a_{-1}/(z-\zeta)+O(1)$ and comparing coefficients gives
\eqref{psp:egf:eq:residue}.

Away from $Q=0$, a zero of $D$ cannot cancel against $N$. Indeed the two
linear combinations in \eqref{psp:egf:eq:trig} have coefficient determinant
\[
 (2E+1)^2+r^2=4E(2E+1)\ne0.
\]
The last inequality follows because $E\ne0$ and $2E+1=0$ would imply
$Q=0$. Simultaneous vanishing would force $\sin(zr/4)=\cos(zr/4)=0$,
which is impossible. This proves exactly \eqref{psp:egf:eq:poleset}.

For a genuine pole define
\begin{equation}\label{psp:egf:eq:Czeta}
 C_\zeta=-\frac{\Res(P,\zeta)}\zeta
 =\frac{2e^{-2\zeta}(4e^\zeta-1)}{\zeta(\zeta+2)}.
\end{equation}
There is no pole at zero. For every fixed $R>0$ with no pole on
$|z|=R$, there are finitely many poles inside, and subtraction of their
principal parts leaves a function analytic on a neighborhood of the
closed disk. Cauchy's bound therefore proves
\begin{equation}\label{psp:egf:eq:finitepole}
 \frac{p_n}{n!}=\sum_{|\zeta|<R}C_\zeta\zeta^{-n}+O_R(R^{-n}).
\end{equation}
This is a finite-radius expansion. It does not assert convergence of an
infinite sum over all poles, a numerical ordering of nonreal poles, or an
effective bound for the constant implicit in the remainder.

\section{Differential algebraicity}\label{psp:egf:sec:algebraic}
Use independent jet variables $p,u,v$ and an auxiliary variable $X$.
Define the following polynomials in $\mathbb Q[z,p,u,v]$:
\begin{align*}
 A&=(z+2)p^2,& B_1&=-8u-4p+4(z+2),& C_1&=2u+2p-(z+2),\\
 A_2&=p^2+2(z+2)pu+2A,&
 B_2&=-8v-4u+4+B_1,& C_2&=2v+2u-1.
\end{align*}
The Riccati equation states
$AX^2+B_1X+C_1=0$ at $X=e^z$, $p=P$, $u=P'$. Differentiating with
$X'=X$ gives $A_2X^2+B_2X+C_2=0$ at $v=P''$. Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{psp:egf:eq:resultant}
 \mathcal F(z,p,u,v)=\det
 \begin{pmatrix}
 A&B_1&C_1&0\\
 0&A&B_1&C_1\\
 A_2&B_2&C_2&0\\
 0&A_2&B_2&C_2
 \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
satisfies $\mathcal F(z,P,P',P'')=0$.
It is not the zero polynomial: at $(z,p,u,v)=(0,1,0,0)$, the two
quadratics are $2X(X+2)$ and $5X^2+8X-1$. Their resultant is
$2^2(-1)(3)=-12$. Thus $P$ is differentially algebraic of order at most
two over $\mathbb Q(z)$, despite being non-D-finite. The polynomial
condition can have extraneous solution germs; neither minimal order nor
uniqueness from this resultant is asserted. Earlier unsuccessful bounded
D-algebraic fitting experiments do not establish differential
transcendence and do not contradict this annihilator.

\section{Exact quadratic arithmetic enumeration}\label{psp:egf:sec:recurrence}
Let $q_n=4-\delta_{n,0}$ for $n\geq0$, with $q_{-1}=0$. For $n\geq0$ put
\begin{align*}
 E_n&=\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nkp_k,&
 S_n&=\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nkp_kp_{n-k},\\
 T_n&=\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk2^{n-k}S_k,&
 G_n&=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\binom nkp_{k+1},
\end{align*}
where $T_{-1}=G_0=0$. Extracting exponential coefficients in
\eqref{psp:egf:eq:riccati} gives
\begin{equation}\label{psp:egf:eq:recurrence}
 \boxed{6p_{n+1}=2p_n-4E_n+2q_n+nq_{n-1}+2T_n+nT_{n-1}-8G_n},
 \qquad p_0=1.
\end{equation}
For example, the left side of \eqref{psp:egf:eq:riccati} contributes
$6p_{n+1}+8G_n$, while the three terms on the right contribute

\section{Unique dominant pole and leading asymptotic}\label{psp:egf:sec:dominance}
For completeness we supply the positive-operator isolation argument,
rather than infer a complex gap from the positive pole equation.
Let $\mathcal K$ denote the integral operator with kernel $K$ from
\eqref{psp:egf:eq:K}. For real $t>0$, multiplication by $\sqrt{w_t}$ is boundedly
invertible on $L^2(X,\mu)$. Thus $A_t$ is similar to the compact
self-adjoint, positivity-preserving operator
\[
 S_t=M_{\sqrt{w_t}}\mathcal K M_{\sqrt{w_t}}.
\]
It need not be positive semidefinite. For any states $s,s'$, every
interval $(a,b)$ with
\[
 0<a<\min(\lo(s),\lo(s')),
 \qquad \max(\hi(s),\hi(s'))<b<1
\]
overlaps both. This set has positive area. Consequently $S_t^2$ has a
strictly positive kernel almost everywhere.

\begin{lemma}\label{psp:egf:lem:perron}
For such an operator, $\lambda(t)=\spr(A_t)=\|S_t\|>0$ is a simple
positive eigenvalue with positive left and right eigenfunctions.
All other eigenvalues have smaller modulus.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The compact self-adjoint spectral theorem gives a top eigenvector of
$S_t^2$. Replacing it by its absolute value cannot decrease its Rayleigh
quotient, so the absolute value is also a top eigenvector. Strict
positivity of the squared kernel makes it positive almost everywhere.
A sign-changing top eigenvector would yield a strictly larger Rayleigh
quotient after taking its absolute value, a contradiction. Hence the top
eigenspace is one-dimensional: two independent real vectors would
produce a nonzero vector orthogonal to a positive vector, which must
change sign. The action of $S_t$ on the positive vector is positive,
so its eigenvalue is $+\|S_t\|$. Any other eigenvalue of the same modulus
would give another top vector of $S_t^2$. Compactness gives the strict
spectral gap. Similarity transfers simplicity and positive left and
right eigenvectors to $A_t$.
\end{proof}
Norm continuity gives continuity of $\lambda$. Increasing $t$ strictly
increases the kernel of $S_t$ wherever $K=1$; testing on its positive
Perron vector proves strict increase of $\lambda$. Also $\lambda(t)\to0$
as $t\downarrow0$. On
$F=\{(a,b):a<1/4,\ b>3/4\}$, all pairs overlap, $\mu(F)=1/16$, and
$w_t\geq t^2e^{t/2}$. Its indicator Rayleigh quotient gives
\[
 \lambda(t)\geq t^2e^{t/2}/16\longrightarrow\infty.
\]
Thus there is a unique $t_*>0$ with $\lambda(t_*)=1$.

The simple eigenvalue depends analytically on the parameter locally.
If $f_t>0$ is its real unit eigenvector for $S_t$, differentiation gives
\begin{equation}\label{psp:egf:eq:lambdaprime}
 \lambda'(t)=\lambda(t)\int_X\frac{w'_t}{w_t}f_t^2\dd\mu>0.
\end{equation}
One can obtain this directly from
$S_t'=\tfrac12(M_{w'_t/w_t}S_t+S_tM_{w'_t/w_t})$ and the normalized
simple-eigenvalue derivative formula. Local analyticity follows from a
Riesz projection around the isolated eigenvalue, choosing the positive
weight square root locally.

At $t_*$ put $h_*=M_{1/\sqrt{w_{t_*}}}f_{t_*}$ and
$\ell_*=M_{\sqrt{w_{t_*}}}f_{t_*}$. Their pairing is one. The local
resolvent singular part is the corresponding analytic rank-one
projection divided by $1-\lambda(z)$. Its scalar numerator in
\eqref{psp:egf:eq:resolvent} is
\[
 \left(\int_X\sqrt{w_{t_*}}f_{t_*}\dd\mu\right)^2>0.
\]
Equation \eqref{psp:egf:eq:lambdaprime} proves that this is a genuine simple
positive scalar pole. For $|z|<t_*$, the absolute kernel comparison
$|A_z^kf|\leq A_{|z|}^k|f|$ and the spectral-radius formula show
$\spr(A_z)\leq\lambda(|z|)<1$; hence there are no poles inside.
The scalar resolvent identity extends over this disk by its invertible
analytic resolvent. It follows that $t_*=\rho_0$ from
\eqref{psp:egf:eq:realpoles}. Write $\rho=\rho_0$.

For $|z|=\rho$, $z\ne\rho$, define
$B_z=\mathcal K M_{|w_z|}$. Its weights are positive and bounded away
from zero. The same squared-kernel argument gives a positive Perron
vector $h_z$ with eigenvalue $\beta=\spr(B_z)$. On the interval component,
\[
 |w_z(a,b)|=\rho^2e^{\operatorname{Re}(z)(b-a)}<w_\rho(a,b),
\]
whereas the singleton weights have equal absolute value. Using the
positive left eigenvector $\ell_*$ of $A_\rho$ yields
\begin{align*}
 (1-\beta)\int_X\ell_*h_z\dd\mu
 &=\int_\Delta(w_\rho-|w_z|)h_z(\mathcal K\ell_*)\dd\mu>0.
\end{align*}
Indeed $\mathcal K\ell_*=\ell_*/w_\rho>0$ almost everywhere.
Thus $\spr(A_z)\leq\beta<1$ at every other point of this circle.
Invertibility near such a point and continuation from the interior
identify the quotient with the analytic scalar resolvent there.

We already know from \eqref{psp:egf:eq:entire} that scalar poles form a discrete
set. Compactness of the circle and discreteness, including at $\rho$,
give some $R>\rho$ with no other scalar pole on or inside that disk.
Subtracting the $\rho$ pole and applying Cauchy's estimate proves
\begin{theorem}\label{psp:egf:thm:leading}
There exist $R>\rho$ and $0<\theta=\rho/R<1$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{psp:egf:eq:leading}
 p_n=C\,n!\rho^{-n}\bigl(1+O(\theta^n)\bigr),\qquad
 C=\frac{2e^{-2\rho}(4e^\rho-1)}{\rho(\rho+2)}>0.
\end{equation}
The positive pole $\rho$ is the unique dominant pole.
\end{theorem}
The residue formula gives $C$ directly; equivalently
$C=e^{-\rho}r(\rho)/(\rho\phi'(\rho))$.
This proof establishes existence of a complex pole-free gap, but supplies
no certified numerical value of $R$, error constant, or onset index.
The real interval certificate below does not fill that separate gap.

\section{Stirling expansions and safe inversion}\label{psp:egf:sec:inverse}
For sufficiently large integers, Theorem~\ref{psp:egf:thm:leading} gives
\begin{equation}\label{psp:egf:eq:logH}
 \log p_n=\mathcal H(n)+\epsilon_n,\quad
 \mathcal H(x)=\log\Gamma(x+1)-x\log\rho+\log C,\quad
 |\epsilon_n|\leq M\theta^n
\end{equation}
for some fixed $M>0$, $0<\theta<1$ and onset $n_0$.
For each fixed $m\geq1$, the ordinary Stirling remainder then gives
\begin{align}
 \log p_n={}&n\log n-(1+\log\rho)n+\tfrac12\log n
 +\log(C\sqrt{2\pi})\notag\\
 &+\sum_{j=1}^m\frac{B_{2j}}{2j(2j-1)n^{2j-1}}
 +O(n^{-2m-1}).\label{psp:egf:eq:stirling}
\end{align}
Exponentiating a sufficiently long finite truncation gives, for every
fixed $K\geq0$,
\[
 p_n=C\sqrt{2\pi n}\left(\frac n{e\rho}\right)^n
 \left(\sum_{k=0}^K\frac{s_k}{n^k}+O(n^{-K-1})\right),
\]
where $s_k$ are the formal coefficients of
\[
 \exp\left(\sum_{j\geq1}\frac{B_{2j}t^{2j-1}}{2j(2j-1)}\right)
 =1+\frac t{12}+\frac{t^2}{288}-\frac{139t^3}{51840}+\cdots.
\]
These are fixed-order Poincar\'e expansions; no convergence of the
infinite Bernoulli series is claimed. Also
$p_{n+1}/p_n=((n+1)/\rho)(1+O(\theta^n))$.

Define $N(y)=\min\{n\geq0:p_n\geq y\}$ for $y>0$. Appending the new
maximum extends the last ascending run and preserves the overlap
inequalities, giving an injection from size $n$ to size $n+1$.
Thus $(p_n)$ is nondecreasing; the ratio formula makes it eventually
strictly increasing and unbounded. For large $y$, let $x$ be the unique
large solution of $\mathcal H(x)=\log y$. This is the eventually
increasing branch because
$\mathcal H'(x)=\psi(x+1)-\log\rho\sim\log x$ and
$\mathcal H''(x)=\psi'(x+1)>0$.

\begin{proposition}[Rounding-safe threshold]\label{psp:egf:prop:ceil}
If \eqref{psp:egf:eq:logH} holds for all $n\geq n_0$ and
\[
 x-2\geq n_0,\qquad \mathcal H'(x-2)>0,\qquad
 \delta(x)=\frac{2M\theta^{x-2}}{\mathcal H'(x-2)}<1,
\]
then
\begin{equation}\label{psp:egf:eq:ceil}
 \ceil{x-\delta(x)}\leq N(y)\leq\ceil{x+\delta(x)}.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Set $a=\ceil{x-\delta}-1$ and $b=\ceil{x+\delta}$. Both lie in
$[x-2,x+2]$. The increasing positive derivative of $\mathcal H$ gives
$\mathcal H(x)-\mathcal H(a)>2M\theta^{x-2}$ and
$\mathcal H(b)-\mathcal H(x)\geq2M\theta^{x-2}$.
The absolute errors at $a,b$ are at most $M\theta^{x-2}$.
Hence $p_a<y\leq p_b$, and monotonicity gives the bracket.
\end{proof}
If the two ceilings agree, they determine the exact threshold; otherwise
exact counting or stronger information is required. In particular one
cannot automatically round an asymptotic root, even if its error tends
to zero. Here $\delta(x)=O(\theta^x/\log x)$.
The certified $\rho,C$ intervals alone do not provide effective
$M,\theta,n_0$ for this implication.

For a convenient real starting estimate, write $L_y=\log y$ and
$u=W(L_y/(e\rho))$ on the positive real Lambert branch, so
$x_0=L_y/u$ satisfies $x_0\log(x_0/(e\rho))=L_y$.
One Newton correction in the Stirling expansion gives
\begin{equation}\label{psp:egf:eq:inverseinit}
 x=x_0-\frac{\tfrac12\log x_0+\log(C\sqrt{2\pi})}{u+1}
 +O\!\left(\frac1{x_0u}\right).
\end{equation}
Indeed the displayed correction is $O(1)$, the omitted residual is
$O(1/x_0)$, and the derivative is asymptotic to $u+1$.
This continuous inverse estimate is not an exact integer-ceiling rule.
\begin{writenote}
Part~II, Section~\ref{psp:op:sec:inverse}, proves the same Stirling expansions (Corollary~\ref{psp:op:cor:stirling}), the same bracket with the same proof (Theorem~\ref{psp:op:thm:bracket}), the same first inverse correction \eqref{psp:op:eq:firstinverse}, and every fixed order of the continuous inverse (Proposition~\ref{psp:op:prop:allinverse}). Its $u=\log(x_0/\rho)$ is the present $u$ plus one, so its denominator $\log(x_0/\rho)$ is the present $u+1$, and its numerator $\frac12\log(2\pi x_0)+\log C$ equals the present one. \part{Factorial exponential growth of pop stacked permutations: an operator proof and asymptotic inversion}\label{psp:op:part}
\partsource{Bundle Report~161, archive \file{Pop_Stacked_Permutations_Asymptotics_and_Inverses_Source.zip}, delivered as \file{Report161.tex} (13 pages). Delivered title block ``Factorial exponential growth of pop stacked permutations / An operator proof and asymptotic inversion / Report 161 / 3 October 2026''. Printed as delivered, with \textbf{[write]} notes; Report~161's Section~$k$ is Section~$k+13$ here, and its statements and equations move with their sections. The opening paragraph ``Attribution and scope'' is the manuscript's own.}
\begin{partabstract}
Let $p_n$ be the number of permutations of $[n]$ in the image of one
pass through a pop stack, equivalently the number whose adjacent
maximal ascending runs overlap. This is OEIS A307030. We prove
\[
 p_n=C\,n!\rho^{-n}\bigl(1+O(q^n)\bigr),
 \qquad \rho>1,\quad C>0,\quad 0<q<1.
\]
The constants are specified exactly by a compact integral operator on
a disjoint union of singleton and interval run states. The proof
includes the labelled-simplex normalization, a self-adjoint Perron
argument, an analytic Fredholm reduction, a positive simple pole,
and strict domination at every other point on its circle of convergence.
It yields all fixed orders of the Stirling expansion, a recursively
specified all-orders expansion for the continuous inverse, and an
exponentially narrow ceiling bracket around the exact log-Gamma root.

The factorial-exponential law was predicted numerically in the
counting literature. The spectral method itself is classical; the
contribution here is its variable-run construction and the resulting

\section{Definitions and main theorem}\label{psp:op:sec:main}
A pop-stack pass reverses each maximal decreasing run of a permutation
and concatenates the resulting increasing blocks. A permutation in
its image is called \emph{pop-stacked}. Put $p_0=1$ for the empty
permutation and let $p_n$ count pop-stacked permutations of $[n]$ for
$n\geq1$. The first values, with the empty term included, are
\[
 1,1,1,3,11,49,263,1653,11877,95991,862047.
\]
All runs below are maximal ascending runs unless stated otherwise.
For consecutive runs with endpoints $(a,b)$ and $(c,d)$ the two
required strict inequalities are
\begin{equation}\label{psp:op:eq:cross}
 a<d,\qquad c<b.
\end{equation}
The second says that the boundary is a descent; the first is the
additional image condition. A singleton has coincident endpoints.
Thus the terminology ``overlap'' means these cross inequalities,
not a positive-length intersection: a singleton may lie strictly
inside the range of a neighboring nonsingleton run. Two singleton
runs can never satisfy both inequalities.

Here is an exact specification of the constants. Let
\[
 I=(0,1),\quad D=\{(a,b):0<a<b<1\},\quad X=I\sqcup D,
 \qquad \mu=\dd x\sqcup\dd a\dd b,
\]
so the Hilbert space is $\mathcal H=L^2(X,\mu)=L^2(I)\oplus L^2(D)$.
For $s=x\in I$ set $\lo(s)=\hi(s)=x$; for $s=(a,b)\in D$ set
$\lo(s)=a$, $\hi(s)=b$. Define the symmetric kernel
\begin{equation}\label{psp:op:eq:kernel}
 J(s,t)=\one\{\lo(s)<\hi(t),\ \lo(t)<\hi(s)\},
 \qquad (Jf)(s)=\int_X J(s,t)f(t)\dd\mu(t),
\end{equation}
and the entire weights
\begin{equation}\label{psp:op:eq:weights}
 w_z(x)=z\quad(x\in I),\qquad
 w_z(a,b)=z^2e^{z(b-a)}\quad((a,b)\in D).
\end{equation}
Write $M_f$ for multiplication by $f$. For $r>0$ put
\[
 S_r=M_{\sqrt{w_r}}JM_{\sqrt{w_r}},\qquad \lambda(r)=\|S_r\|.
\]
As proved below, $\lambda(r)$ is the simple positive leading eigenvalue,
strictly increases from zero to infinity, and has a unique crossing
\begin{equation}\label{psp:op:eq:rho}
 \lambda(\rho)=1.
\end{equation}
Let $v=v_\rho>0$ be its real eigenfunction normalized by $\|v\|_2=1$.
Define
\begin{align}
 d_\rho&=\frac1\rho\int_I v(x)^2\dd x
       +\int_D\left(\frac2\rho+b-a\right)v(a,b)^2\dd a\dd b,
       \label{psp:op:eq:derivative}\\
 C&=\frac{\left(\int_X\sqrt{w_\rho(s)}v(s)\dd\mu(s)\right)^2}
             {\rho d_\rho}.\label{psp:op:eq:amplitude}
\end{align}
There is no fitted or numerical parameter in these definitions.

\begin{theorem}[Factorial-exponential equivalent]\label{psp:op:thm:main}
The definitions above give unique constants $\rho$ and $C$, with
\begin{equation}\label{psp:op:eq:rhobounds}
 (24/23)^{1/4}\leq\rho\leq2,\qquad C>0.
\end{equation}
For some $R>\rho$,
\begin{equation}\label{psp:op:eq:main}
 \frac{p_n}{n!}=C\rho^{-n}+O(R^{-n}).
\end{equation}
Equivalently, with $q=\rho/R\in(0,1)$,
$p_n=Cn!\rho^{-n}(1+O(q^n))$.
The exponential generating function
$P(z)=\sum_{n\geq0}p_nz^n/n!$ continues meromorphically to $\CC$.
Its only singularity in some closed disk of radius greater than
$\rho$ is the simple pole at $\rho$, with principal part
$C/(1-z/\rho)$.
\end{theorem}

The scalar function may have cancellations of other operator poles.
No assertion is made about their number, positions, or existence.
In particular, meromorphic continuation and one pole do not imply
non-D-finiteness. The published predictions
$\rho\approx1.113439041736727\ldots$ and
$C\approx0.695688549070635\ldots$~\cite[Section 3.3]{cgp}
are useful context only. We do not certify any of their decimal places
or identify a numerical isolation interval narrower than
\eqref{psp:op:eq:rhobounds}.

\section{The labelled run integral}\label{psp:op:sec:integral}
\begin{lemma}[Image characterization]\label{psp:op:lem:characterization}
A permutation is pop-stacked if and only if every consecutive pair
of its maximal ascending runs satisfies \eqref{psp:op:eq:cross}.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For necessity, write an input permutation as its maximal decreasing
blocks, and let $B_1,B_2,\ldots$ be their reversals. The boundary between
two original blocks is an ascent, so
$\min B_i<\max B_{i+1}$. Each output block is increasing.
The maximal ascending runs of the output are obtained by merging
successive blocks at ascent boundaries. Between two consecutive
merged groups, let $B_i$ be the last block of the left group and
$B_{i+1}$ the first block of the right group. Since the merged groups
are increasing,
\[
 \min(\text{left})\leq\min B_i<\max B_{i+1}
 \leq\max(\text{right}).
\]
The other cross inequality holds because the boundary of maximal
ascending runs is a descent.

Conversely, reverse each maximal ascending run of a permutation
satisfying \eqref{psp:op:eq:cross}. Each reversed block is decreasing, and
the boundary between reversed blocks is an ascent because
$\min(\text{left})<\max(\text{right})$. They are therefore the maximal
decreasing runs of the resulting input permutation. A pop-stack pass
reverses them back. This proves the characterization, which is the
established result in~\cite[Theorem 1]{abbhl} and~\cite[Theorem 3]{abh}.
\end{proof}

Fix a composition $n=\ell_1+\cdots+\ell_k$ into positive run lengths.
Place one real coordinate at each of the $n$ actual permutation
positions, independently in $(0,1)$, with product Lebesgue measure.
Impose increasing inequalities within the proposed blocks and both
cross inequalities at every adjacent block boundary. Away from the
null sets where coordinates tie, the rank order of the coordinates
is a permutation. Each of the $n!$ labelled rank-order simplexes has
volume exactly $1/n!$; this follows by coordinate permutation from
the disjoint partition of the unit cube into these simplexes.
Thus the constrained volume is exactly the number of admissible
permutations with this run composition divided by $n!$.

There is no further multinomial coefficient or run factorial.
The coordinates belong to actual positions. Also there is no
multiple counting over compositions: the second cross inequality
makes every proposed boundary a descent, so the composition is
exactly the unique maximal-ascending-run composition.

For a block of size $\ell\geq2$, retain its first and last coordinates
$a<b$. Its $\ell-2$ interior coordinates are increasing between them,
so their integrated volume is
\[
 \frac{(b-a)^{\ell-2}}{(\ell-2)!}.
\]
After multiplying by $z^\ell$ and summing over all such lengths this is
\[
 \sum_{\ell\geq2}\frac{z^\ell(b-a)^{\ell-2}}{(\ell-2)!}
   =z^2e^{z(b-a)}.
\]
A singleton instead contributes $z\dd x$ on its separate
one-dimensional component. It is not the zero-measure diagonal of
$D$. For a useful check, the contribution of all single-run objects is
\begin{equation}\label{psp:op:eq:onerun}
 \int_Xw_z\dd\mu
   =z+z^2\int_0^1(1-t)e^{zt}\dd t=e^z-1,
\end{equation}
whose coefficient is $1/n!$, as required for the unique increasing
permutation of every positive size.

Put $A_z=JM_{w_z}$. The contribution from $k\geq1$ runs is
\[
 \int_{X^k}\prod_{i=1}^kw_z(s_i)
       \prod_{i=1}^{k-1}J(s_i,s_{i+1})\dd\mu^k
 =\int_X w_z A_z^{k-1}\one\dd\mu.
\]
Since $\|A_z\|\to0$ as $z\to0$, the Neumann series converges in
operator norm near zero. The sum of absolute values of these
integrals is finite there as well, by the same bound with $|w_z|$.
The simplex argument therefore proves the analytic identity
\begin{equation}\label{psp:op:eq:resolvent}
 P(z)=1+\int_Xw_z(s)\bigl[(I-A_z)^{-1}\one\bigr](s)\dd\mu(s)
 \qquad (|z|\text{ small}).
\end{equation}
The scalar pairing here is bilinear, without complex conjugation;
it preserves holomorphic dependence on $z$.

Lemma~\ref{psp:op:lem:characterization} is the characterization of \cite[Theorem~1]{abbhl} and \cite[Theorem~3]{abh}, proved here; Parts~I and~III cite it.

\section{Compactness and the Perron crossing}\label{psp:op:sec:perron}
Since $\mu(X)=3/2$ and $J$ is a measurable bounded kernel, $J$ is
Hilbert--Schmidt, hence compact. The map $z\mapsto w_z$ is entire in
$L^\infty(X)$: on every compact $z$-disk the exponential series and
all its derivatives converge uniformly because $0<b-a<1$.
Consequently $A_z$ is an entire family of compact operators, and
$A_0=0$.

For every fixed $r>0$, $w_r$ is bounded above and away from zero on
both components of $X$. Thus $T_r=M_{\sqrt{w_r}}$ is boundedly
invertible and
\begin{equation}\label{psp:op:eq:similarity}
 T_rA_rT_r^{-1}=S_r=T_rJT_r.
\end{equation}
The operator $S_r$ is compact, self-adjoint, and positivity preserving.
We do not assert that $S_r$ is positive semidefinite.

For any states $s,t$ set
$m=\min(\lo(s),\lo(t))>0$ and
$M=\max(\hi(s),\hi(t))<1$. Every interval state
$u=(a,b)$ with $0<a<m$ and $M<b<1$ satisfies
$J(s,u)J(u,t)=1$. This is a set of area $m(1-M)>0$.
It follows that $J^2$ has a strictly positive integral kernel;
the kernel of $S_r^2$ is likewise strictly positive almost everywhere,
as all intervening weights are positive.

\begin{lemma}[The needed Perron conclusion]\label{psp:op:lem:perron}
Suppose $S$ is a nonzero compact self-adjoint operator with a
nonnegative symmetric integral kernel, and $S^2$ has a strictly
positive kernel almost everywhere. Then $\lambda=\|S\|>0$ is a simple
positive eigenvalue with a real eigenfunction $v>0$ almost everywhere.
Every other eigenvalue has modulus strictly smaller than $\lambda$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
By the compact self-adjoint spectral theorem, the positive-semidefinite
operator $S^2$ has a real top eigenfunction $f$ with eigenvalue
$\lambda^2$. Its nonnegative kernel gives
\[
 \langle |f|,S^2|f|\rangle\geq
 \langle f,S^2f\rangle=\lambda^2\|f\|_2^2.
\]
The norm bound forces equality, and the spectral theorem then shows
that $|f|$ is a top eigenfunction. Applying the strictly positive
kernel shows $|f|>0$ almost everywhere. If $f$ had positive and negative
sets both of positive measure, the displayed inequality would be
strict, by integrating the strictly positive kernel across those sets.
Hence every nonzero real vector in this top eigenspace has constant
sign. The eigenspace is one-dimensional: two independent real vectors
would give a nonzero one orthogonal to a positive vector, which must
change sign. Since $S$ preserves this eigenspace and positivity, its
action on the positive unit vector is $+\lambda$, not $-\lambda$.
Any eigenvalue of modulus $\lambda$ would give another vector in the
top eigenspace of $S^2$. Finally compactness excludes accumulation of
nonzero eigenvalues at $\lambda$ or $-\lambda$, giving the strict gap.
\end{proof}

Apply the lemma to $S_r$. Similarity transfers algebraic simplicity
to $A_r$ and gives positive right and left eigenfunctions. In
particular $\lambda(r)=\spr(A_r)=\|S_r\|$.
Norm continuity gives continuity of $\lambda$. If $r_2>r_1>0$,
the kernel of $S_{r_2}-S_{r_1}$ is positive wherever $J=1$.
Testing it against the positive unit Perron vector at $r_1$ and
using the Rayleigh bound gives
\[
 \lambda(r_2)\geq\langle v_{r_1},S_{r_2}v_{r_1}\rangle
  >\lambda(r_1).
\]
Also $\lambda(r)\to0$ as $r\downarrow0$.
On $E=\{(a,b):0<a<1/4,\ 3/4<b<1\}$ all pairs satisfy $J=1$,
$\mu(E)=1/16$, and $w_r\geq r^2e^{r/2}$. Testing against
$\one_E/\sqrt{\mu(E)}$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{psp:op:eq:large-r}
 \lambda(r)\geq r^2e^{r/2}/16\longrightarrow\infty.
\end{equation}
This proves the existence and uniqueness in \eqref{psp:op:eq:rho}.

The simple eigenvalue is analytic locally near any positive $r$.
For completeness, choose the analytic square root of $z$ near $r$
on $I$, and $ze^{z(b-a)/2}$ on $D$. This gives a locally analytic
$S_z$. A small contour around the isolated simple eigenvalue defines
an analytic rank-one Riesz projection by its resolvent; its rank
stays one, and the restricted operator gives the analytic eigenvalue.
For real $r$, put $D_r=M_{w'_r/w_r}$. Differentiation yields
\[
 S'_r=\tfrac12(D_rS_r+S_rD_r),\qquad
 \lambda'(r)=\lambda(r)\int_X\frac{w'_r}{w_r}v_r^2\dd\mu>0.
\]
At $r=\rho$ this is $\lambda'(\rho)=d_\rho$, exactly
\eqref{psp:op:eq:derivative}. This proves a simple, transverse crossing.
\begin{writenote}
Lemma~\ref{psp:op:lem:perron} is stated here for a general operator; Part~I's Lemma~\ref{psp:egf:lem:perron} and Part~III's Lemma~\ref{psp:run:lem:perron} are the same argument for $S_t$.
\end{writenote}

\section{Meromorphic continuation and the positive pole}\label{psp:op:sec:fredholm}
We give the analytic Fredholm argument used here rather than merely
invoking its conclusion. Fix $z_0$. Compactness allows a finite-rank
$F=UV$, with $U:\CC^m\to\mathcal H$ and $V:\mathcal H\to\CC^m$,
such that $\|A_{z_0}-F\|<1$. In a small disk around $z_0$ set
$E_z=A_z-F$; there $B_z=I-E_z$ has an analytic Neumann inverse.
Then
\[
 I-A_z=B_z(I-B_z^{-1}UV).
\]
The second factor is invertible exactly when the finite-dimensional
matrix $D(z)=I-VB_z^{-1}U$ is invertible. Where it is,
\begin{equation}\label{psp:op:eq:woodbury}
 (I-A_z)^{-1}=B_z^{-1}
    +B_z^{-1}U D(z)^{-1}VB_z^{-1}.
\end{equation}
The determinant of $D$ is analytic. Either it vanishes identically in
this disk, or its zeros are isolated and \eqref{psp:op:eq:woodbury} is
meromorphic there with finite-order poles. At zero the inverse
exists because $A_0=0$. Continue along a path from zero to any
specified point using a finite chain of overlapping disks of the
kind just described. The overlap with a previously reached disk
contains invertible points outside its isolated exceptional set;
therefore the determinant in the next disk cannot vanish identically.
This excludes the first alternative throughout the connected plane.
It proves that the noninvertible set is locally discrete and the
operator inverse is meromorphic on $\CC$.

The functional $f\mapsto\int_Xw_zf\dd\mu$ is entire in operator
norm, and $\one\in\mathcal H$, so \eqref{psp:op:eq:resolvent} continues
$P$ meromorphically. At the crossing, put
\[
 h=T_\rho^{-1}v,\qquad \ell=T_\rho v,
 \qquad \int_X\ell h\dd\mu=1.
\]
These are positive right and left eigenvectors of $A_\rho$. The
rank-one spectral projection is
$f\mapsto h\int_X\ell f\dd\mu$. Locally the singular resolvent
part is this analytic projection divided by $1-\lambda(z)$.
Its scalar numerator at $\rho$ is
\begin{equation}\label{psp:op:eq:numerator}
 \left(\int_Xw_\rho h\dd\mu\right)
 \left(\int_X\ell\dd\mu\right)
 =\left(\int_X\sqrt{w_\rho}\,v\dd\mu\right)^2>0.
\end{equation}
Since
\[
 1-\lambda(z)=\rho\lambda'(\rho)(1-z/\rho)+O((z-\rho)^2),
\]
we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{psp:op:eq:pole}
 P(z)=\frac{C}{1-z/\rho}+\text{an analytic germ at }\rho,
\end{equation}
with exactly the positive amplitude \eqref{psp:op:eq:amplitude}. In
particular the scalar pole does not cancel.

\section{Strict complex domination and coefficient extraction}\label{psp:op:sec:circle}
For $|z|=r>0$ define $Q_z=JM_{|w_z|}$. On $I$, $|w_z|=r$;
on $D$,
\begin{equation}\label{psp:op:eq:strictweight}
 |w_z(a,b)|=r^2e^{\operatorname{Re}(z)(b-a)}\leq w_r(a,b).
\end{equation}
For every $f\in\mathcal H$ and every positive integer $k$,
\[
 |A_z^kf|\leq Q_z^k|f|\leq A_r^k|f|.
\]
Norms and the spectral-radius formula give
\begin{equation}\label{psp:op:eq:domination}
 \spr(A_z)\leq\spr(Q_z)\leq\lambda(r).
\end{equation}
Thus $I-A_z$ is invertible for $|z|<\rho$.

Suppose now that $|z|=\rho$ and $z\ne\rho$.
Then $\operatorname{Re}(z)<\rho$, and \eqref{psp:op:eq:strictweight} is
strict on the entire interval component $D$. The positive weight
$|w_z|$ is bounded above and away from zero. Applying the previous
similarity and squared-kernel proof to $Q_z$, let
$Q_zh_z=\beta h_z$ with $h_z>0$ and $\beta=\spr(Q_z)>0$.
Use the positive left eigenvector $\ell$ of $A_\rho$ above. All
quantities in the following identity are real:
\begin{align}
 (1-\beta)\int_X\ell h_z\dd\mu
 &=\int_X\ell(A_\rho-Q_z)h_z\dd\mu\notag\\
 &=\int_D(w_\rho-|w_z|)h_z(J\ell)\dd\mu>0.
 \label{psp:op:eq:strictdomination}
\end{align}
Indeed $A_\rho^*\ell=w_\rho J\ell=\ell$, so
$J\ell=\ell/w_\rho>0$ almost everywhere. Thus $\beta<1$, and
\eqref{psp:op:eq:domination} proves invertibility at every other point of
the circle. This establishes strictness despite the unchanged
absolute singleton weight; no unproved aperiodicity assertion is
needed.

The noninvertible set is locally discrete and has no point in
$|z|\leq\rho$ except $\rho$. Consequently there is an $R>\rho$
with no other exceptional point on or inside $|z|\leq R$.
To see the uniformity explicitly, otherwise exceptional points with
moduli decreasing to $\rho$ would have a convergent subsequence to
a point of the compact circle; that would contradict local
discreteness, including at $\rho$. Shrink $R$ if necessary so that
an open neighborhood of its closed disk has the same property.
By \eqref{psp:op:eq:pole},
\[
 G(z)=P(z)-\frac{C}{1-z/\rho}
\]
is analytic on that neighborhood. Cauchy's coefficient bound gives
$[z^n]G=O(R^{-n})$, which proves \eqref{psp:op:eq:main}.

It remains to prove \eqref{psp:op:eq:rhobounds}. Four consecutive decreasing
entries $a>b>c>d$ force the two middle entries to be consecutive
singleton ascending runs. This is forbidden by \eqref{psp:op:eq:cross}.
For a uniform random permutation, the relative orders in disjoint
positional blocks of four are independent and uniform, as follows
also from independent continuous coordinates. Hence
\begin{equation}\label{psp:op:eq:four}
 \frac{p_n}{n!}\leq(23/24)^{\floor{n/4}}.
\end{equation}
Taking $n$th roots and using \eqref{psp:op:eq:main} gives
$\rho\geq(24/23)^{1/4}>1$. Four entries, rather than three, are
essential: $14325$ is pop-stacked and contains the decreasing triple
$432$.

For the upper bound, interleave any ordering of the lower $m$ values
with any ordering of the upper $m$ values, beginning with a lower
value. The maximal ascending runs are the $m$ low--high pairs, and
all neighboring pairs satisfy \eqref{psp:op:eq:cross}. Thus
$p_{2m}\geq(m!)^2$ and
$p_{2m}/(2m)!\geq\binom{2m}{m}^{-1}$. Taking $2m$th roots gives
$\rho\leq2$. Theorem~\ref{psp:op:thm:main} is proved.

This Part does not cite them. The bracket \eqref{psp:op:eq:rhobounds}, $1.0107\ldots\le\rho\le2$, is true and contains Part~I's certified $\rho=1.11343904\ldots$; it is kept as an elementary bound.

\section{All algebraic orders and inverse thresholds}\label{psp:op:sec:inverse}
The exponentially small relative error implies, for sufficiently
large integers $n$,
\begin{equation}\label{psp:op:eq:loggamma}
 \log p_n=H(n)+\epsilon_n,\qquad
 H(x)=\log\Gamma(x+1)-x\log\rho+\log C,
 \qquad |\epsilon_n|\leq Mq^n,
\end{equation}
for some fixed $M>0$ and $q\in(0,1)$. The constants $M$, $q$, and
an onset $n_0$ exist; numerical certified values are not supplied.

\begin{corollary}[Complete fixed-order Stirling expansions]\label{psp:op:cor:stirling}
For every fixed integer $m\geq1$,
\begin{align}
 \log p_n={}&n\log n-(1+\log\rho)n+\tfrac12\log n
       +\log(C\sqrt{2\pi})\notag\\
 &+\sum_{j=1}^m\frac{B_{2j}}{2j(2j-1)n^{2j-1}}
       +O(n^{-2m-1}).\label{psp:op:eq:stirling}
\end{align}
Here $B_{2j}$ are the Bernoulli numbers. For every fixed $K\geq0$,
\begin{equation}\label{psp:op:eq:multstirling}
 p_n=C\sqrt{2\pi n}\left(\frac{n}{e\rho}\right)^n
 \left(\sum_{k=0}^K\frac{s_k}{n^k}+O(n^{-K-1})\right),
\end{equation}
where $s_0=1$ and the $s_k$ are the formal coefficients of
\begin{equation}\label{psp:op:eq:stirlingcoeff}
 \exp\left(\sum_{j\geq1}\frac{B_{2j}t^{2j-1}}{2j(2j-1)}\right)
 =1+\frac t{12}+\frac{t^2}{288}-\frac{139t^3}{51840}+\cdots.
\end{equation}
Also $p_{n+1}/p_n=((n+1)/\rho)(1+O(q^n))$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Insert the ordinary finite-order Stirling expansion of
$\log\Gamma(n+1)$ into \eqref{psp:op:eq:loggamma}; the exponential error is
smaller than every fixed algebraic order. Exponentiating a sufficiently
long finite truncation gives \eqref{psp:op:eq:multstirling}. Dividing two
successive instances of the leading equivalent gives the ratio.
These are Poincar\'e expansions at fixed order, not claims that an
infinite Bernoulli series converges.
\end{proof}

Define the first threshold by
\[
 N(y)=\min\{n\geq0:p_n\geq y\}\qquad(y>0).
\]
The sequence is nondecreasing: appending the new maximum $n+1$ extends
the last ascending run and preserves both neighboring cross
inequalities. This is injective, including from the empty permutation.
The sequence is eventually strictly increasing by the ratio formula,
and it tends to infinity.

For $L=\log y$ sufficiently large, there is a unique large real root
$x=x(y)$ of $H(x)=L$, since
\[
 H'(x)=\psi(x+1)-\log\rho\sim\log x,
 \qquad H''(x)=\psi'(x+1)>0\quad(x>-1).
\]
We refer only to this eventually increasing branch.

\begin{theorem}[Precise integer bracket]\label{psp:op:thm:bracket}
Fix constants $M,q,n_0$ for which \eqref{psp:op:eq:loggamma} holds for all
integers $n\geq n_0$. Let $x$ be the large root just defined. If
\begin{equation}\label{psp:op:eq:deltacond}
 x-2\geq n_0,\qquad H'(x-2)>0,\qquad
 \delta(x)=\frac{2Mq^{x-2}}{H'(x-2)}<1,
\end{equation}
then
\begin{equation}\label{psp:op:eq:ceilingbracket}
 \ceil{x-\delta(x)}\leq N(y)\leq\ceil{x+\delta(x)}.
\end{equation}
In particular $\delta(x)=O(q^x/\log x)$. If the two ceilings agree,
they determine the threshold; otherwise exact counts or sharper
certified information are needed.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $a=\ceil{x-\delta}-1$ and $b=\ceil{x+\delta}$.
Both belong to $[x-2,x+2]$, with $a<x-\delta$ and $b\geq x+\delta$.
Since $H'$ is increasing and positive there, the mean value theorem
gives
\[
 H(x)-H(a)>H'(x-2)\delta=2Mq^{x-2},\qquad
 H(b)-H(x)\geq2Mq^{x-2}.
\]
Each logarithmic error at $a,b$ is at most $Mq^{x-2}$ because
$q<1$. Hence $p_a<y\leq p_b$. Monotonicity gives $a<N(y)\leq b$,
which is the claim. The stated order of $\delta$ follows from
$H'(x-2)\sim\log x$.
\end{proof}

This theorem is an exact implication once its constants and
hypotheses are supplied. It is not yet a numerically executable
asymptotic certificate: this report does not give effective certified
$M,q,n_0,\rho,C$. The companion's bounded threshold search uses
actual integer counts and has a different, finite scope.

\subsection{Lambert initialization and every fixed inverse order}
Let $W$ be the positive real branch of Lambert's function,
$W(s)e^{W(s)}=s$, and put
\begin{equation}\label{psp:op:eq:xzero}
 x_0=\frac{L}{W(L/(e\rho))},\qquad
 u=\log(x_0/\rho),\qquad
 b=\tfrac12\log(2\pi x_0)+\log C.
\end{equation}
Then $x_0\log(x_0/(e\rho))=L$ exactly. This supplies an initializer,
not an exact threshold. Taylor expansion of $H$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{psp:op:eq:firstinverse}
 x=x_0-\frac{\tfrac12\log(2\pi x_0)+\log C}{\log(x_0/\rho)}
       +O\!\left(\frac1{x_0\log x_0}\right).
\end{equation}
In particular $x-x_0\to-1/2$. Taking a bare ceiling of $x_0$ is
therefore not justified even by an absolute $o(1)$ approximation.

Here is an explicit recursive specification to all fixed orders.
In formal power series in $t$, with $u,b$ treated as parameters,
define
\begin{align}
 \mathcal F(d,t;u,b)={}&ud+b
 +\sum_{k\geq2}\frac{(-1)^kd^kt^{k-1}}{k(k-1)}
 +\tfrac12\log(1+td)\notag\\
 &+\sum_{j\geq1}\frac{B_{2j}t^{2j-1}}
            {2j(2j-1)(1+td)^{2j-1}}.\label{psp:op:eq:formalF}
\end{align}
Every fixed coefficient uses only finitely many displayed terms.
Set
\begin{align}
 d_0(u,b)&=-b/u,\notag\\
 d_m(u,b)&=-\frac1u[t^m]\mathcal F
       \left(\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}d_j(u,b)t^j,t;u,b\right)
       \qquad(m\geq1).\label{psp:op:eq:inverserecurrence}
\end{align}
These are rational functions of $u,b$; for example,
\begin{align}
 d_1&=-\frac{d_0^2/2+d_0/2+1/12}{u},\notag\\
 d_2&=-\frac{(d_0+1/2)d_1-d_0^3/6-d_0^2/4-d_0/12}{u}.
 \label{psp:op:eq:firstcoeffs}
\end{align}

\begin{proposition}[All fixed orders of the continuous inverse]\label{psp:op:prop:allinverse}
For each fixed integer $m\geq0$, with the parameters of
\eqref{psp:op:eq:xzero},
\begin{equation}\label{psp:op:eq:allinverse}
 x=x_0+\sum_{j=0}^{m}\frac{d_j(u,b)}{x_0^j}
    +O\!\left(\frac{x_0^{-m-1}}{\log x_0}\right)
 \qquad(y\to\infty).
\end{equation}
The result concerns the continuous root $x$; no fixed truncation is
an exact integer rounding rule for $N(y)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Here $b=u/2+\tfrac12\log(2\pi\rho)+\log C$, so $d_0$ is bounded.
Induction in \eqref{psp:op:eq:inverserecurrence} gives $d_j=O(1/u)$ for
every fixed $j\geq1$: the new coefficient in the nonlinear terms is
a finite polynomial in previously bounded $d_j$, and division by
$u$ remains. For bounded real $d$, finite Taylor expansion of
$(x_0+d)\log((x_0+d)/(e\rho))$, of
$\frac12\log(2\pi(x_0+d))$, and of a sufficiently long finite
Stirling expansion shows that
\[
 H(x_0+d)-L
\]
has the fixed-order expansion \eqref{psp:op:eq:formalF} with $t=x_0^{-1}$.
After truncation through degree $m$ its remainder is $O(t^{m+1})$
uniformly for bounded $d$. The only unexpanded term with the large
parameter $u$ is the exact linear term $ud$.
Substitute $d=\sum_{j=0}^m d_jt^j$; the defining recurrence cancels
the coefficients through degree $m$, leaving residual
$O(x_0^{-m-1})$.

To locate the root on the same bounded neighborhood, note that
$H(x_0)-L=b+O(x_0^{-1})$ and, for example,
$H(x_0-2)-L=b-2u+O(x_0^{-1})<0$ for large $x_0$,
whereas $H(x_0)-L>0$. Thus $x\in(x_0-2,x_0)$.
The proposed approximation also lies in a fixed bounded neighborhood
of $x_0$. On the interval between the two,
$H'\asymp\log x_0$. The mean value theorem converts the residual into
\eqref{psp:op:eq:allinverse}. For $m=0$ it gives \eqref{psp:op:eq:firstinverse}.
\end{proof}

The integer inverse has $N(y)=x+O(1)$ and the stronger ceiling bracket
\eqref{psp:op:eq:ceilingbracket}; an ordinary algebraic expansion cannot
remove rounding at thresholds arbitrarily close to integers.
Neither differentiating an unknown remainder nor silently rounding
an approximation is used here.
\begin{writenote}
Corollary~\ref{psp:op:cor:stirling} and Theorem~\ref{psp:op:thm:bracket} are Part~I's \eqref{psp:egf:eq:stirling} and Proposition~\ref{psp:egf:prop:ceil}, with the same proof; \eqref{psp:op:eq:firstinverse} is Part~I's \eqref{psp:egf:eq:inverseinit}, because this Part's $u$ is Part~I's $u$ plus one. Proposition~\ref{psp:op:prop:allinverse} goes beyond Part~I. The front matter (``The inverses and the transseries volume'') shows that the recursion \eqref{psp:op:eq:inverserecurrence} is an instance of the transseries volume's reversion about a core, and classifies the other inverse statements.
\end{writenote}

The prior endpoint recurrence~\cite{cgp} can be stated as follows.
Let $f_n(c,d)$ count admissible permutations whose last ascending
run has minimum $c$ and maximum $d$, and define the rectangular sum
\[
 F_n(a,b)=\sum_{1\leq c\leq a}\sum_{b\leq d\leq n}f_n(c,d),
\]
with out-of-range endpoints giving the natural empty sum. For $n=1$,
$f_1(1,1)=1$. For $n\geq2$,
\begin{align}
 f_n(c,c)&=F_{n-1}(c-1,c),\label{psp:op:eq:recursingle}\\
 f_n(c,d)&=\one\{c=1,d=n\}
 +\sum_{\ell=0}^{d-c-1}\binom{d-c-1}{\ell}
       F_{n-\ell-2}(d-\ell-2,c)\quad(c<d),\label{psp:op:eq:recurmulti}
\end{align}
where terms with $n-\ell-2<1$ in the sum are zero. To justify the
indices, delete the final run, whose $\ell$ interior values are
chosen from the $d-c-1$ values between $c,d$. After relabelling,
the preceding run minimum must be at most $d-\ell-2$ and its maximum
at least $c$. These are precisely the two overlap constraints.
The isolated all-increasing permutation supplies the first term.
For a singleton final run the preceding minimum is at most $c-1$
and maximum at least $c$ after relabelling. Rectangle sums are formed
by two-dimensional prefix/suffix accumulation, and $p_n=F_n(n,1)$.

\section{Counting model and main results}\label{psp:run:sec:main}
A pop-stack pass reverses each maximal decreasing run and concatenates
the resulting increasing blocks. Its distinct output permutations are
called \emph{pop-stacked}. Let $p_{n,k}$ count those of size $n$ with $k$
maximal ascending runs. Put
\[
 p_n(u)=\sum_{k\geq0}p_{n,k}u^k,\qquad
 P(z,u)=\sum_{n\geq0}p_n(u)\frac{z^n}{n!},\qquad p_0(u)=1.
\]
Thus the empty permutation has zero runs. For $n\geq1$, let $K_n$ be the
run count of a permutation chosen uniformly from the $p_n(1)$ distinct
outputs. Then $\EE u^{K_n}=p_n(u)/p_n(1)$. Different input permutations
can produce the same output; weighting by their preimage sizes gives a
different distribution and is not considered here.

Define
\begin{align}
 Q&=4u+4u^2(e^z-1)-1,& E&=e^{z/2},\notag\\
 B&=2uE+2u-1,& h_0&=1+u(e^z-1),\label{psp:run:eq:definitions}\\
 H(z,u)&=\sum_{j\geq0}\frac{(-1)^jz^{2j}Q^j}{16^j(2j)!},&
 J_0(z,u)&=\frac z4\sum_{j\geq0}\frac{(-1)^jz^{2j}Q^j}{16^j(2j+1)!}.
 \label{psp:run:eq:entireHJ}
\end{align}
The last two series converge uniformly on compact subsets of $\CC^2$.
\begin{theorem}[Bivariate exponential generating function]\label{psp:run:thm:egf}
As a germ at $z=0$, and by single-valued meromorphic continuation,
\begin{equation}\label{psp:run:eq:branchfree}
 \boxed{P(z,u)=\frac{Q(H+BJ_0)}{h_0(BH-QJ_0)}}.
\end{equation}
Removable quotient presentations are filled in wherever the counting
germ is analytic. Locally near $(z,u)=(0,1)$ choose $r=\sqrt Q$ with
$r(0,1)=\sqrt3$ and put $\theta=zr/4$. Then equivalently
\begin{equation}\label{psp:run:eq:trig}
 P(z,u)=\frac r{h_0}
 \frac{r\cos\theta+B\sin\theta}{B\cos\theta-r\sin\theta}.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
For instance, $P(0,u)=1$ and $[u]P=e^z-1$. At exceptional parameters such
as $u=1/4$, the displayed numerator and denominator can both vanish at
$z=0$; the counting series still supplies the analytic value. We do not
assert that every zero of the written denominator is a pole.
\begin{writenote}
At $u=1$ the definitions give $Q=4e^z-1$, $B=2e^{z/2}+1$ and $h_0=e^z$, so \eqref{psp:run:eq:branchfree} is Part~I's \eqref{psp:egf:eq:entire}, and \eqref{psp:run:eq:trig} is Part~I's \eqref{psp:egf:eq:trig}: a second derivation of Part~I's formula. At $u=1$ Part~I answers the question left open in the last sentence: the genuine poles of $P(z,1)$ are exactly the zeros of $D(z,1)$ with $Q\ne0$ (Part~I, \eqref{psp:egf:eq:poleset}).
\end{writenote}

Let $\rho$ be the unique positive solution of
\begin{equation}\label{psp:run:eq:rho}
 \frac{\rho\sqrt{4e^\rho-1}}4+
 \arctan\frac{\sqrt{4e^\rho-1}}{2e^{\rho/2}+1}=\frac\pi2,
 \qquad \rho=1.113439041736727\ldots.
\end{equation}
The arctangent here is the positive real branch. Section~\ref{psp:run:sec:dominance}
proves that $\rho$ is the unique dominant univariate pole. Write
$x=\rho$, $T=e^\rho$. Define
\begin{align}
 \mu&=\frac{2Tx-x-1}{Tx(x+2)},\label{psp:run:eq:mu}\\
 v&=\frac{4T^2x^3-3Tx^4-10Tx^3-2Tx^2+4Tx
            +x^4+4x^3+5x^2+4x+2}{T^2x^2(x+2)^3}.
 \label{psp:run:eq:v}
\end{align}
\begin{theorem}[Moments and Gaussian laws]\label{psp:run:thm:gaussian}
There are real constants $\beta,\gamma$ and $0<\eta<1$ such that
\begin{align}
 \EE K_n&=\mu n+\beta+O(\eta^n),&
 \Var K_n&=vn+\gamma+O(\eta^n).\label{psp:run:eq:moments}
\end{align}
The constants $\beta,\gamma$ are defined analytically in
\eqref{psp:run:eq:betagamma}. The variance slope $v$ is strictly positive, and
\begin{equation}\label{psp:run:eq:clt}
 \frac{K_n-\mu n}{\sqrt{vn}}\ \Longrightarrow\ \mathcal N(0,1).
\end{equation}
Moreover, uniformly over every integer $k$,
\begin{equation}\label{psp:run:eq:llt}
 \sup_{k\in\mathbb Z}\left|
 \PP(K_n=k)-\frac{\exp(-(k-\mu n)^2/(2vn))}{\sqrt{2\pi vn}}
 \right|=O(n^{-1}).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
The error in \eqref{psp:run:eq:llt} is absolute, not a uniform relative error in
the tails. Its constants and onset are not numerically certified.
Using the exact mean and variance also yields \eqref{psp:run:eq:clt}; replacing
$\mu n$ by $\mu n+\beta$ in \eqref{psp:run:eq:llt} preserves its stated accuracy.

\section{Run weights and factorial normalization}\label{psp:run:sec:model}
The established overlapping-run characterization~\cite{abbhl,abh} says
that consecutive maximal ascending runs with endpoints $(a,b)$ and
$(c,d)$ satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{psp:run:eq:overlap}
 a<d,\qquad c<b.
\end{equation}
The second inequality is the descent between runs; the first is the
extra image condition. A singleton has equal endpoints, so it can occur
strictly inside the range of a neighboring nonsingleton run, but two
singletons cannot be adjacent.

Use the disjoint union
\[
 I=(0,1),\quad \Delta=\{(a,b):0<a<b<1\},\quad
 X=I\sqcup\Delta,\quad \nu=\dd x\sqcup\dd a\dd b.
\]
A state in $I$ has $\lo(x)=\hi(x)=x$; an interval state has $\lo=a,\hi=b$.
Define the symmetric overlap kernel and weights
\begin{align}
 K(s,t)&=\one\{\lo(s)<\hi(t),\ \lo(t)<\hi(s)\},\notag\\
 w_z(x)&=z,& w_z(a,b)&=z^2e^{z(b-a)},\label{psp:run:eq:weight}\\
 (A_zf)(s)&=\int_X K(s,t)w_z(t)f(t)\dd\nu(t).\label{psp:run:eq:Az}
\end{align}
On $L^2(X,\nu)$, $A_z$ is compact and entire in operator norm: its
bounded kernel is square integrable, and the weights and their series
are uniformly bounded on compact $z$-sets. Also $\|A_z\|=O(|z|)$ near zero.

For a run of length $\ell\geq2$ with endpoints $a<b$, the ordered
interior coordinates occupy volume $(b-a)^{\ell-2}/(\ell-2)!$.
Summing $z^\ell$ times this volume gives $w_z(a,b)$. Every rank order of
$n$ distinct coordinates has volume $1/n!$. Thus the product of weights
and kernels counts precisely the labeled output permutations with EGF
normalization. No run-order factorial is needed. The check
\[
 \int_Xw_z\dd\nu=z+z^2\int_0^1(1-t)e^{zt}\dd t=e^z-1
\]
recovers the unique one-run permutation in each positive size.

Multiplying every run weight by $u$ marks the number of runs exactly
once. Summing the chain of run states gives
\begin{equation}\label{psp:run:eq:resolvent}
 h=(I-uA_z)^{-1}\one,\qquad
 P(z,u)=1+u\int_Xw_z(s)h(s)\dd\nu(s).
\end{equation}
The pairing in this formula is bilinear. For bounded $u$ and sufficiently
small $z$, the Neumann series converges uniformly, proving joint
analyticity and the counting identity. Each $p_n(u)$ has degree at most
$n$. Integral-operator enumeration has classical precedents, for example
\cite{ekp}; the particular state space and calculations are given here.
\begin{writenote}
This is the model of Part~I, Section~\ref{psp:egf:sec:model}, and Part~II, Section~\ref{psp:op:sec:integral}, with every run weight multiplied by $u$.
\end{writenote}

\section{Endpoint equations and a sufficient boundary problem}\label{psp:run:sec:bvp}
For small $z$ set
\begin{align*}
 U(x)&=u\int_{\hi(s)\leq x}w_z(s)h(s)\dd\nu(s),&
 V(x)&=u\int_{\lo(s)<x}w_z(s)h(s)\dd\nu(s),\\
 T_0&=P-1=U(1)=V(1),& U(0)&=V(0)=0.
\end{align*}
Boundary equality sets have measure zero. Complementing the two
nonoverlap regions yields
\begin{equation}\label{psp:run:eq:hUV}
 h(x)=1+V(x)-U(x),\qquad h(a,b)=1+V(b)-U(a).
\end{equation}
These identities provide continuous representatives and then the
smoothness needed for differentiation. Put
\[
 I_0(x)=\int_0^xe^{z(x-a)}U(a)\dd a,\qquad
 J_1(x)=\int_x^1e^{z(b-x)}V(b)\dd b.
\]
Differentiating the endpoint cumulatives gives
\begin{align}
 U'&=uze^{zx}(1+V)-uzU-uz^2I_0,& I_0'&=U+zI_0,\label{psp:run:eq:Ufirst}\\
 V'&=uzV+uze^{z(1-x)}(1-U)+uz^2J_1,& J_1'&=-V-zJ_1.
 \label{psp:run:eq:Vfirst}
\end{align}
For example, the interval part of $U'$ is
$uz^2\int_0^xe^{z(x-a)}(1+V(x)-U(a))\dd a$; combining its constant term
with the singleton contribution gives \eqref{psp:run:eq:Ufirst}. Differentiating
once more cancels the integrals. With $p=U'$, $q=V'$,
\begin{align}
 p'&=(1-u)zp+uze^{zx}q,& p(0)&=uz,\notag\\
 q'&=(u-1)zq-uze^{z(1-x)}p,& q(1)&=uz.\label{psp:run:eq:system}
\end{align}
The last boundary condition uses $U(1)=V(1)$ and is essential.
Eliminating $q$, locally near $u=1$, gives the Robin problem
\begin{align}
 p''-zp'+z^2\kappa p&=0,& \kappa&=u+u^2(e^z-1),\notag\\
 p(0)&=uz,&p'(1)-(1-u)zp(1)&=u^2z^2e^z.\label{psp:run:eq:Robin}
\end{align}
At $z=0$, the homogeneous two-point first-order system has only the
zero solution. Analytic dependence of its fundamental matrix gives
local existence and uniqueness, uniformly near $(z,u)=(0,1)$.

\begin{lemma}[Sufficiency]\label{psp:run:lem:sufficiency}
The local solution of \eqref{psp:run:eq:system} reconstructs the counting
resolvent \eqref{psp:run:eq:resolvent}; differentiation has lost no condition.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Reflection sends $(p(x),q(x))$ to $(q(1-x),p(1-x))$ with the same
system and boundary data. Uniqueness therefore gives $p(x)=q(1-x)$.
Integrating $p,q$ from zero yields $U,V$ with $U(1)=V(1)$.
Define $I_0,J_1$ as above and the residuals
\begin{align*}
 R_1&=p-uze^{zx}(1+V)+uzU+uz^2I_0,\\
 R_2&=q-uzV-uze^{z(1-x)}(1-U)-uz^2J_1.
\end{align*}
Direct use of \eqref{psp:run:eq:system} gives $R_1'=zR_1$, $R_2'=-zR_2$,
with $R_1(0)=0$ and $R_2(1)=uz(U(1)-V(1))=0$. Thus both vanish.
The endpoint cumulatives of the $h$ reconstructed from \eqref{psp:run:eq:hUV}
have derivatives $U',V'$ and zero initial values; they equal $U,V$.
The overlap complement then gives $h=1+uA_zh$. Local Neumann uniqueness
identifies this $h$ with \eqref{psp:run:eq:resolvent}.
\end{proof}
Apparent divisions by $z$ in the scalar elimination are used for nonzero
$z$ first and extended analytically to zero.

\section{Explicit solution and half angle cancellation}\label{psp:run:sec:cancel}
Write $s=2u-1$, $b=2ue^{z/2}$, $r=\sqrt Q$, and $\theta=zr/4$.
Then $Q=b^2-s^2$ and $B=b+s$. Solving \eqref{psp:run:eq:Robin} gives
\begin{align}
 p(x)&=e^{zx/2}\{uz\cos(zrx/2)+A\sin(zrx/2)\},\notag\\
 a:=\frac A{uz}&=
 \frac{b-s\cos(2\theta)+r\sin(2\theta)}
 {r\cos(2\theta)+s\sin(2\theta)}.\label{psp:run:eq:asolution}
\end{align}
For $c=\cos\theta$, $d=\sin\theta$ define
\[
 D_+=(b+s)c-rd,\qquad D_-=(b-s)c+rd,\qquad L_0=rc+(b+s)d.
\]
Direct multiplication, using $r^2=b^2-s^2$, proves
\begin{align}
 D_+D_-&=r\{r\cos(2\theta)+s\sin(2\theta)\},\notag\\
 D_-L_0&=r\{b-s\cos(2\theta)+r\sin(2\theta)\}.\label{psp:run:eq:cancel}
\end{align}
At $z=0$, $D_-=1$. The common factor cancels in the germ and $a=L_0/D_+$.
Zeros of the canceled factor alone are not scalar poles.

Reflection and the first equation of \eqref{psp:run:eq:system} at zero give
$p(1)=q(0)=z(s+ar)/2$. Integrating \eqref{psp:run:eq:Robin}, with both endpoint
derivatives retained, gives
\[
 z^2\kappa T_0=2uzp(1)-u^2z^2(e^z+1),\qquad
 P=1+T_0=\frac{ura}{\kappa}=\frac{ra}{h_0}.
\]
This proves \eqref{psp:run:eq:trig} including its prefactor. The local identities
$H=\cos\theta$, $J_0=\sin\theta/r$ prove \eqref{psp:run:eq:branchfree}.
The entire definitions in \eqref{psp:run:eq:entireHJ} give a single-valued
meromorphic continuation. The proof does not choose a global square
root or classify the global bivariate polar set.

\section{Isolation of the dominant univariate pole}\label{psp:run:sec:dominance}
We give the spectral isolation needed for a complex parameter transfer,
not only a real-root calculation. Let $\mathcal K$ have kernel $K$.
For real $t>0$, $A_t$ is similar to the compact self-adjoint operator
\[
 S_t=M_{\sqrt{w_t}}\mathcal K M_{\sqrt{w_t}}.
\]
The multiplier and its inverse are bounded. The operator preserves
positivity but need not be positive semidefinite. Every pair of states
$s,s'$ is overlapped by all intervals satisfying
\[
 0<a<\min(\lo(s),\lo(s')),
 \qquad \max(\hi(s),\hi(s'))<b<1.
\]
This is a positive-area set; hence $S_t^2$ has a strictly positive kernel
almost everywhere.

\begin{lemma}\label{psp:run:lem:perron}
The spectral radius $\lambda(t)$ of $A_t$ is a simple positive eigenvalue
with positive left and right eigenfunctions. All its other eigenvalues
have strictly smaller modulus.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The compact self-adjoint spectral theorem gives a top eigenvector of
$S_t^2$. Its absolute value cannot lower its Rayleigh quotient and is
therefore also a top eigenvector. Strict kernel positivity makes that
vector positive almost everywhere. A sign-changing top vector would
strictly increase its quotient upon taking absolute values, which is
impossible. Two independent real top vectors would have a nonzero linear
combination orthogonal to the positive one and thus changing sign.
The top eigenspace is consequently one-dimensional. Positivity of $S_t$
selects the eigenvalue $+\|S_t\|$ on it. Any other eigenvalue of the same
modulus would give another top vector of $S_t^2$. Similarity transfers
the conclusion to $A_t$, and compactness gives the gap.
\end{proof}
Continuity and strict increase of the weights give continuity and strict
increase of $\lambda(t)$, by testing the Rayleigh quotient on its positive
Perron vector. It tends to zero as $t\downarrow0$. On
$F=\{(a,b):a<1/4,\ b>3/4\}$, every pair overlaps and
$w_t\geq t^2e^{t/2}$; the indicator Rayleigh quotient gives
$\lambda(t)\geq t^2e^{t/2}/16\to\infty$.
There is a unique $t_*>0$ with $\lambda(t_*)=1$.

A Riesz projection at this isolated simple eigenvalue gives local
analytic dependence, as in standard perturbation theory~\cite{kato}.
For the real unit Perron eigenvector $f_t$ of $S_t$,
\begin{equation}\label{psp:run:eq:lambda}
 \lambda'(t)=\lambda(t)\int_X\frac{w_t'}{w_t}f_t^2\dd\nu>0.
\end{equation}
Indeed differentiating $S_t$ gives
$S_t'=\tfrac12(M_{w_t'/w_t}S_t+S_tM_{w_t'/w_t})$, and the simple-eigenvalue
derivative formula proves \eqref{psp:run:eq:lambda}. The left and right
$A_{t_*}$ eigenvectors are $\ell_*=\sqrt{w_{t_*}}f_{t_*}$ and
$h_*=f_{t_*}/\sqrt{w_{t_*}}$, paired to one. The scalar resolvent's
singular numerator is
\[
 \left(\int_X\sqrt{w_{t_*}}f_{t_*}\dd\nu\right)^2>0.
\]
Thus $t_*$ is a genuine simple pole of $P(z,1)$.
For $|z|<t_*$, kernel comparison and the spectral-radius formula give
$\spr(A_z)\leq\lambda(|z|)<1$, so there are no poles inside.

At $u=1$, \eqref{psp:run:eq:trig} becomes $P(z,1)=e^{-z}r\tan\phi(z)$, where
\[
 \phi(z)=\frac{zr}4+\arctan\frac r{2e^{z/2}+1},\qquad
 \phi'(z)=\frac{e^z(z+2)}{2r}>0\quad(z\geq0).
\]
Since $\phi(0)=\pi/6$ and $\phi(z)\to\infty$, its first positive pole is
the $\rho$ in \eqref{psp:run:eq:rho}. The preceding paragraph identifies $t_*=\rho$.

For $|z|=\rho$ with $z\ne\rho$, let
$B_z^{\rm abs}=\mathcal K M_{|w_z|}$. The same positive-kernel argument
gives a positive Perron vector $h_z$ with eigenvalue
$b_z=\spr(B_z^{\rm abs})$. On the interval component,
\[
 |w_z(a,b)|=\rho^2e^{\operatorname{Re}(z)(b-a)}<w_\rho(a,b).
\]
The singleton absolute weights are equal. Pairing with $\ell_*>0$ gives
\[
 (1-b_z)\int_X\ell_*h_z\dd\nu
 =\int_\Delta(w_\rho-|w_z|)h_z(\mathcal K\ell_*)\dd\nu>0,
\]
since $\mathcal K\ell_*=\ell_*/w_\rho>0$ almost everywhere. Therefore
$\spr(A_z)\leq b_z<1$ at every other point of the circle. The scalar
resolvent is analytic there. Meromorphic discreteness of the explicit
quotient now supplies a radius larger than $\rho$ containing no other
scalar pole.
\begin{writenote}
The same isolation as Part~I, Section~\ref{psp:egf:sec:dominance}, and Part~II, Sections~\ref{psp:op:sec:perron}--\ref{psp:op:sec:circle}; \eqref{psp:run:eq:rho} is the case $k=0$ of Part~I's \eqref{psp:egf:eq:realpoles} (Part~I writes the phase with $\frac12\arccos(1/(2e^{z/2}))$, equal to the arctangent here for $z\ge0$).
\end{writenote}

\section{Complex uniform transfer with a moving pole}\label{psp:run:sec:transfer}
The rational certificate in Section~\ref{psp:run:sec:certificate} implies
$\rho<\log4$. Choose $R$ with $\rho<R<\log4$ sufficiently close to
$\rho$. At $u=1$, $Q=4e^z-1$ and $h_0=e^z$ do not vanish on
$|z|\leq R$. Put
\[
 D(z,u)=BH-QJ_0.
\]
No zero of $D(z,1)$ in this disk cancels against the numerator: on a
local square-root branch the two linear combinations in
\eqref{psp:run:eq:trig} have determinant
\[
 B^2+r^2=4e^{z/2}(2e^{z/2}+1)\ne0.
\]
Here $B=0$ would imply $Q=0$. Simultaneous vanishing would force sine
and cosine to vanish together. The spectral result therefore implies
that $D(z,1)$ has exactly the one simple zero $\rho$ in the disk, and
none on its boundary.

For complex $u$ sufficiently near one, compactness keeps $Q$ and $h_0$
nonzero throughout the closed disk. Rouch\'e's theorem preserves exactly
one zero of $D$, counting multiplicity. The implicit function theorem
gives its holomorphic position $\rho(u)$, with $\rho(1)=\rho$. Its
numerator and $z$ derivative remain nonzero. Analytic division at the
moving simple zero consequently gives
\begin{equation}\label{psp:run:eq:decomposition}
 P(z,u)=\frac{C(u)}{1-z/\rho(u)}+G(z,u),
\end{equation}
where $C$ is holomorphic and nonzero, and $G$ is jointly holomorphic on
a neighborhood of the closed $z$-disk times a smaller $u$-disk.
Cauchy's estimate on the fixed circle proves
\begin{equation}\label{psp:run:eq:uniform}
 \frac{p_n(u)}{n!}=C(u)\rho(u)^{-n}+O(R^{-n}),
 \quad\text{uniformly for complex $u$ near 1}.
\end{equation}
The complex neighborhood and uniform error are important: pointwise
real-parameter estimates alone would not justify moment differentiation.
This is the usual singularity-perturbation mechanism underlying
quasi-powers~\cite[Chapter IX]{fs}; the needed uniform argument is supplied
here rather than assumed from a real-variable version.

For explicit local formulas choose the analytic phase near $(\rho,1)$
\[
 \Phi(z,u)=\frac{zr}4+\arctan\frac rB.
\]
Choose its branch continuously from \eqref{psp:run:eq:rho}. Then
$P=(r/h_0)\tan\Phi$, $\Phi(\rho(u),u)=\pi/2$, and
\begin{align}
 \Phi_z&=\frac{u^2e^z(z+2)-(u-1)(2u-1)}{2r},\notag\\
 \Phi_u&=\frac{z\{2u(e^z-1)+1\}-1/u}{2r}.\label{psp:run:eq:phiderivatives}
\end{align}
In particular $\Phi_z(\rho,1)=e^\rho(\rho+2)/(2r)>0$.
Writing
$N(z,u)=u^2e^z(z+2)-(u-1)(2u-1)$, the residue gives
\begin{equation}\label{psp:run:eq:C}
 C(u)=\frac{2Q(\rho(u),u)}
 {\rho(u)h_0(\rho(u),u)N(\rho(u),u)}.
\end{equation}
These formulas use a local phase only; no inverse trigonometric branch
has been selected globally.

\section{Exact moments and the variance slope}\label{psp:run:sec:moments}
Define, for complex $t$ near zero,
\begin{equation}\label{psp:run:eq:HG}
 H_c(t)=\log\frac\rho{\rho(e^t)},\qquad
 G_c(t)=\log\frac{C(e^t)}{C(1)},
\end{equation}
with logs zero at $t=0$. Shrink the disk so that $|\rho(e^t)|/R$ is

\begin{equation}\label{psp:run:eq:quasi}
 \EE e^{tK_n}=\exp\{nH_c(t)+G_c(t)\}(1+O(\eta^n))
\end{equation}

\section{Central and uniform lattice local limits}\label{psp:run:sec:limits}
For fixed real $y$, substitute $t=iy/\sqrt{vn}$ in \eqref{psp:run:eq:quasi}.
Taylor expansion gives
\[
 n\{H_c(t)-\mu t\}=-y^2/2+O_y(n^{-1/2}),\qquad
 G_c(t)=O_y(n^{-1/2}).
\]
The centered characteristic functions therefore converge to
$e^{-y^2/2}$. The characteristic-function continuity theorem proves
\eqref{psp:run:eq:clt}. The moment estimates show that exact mean and variance
standardization gives the same limit.

The local limit also needs uniform control away from zero frequency.
For a fixed $0<\delta<\pi$, put
$U_\delta=\{e^{i\theta}:\delta\leq|\theta|\leq\pi\}$.
For $u\in U_\delta$ and $|z|<\rho$, the operator $I-uA_z$ is invertible
because $\spr(uA_z)<1$. The strict comparison in
Section~\ref{psp:run:sec:dominance} proves the same for $|z|=\rho$, $z\ne\rho$.
At $z=\rho$, the only eigenvalue of $A_\rho$ on the unit circle is its
simple eigenvalue $1$, whereas $1/u\ne1$. Hence $I-uA_\rho$ is invertible
as well.

Invertibility is open in operator norm and the inverse is continuous.
Compactness of $\{|z|\leq\rho\}\times U_\delta$ therefore supplies
$R_\delta>\rho$ such that $I-uA_z$ is invertible for every
$|z|\leq R_\delta$, $u\in U_\delta$. Explicitly, otherwise a sequence
of noninvertible pairs approaching that compact set would have a
subsequence converging to an invertible point, contradicting openness.
After decreasing $R_\delta$ slightly if needed, the boundary resolvents
are uniformly bounded. Cauchy estimates in \eqref{psp:run:eq:resolvent} and the
positive leading coefficient asymptotic from \eqref{psp:run:eq:uniform} give
\begin{equation}\label{psp:run:eq:away}
 \sup_{\delta\leq|\theta|\leq\pi}
 |\EE e^{i\theta K_n}|=O((\rho/R_\delta)^n).
\end{equation}
This proves the needed lattice aperiodicity from the operator rather
than guessing it from finite coefficient rows.

Near zero set $h(\theta)=H_c(i\theta)-i\mu\theta$. Since $v>0$,
\[
 h(\theta)=-v\theta^2/2+O(|\theta|^3),\qquad
 \operatorname{Re}h(\theta)\leq-c\theta^2
\]
for sufficiently small fixed $\delta>0$. Also
$e^{G_c(i\theta)}=1+O(|\theta|)$. The identity
\[
 e^A-e^B=(A-B)\int_0^1e^{tA+(1-t)B}\dd t
\]
with $A=nh(\theta)$, $B=-vn\theta^2/2$ gives, uniformly for
$|\theta|\leq\delta$,
\begin{align}
 \left|e^{-i\mu n\theta}\EE e^{i\theta K_n}
                    -e^{-vn\theta^2/2}\right|
 &\leq C(|\theta|+n|\theta|^3)e^{-c'n\theta^2}+C\eta^n.
 \label{psp:run:eq:near}
\end{align}
All constants here are fixed. Fourier inversion on $[-\pi,\pi]$,
together with the whole-line Fourier integral of the Gaussian, now
proves \eqref{psp:run:eq:llt}. Indeed,
\[
 \int_{-\delta}^{\delta}|\theta|e^{-c'n\theta^2}\dd\theta=O(n^{-1}),
 \quad
 n\int_{-\delta}^{\delta}|\theta|^3e^{-c'n\theta^2}\dd\theta=O(n^{-1}).
\]
The omitted Gaussian tail and \eqref{psp:run:eq:away} are exponentially small.
The remaining oscillatory factor has modulus one for every integer
$k$, so the absolute error is uniform over all $\mathbb Z$.
This is a direct proof of the claimed rate, with no appeal to a local
limit theorem whose hypotheses have not been checked.

\begin{theorem}[Fixed-run representation]\label{psp:run:thm:fixed}
For every $k\geq1$,
\begin{equation}\label{psp:run:eq:fixeddenominator}
 F_k(x)=\frac{N_k(x)}{D_k(x)},\qquad
 D_k(x)=\prod_{j=1}^k(1-jx)^{k-j+1},\qquad N_k\in\mathbb Z[x],
\end{equation}
with $\deg N_k=k(k+1)/2$ and leading coefficient
\[
 (-1)^{k(k+1)/2+1}\prod_{j=1}^k j!.
\]
No reduced or minimal denominator assertion is included.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Temporarily put $q=e^z$, $s=2u-1$, $L_1=1+u(q-1)$ and
\[
 \delta=\sqrt{(1-2u)^2-4u^2q},\qquad \delta|_{u=0}=1.
\]
Using $r=i\delta$ in the full-angle form \eqref{psp:run:eq:asolution} gives
\[
 P=\frac\delta{L_1}
 \frac{2ue^{z/2}-s\cosh(z\delta/2)-\delta\sinh(z\delta/2)}
      {\delta\cosh(z\delta/2)+s\sinh(z\delta/2)}.
\]
Multiply numerator and denominator by $2e^{z\delta/2}$ and divide by
$\delta-s$ to obtain
\begin{align}
 P&=\frac\delta{L_1}\frac{1-A+T_1}{1+A},\notag\\
 A&=\frac{\delta+s}{\delta-s}\,q e^{z(\delta-1)},&
 T_1&=\frac{4u}{\delta-s}\,q e^{z(\delta-1)/2}.\label{psp:run:eq:hyperbolic}
\end{align}
This identity applies to the $u=0$ counting germ. To justify moving
from the derivation near $u=1$, the Neumann model is jointly analytic
for small $z$ and bounded $u$. The branch-free quotient is meromorphically
identical there by the identity theorem, so it continues to $u=0$.
Equivalently the full-angle expression is invariant under $r\mapsto-r$.
At $u=0$, $\delta-s=2$ and $L_1=1$, so \eqref{psp:run:eq:hyperbolic} is analytic
as a $u$-series; apparent intermediate square-root presentations cause
no change in the counting branch.

Now regard $q,z$ as independent formal variables. Let $\mathcal R$ be
the ring of formal $u$-series whose $u^k$ coefficient is in $\QQ[q,z]$
and has total degree at most $k$. A member with nonzero rational
constant coefficient has its inverse in $\mathcal R$, by the coefficient
recurrence. If $F\in\mathcal R$ has zero $u$-constant term, then
$\exp F\in\mathcal R$ as well, because each coefficient is a finite sum
of products and total degrees add.

The expansion
\[
 \delta=(1-2u)\sum_{m\geq0}\binom{1/2}{m}
           (-4u^2q)^m(1-2u)^{-2m}
\]
shows that its $u^k$ coefficient has $q$-degree at most $\lfloor k/2\rfloor$,
apart from the explicit terms $1-2u$. In particular for $k\geq1$,
$[u^k](\delta-1)$ has $q$-degree at most $k-1$. Therefore
$z(\delta-1)\in\mathcal R$, with zero $u$-constant term. Both
exponentials in \eqref{psp:run:eq:hyperbolic} belong to $\mathcal R$.
Also $\delta$, $1/L_1$ and $1/(\delta-s)$ belong to $\mathcal R$.
The series $\delta+s$ starts at $u^2$, and its $u^k$ coefficient has
$q$-degree at most $k-1$. Thus both $q(\delta+s)/(\delta-s)$ and
$4uq/(\delta-s)$ belong to $\mathcal R$. So do $A,T_1,(1+A)^{-1}$ and $P$.
It follows that
\[
 [u^k]P(z,u)=\sum_{j=0}^k P_{k,j}(z)e^{jz},\qquad
 \deg P_{k,j}\leq k-j.
\]
Here $q=e^z$ is substituted only after polynomial coefficient extraction.
At formal $q=0$, $A=T_1=0$ and $\delta=1-2u$, so
\[
 P|_{q=0}=\frac{1-2u}{1-u}=1-\sum_{k\geq1}u^k.
\]
Consequently, for every $k\geq1$,
\begin{equation}\label{psp:run:eq:expopoly}
 [u^k]P(z,u)=-1+\sum_{j=1}^kP_{k,j}(z)e^{jz},\qquad
 \deg P_{k,j}\leq k-j.
\end{equation}
The constant $-1$ is consistent with $[u^k]P(0,u)=0$, because the other
terms cancel it at zero.

For $j\geq1$ and $\ell\geq0$,
\[
 n![z^n](z^\ell e^{jz})=(n)_\ell j^{n-\ell},\qquad
 \sum_{n\geq0}(n)_\ell j^{n-\ell}x^n
      =\frac{\ell!x^\ell}{(1-jx)^{\ell+1}}.
\]
The summand is zero for $n<\ell$. Since $\ell\leq k-j$, each denominator
divides $D_k$. Each rational summand is proper, so
$F_k(x)=-1+\text{proper rational function}$. Multiplying by $D_k$,
all terms other than $-D_k$ have degree below
$\deg D_k=k(k+1)/2$. The leading coefficient of $-D_k$ is the stated
sign times $\prod_{j=1}^k j^{k-j+1}=\prod_{j=1}^k j!$.
Finally $D_kF_k$ has integer formal coefficients and is already known
to terminate, so $N_k\in\mathbb Z[x]$.
\end{proof}
This proof does not determine which individual poles survive
cancellation or the minimal denominator. It also supplies no new
complexity bound for computing the full refined triangle.
