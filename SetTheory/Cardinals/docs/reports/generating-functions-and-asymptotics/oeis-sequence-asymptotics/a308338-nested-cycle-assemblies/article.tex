\begin{abstract}
Let $P(z)=\prod_{m\geq1}(1+z^m/m)$ and
$F(z,u)=\exp\{u(P(z)-1)\}$. The labeled count
$a_n=n![z^n]F(z,1)$ is OEIS A308338, and the coefficient of $u^k$ counts
objects with $k$ nonempty nested-cycle components, giving A392471.
With $c=e^{-\gamma}$, we prove
\[
 a_n\sim \frac{e^{c(1/2-\log2)-1}c^{1/4-c/2}}{2\sqrt\pi}
             n!n^{c/2-3/4}e^{2\sqrt{cn}}.
\]
More generally, every fixed order has a complex-uniform expansion in
$n^{-1/2}$ with polynomial coefficients in $\log n$; we give the first
two corrections explicitly. A whole-circle estimate controls all
secondary arcs, and a Gaussian Taylor lemma gives the stated sharp
logarithmic remainder without crossing a natural boundary.

For the component count, holomorphic normalization justifies
differentiation and gives explicit logarithmic and constant corrections
to the mean and variance. In particular the mean divided by $\sqrt n$
tends to $e^{-\gamma/2}$. We recover the established Gaussian regime
with variance equivalent $\sqrt{cn}/2$ and prove a rounding-safe
asymptotic inverse for the integer count sequence.

The component asymptotics and generic expansive-assembly limit laws
are prior results. The contribution developed here is the explicit
sequence-specific marked expansion and its refinements. The companion
contains bounded exact integer and symbolic checks. No worldwide
priority claim, complete beyond-all-orders transseries, or certified
numerical asymptotic error bound is asserted.
\end{abstract}

\part{Asymptotics of assemblies of nested cycles: exact counts and fixed order marked saddle expansions}\label{ncy:part:one}


\section{Exact objects and established input}\label{ncy:sec:exact}
A labeled cycle on an $m$-element set has $(m-1)!$ possible cyclic
orders and exponential generating function $z^m/m$. Choosing at most
one cycle of each length therefore gives
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:eq:P}
 P(z)=\prod_{m\geq1}\left(1+\frac{z^m}{m}\right)
     =\sum_{m\geq0}B_m\frac{z^m}{m!},\qquad p_m=\frac{B_m}{m!}.
\end{equation}
The integers $B_m$ form A007838. A nonempty collection of cycles of
distinct lengths has a unique decreasing-length nesting order. It is
one nested component in Riedel's interpretation; there is no extra
ordering or geometric-embedding choice. A set of such nonempty labeled
components has marked exponential generating function
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:eq:F}
 F(z,u)=\exp\{u(P(z)-1)\},\qquad f_n(u)=[z^n]F(z,u).
\end{equation}
Thus
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:eq:counts}
 T(n,k)=\frac{n!}{k!}[z^n](P(z)-1)^k,
 \qquad a_n=n!f_n(1)=\sum_{k=0}^nT(n,k).
\end{equation}
The $a_n$ are A308338, with $a_0=1$, and the published triangle A392471
starts at $n=1$, $1\leq k\leq n$. We use the natural empty extension
$T(0,0)=1$ and $T(n,0)=0$ for $n>0$ only for recurrences. In particular
$T(n,1)=B_n$ for $n\geq1$.

Ilya Gutkovskiy entered A308338 and its defining EGF on 20 May 2019
\cite{oeis308338}. Riedel's January 2026 note supplies the nested-cycle
interpretation, the marked triangle, and recurrences
\cite{riedel,oeis392471}. The component EGF is much older. The prior
estimate we use is
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:eq:prior}
 p_m=c+\frac{c}{m}+O\!\left(\frac{\log m}{m^2}\right),
 \qquad c=e^{-\gamma},\qquad m\longrightarrow\infty,
\end{equation}
where $\gamma$ is Euler's constant. Flajolet, Fusy, Gourdon, Panario,
and Pouyanne \cite[Section 3, equation (22)]{hybrid} attribute this
estimate to Greene and Knuth. Their Proposition 1 gives the full
component coefficient expansion, including periodic terms from roots
of unity. We use~\eqref{ncy:eq:prior} as a credited theorem; their theorem
for $P$ is not itself a coefficient theorem for the outer exponential
$F$.

For a component spectrum $(j_1,\ldots,j_n)$ with $\sum m j_m=n$, the
number of objects is
\[
 n!\prod_{m=1}^n\frac{p_m^{j_m}}{j_m!}.
\]
After division by $a_n$, this is the Gibbs assembly law with weights
$p_m\sim c$, so its expansive exponent is $1$. The weights are $p_m$,
not $B_m$ or $p_m/m$. The square-root component law and normal
fluctuations lie within established expansive-assembly theory.
Erlihson and Granovsky \cite[Corollary 4.5]{eg2008} explicitly attribute
the total-component CLT to their 2004 work~\cite{eg2004}. We derive the
precise normalization and lower-order terms directly below.

\section{The marked coefficient theorem}\label{ncy:sec:main}
Fix a sufficiently small open complex disc about $u=1$. Choose
$\log(cu)$ and $\sqrt{cu}$ to be real on positive real $u$, and set
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:eq:C}
 A=\frac12-\log2,\qquad
 C(u)=\frac{e^{u(cA-1)}(cu)^{1/4-cu/2}}{2\sqrt\pi}.
\end{equation}
All statements about fixed order mean that the order is fixed as
$n\to\infty$; constants need not be uniform when that order grows.

\begin{theorem}[Uniform fixed-order expansion]\label{ncy:thm:main}
For every fixed integer $J\geq0$ there are polynomials $R_j(L;u)$ of
degree at most $2j$ in $L$, with coefficients holomorphic near $1$, such
that
\begin{align}\label{ncy:eq:main}
 f_n(u)&=C(u)n^{cu/2-3/4}e^{2\sqrt{cun}}
 \left\{1+\sum_{j=1}^J n^{-j/2}R_j(\log n;u)
       +E_{n,J}(u)\right\},\\
 E_{n,J}(u)&=O\!\left(n^{-(J+1)/2}(1+\log n)^{2J+2}\right).
 \notag
\end{align}
The estimate is uniform on every sufficiently small fixed closed disc
about $1$. The exact normalized residual $E_{n,J}$ is holomorphic on
an open disc containing any smaller disc on which derivatives are
taken, and each fixed derivative obeys the same order bound there.
\end{theorem}

Taking $u=1$, $J=0$ gives the equivalent
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:eq:equivalent}
 a_n\sim C n!n^{c/2-3/4}e^{2\sqrt{cn}},\qquad
 C=\frac{e^{c(1/2-\log2)-1}c^{1/4-c/2}}{2\sqrt\pi}.
\end{equation}
For orientation only,
$C\approx0.0947779253874791584250013230745944160$; this is an
uncertified floating evaluation of the exact expression. The logarithmic
term of $P$ supplies the power $n^{c/2}$ in~\eqref{ncy:eq:equivalent}.
Discarding that term would give an incorrect equivalent.

The theorem is an all-fixed-orders algebraic expansion with logarithmic
polynomials. It makes no claim about convergence, optimal truncation,
Stokes phenomena, or a complete description of terms beyond all orders.

\section{Local component expansion in a right half plane}\label{ncy:sec:sector}
We first prove the needed local expansion without continuing $P$
through its natural boundary. For $\Re t>0$, isolating the factor $m=1$
gives the absolutely convergent identity
\begin{align}\label{ncy:eq:polylog}
 \log P(e^{-t})={}&\log(1+e^{-t})+\Li_1(e^{-t})-e^{-t}\\
 &+\sum_{k\geq2}\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k}
       \{\Li_k(e^{-kt})-e^{-kt}\}.\notag
\end{align}
Fix a desired order $H$ and choose an integer $K>H+2$. The tail $k>K$
can be written as a double sum over $k>K$, $m\geq2$. For
$0\leq h\leq H+1$, its $h$th derivative in $t$ is bounded absolutely
and uniformly on the closed right half plane by
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:eq:tail}
 \sum_{k>K}k^{h-1}\sum_{m\geq2}m^{h-k}<\infty.
\end{equation}
Indeed $k-h>1$ and the inner sum decreases exponentially as $k$ grows.
Termwise differentiation up to that order is consequently valid. The
integral remainder along the segment from $0$ to $t$ gives a Taylor
polynomial for the tail with error $O(|t|^{H+1})$, uniformly in the
closed right half plane near $0$.

Each retained polylogarithm has the classical finite-order expansion
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:eq:li}
 \Li_k(e^{-x})=
 \frac{(-x)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}(H_{k-1}-\log x)
 +\sum_{\substack{r\geq0\\r\ne k-1}}
       \zeta(k-r)\frac{(-x)^r}{r!},
\end{equation}
where $H_j=\sum_{m=1}^j1/m$ and $H_0=0$. For a fixed finite set of
$k$, truncations and their remainders are uniform in every closed
subsector of $\Re t>0$. Combining them with~\eqref{ncy:eq:tail} gives each
fixed order of $\log P(e^{-t})$. At every positive power $t^r$, the
coefficient is affine in $\log t$. Its logarithmic coefficient comes
only from $k=r+1$ in~\eqref{ncy:eq:polylog} and is
\[
 -\frac{(r+1)^{r-1}}{r!}\qquad(r\geq1).
\]
The constant after removing $-\log t$ is $-\gamma$, by the convergent
Euler product with the harmonic term subtracted. Explicitly,
\begin{align}\label{ncy:eq:logP}
 \log P(e^{-t})={}&-\log t-\gamma+t(-\log t+A)
       +t^2\left(-\frac32\log t+B\right)\notag\\
 &+t^3\left(-\frac83\log t+B_3\right)
       +O\bigl(|t|^4(1+|\log t|)\bigr),\\
 B={}&\frac{41}{24}-\frac{\zeta(2)}2-\frac32\log3,\qquad
 B_3=\frac{49}{12}-\frac{\pi^2}{4}
             +\frac{\zeta(3)}3-\frac83\log4.\notag
\end{align}

For completeness, the $t^2$ constant contributions from the isolated
prefactor, $k=2$, $k=3$, and $k\geq4$ are, respectively,
\[
 -\frac5{12},\qquad \frac32,\qquad
 \frac34-\frac32\log3,\qquad -\frac{\zeta(2)}2-\frac18.
\]
The last follows by summing a rational series:
\begin{align*}
 \frac12\sum_{k\geq4}(-1)^{k+1}k(\zeta(k-2)-1)
 &=-\frac12\sum_{m\geq2}\frac{4m+3}{m(m+1)^2}\\
 &=-\frac{\zeta(2)}2-\frac18.
\end{align*}
For $B_3$, the corresponding contributions (now separating $k=4$)
are $1/6$, $-13/18$, $9/4$, $20/9-(8/3)\log4$, and
\[
 -\frac16\sum_{m\geq2}\frac{25m^2+39m+16}{m(m+1)^3}
   =\frac16-\frac32\zeta(2)+\frac13\zeta(3).
\]
These calculations supply the constants rather than attributing them
to a short displayed formula in the component source.

Exponentiation to any prescribed fixed order yields
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:eq:localP}
 P(e^{-t})=\frac ct+c(-\log t+A)
     +\sum_{j=1}^{M}t^jU_j(\log t)
     +O\bigl(|t|^{M+1}(1+|\log t|)^{M+2}\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $\deg U_j\leq j+1$. To obtain the stated error, first expand
$\log P$ through degree $M+1$, with error
$O(|t|^{M+2}(1+|\log t|))$, and then exponentiate. The largest omitted
logarithmic degree comes from $M+2$ copies of $t\log t$.
Define
\begin{align}\label{ncy:eq:QZ}
 D&=\log2-2,\qquad E=B+A^2/2,\qquad Q(X)=X^2/2+DX+E,\notag\\
 Z(X)&=\frac{(-X+A)^3}{6}+(-X+A)\left(-\frac32X+B\right)
                  -\frac83X+B_3.
\end{align}
Then $U_1=cQ$ and $U_2=cZ$. All these are sector expansions inside the
right half plane. No analytic continuation of $P$ across $|z|=1$ has
been used.

\section{The complete circle and complex saddle}\label{ncy:sec:circle}
The global step comes from the prior coefficient estimate rather than
from a claim that the positive real singularity is the only singularity.
From~\eqref{ncy:eq:prior}, absolute summation of
$|p_m-c|e^{-am}$, including the bounded $m=0$ adjustment, gives uniformly
for $0<a<1$, $|y|\leq\pi$,
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:eq:globalP}
 P(e^{-a-iy})=\frac{c}{1-e^{-a-iy}}+O(1+\log(1/a))
             =\frac{c}{a+iy}+O(1+\log(1/a)).
\end{equation}
For the second equality, $(1-e^{-t})^{-1}-t^{-1}$ is bounded on
$0\leq\Re t\leq1$, $|\Im t|\leq\pi$ after filling its removable
value at $0$. Thus~\eqref{ncy:eq:globalP} includes every secondary
root-of-unity arc.

Put $v=cu$ and $s=\sqrt{v/n}=a+ib$, where $a>0$. Coefficient extraction
on $|z|=e^{-a}$, with $z=e^{-t}$, gives
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:eq:cauchy}
 f_n(u)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{a-i\pi}^{a+i\pi}
        \exp\{u(P(e^{-t})-1)+nt\}\dd t.
\end{equation}
The sign from $\dd z/z=-\dd t$ is canceled by reversal of the circle
parameter. The principal phase is $\Phi(t)=v/t+nt$, with saddle $s$.
Since $v=ns^2$, direct algebra gives the exact loss identity
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:eq:loss}
 \Re\Phi(a+iy)=2na-na\frac{(y-b)^2}{a^2+y^2}.
\end{equation}
Uniformly on the small marking disc, $a\asymp n^{-1/2}$ and $|b|/a$
is bounded. Take $h=a^{3/2}\log n$. Outside $|y-b|<h$, the loss in
\eqref{ncy:eq:loss} is at least $\kappa(\log n)^2$ for a positive
constant $\kappa$. To see this, when $|y|\leq C_0a$ the denominator
is $O(a^2)$ and $na h^2/a^2\asymp(\log n)^2$; when
$|y|>C_0a$ with $C_0$ sufficiently large, the fraction is bounded
below, and the loss is of order $\sqrt n$.

The error from~\eqref{ncy:eq:globalP} costs only $O(\log n)$ in the real
exponent, with $u$ bounded. The entire off-saddle contribution is
therefore
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:eq:off}
 O\!\left(e^{2na-\kappa'(\log n)^2}\right)
\end{equation}
for a uniform $\kappa'>0$. The modulus of the leading term in
\eqref{ncy:eq:main} is $e^{2na}$ times a quantity bounded above and below
by fixed powers of $n$. Hence~\eqref{ncy:eq:off}, relatively to that term,
is smaller than every fixed inverse power of $n$. This proves the
needed suppression for the whole circle, including the natural-boundary
points, rather than relying only on positivity or aperiodicity.

\section{The local Gaussian expansion and its remainder}\label{ncy:sec:gaussian}
On $|t-s|\leq h$, $t$ remains in a fixed right-half-plane subsector
and $|t|\asymp n^{-1/2}$. Equation~\eqref{ncy:eq:localP} gives, for any
fixed $J$,
\begin{align}\label{ncy:eq:amplitude}
 e^{u(P(e^{-t})-1)}={}&e^{u(cA-1)}t^{-v}e^{v/t}\\
 &\quad\times\left\{\sum_{j=0}^Jt^jQ_j(\log t;u)
   +O\bigl(|t|^{J+1}(1+|\log t|)^{2J+2}\bigr)\right\}.\notag
\end{align}
Here $Q_0=1$, and all $Q_j$ are defined by the formal identity
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:eq:Qj}
 \exp\left(u\sum_{j\geq1}t^jU_j(X)\right)
             =\sum_{j\geq0}t^jQ_j(X;u).
\end{equation}
A product of $r$ positive-degree factors with total $t$-degree $j$
has logarithmic degree at most $j+r\leq2j$; therefore
$\deg_XQ_j\leq2j$. Truncating the ordinary exponential gives the error
in~\eqref{ncy:eq:amplitude}, since $t(1+|\log t|)^2\to0$ on the segment.
The component error in~\eqref{ncy:eq:localP} is no larger. In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:eq:Q12}
 Q_1=vQ,\qquad Q_2=vZ+\frac{v^2}{2}Q^2.
\end{equation}

The next lemma records the integrated Taylor remainder carefully;
replacing powers of the integration variable by endpoint bounds would
lose unnecessary powers of $\log n$.

\begin{lemma}[Uniform Gaussian Taylor remainder]\label{ncy:lem:gaussian}
Set $\delta=n^{-1/4}$, $\lambda=\sqrt{vn}$, and use the actual
vertical contour parametrization
\[
 t=s+in^{-3/4}x=s(1+i\delta x/\sqrt v),\qquad
 |x|\leq X_n=(\Re\sqrt v)^{3/2}\log n.
\]
For fixed nonnegative integers $j,k,S$, the integral of the monomial
$t^{j-v}(\log t)^k$ times $e^{nt+v/t}$ has an expansion, after removing
its common leading scale, in powers $n^{-r/2}$, $0\leq r\leq S$.
Its relative-to-that-scale remainder is
$O(n^{-(S+1)/2}(1+|\log s|)^k)$, uniformly in $u$ on a sufficiently
small fixed closed disc. Each coefficient is polynomial in $\log s$
of degree at most $k$ and holomorphic in $u$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The exact phase, with no truncated exponential yet, is
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:eq:exactphase}
 nt+\frac vt=2\lambda-\frac{x^2}{\sqrt v}
             +\frac{i\delta x^3}{v(1+i\delta x/\sqrt v)}.
\end{equation}
Write $\ell=\log s=(\log v-\log n)/2$ and remove $s^{j-v}$ from the
monomial. The residual analytic factor multiplying the Gaussian is
\begin{align}\label{ncy:eq:H}
 H_{j,k}(\delta,x;v,\ell)={}&(1+i\delta x/\sqrt v)^{j-v}
       [\ell+\log(1+i\delta x/\sqrt v)]^k\notag\\
 &\times\exp\left(\frac{i\delta x^3}{v(1+i\delta x/\sqrt v)}\right).
\end{align}
In a Taylor expansion in $\delta$, hold $\ell$ fixed; substitute its
stated value afterwards. Let $d>0$ be a uniform lower bound for
$\Re(v^{-1/2})$ on the marking disc. For every fixed derivative order
$m$ and all sufficiently large $n$, one has, for $0\leq\xi\leq\delta$
and $|x|\leq X_n$,
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:eq:derivbound}
 |\partial_\delta^mH_{j,k}(\xi,x;v,\ell)|
 \leq C_{j,k,m}(1+|\ell|)^k
            P_{j,k,m}(|x|)e^{dx^2/2},
\end{equation}
with a fixed polynomial $P_{j,k,m}$ having nonnegative coefficients.
Indeed, $|\xi x/\sqrt v|\leq1/2$ eventually. Differentiation of the
rational exponent and logarithm gives fixed polynomial bounds in
$|x|$, with denominators uniformly separated from zero. The exponential
modulus is at most $\exp(C\delta |x|^3)$, and
$C\delta X_n\leq d/2$ eventually. The complex power and its fixed
number of derivatives are bounded in the same way by polynomials.
Finite product and chain rules prove~\eqref{ncy:eq:derivbound}.

Expand~\eqref{ncy:eq:H} through degree $2S+1$. The integral Taylor
remainder, multiplied by $e^{-x^2/\sqrt v}$ and integrated, is at most
\begin{align}\label{ncy:eq:integratedrem}
 C\delta^{2S+2}(1+|\ell|)^k
       \int_{\mathbb R}P_{j,k,2S+2}(|x|)e^{-dx^2/2}\dd x
  =O\bigl(n^{-(S+1)/2}(1+|\ell|)^k\bigr).
\end{align}
This uses Gaussian moments, not the bound $|x|\leq X_n$ for the
polynomial factor, so it introduces no extra powers of $\log n$.
The identity $H_{j,k}(-\delta,-x)=H_{j,k}(\delta,x)$ implies that
the coefficient of $\delta^m$ has parity $m$ in $x$. All odd
coefficients integrate to zero on the symmetric centered segment,
also for complex $u$ because its center is the actual saddle $s$.
The remaining even coefficients can be integrated over $\mathbb R$:
the tails beyond $X_n\asymp\log n$ are smaller than every fixed
inverse power of $n$.

The moments used are
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:eq:moments}
 \int_{\mathbb R}x^{2r}e^{-x^2/\sqrt v}\dd x
  =\sqrt\pi\frac{(2r-1)!!}{2^r}v^{(2r+1)/4},\qquad
 \int_{\mathbb R}x^{2r+1}e^{-x^2/\sqrt v}\dd x=0,
\end{equation}
with $(-1)!!=1$. They hold first for real positive $v$ by substitution
and then throughout the disc by holomorphy of dominated Gaussian
integrals. This use of continuation concerns the Gaussian integral
only. Polynomial dependence on $\ell$ and holomorphic dependence on
$u$ are preserved by the finite Taylor and moment operations.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{ncy:thm:main}]
For a monomial in $Q_j$, apply Lemma~\ref{ncy:lem:gaussian} with $S=J-j$.
Its $s^j$ contributes $n^{-j/2}$, and its logarithmic degree is at most
$2j$. Its total relative error is therefore
\[
 O\bigl(n^{-(J+1)/2}(1+\log n)^{2j}\bigr).
\]
The remainder in~\eqref{ncy:eq:amplitude}, integrated absolutely with the
local Gaussian, contributes
$O(n^{-(J+1)/2}(1+\log n)^{2J+2})$ relative to the leading scale.
Equation~\eqref{ncy:eq:loss}, or~\eqref{ncy:eq:exactphase} with its small
correction, supplies a fixed Gaussian majorant; $t^{-v}/s^{-v}$ is
bounded on the segment. Equation~\eqref{ncy:eq:off} disposes of the
rest of the circle.

The Jacobian and leading Gaussian integral give precisely
\begin{align*}
 \frac{n^{-3/4}}{2\pi}e^{u(cA-1)}s^{-v}e^{2\lambda}
           \sqrt\pi v^{1/4}
       =C(u)n^{v/2-3/4}e^{2\sqrt{vn}}.
\end{align*}
Every even correction coming from $Q_j$ has logarithmic degree at
most $2j$. At total order $n^{-r/2}$, $j\leq r$, proving the degree
bound $2r$ and the coefficient holomorphy.

Finally, let $L_n(u)=C(u)n^{cu/2-3/4}e^{2\sqrt{cun}}$. It is
holomorphic and nonzero on a small fixed open disc. Since $f_n(u)$ is
a polynomial, the exact expression
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:eq:residual}
 E_{n,J}(u)=\frac{f_n(u)}{L_n(u)}-1
              -\sum_{j=1}^Jn^{-j/2}R_j(\log n;u)
\end{equation}
is holomorphic there. The proof gives its bound on every sufficiently
small closed disc. Choose two concentric discs whose closures are
inside the open disc; Cauchy's derivative estimates transfer the bound
on the larger disc to all fixed derivatives on the smaller disc,
without changing its powers of $n$ or $\log n$. The contour radius
$a=\Re\sqrt{cu/n}$ need not vary holomorphically: it is a device for
proving a bound on the already holomorphic expression~\eqref{ncy:eq:residual}.
\end{proof}

\section{Explicit corrections and a fixed order calculator}\label{ncy:sec:corrections}
Retain $v=cu$, $\ell=(\log v-L)/2$ and $Q,Z,D$ from
\eqref{ncy:eq:QZ}. The first two correction polynomials are
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:eq:R1}
 R_1(L;u)=v^{3/2}Q(\ell)
              -\frac{4(v-1)^2-1}{16\sqrt v}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{align}\label{ncy:eq:R2}
 R_2(L;u)={}&\frac{[4(v-1)^2-1][4(v-1)^2-9]}{512v}\notag\\
 &+v\left\{\frac{1-4(v-2)^2}{16}Q(\ell)
              +\frac{v-2}{2}(\ell+D)-\frac14\right\}\notag\\
 &+v^2Z(\ell)+\frac{v^3}{2}Q(\ell)^2.
\end{align}
In particular the coefficient of $L^2$ in $R_1$ is $v^{3/2}/8$.

Here is a direct Gaussian check of the first correction. The
unperturbed amplitude $t^{-v}$ has order-$\delta^2$ integrand
\[
 -\frac{v+1}{2}x^2+\frac{v+1}{v^{3/2}}x^4-\frac1{2v^2}x^6.
\]
The normalized Gaussian moments are $\sqrt v/2$, $3v/4$, and
$15v^{3/2}/8$, giving $-[4(v-1)^2-1]/(16\sqrt v)$. The $tQ_1$ term
contributes $v^{3/2}Q(\ell)$, proving~\eqref{ncy:eq:R1}.

For clarity, the four parts of~\eqref{ncy:eq:R2} have separate origins.
The unperturbed fourth-order term gives its first line. The
order-$\delta^2$ correction to $tQ_1$ gives its second line. The
leading Gaussian integral of $t^2Q_2$ gives its last line, by
\eqref{ncy:eq:Q12}. Each follows from~\eqref{ncy:eq:H} and the exact moments
\eqref{ncy:eq:moments}; the companion repeats these identities symbolically.
This makes $R_2$ a finite algebraic consequence of the same local
calculation, rather than an additional asymptotic assumption.

For general fixed order, equations~\eqref{ncy:eq:polylog},
\eqref{ncy:eq:Qj}, and~\eqref{ncy:eq:H} are an algorithm using only finitely
many coefficients and Gaussian moments. An equivalent compact
formal calculator for a monomial $t^{-\beta}$ is
\begin{align}\label{ncy:eq:calculator}
 \mathcal B_\beta(v,n)={}&
 \left(\frac nv\right)^{(\beta-1)/2}
 \frac{e^{2\sqrt{vn}}}{\sqrt{4\pi\sqrt{vn}}}\notag\\
 &\times\sum_{r\geq0}
 \frac{(-1)^r\prod_{l=1}^r[4(\beta-1)^2-(2l-1)^2]}
      {r!(16\sqrt{vn})^r}.
\end{align}
For $t^{j-v}(\log t)^k$, replace the monomial by
$(-\partial_\beta)^k\mathcal B_\beta(v,n)$ at $\beta=v-j$,
holding $v$ fixed, multiply by $e^{u(cA-1)}$, and retain terms with
$j+r\leq J$. Equation~\eqref{ncy:eq:calculator} is the dominant-saddle
formal series familiar from the modified Bessel function. Its use
here is a coefficient calculator: the original full contour has not
been identified with a Bessel contour, and no convergence of the
formal series is claimed. The remainder proof is the Gaussian
argument of Section~\ref{ncy:sec:gaussian}.

\section{Refined component moments and the Gaussian limit}\label{ncy:sec:probability}
Let $K_n$ be the number of nested components in an object chosen
uniformly from the $a_n$ labeled objects. Its probability generating
function is
\[
 \EE u^{K_n}=\frac{f_n(u)}{f_n(1)}.
\]
Since $f_n/L_n\to1$ uniformly near $u=1$, the ratio is nonzero on a
fixed smaller disc for all sufficiently large $n$ and has the analytic
logarithm tending to zero there. The theorem and finite logarithmic
expansion imply
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:eq:logf}
 \log f_n(u)=\log L_n(u)+n^{-1/2}R_1(\log n;u)
          +O\bigl(n^{-1}(1+\log n)^4\bigr),
\end{equation}
with the same order for any fixed number of derivatives on a smaller
disc. This follows by applying Cauchy's estimates to the exact
normalized residual before differentiation. Thus no real-axis
asymptotic is being differentiated without justification.

\begin{theorem}[Mean and variance]\label{ncy:thm:moments}
As $n\to\infty$,
\begin{align}\label{ncy:eq:mean}
 \EE K_n&=\sqrt{cn}+\frac c2\log n+m_0
             +O\bigl(n^{-1/2}(1+\log n)^2\bigr),\\
 m_0&=-\frac34-c\log2+\frac{c\gamma}{2},\notag\\
 \Var K_n&=\frac{\sqrt{cn}}2+\frac c2\log n+v_0
             +O\bigl(n^{-1/2}(1+\log n)^2\bigr),\label{ncy:eq:variance}\\
 v_0&=-1-c\left(\log2+\frac12-\frac\gamma2\right).\notag
\end{align}
The next corrections are respectively $n^{-1/2}M_1(\log n)$ and
$n^{-1/2}V_1(\log n)$, after which both errors are
$O(n^{-1}(1+\log n)^4)$, where
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:eq:M1V1}
 M_1(L)=\left.c\partial_v\mathcal R_1(L,v)\right|_{v=c},\qquad
 V_1(L)=\left.(c\partial_v+c^2\partial_v^2)
                      \mathcal R_1(L,v)\right|_{v=c}.
\end{equation}
Here $\mathcal R_1(L,v)$ is the right side of~\eqref{ncy:eq:R1} with
$\ell=(\log v-L)/2$, and $L$ is held fixed.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The cumulant operator for marking by $u$ is
$\mathcal D=u\partial_u=v\partial_v$. Consequently the mean and
variance are $\mathcal D\log f_n|_{u=1}$ and
$\mathcal D^2\log f_n|_{u=1}$. The logarithm of the leading term is
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:eq:logleading}
 \log L_n(u)=2\sqrt{vn}+\left(\frac v2-\frac34\right)\log n
       +u(cA-1)+\left(\frac14-\frac v2\right)\log v
       -\log(2\sqrt\pi).
\end{equation}
Differentiating this explicit holomorphic function and using
$\log c=-\gamma$ gives~\eqref{ncy:eq:mean}--\eqref{ncy:eq:variance}.
Differentiating the next term in~\eqref{ncy:eq:logf} gives
\eqref{ncy:eq:M1V1}; its residual is controlled as just explained.
\end{proof}

An entirely explicit form of these first refined polynomials is also
useful. Write $q=Q(\ell_c)$, $q'=\ell_c+D$, and
$\ell_c=(\log c-L)/2$. Then
\begin{align}\label{ncy:eq:explicitM1}
 M_1(L)={}&c^{3/2}\left(\frac32q+\frac12q'\right)
       -\frac38c^{3/2}+\frac14c^{1/2}+\frac3{32}c^{-1/2},\\
 V_1(L)={}&c^{3/2}\left(\frac94q+\frac32q'+\frac14\right)
       -\frac9{16}c^{3/2}+\frac18c^{1/2}-\frac3{64}c^{-1/2}.
       \label{ncy:eq:explicitV1}
\end{align}
These follow by writing the nonlogarithmic term in~\eqref{ncy:eq:R1}
as $-v^{3/2}/4+v^{1/2}/2-3v^{-1/2}/16$ and using
$v\partial_v\ell=1/2$.

In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:eq:meanlimit}
 \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\EE K_n}{\sqrt n}
       =e^{-\gamma/2}\approx0.749306001288449.
\end{equation}
The decimal is uncertified. Riedel's numerical estimate
$0.7719351399$~\cite[p. 3]{riedel} is a finite-data estimate of the
constant, accompanied there by caution about the fitted exponent.
The positive $\frac c2\log n$ term explains a slow approach to the
limit. The exact square-root limit belongs to the pre-existing
expansive-assembly regime; the displayed lower-order corrections are
specific to these component weights.

\begin{corollary}[Credited Gaussian regime]\label{ncy:cor:clt}
With $\sigma_n^2=\sqrt{cn}/2$,
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:eq:clt}
 \frac{K_n-\EE K_n}{\sigma_n}\ \Longrightarrow\ N(0,1).
\end{equation}
The same statement holds with $\sigma_n^2$ replaced by $\Var K_n$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Use the analytic logarithm of $f_n(e^t)/f_n(1)$ in a fixed complex
neighborhood of $t=0$. By~\eqref{ncy:eq:logf} and Cauchy's estimates,
its third derivative is $O(\sqrt n+\log n)$ there. Its second derivative
at $0$ is $\sigma_n^2+O(\log n)$. For fixed real $\theta$, substitute
$t=i\theta/\sigma_n$ and subtract $t\EE K_n$. Taylor's theorem gives
\[
 \log\EE\exp\left(i\theta\frac{K_n-\EE K_n}{\sigma_n}\right)
 =-\frac{\theta^2}{2}+o(1),
\]
with cubic error $O(n^{-1/4})$ and second-derivative replacement error
$O(n^{-1/2}\log n)$. The continuity theorem for characteristic
functions proves~\eqref{ncy:eq:clt}. Equation~\eqref{ncy:eq:variance} and
Slutsky's theorem justify the variance normalization.
\end{proof}

The proof recovers a known generic limit-law phenomenon with the
normalization needed here. It is not a new universality assertion.
No local limit theorem or quantitative distribution-distance bound
is inferred from this fixed-frequency argument.

\section{Asymptotic inversion with safe integer rounding}\label{ncy:sec:inverse}
For sufficiently large real $y$, define
\[
 N(y)=\min\{n\geq0:a_n\geq y\}.
\]
The sequence is strictly increasing for $n\geq1$. Adding label $n+1$
as a new singleton component is an injection; a single cycle on all
$n+1$ labels is outside its image. The equality $a_0=a_1=1$ causes
no problem for large $y$.

Put $\beta=c/2-3/4$. For fixed $J\geq0$, define at sufficiently large
real $x$
\begin{align}\label{ncy:eq:G}
 G_J(x)={}&\log\Gamma(x+1)+2\sqrt{cx}+\beta\log x+\log C\notag\\
 &+\log\left(1+\sum_{j=1}^Jx^{-j/2}R_j(\log x;1)\right).
\end{align}
The last logarithm is real and well defined eventually, and
$G_J'(x)\sim\log x$. At integer $n$, Theorem~\ref{ncy:thm:main} implies
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:eq:logerror}
 \log a_n=G_J(n)+O(n^{-q}(\log n)^p),\qquad
 q=(J+1)/2,\quad p=2J+2.
\end{equation}
For large $y$, let $x_J$ be the unique sufficiently large real solution
of $G_J(x_J)=\log y$.

\begin{theorem}[Two-ceiling enclosure]\label{ncy:thm:inverse}
For each fixed $J$ there exist $M_J>0$ and a threshold such that, with
\[
 \epsilon_J=M_Jx_J^{-q}(\log x_J)^{p-1},
\]
one has
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:eq:inverse}
 \lceil x_J-\epsilon_J\rceil\ \leq N(y)\ \leq
                       \lceil x_J+\epsilon_J\rceil.
\end{equation}
If $\dist(x_J,\mathbb Z)>\epsilon_J$, the two ceilings coincide and
$N(y)=\lceil x_J\rceil$. No explicit certified $M_J$ or onset threshold
is supplied.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Bound the integer error in~\eqref{ncy:eq:logerror} by
$Kn^{-q}(\log n)^p$. For $x=x_J$ sufficiently large,
$\epsilon_J<1$, $G_J'\geq(\log x)/2$ on $[x-2,x+2]$, and the
error at integers in that interval is at most $2Kx^{-q}(\log x)^p$.
Choose $M_J>4K$. Then $n_+=\lceil x+\epsilon_J\rceil$ satisfies
\[
 G_J(n_+)-G_J(x)\geq\frac{\log x}{2}\epsilon_J
                       >2Kx^{-q}(\log x)^p,
\]
so $\log a_{n_+}>\log y$. Similarly
$n_-=\lceil x-\epsilon_J\rceil-1<x-\epsilon_J$ satisfies
$\log a_{n_-}<\log y$. Monotonicity gives~\eqref{ncy:eq:inverse}.
Enlarging the threshold ensures all comparisons used above.
\end{proof}

A single ceiling of a finite continuous asymptotic expansion is not
valid unconditionally: a target may lie arbitrarily close to an exact
integer threshold. One must use the two-ceiling enclosure, prove the
stated separation, or compare exact counts.

For a convenient starting approximation let $L=\log y$ and use the
positive real branch of Lambert's $W$ function:
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:eq:W}
 x_0^*=\frac{L}{W(L/e)}.
\end{equation}
It solves $x(\log x-1)=L$, and $N(y)\sim x_0^*$. With
$\alpha=c/2-1/4$ and $K_0=\log(C\sqrt{2\pi})$, Stirling's formula gives
\begin{align}\label{ncy:eq:newton}
 x_*&=x_0^*-
 \frac{2\sqrt{cx_0^*}+\alpha\log x_0^*+K_0}{\log x_0^*},\\
 x_J&=x_*+O\bigl((\log x_0^*)^{-2}\bigr).\notag
\end{align}
For justification, the displacement from $x_0^*$ is
$O(\sqrt{x_0^*}/\log x_0^*)$. Substituting~\eqref{ncy:eq:newton} into
$G_J-L$, the uncanceled derivative correction is $O(1/\log x_0^*)$;
the quadratic curvature term is $O((\log x_0^*)^{-2})$ and the
omitted Stirling and fixed-order corrections are smaller. Dividing
by $G_J'\sim\log x_0^*$ proves the displayed error. Newton iteration
or formal reversion of~\eqref{ncy:eq:G} gives further continuous orders.
Every integer conclusion still needs~\eqref{ncy:eq:inverse} or an exact
comparison. The companion's bounded exact inverse compares integer
counts directly and does not use an uncertified asymptotic rounding
rule.

\section{Exact recurrences and reproducible checks}\label{ncy:sec:computation}
The infinite products in the definitions reduce to finite exact
computations at every fixed size. The companion uses a deliberately
bounded range, not an unbounded enumeration promise.

Let $B_n^{(m)}$ count labeled permutations of $n$ labels having at
most one cycle of each length in $\{1,\ldots,m\}$. With
$B_0^{(0)}=1$ and all other entries zero,
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:eq:integercycle}
 B_n^{(m)}=B_n^{(m-1)}+
 \begin{cases}
  \binom nm(m-1)!B_{n-m}^{(m-1)},& n\geq m,\\
  0,&n<m.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
The cycle's labels are chosen first and its cyclic order next; using
the previous row enforces at most one such cycle. Thus $B_n=B_n^{(n)}$.
The component containing label $1$ gives the partial Bell recurrence
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:eq:bell}
 T(n,k)=\sum_{m=1}^n\binom{n-1}{m-1}B_mT(n-m,k-1),
 \qquad n,k\geq1.
\end{equation}
All arithmetic in these recurrences is integer arithmetic. Their
first five nonempty rows and row sums are
\[
\begin{array}{c|l|r}
 n&T(n,1),\ldots,T(n,n)&a_n\\\hline
 1&1&1\\
 2&1,1&2\\
 3&5,3,1&9\\
 4&14,23,6,1&44\\
 5&74,120,65,10,1&270
\end{array}
\]

An independent rational-series check follows from
\[
 \log P(z)=\sum_{r\geq1}\ell_rz^r,\qquad
 \ell_r=\sum_{m\mid r}\frac{(-1)^{r/m+1}}{(r/m)m^{r/m}}.
\]
Formal differentiation gives
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:eq:rational}
 np_n=\sum_{r=1}^n r\ell_rp_{n-r},\qquad
 nf_n(1)=\sum_{r=1}^n rp_rf_{n-r}(1),\qquad p_0=f_0(1)=1.
\end{equation}
A separate power-series multiplication checks
$T(n,k)=n![z^n](P-1)^k/k!$ directly. At small sizes, literal
permutation cycle decomposition followed by enumeration of admissible
groupings of cycles gives an additional combinatorial check.

The frozen OEIS fixtures contain the displayed numeric terms with
public source URLs and retrieval metadata: 23 values of A007838,
22 values of A308338, and 55 entries of A392471. They are independent
expected values, not values generated during a verification run.
The companion checks those prefixes, complete counts and triangle
through $n=100$, independent rational count recurrences through
$n=100$, power-series triangle identities through $n=30$, and literal
enumeration through $n=7$. Exact rational mean and variance samples
come directly from the triangle. Optional SymPy checks verify the
finite symbolic correction identities. See the package README for
the commands and dependency versions used.

\textbf{Evidence levels.} The fixtures, finite recurrences, exact
integer comparisons, rational moments, and symbolic identities are
exact. They are finite checks and do not prove an asymptotic theorem.
The fixed-order remainder, complex uniformity, all-circle suppression,
moment expansion, CLT, and inverse enclosure are proved in the text
using the credited component estimate. Their implied constants and
onset thresholds have not been made effective here. All decimal
constants or other numerical asymptotic diagnostics are uncertified;
high arithmetic precision alone would not certify their errors.
The package supplies no interval enclosure for the transcendental
constant $C$ and no empirical fitted constant as a substitute for its
exact definition.

\emph{(b) The correction.} The denominator should be
$C\Gamma(p+2)=b_{p+1}(0)$:
\[
 e^{\mathrm{corr}}_{mk}=b_{p-1}(u_s)-\frac{b_p(u_m)b_p(u_k)}{C\,\Gamma(p+2)},
\]
which coincides with the printed display exactly when $C=1$. This is what the
conditioning computation gives. In the Boltzmann model at $\delta=1/r_N$ the
counts $n_k$ are independent Poisson variables of means $a_ke^{-\delta k}$;
with $\nu_j=\sum_{k\ge u_jr_N}n_k$ and the total mass $Z=\sum_kkn_k$, Riemann
sums give
$r_N^{-p}\operatorname{Cov}(\nu_m,\nu_k)\to C\Gamma(p,u_s)=b_{p-1}(u_s)$,
$r_N^{-p-1}\operatorname{Cov}(\nu_m,Z)\to C\Gamma(p+1,u_m)=b_p(u_m)$ and
$r_N^{-p-2}\operatorname{Var}Z\to C\Gamma(p+2)=b_{p+1}(0)$, and the
covariance of a Gaussian vector conditioned on $Z$ is
$\operatorname{Cov}(\nu_m,\nu_k)-\operatorname{Cov}(\nu_m,Z)\operatorname{Cov}(\nu_k,Z)/\operatorname{Var}Z$.
The corrected display is a variance: $e^{\mathrm{corr}}_{11}(u)
=C\bigl(\Gamma(p,u)-\Gamma(p+1,u)^2/\Gamma(p+2)\bigr)>0$, because the
Cauchy--Schwarz inequality for $x^{(p-1)/2}e^{-x/2}$ and
$x^{(p+1)/2}e^{-x/2}$ on $(u,\infty)$ gives
$\Gamma(p+1,u)^2<\Gamma(p,u)\Gamma(p+2,u)\le\Gamma(p,u)\Gamma(p+2)$. In the
example of~(a) it is $3/e-6/e^2=0.29163\ldots$. The write checked these
limits and the conditioning formula, not the remaining steps of Section~5
of~\cite{eg2008}.

under $a_k\sim Ck^{p-1}$ the right side is $C\Gamma(p+2)\delta^{-(p+2)}$.

printed holds only for $C=1$: the factor is lost with (5.62) and carried
through~$T_q$, while the quadratic form $\Theta^\star$, built from the $f_j$
and~$w_j$, carries~$C$ correctly. The inversion that produces (4.13) was not
checked. \textup{(Sentence strengthened after the independent check of

\emph{(d) The display against this report.} The model here has weights
$p_m\to c$ (Section~\ref{ncy:sec:exact}), that is $p=1$ and $C=c$ in the
letters of~\cite{eg2008}, with $N=n$ and $r_N=(n/c)^{1/2}$. Read at $u=0$, as
their Corollary~4.5 is read there, with the normalization $r_N^{-1/2}$ (the
limit of their scaling factor for $u_1>0$), the printed display gives
$(K_n-S)/r_N^{1/2}\Rightarrow N\bigl(0,c(1-c/2)\bigr)=N(0,0.40384\ldots)$ for
a deterministic centring~$S$. Corollary~\ref{ncy:cor:clt}
and~\eqref{ncy:eq:variance}, proved here without their display, give

\part{Sharp Gumbel errors and optimal continuous calibration for the largest outer component}\label{ncy:gb:part}
\partsource{A research manuscript of 8~October 2026, archive
\file{sharp_gumbel_assemblies.zip}, delivered as
\file{sharp_gumbel_assemblies/article.tex} with two section files and a
generated table file (27~pages). Delivered title block ``Sharp Gumbel Errors
and Optimal Continuous Calibration for Random Assemblies / Largest components
of nested-cycle partitions and other assemblies with asymptotically constant
weights / Research manuscript prepared for Vladimir Reshetnikov / 8 October
2026''. Printed as delivered after Section~\ref{ncy:gb:sec:front}, which the
write added, with \textbf{[write]} notes; the manuscript's Section~$k$ is
Section~$k+12$ here, its statements and equations ($k.m$) are ($k{+}12.m$),
and its Appendices~A and~B, Tables~1 and~2 and Figures~1 and~2 keep their

Part~I for the model, its counts and its credits, not as a premise of any

\begin{partabstract}
For random assemblies with nonnegative component weights
\(p_k=c+O(k^{-1})\), \(c>0\), we prove a uniform second-order law for
the largest component. If \(\tau\) is the exact size saddle,
\(L=\log(c/\tau)\), and \(G(x)=\exp(-e^{-x})\), the ordinary Gumbel
approximation has error
\[
 \DK(F_n,G)\sim
 \frac{(2+\sqrt5)e^{-(3+\sqrt5)/2}}{16\sqrt c}
 \frac{\log^2 n}{\sqrt n}.
\]
No affine normalization removes this order. An explicit increasing
nonlinear calibration attains error
\(\tau/(2e)+O(\tau/L)\); we prove that \(\tau/(2e)\) is the smallest
possible leading error among all continuous distribution functions.
Retaining the lattice phase improves the error to \(O(\tau/L)\).
Under \(p_k=c+a_*/k+O(\log k/k^2)\), an exponential-integral correction
reduces it to \(O(\tau^{3/2}L^3)\). The proof compares the conditioned
and unconditioned Fourier integrals before approximation, preserving
a cancellation needed below the lattice scale. We apply the results
to nested-cycle partitions, counted by OEIS A308338, and to labelled
forests of paths. The package includes exact integer checks and
independent numerical coefficient-ratio calculations.

\smallskip\noindent
\textbf{Research status.}
The quantitative refinements have complete conventional proofs in this
manuscript and were independently checked during preparation. The
first-order Gumbel laws and the component coefficient asymptotics are
credited to prior work. The literature comparison supports these as
proposed new refinements; it does not establish worldwide priority.
The manuscript is unrefereed and is not a Lean or Rocq formalization.
\end{partabstract}

\section{The question and the contribution}\label{ncy:gb:sec:intro}

A limiting distribution is often much less accurate than its elegant
formula suggests. This is particularly visible for the largest component
of a random assembly. At a first approximation, rare large components
behave like independent Poisson events, producing a Gumbel law. The
assembly, however, is sampled with its \emph{total size fixed}. Removing
large components changes both the mean and the variance of that total.
Although these changes are small on the central-limit scale, their
effect on the extreme-value distribution is larger than the spacing of
the integer lattice by a factor of order \(\log^2 n\).

This manuscript identifies that effect, calculates its exact profile,
and removes it. The final continuous approximation is as accurate, to
first order, as any continuous approximation can be. We then retain the
integer lattice and resolve a further, model-dependent correction.

The concrete motivating sequence is
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:gb:eq:nested-egf}
 A_n=n![z^n]\exp\bigl(P(z)-1\bigr),
 \qquad P(z)=\prod_{k\ge1}\left(1+\frac{z^k}{k}\right).
\end{equation}
These are the nested-cycle partitions of OEIS A308338
\cite{oeis308338}. The existing ProveIt report on this sequence develops
marked enumeration asymptotics and fluctuations of the \emph{number}
of components \cite{ProveItNested}. Here the random variable is the
\emph{size of the largest} outer component. The distinction matters:
a largest outer component contains a permutation with distinct cycle
lengths; it is not just the longest individual cycle.

\subsection{What is already known}

The exponential formula and conditioned independent-Poisson
representation of assemblies are standard. Panagiotou and Ramzews
prove Gumbel convergence for largest clusters in a much broader class
of expansive assemblies and multisets \cite[Theorem~1.2]{PR}.
Bousquet-M\'elou and Weller previously obtained a Gumbel law for the
largest path in a random labelled forest of paths
\cite[Proposition~9 in the conference version]{BMWconf,BMW}.
Thus a first-order Gumbel limit for our examples is established
background, not a new theorem of this manuscript.

For the component series \(P\) in \eqref{ncy:gb:eq:nested-egf}, Flajolet,
Fusy, Gourdon, Panario, and Pouyanne record the estimate
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:gb:eq:known-p}
 [z^k]P(z)=e^{-\gamma}
 +\frac{e^{-\gamma}}{k}
 +O\left(\frac{\log k}{k^2}\right),
\end{equation}
and develop fuller expansions, including periodic terms
\cite[Section~3, equation~(22), and Proposition~1]{hybrid}.
Their estimate is the only non-elementary coefficient input needed
for the nested-cycle application. The general probability theorem
below is proved from its explicitly stated weight hypothesis.

\section{The assembly model and the formulas}\label{ncy:gb:sec:model}

\begin{definition}[Weight hypothesis]\label{ncy:gb:def:weights}
Throughout Sections~\ref{ncy:gb:sec:model}--\ref{ncy:gb:sec:affine} and the continuous
calibration results, the weights satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:gb:eq:weight-hyp}
 p_k\ge0,\qquad p_k=c+O(k^{-1}),\qquad c>0.
\end{equation}
Equivalently, after increasing a constant to cover finitely many
indices, there is \(C<\infty\) such that
\(|p_k-c|\le C/k\) for every \(k\ge1\).
\end{definition}

For \(n\ge1\), give each vector
\[
 (N_1,\ldots,N_n)\in\Z_{\ge0}^n,\qquad \sum_{k=1}^n kN_k=n,
\]
probability
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:gb:eq:assembly-law}
 \frac{1}{Z_n}\prod_{k=1}^n\frac{p_k^{N_k}}{N_k!},
 \qquad
 Z_n=[z^n]\exp\left(\sum_{k\ge1}p_kz^k\right).
\end{equation}
The law exists for every sufficiently large \(n\), since \(p_n>0\)
eventually. Set
\[
 M_n=\max\{k:N_k>0\}.
\]
If \(k!p_k\) is the number of available labelled components of size
\(k\), \eqref{ncy:gb:eq:assembly-law} is precisely the component spectrum of
a uniformly selected labelled assembly.

There is a unique \(\tau=\tau_n>0\) solving
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:gb:eq:saddle}
 B_1(\tau):=\sum_{k\ge1}kp_ke^{-\tau k}=n.
\end{equation}
The left side is continuous, strictly decreasing from infinity to
zero. Define
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:gb:eq:main-notation}
 L=\log(c/\tau),\quad Y_n=\tau M_n-L,\quad
 F_n(x)=\Pbb(Y_n\le x),\quad G(x)=e^{-e^{-x}},
 \quad \delta=\frac{\tau L^2}{4c}.
\end{equation}
Only sufficiently large \(n\), for which \(L>0\), will be used.
For each real \(x\), abbreviate
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:gb:eq:local-notation}
 \beta=e^{-x},\qquad q=L+x,\qquad
 m=\left\lfloor\frac q\tau\right\rfloor,\qquad
 \theta_n(x)=\frac q\tau-\left\lfloor\frac q\tau\right\rfloor.
\end{equation}
Thus the fractional part is exactly zero at the lattice points
\(x=\tau m-L\).

The continuous correction is
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:gb:eq:hfull}
 h_\tau(x)=x+\frac{\tau}{4c}
 \left[q^2-q-1-\beta(q+1)^2\right],
 \qquad J_n(x)=G(h_\tau(x)).
\end{equation}
The lattice approximation is
\begin{align}
 U_n(x)={}&G(x)
 +\frac{\tau G(x)}{4c}
 \left[(\beta-\beta^2)q^2-(\beta+2\beta^2)q-\beta-\beta^2\right]
 \notag\\
 &+\tau\left(\frac12-\theta_n(x)\right)G'(x).
 \label{ncy:gb:eq:Un}
\end{align}
An approximation such as \(U_n\) need not itself be a monotone CDF.
Its uniform absolute error is the asserted property.
In contrast, \(J_n\) is a continuous CDF for all sufficiently large
\(n\), as proved below.

\begin{theorem}[Main quantitative conclusions]\label{ncy:gb:thm:main}
Under \eqref{ncy:gb:eq:weight-hyp}, the following hold.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Uniformly on \(\R\),
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:gb:eq:main-leading}
 F_n(x)=G(x)+\delta G(x)e^{-x}(1-e^{-x})+O(\tau L).
\end{equation}
Consequently
\[
 \DK(F_n,G)=\kappa\delta+O(\tau L),
 \qquad
 \kappa=(2+\sqrt5)e^{-(3+\sqrt5)/2}.
\]
Every affine normalization still has an error of order at least
\(\delta\), in the precise sense of Theorem~\ref{ncy:gb:thm:affine}.

\item Uniformly on \(\R\),
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:gb:eq:main-lattice}
 F_n(x)=J_n(x)+
 \tau\left(\frac12-\theta_n(x)\right)G'(x)+O(\tau/L).
\end{equation}
In particular
\[
 \DK(F_n,J_n)=\frac{\tau}{2e}+O(\tau/L).
\]
This leading constant is optimal among all continuous CDFs.

\item \(\norminf{F_n-U_n}=O(\tau/L)\).
If, more precisely,
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:gb:eq:refined-hyp}
 p_k=c+\frac{a_*}{k}+O\left(\frac{\log(k+1)}{k^2}\right),
\end{equation}
then, with
\(E_1(v)=\int_v^\infty e^{-u}u^{-1}\dd u\),
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:gb:eq:main-e1}
 F_n(x)=U_n(x)
 -a_*G(x)E_1(\max\{1,L+x\})
 +O(\tau^{3/2}L^3)
\end{equation}
uniformly on \(\R\).
For \(a_*\ne0\),
\[
 \norminf{F_n-U_n}\sim\frac{|a_*|}{ce}\frac{\tau}{L}.
\]
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

Theorem~\ref{ncy:gb:thm:tailmoment} proves the common Fourier estimate.
Section~\ref{ncy:gb:sec:lattice} derives the universal lattice law.
Section~\ref{ncy:gb:sec:affine} treats the ordinary and affine-normalized
errors. The optimal continuous and atom results follow in
Section~\ref{ncy:gb:sec:optimal}. Section~\ref{ncy:gb:sec:refinement} proves
\eqref{ncy:gb:eq:main-e1}. These proofs also explain why the successive
error scales differ.

\subsection{An exact probability representation}

Let independent \(Z_k\) have Poisson means
\[
 a_k=p_ke^{-\tau k},\qquad
 T=\sum_{k\ge1}kZ_k,\qquad T_m=\sum_{1\le k\le m}kZ_k.
\]
Their total number is almost surely finite, since
\(\sum_k a_k<\infty\). Conditional on \(T=n\), the exponential factor
\(\prod e^{-\tau kN_k}=e^{-\tau n}\) is constant, and the law of
\((Z_k)\) is \eqref{ncy:gb:eq:assembly-law}. Define
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:gb:eq:moments}
 B_j=\sum_{k\ge1}k^ja_k,\qquad V=B_2,\qquad
 S_j(m)=\sum_{k>m,\ k\ge1}k^ja_k.
\end{equation}
At the saddle, \(B_1=n\). Independence gives the exact identity
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:gb:eq:conditioning}
 \Pbb(M_n\le m)
 =e^{-S_0(m)}\frac{\Pbb(T_m=n)}{\Pbb(T=n)}.
\end{equation}
The factor \(e^{-S_0(m)}\) is the independent rare-component
approximation. The ratio is the correction for conditioning on size.

\section{A uniform Fourier estimate for removing the upper tail}
\label{ncy:gb:sec:fourier}

This section proves the estimate from which the extreme-value expansions
follow.  Its assumptions concern only the component weights:
\[
 p_k\geq0,\qquad p_k=c+O(k^{-1}),\qquad c>0.
\]
All constants below may depend on this fixed sequence.  For each sufficiently
large integer $n$, let $\tau>0$ be the solution of
\[
 B_1(\tau)=n,\qquad
 a_k=p_ke^{-\tau k},\qquad
 B_j=\sum_{k\geq1}k^j a_k,\qquad V=B_2,
 \qquad L=\log(c/\tau).
\]
We suppress the dependence on $n$ and $\tau$.  Let $Z_k$ be independent
Poisson random variables with means $a_k$, and write
\[
 T=\sum_{k\geq1}kZ_k,\qquad
 T_m=\sum_{1\leq k\leq m}kZ_k,
 \qquad S_j(m)=\sum_{k>m}k^j a_k.
\]
These sums are finite almost surely.  Conditional on $T=n$, the vector
$(Z_k)$ has precisely the assembly distribution: its weight is proportional
to $\prod_k p_k^{Z_k}/Z_k!$, since the factor $e^{-\tau n}$ is constant
under the conditioning.  In this section $M_n$ denotes its largest occupied
component size, and
\[
 F_n(x)=\Pbb\{\tau M_n-L\leq x\}.
\]
The proof uses Fourier inversion on the real circle; no continuation
through the circle of convergence is required.

\begin{lemma}[Cumulants and the saddle]
\label{ncy:gb:lem:cumulants}
The saddle exists and is unique.  For each fixed integer $j\geq1$,
\[
 B_j=c\,j!\,\tau^{-j-1}+O(\tau^{-j}),
 \qquad
 B_0=\frac c\tau+O\bigl(\log(1/\tau)\bigr).
\]
In particular,
\[
 V=2c\tau^{-3}(1+O(\tau)),\qquad
 B_3=6c\tau^{-4}(1+O(\tau)),\qquad
 \tau=\sqrt{c/n}+O(n^{-1}).
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The weights are bounded and are at least $c/2$ eventually.  Thus $B_1$ is
continuous and strictly decreasing from infinity to zero as $\tau$ runs
from zero to infinity; its derivative is $-B_2<0$.  The fixed-order
geometric-sum estimates give
\[
 \sum_{k\geq1}k^je^{-\tau k}
 =j!\tau^{-j-1}+O(\tau^{-j})\qquad(j\geq1).
\]
Multiplying the error $p_k-c=O(k^{-1})$ by $k^je^{-\tau k}$ adds at most
$O(\tau^{-j})$.  At order zero the perturbation is bounded by a constant
times $\sum e^{-\tau k}/k=O(\log(1/\tau))$.  The final assertion follows
by substituting $B_1=n$ into $n=c\tau^{-2}+O(\tau^{-1})$ and solving for
$\tau$.  Since $p_n>0$ eventually, $\Pbb(T=n)>0$ for every sufficiently
large $n$, so the conditional laws used here are well defined.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[A common block controls the whole circle]
\label{ncy:gb:lem:fourierblock}
Put $N=\lfloor1/\tau\rfloor$.  There are positive constants $d,C$ such
that, for all sufficiently small $\tau$, all $m\geq N$, and
$|\theta|\leq\pi$, both characteristic functions satisfy
\[
 \bigl|\E e^{i\theta T}\bigr|,
 \ \bigl|\E e^{i\theta T_m}\bigr|
 \leq
 \exp\!\left[-d\min\{\tau^{-3}\theta^2,\tau^{-1}\}\right].
\]
Consequently,
\[
 \sup_{j\in\Z}\Pbb(T=j)\leq C\tau^{3/2},\qquad
 \sup_{j\in\Z}\Pbb(T_m=j)\leq C\tau^{3/2}\quad(m\geq N).
\]
For either variable, the part of its Fourier integral where
$|\theta|\sqrt V>L$ is smaller than every fixed power of $\tau$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Choose $k_0$ such that $p_k\geq c/2$ for $k\geq k_0$.  On the common
block $k_0\leq k\leq N$, we have $a_k\geq c/(2e)$.  We claim that
\[
 \sum_{k=k_0}^N(1-\cos k\theta)
 \geq d_0\min\{N^3\theta^2,N\}
 \qquad(|\theta|\leq\pi)
\]
for a fixed $d_0>0$ and all sufficiently large $N$.  If
$|\theta|\leq1/N$, use $1-\cos u\geq C_0u^2$ for $|u|\leq1$ and sum
$k^2$.  If $1/N\leq|\theta|\leq A/N$, where $A$ is a fixed large
constant, the same argument restricted to
$k\leq\lfloor1/|\theta|\rfloor$ gives a lower bound
$C_1/|\theta|\geq C_1N/A$.  The lower endpoint $k_0$ only changes the
constant when $N$ is sufficiently large.  Finally,
\[
 \left|\sum_{k=k_0}^N e^{ik\theta}\right|
 \leq\frac{2}{|1-e^{i\theta}|}
 \leq\frac{\pi}{|\theta|}.
\]
For $|\theta|\geq A/N$, choose, for example, $A=8\pi$ and subtract
this bound from the number of summands.  This proves the claim.

The modulus of a Poisson characteristic function is
$\exp[-\sum a_k(1-\cos k\theta)]$.  Keeping only the common block proves
the asserted bounds for both $T$ and $T_m$.  Fourier inversion then gives
the bounds on point probabilities.  Since $V\asymp\tau^{-3}$, the
integral outside $|\theta|\sqrt V\leq L$ is at most
\[
 C\tau^{3/2}e^{-d_1L^2}+C e^{-d_1/\tau}
\]
for a possibly smaller $d_1>0$.  As $L=\log(1/\tau)+O(1)$, this is
smaller than every fixed power of $\tau$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[The central point probability]
\label{ncy:gb:lem:localmass}
At the saddle,
\[
 \Pbb(T=n)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi V}}(1+O(\tau)).
\]
There is also the following shifted version.  If $m\geq N$ and integers
$\ell$ satisfy
\[
 S_2(m)=o(V),\qquad S_1(m)+\ell=o(\sqrt V),
\]
then
\[
 \Pbb(T_m=n+\ell)\sim\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi V}}.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The centered characteristic function of $T$ is
\[
 \psi(\theta)
 =\exp\!\left\{\sum_{k\geq1}a_k
       (e^{ik\theta}-1-ik\theta)\right\}.
\]
Put $u=\theta\sqrt V$ and $b=B_3/(6V^{3/2})=O(\sqrt\tau)$.  On
$|u|\leq L$, Taylor's theorem and Lemma~\ref{ncy:gb:lem:cumulants} give
\begin{equation}
 \psi(u/\sqrt V)
 =e^{-u^2/2}\left[1-ibu^3
       +O\bigl(\tau(u^4+u^6)\bigr)\right].
 \label{ncy:gb:eq:fourier-full}
\end{equation}
Indeed the logarithmic remainder is bounded by
$B_4u^4/(24V^2)=O(\tau u^4)$, and
$\sqrt\tau L^3+\tau L^4\to0$, so the exponential Taylor remainder has a
uniformly bounded factor.  The cubic term integrates to zero.  Integrating
the remaining error against the Gaussian costs $O(\tau)$; extending the
core to the real line and discarding the rest of the original circle is
justified by Lemma~\ref{ncy:gb:lem:fourierblock}.  This proves the first formula,
including the lower bound $\Pbb(T=n)\geq C^{-1}\tau^{3/2}$.

For the shifted assertion, the variance of $T_m$ is $V-S_2(m)$, and the
target $n+\ell$ is displaced from its mean by $S_1(m)+\ell$.  On every
fixed standardized interval its Fourier integrand therefore converges to
$e^{-u^2/2}$.  The cubic remainder tends to zero because the third
cumulant of $T_m$ is at most $B_3$.  The common-block bound supplies an
integrable Gaussian majorant on the expanding inner arc and a negligible
outer arc.  Dominated convergence proves the formula.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Uniform approximation by tail moments]
\label{ncy:gb:thm:tailmoment}
For $m\in\Z_{\geq0}$, define
\[
 Q_n(m)=e^{-S_0(m)}
 \left[1+\frac{S_2(m)-S_1(m)^2}{2V}
              -\frac{B_3S_1(m)}{2V^2}\right].
\]
Then
\[
 \sup_{m\in\Z_{\geq0}}
 \bigl|\Pbb(M_n\leq m)-Q_n(m)\bigr|
 =O(\tau^{3/2}L^3)=o(\tau).
\]
The formula $Q_n$ is an approximation evaluated at the integer cutoff;
no assertion that it is a distribution function for every finite $n$ is
needed.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Write $\lambda=S_0(m)$.  Independence gives the exact identity
\begin{equation}
 \Pbb(M_n\leq m)
 =e^{-\lambda}\frac{\Pbb(T_m=n)}{\Pbb(T=n)}.
 \label{ncy:gb:eq:fourier-conditioning}
\end{equation}
We first treat a growing central range and then control both complementary
tails.

For a real $x$, put
\[
 m=\left\lfloor\frac{L+x}{\tau}\right\rfloor,
 \qquad \beta=e^{-x},\qquad s=\tau m.
\]
Consider the range
\begin{equation}
 \tau^2\leq\beta\leq16L.
 \label{ncy:gb:eq:fourier-window}
\end{equation}
Here $L/2\leq s\leq4L$ eventually, and hence $m\geq N$.  Exponential
tail sums and $p_k=c+O(k^{-1})$ imply
\begin{equation}
 \lambda=\beta(1+O(\tau)),\qquad
 S_j(m)\leq C\beta\tau^{-j}L^j\quad(1\leq j\leq3).
 \label{ncy:gb:eq:fourier-tailbounds}
\end{equation}
For completeness, the constant-weight tail at order zero is
$ce^{-\tau(m+1)}/(1-e^{-\tau})=\beta(1+O(\tau))$.  The relative
perturbation from $p_k-c$ is $O(1/m)=O(\tau/L)$.  Higher moments follow
by comparing $\sum_{k>m}k^je^{-\tau k}$ with
$\tau^{-j-1}\int_s^\infty u^je^{-u}\dd u$; the latter is
$\tau^{-j-1}e^{-s}$ times a fixed polynomial of degree $j$.  These
arguments are uniform throughout \eqref{ncy:gb:eq:fourier-window}.

The useful cancellation comes from taking the difference of the two
Fourier integrals before estimating them.  Define
\[
 D_m(u)=-\sum_{k>m}a_k(e^{iku/\sqrt V}-1),\qquad
 \alpha=\frac{S_1(m)}{\sqrt V},\qquad
 \eta=\frac{S_2(m)}V.
\]
On $|u|\leq L$,
\[
 D_m(u)=-i\alpha u+\tfrac12\eta u^2+R_3(u),
 \qquad
 |R_3(u)|\leq\frac{S_3(m)}{6V^{3/2}}|u|^3.
\]
The bounds above give
\[
 |\alpha|\leq C\beta\sqrt\tau L,\qquad
 \eta\leq C\beta\tau L^2,\qquad
 \frac{S_3(m)}{V^{3/2}}\leq C\beta\tau^{3/2}L^3.
\]
In particular $\sup_{|u|\leq L}|D_m(u)|\to0$ uniformly in the growing
range.  A second-order expansion thus gives
\begin{equation}
 e^{D_m(u)}-1
 =-i\alpha u+\frac{\eta-\alpha^2}{2}u^2+R_m(u),
 \label{ncy:gb:eq:fourier-tailperturb}
\end{equation}
where
\[
 |R_m(u)|\leq C\tau^{3/2}L^3
 (\beta+\beta^2+\beta^3)(|u|^3+|u|^6).
\]
The terms responsible for this bound are $S_3/V^{3/2}$,
$|\alpha|\eta$, and $|\alpha|^3$.  The term $\eta^2$ is smaller,
because $\sqrt\tau L\to0$.  All exponential remainder factors are
uniformly bounded.

Exactly,
\[
 \Pbb(T_m=n)-\Pbb(T=n)
 =\frac1{2\pi\sqrt V}
 \int_{-\pi\sqrt V}^{\pi\sqrt V}
 \psi(u/\sqrt V)(e^{D_m(u)}-1)\dd u.
\]
On the core, multiply \eqref{ncy:gb:eq:fourier-tailperturb} by
\eqref{ncy:gb:eq:fourier-full}.  The linear and cubic terms are odd and integrate
to zero.  The quadratic term contributes $(\eta-\alpha^2)/2$.  The
cubic--linear product is
\[
 (-ibu^3)(-i\alpha u)=-b\alpha u^4,
\]
so its Gaussian integral contributes
\[
 -3b\alpha=-\frac{B_3S_1(m)}{2V^2}.
\]
This determines the negative sign of the skewness term.  Every remaining
product has Gaussian integral at most
\[
 C\tau^{3/2}\left[1+L^3(\beta+\beta^2+\beta^3)\right].
\]
In particular the $O(\tau(u^4+u^6))$ error in the full characteristic
function is multiplied by $e^{D_m}-1$, whose leading coefficient is
$O(\beta\sqrt\tau L)$; there is no uncancelled $O(\tau)$ constant
error.  This is why separate estimates of the two probabilities would
lose the stated precision.

Outside the core one must bound the original characteristic functions:
\[
 |\psi(e^{D_m}-1)|\leq|\psi e^{D_m}|+|\psi|.
\]
The two terms are the moduli of the characteristic functions of $T_m$
and $T$, respectively.  Lemma~\ref{ncy:gb:lem:fourierblock} therefore makes
their integrals negligible uniformly; no separate bound on $e^{D_m}$
outside the core is used.  We have proved
\begin{align*}
 \sqrt{2\pi V}\,[\Pbb(T_m=n)-\Pbb(T=n)]
 ={}&\frac{S_2(m)-S_1(m)^2}{2V}
       -\frac{B_3S_1(m)}{2V^2}\\
 &+O\!\left(\tau^{3/2}
       [1+L^3(\beta+\beta^2+\beta^3)]\right).
\end{align*}
Divide by Lemma~\ref{ncy:gb:lem:localmass} and use
\eqref{ncy:gb:eq:fourier-conditioning}.  Multiplication by $e^{-\lambda}$
bounds every fixed power of $\beta$, by
\eqref{ncy:gb:eq:fourier-tailbounds}.  The extra product from division by
$1+O(\tau)$ is
$O(\tau^2L^2(\beta+\beta^2))$ before exponential weighting, and is
absorbed into $O(\tau^{3/2}L^3)$.  This proves the theorem in the
central range.

It remains to check that this range did not exclude a larger error.
Every integer cutoff can be represented by $x=\tau m-L$, so the
following two bounds complete the supremum over all $m$.

If $\beta<\tau^2$, the Poisson identity
\[
 \E[Z_k\mid T=n]
 =a_k\frac{\Pbb(T=n-k)}{\Pbb(T=n)}
 \leq C a_k
\]
follows from Lemmas~\ref{ncy:gb:lem:fourierblock} and
\ref{ncy:gb:lem:localmass}; probabilities at negative integers are zero.
Consequently $\Pbb(M_n>m)\leq C S_0(m)\leq C\beta=O(\tau^2)$.
Here $s\geq3L+O(1)$, and bounded weights give
\[
 S_j(m)\leq C\beta\tau^{-j}(1+s)^j\quad(j=0,1,2).
\]
The three moment corrections in $Q_n$ are bounded by constants times
$\tau\beta(1+s)^2$, $\tau\beta^2(1+s)^2$, and
$\tau\beta(1+s)$.  Since $\beta=(c/\tau)e^{-s}$, their maxima on this
tail are $O(\tau^3L^2)$.  Thus $Q_n(m)=1+O(\tau^2)$, which is more
accurate than required.

If $\beta>16L$, put
\[
 m_0=\left\lfloor\frac{L-\log(16L)}{\tau}\right\rfloor.
\]
Eventually $m_0\geq N$, $m\leq m_0$, and
$S_0(m_0)\geq8L$.  Conditioning and the point-probability bounds give
\[
 \Pbb(M_n\leq m)\leq\Pbb(M_n\leq m_0)
 \leq C e^{-S_0(m_0)}=O(\tau^8).
\]
For $Q_n$, use $S_j(m)\leq B_j$ and Lemma~\ref{ncy:gb:lem:cumulants} to bound
the absolute value of its bracket by $C\tau^{-1}$.  Hence
$|Q_n(m)|\leq C\tau^{-1}e^{-8L}=O(\tau^7)$, including cutoffs below
$N$.  Both errors are negligible.  The central estimate and the two tails
prove the asserted uniform bound.  Finally
$\tau^{3/2}L^3=o(\tau)$ because $\sqrt\tau L^3\to0$.
\end{proof}

\section{The universal lattice expansion}\label{ncy:gb:sec:lattice}

We now extract the explicit formulas from the tail-moment estimate.
The argument keeps the fractional part of the cutoff. At precision
\(o(\tau)\), replacing an integer cutoff by a real one without this
term changes the answer.

\begin{lemma}[Geometric tail moments]\label{ncy:gb:lem:tails}
In the growing central range
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:gb:eq:central-range}
 \tau^2\le\beta=e^{-x}\le16L,
\end{equation}
one has \(q=L+x\asymp L\) and, for \(j=1,2,3\),
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:gb:eq:tail-polys}
 S_j(m)=\beta\tau^{-j}R_j(q)
 +O\left(\tau\beta\tau^{-j}(1+q)^j\right),
\end{equation}
where
\[
 R_1(q)=q+1,\quad R_2(q)=q^2+2q+2,\quad
 R_3(q)=q^3+3q^2+6q+6.
\]
In addition,
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:gb:eq:S0-lattice}
 S_0(m)=\beta+
 \tau\beta\left(\theta_n(x)-\frac12\right)
 +O\left(\frac{\tau\beta}{L}+\tau^2\beta\right).
\end{equation}
All estimates are uniform in \eqref{ncy:gb:eq:central-range}.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For small \(\tau\), \eqref{ncy:gb:eq:central-range} gives
\(L-\log(16L)\le q\le L+2\log(1/\tau)\), so \(L/2\le q\le4L\).
The constant-weight tail is an elementary exponential sum. Comparing it
with the integral gives
\[
 c\sum_{k>m}k^je^{-\tau k}
 =c\tau^{-j-1}\Gamma(j+1,\tau m)
 +O\left(e^{-\tau m}(1+m)^j\right).
\]
For an integer \(j\ge0\),
\(\Gamma(j+1,s)=e^{-s}j!\sum_{\ell=0}^j s^\ell/\ell!\).
The identity \(\tau m=q-\tau\theta_n(x)\), together with
\(ce^{-q}/\tau=\beta\), gives \eqref{ncy:gb:eq:tail-polys}.
The perturbation \(|p_k-c|\le C/k\) contributes at most the
corresponding tail with one lower power of \(k\); its relative
size here is \(O(\tau/L)\), which is within the displayed error.

For \(S_0\) the constant-weight sum is exact:
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:gb:eq:exact-geometric-tail}
 c\sum_{k>m}e^{-\tau k}
 =\beta\,\frac{\tau}{e^\tau-1}\,e^{\tau\theta_n(x)}.
\end{equation}
Since \(0\le\theta_n(x)<1\),
\[
 \frac{\tau}{e^\tau-1}e^{\tau\theta_n(x)}
 =1+\tau\left(\theta_n(x)-\frac12\right)+O(\tau^2)
\]
uniformly. The weight perturbation is at most
\(C\sum_{k>m}e^{-\tau k}/k=O(e^{-q}/q)=O(\tau\beta/L)\).
This proves \eqref{ncy:gb:eq:S0-lattice}.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Uniform lattice law]\label{ncy:gb:thm:lattice}
Under \eqref{ncy:gb:eq:weight-hyp}, the approximation \(U_n\) in
\eqref{ncy:gb:eq:Un} satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:gb:eq:lattice-bound}
 \norminf{F_n-U_n}=O(\tau/L).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Apply Theorem~\ref{ncy:gb:thm:tailmoment} and Lemma~\ref{ncy:gb:lem:tails}.
Lemma~\ref{ncy:gb:lem:cumulants} gives
\(V=2c\tau^{-3}(1+O(\tau))\) and
\(B_3=6c\tau^{-4}(1+O(\tau))\).
Consequently, in \eqref{ncy:gb:eq:central-range},
\begin{align}
 \frac{S_2-S_1^2}{2V}
 -\frac{B_3S_1}{2V^2}
 ={}&\frac{\tau}{4c}
 \left[\beta(q^2+2q+2)-\beta^2(q+1)^2
       -3\beta(q+1)\right]+\cE_n(x)\notag\\
 ={}&\frac{\tau}{4c}
 \left[(\beta-\beta^2)q^2-(\beta+2\beta^2)q-\beta-\beta^2\right]
 +\cE_n(x),
 \label{ncy:gb:eq:poly-algebra}
\end{align}
where
\(e^{-\beta/2}|\cE_n(x)|=O(\tau^2L^2)\).
The coefficient \(-3\beta(q+1)\) comes from the skewness term.
Omitting it would leave an error of order \(\tau L\).

Exponentiating \eqref{ncy:gb:eq:S0-lattice} yields
\[
 e^{-S_0}
 =G(x)+\tau\left(\frac12-\theta_n(x)\right)G(x)\beta
 +O(\tau/L)
\]
uniformly in the central range. To see the uniformity directly,
the perturbation of \(S_0\) is \(o(1)\) there, and the remainders are
bounded by fixed multiples of
\[
 e^{-\beta/2}
 \left(\frac{\tau\beta}{L}+\tau^2\beta+\tau^2\beta^2\right).
\]
Each polynomial in \(\beta\) times \(e^{-\beta/2}\) is bounded.
Products with \eqref{ncy:gb:eq:poly-algebra} cost
\(O(\tau^2L^2)\). The Fourier remainder is
\(O(\tau^{3/2}L^3)\). Both are \(o(\tau/L)\).
This proves \eqref{ncy:gb:eq:lattice-bound} on the central range.

For completeness, the extension is uniform on the entire real line.
If \(\beta<\tau^2\), the Poisson single-component identity and
the local concentration bounds in Section~\ref{ncy:gb:sec:fourier} give
\[
 1-F_n(x)\le C\sum_{k>m}a_k\le C\beta.
\]
The upper tails of \(G\) and the displayed corrections in \(U_n\)
are \(O(\tau^2)\) in this range. If \(\beta>16L\), use the cutoff
at \(\beta=16L\). Formula~\eqref{ncy:gb:eq:conditioning} and the common
local upper and lower bounds give probability \(O(e^{-8L})\)
there. Monotonicity transfers that bound farther left. Every
explicit term of \(U_n\) is bounded by a polynomial in
\(L,\beta,|\log\beta|\) times \(e^{-\beta}\), hence is also
negligible on this tail. This includes negative integer cutoffs.
\end{proof}

\section{The sharp ordinary error and the affine obstruction}
\label{ncy:gb:sec:affine}

Define
\[
 H(x)=G'(x)(1-e^{-x})=G(x)e^{-x}(1-e^{-x}).
\]
Since \(q=L-\log\beta\), extracting the \(L^2\) term from
\eqref{ncy:gb:eq:Un} gives
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:gb:eq:leading-profile}
 F_n(x)=G(x)+\delta H(x)+O(\tau L)
\end{equation}
uniformly. Indeed, for every fixed nonnegative integer \(r\) and
positive integer \(j\),
\[
 \sup_{\beta>0}e^{-\beta}\beta^j|\log\beta|^r<\infty.
\]
All remaining polynomial terms are therefore \(O(\tau L)\);
the fractional-part term is \(O(\tau)\).

\begin{proposition}[Exact leading Kolmogorov constant]\label{ncy:gb:prop:kappa}
Let
\[
 \beta_+=\frac{3+\sqrt5}{2},\qquad
 \kappa=(2+\sqrt5)e^{-\beta_+}=0.309004785987676\ldots.
\]
Then
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:gb:eq:sharp-ordinary}
 \DK(F_n,G)=\kappa\delta+O(\tau L)
 \sim \frac{\kappa}{16\sqrt c}\frac{\log^2 n}{\sqrt n}.
\end{equation}
The same leading equivalent holds for the elementary normalization
\(\sqrt{c/n}\,M_n-\frac12\log(cn)\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The triangle inequality in the supremum norm applied to
\eqref{ncy:gb:eq:leading-profile} gives
\(\DK(F_n,G)=\delta\norminf H+O(\tau L)\).
For \(\beta>0\),
\(|H|=e^{-\beta}\beta|1-\beta|\).
Its two interior stationary points are
\(\beta_\pm=(3\pm\sqrt5)/2\). Their values are
\((\sqrt5-2)e^{-\beta_-}\) and
\((2+\sqrt5)e^{-\beta_+}\), respectively, and the latter is larger.
For example their ratio is
\((2+\sqrt5)^2e^{-\sqrt5}>1\);
this follows from \(\sqrt5<9/4\), \(e<3\), and
\(3^{9/4}<16<(2+\sqrt5)^2\).
The boundary values are zero. Hence \(\norminf H=\kappa\).

The cumulant estimate gives
\(\tau=\tau_0+O(\tau_0^2)\), \(\tau_0=\sqrt{c/n}\).
If \(L_0=\log(c/\tau_0)\), then
\[
 \tau M_n-L
 =a(\tau_0M_n-L_0)+b,\qquad
 a=\tau/\tau_0=1+O(\tau_0),\quad
 b=(a-1)L_0+\log a=O(\tau_0L_0).
\]
The functions \(G',xG',H',xH'\) are bounded, so this change of
normalization changes \eqref{ncy:gb:eq:leading-profile} by
\(O(\tau_0L_0)\). Finally
\(\delta\sim\log^2 n/(16\sqrt{cn})\).
\end{proof}

The error profile changes sign at \(x=0\). Its ratio to \(G'\)
is \(1-e^{-x}\), which is nonlinear. That observation is the
obstruction to eliminating the leading error by shifting and scaling.

\begin{theorem}[Best affine normalization]\label{ncy:gb:thm:affine}
Define the finite positive constant
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:gb:eq:Estar}
 E_*=\inf_{\alpha,b\in\R}
 \sup_{x\in\R}G'(x)\left|1-e^{-x}-\alpha x-b\right|.
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:gb:eq:affine-minimax}
 \inf_{a>0,\ b\in\R}
 \DK\bigl(\mathcal L(aY_n+b),G\bigr)
 =E_*\delta+o(\delta).
\end{equation}
Here \(\DK(\mathcal L(X),G)\) denotes the distance between the CDF
of \(X\) and \(G\). In particular no sequences of centers and
positive scales yield \(o(\log^2 n/\sqrt n)\) error.
A concrete bound is
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:gb:eq:Estar-bound}
 \frac{1}{2(e^2/2+2e+2\sqrt e)}
 \le E_*\le\kappa.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For fixed \(\alpha,b\), set \(a_n=1+\delta\alpha\) and
\(b_n=\delta b\). Uniform Taylor expansion of
\eqref{ncy:gb:eq:leading-profile} at \((x-b_n)/a_n\) gives
\[
 F_n((x-b_n)/a_n)-G(x)
 =\delta\{H(x)-(\alpha x+b)G'(x)\}+o(\delta).
\]
Uniformity follows from bounded weighted first and second
derivatives of \(G\) and \(H\). Taking the infimum yields the
upper bound in \eqref{ncy:gb:eq:affine-minimax}.

For the reverse inequality, take any near-minimizing affine
parameters. The identity choice already has error \(O(\delta)\).
Thus
\[
 \norminf{G((\,\cdot-b_n)/a_n)-G}=O(\delta).
\]
Evaluating at \(x=0,1\), strict monotonicity of \(G\) first gives
\(b_n\to0\), \(a_n\to1\), and then the local positive lower bound
on \(G'\) gives \(b_n=O(\delta)\), \(a_n-1=O(\delta)\).
Every subsequence has a further subsequence on which
\((a_n-1)/\delta\) and \(b_n/\delta\) converge. Divide the
pointwise expansion by \(\delta\) along that subsequence and take
a supremum to obtain at least \(E_*\). This proves the lower bound.

For positivity and the explicit estimate, put \(r=\log2\) and
\(f(x)=1-e^{-x}\). Its second difference at \(-r,0,r\) is
\[
 f(-r)-2f(0)+f(r)=-\frac12.
\]
An affine function has zero second difference. If the weighted
uniform error is \(E\), the triangle inequality gives
\[
 \frac12\le E\left(\frac1{G'(-r)}
                     +\frac2{G'(0)}+\frac1{G'(r)}\right)
 =E(e^2/2+2e+2\sqrt e).
\]
This proves the lower bound. Choosing the zero affine function
gives \(E_*\le\norminf H=\kappa\).
The objective in \eqref{ncy:gb:eq:Estar} is coercive in its two
parameters, as is already seen by evaluating at \(0\) and \(1\);
thus the infimum is attained. No numerical solution of this
auxiliary minimax problem is used.
\end{proof}

\section{Largest atoms and the obstruction to continuous approximation}
\label{ncy:gb:sec:atoms}

The lattice spacing of $Y_n=\tau M_n-L$ is $\tau$.  To determine the
accuracy attainable by a continuous approximation, we need its largest
atom.  An $O(\tau)$ approximation to the distribution function cannot be
differenced to obtain this information.  Instead, we condition directly
on the number of components of one specified size.

\begin{theorem}[Largest atom and continuous approximation]
\label{ncy:gb:thm:atom}
Under the standing assumptions,
\begin{equation}
\label{ncy:gb:eq:largest-atom}
 \max_{1\le m\le n}\Pbb(M_n=m)
   =\frac{\tau}{e}+O(\tau^2L^2).
\end{equation}
Let $\mathcal C$ be the set of continuous distribution functions on
$\R$.  Then
\begin{equation}
\label{ncy:gb:eq:continuous-minimax}
 \inf_{J\in\mathcal C}\|F_n-J\|_\infty
 =\frac12\max_m\Pbb(M_n=m)
 =\frac{\tau}{2e}+O(\tau^2L^2).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Write $a_k=p_ke^{-\tau k}$ for the Poisson means.  For an integer $m$,
put
\[
 \beta_m=\frac c\tau e^{-\tau m},\qquad
 R_m=\sum_{k<m}kZ_k,\qquad
 Q_m=S_1(m)+ma_m,\qquad
 W_m=V-S_2(m)-m^2a_m.
\]
Thus $\E R_m=n-Q_m$ and $\operatorname{Var}(R_m)=W_m$.
Independence, followed by conditioning on $Z_m$, gives the exact identity
\begin{equation}
\label{ncy:gb:eq:atom-decomposition}
 \Pbb(M_n=m)
 =e^{-S_0(m)-a_m}
   \sum_{\ell\ge1}\frac{a_m^\ell}{\ell!}
     \frac{\Pbb(R_m=n-\ell m)}{\Pbb(T=n)}.
\end{equation}
Probabilities on the right are under the independent Poisson law;
a negative target for $R_m$ has probability zero.

Fix a sufficiently large constant $B$, for example $B=16$, and first
consider the integer window
\begin{equation}
\label{ncy:gb:eq:atom-window}
 \tau^2\le\beta_m\le BL.
\end{equation}
Here $\tau m\asymp L$, uniformly, and $m-1$ eventually exceeds
$\lfloor1/\tau\rfloor$.  The uniform concentration estimate of
Lemma~\ref{ncy:gb:lem:fourierblock} and the full-law local lower bound of
Lemma~\ref{ncy:gb:lem:localmass} imply
\begin{equation}
\label{ncy:gb:eq:atom-ratio-bound}
 \sup_{\ell\ge1}
 \frac{\Pbb(R_m=n-\ell m)}{\Pbb(T=n)}\le C.
\end{equation}
The tail estimates give, uniformly in this window,
\begin{equation}
\label{ncy:gb:eq:atom-tail-estimates}
 \begin{split}
 a_m&=\tau\beta_m\bigl(1+O(\tau/L)\bigr),\qquad
 S_0(m)=\beta_m\bigl(1+O(\tau)\bigr),\\
 Q_m&=O\bigl(\beta_mL/\tau\bigr),\qquad
 \frac{V-W_m}{V}=O(\tau\beta_mL^2).
 \end{split}
\end{equation}
In particular, $a_m=o(1)$ and $W_m/V\to1$ uniformly.

The terms $\ell\ge2$ in \eqref{ncy:gb:eq:atom-decomposition} are bounded,
using \eqref{ncy:gb:eq:atom-ratio-bound}, by
\[
 C e^{-S_0(m)}e^{-a_m}(e^{a_m}-1-a_m)
 \le C e^{-S_0(m)}a_m^2
 \le C\tau^2\beta_m^2e^{-\beta_m}
 =O(\tau^2).
\]
The constant-factor comparison between $e^{-S_0(m)}$ and
$e^{-\beta_m}$ is uniform because $\tau\beta_m=O(\tau L)=o(1)$.

We record the quantitative local estimate needed for the term $\ell=1$.
Put $C_{3,m}=\sum_{k<m}k^3a_k$ and
$\phi(u)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}e^{-u^2/2}$.  Uniformly over integers $j$ and
truncations in \eqref{ncy:gb:eq:atom-window},
\begin{equation}
\label{ncy:gb:eq:atom-local-edgeworth}
 \begin{split}
 \Pbb(R_m=j)
 &=\frac{\phi(u)}{\sqrt{W_m}}
   \left[1+\frac{C_{3,m}}{6W_m^{3/2}}(u^3-3u)\right]
   +O\left(\frac{\tau}{\sqrt{W_m}}\right),\\
 u&=\frac{j-\E R_m}{\sqrt{W_m}}.
 \end{split}
\end{equation}
To justify the uniform remainder, apply the characteristic-function
expansion used in Lemma~\ref{ncy:gb:lem:localmass} to $R_m$, with its own
variance.  For $|t|\le L$, it reads
\[
 \E\exp\left(\frac{it(R_m-\E R_m)}{\sqrt{W_m}}\right)
 =e^{-t^2/2}\left[
    1-\frac{iC_{3,m}t^3}{6W_m^{3/2}}
        +O\bigl(\tau(t^4+t^6)\bigr)\right].
\]
Here $W_m\asymp\tau^{-3}$, the standardized third cumulant is
$O(\sqrt\tau)$, and the fourth-order logarithmic remainder is
$O(\tau t^4)$, all uniformly in $m$.
The common Fourier block from Lemma~\ref{ncy:gb:lem:fourierblock} bounds
the omitted arc by a quantity smaller than every fixed power of
$\tau$.  Fourier inversion of the cubic term gives
$(u^3-3u)\phi(u)$; the absolute integral of the remaining Gaussian
error is $O(\tau)$.  The external phase has modulus one for every
$j$, which proves the stated uniformity in the target integer.

For $j=n-m$, the standardized displacement in this estimate is
\[
 u_m=\frac{Q_m-m}{\sqrt{W_m}}
     =O\bigl(\sqrt\tau L(1+\beta_m)\bigr)=o(1)
\]
uniformly in \eqref{ncy:gb:eq:atom-window}.  The variance factor
differs from one by $O(\tau\beta_mL^2)$; the Gaussian factor differs
from one by $O(u_m^2)$; and the first Edgeworth term is
$O(\sqrt\tau\,|u_m|)$, since its Hermite polynomial is
$u^3-3u$.  The normalized local remainder is $O(\tau)$.
The full-law denominator has normalized value $1+O(\tau)$.
Consequently,
\begin{equation}
\label{ncy:gb:eq:single-occupancy-ratio}
 \frac{\Pbb(R_m=n-m)}{\Pbb(T=n)}
  =1+O\bigl(\tau L^2(1+\beta_m)^2\bigr).
\end{equation}
Inserting \eqref{ncy:gb:eq:atom-tail-estimates} and
\eqref{ncy:gb:eq:single-occupancy-ratio} into
\eqref{ncy:gb:eq:atom-decomposition} yields
\begin{equation}
\label{ncy:gb:eq:local-largest-component-mass}
 \Pbb(M_n=m)
  =\tau\beta_me^{-\beta_m}+O(\tau^2L^2),
\end{equation}
uniformly in the window.  Indeed, every error involving $\beta_m$
is multiplied by an exponentially decreasing factor, and
$\sup_{\beta>0}e^{-\beta}\beta(1+\beta)^2<\infty$.

The omitted atoms cannot change the maximum at this precision.  If
$\beta_m<\tau^2$, the same uniform local upper bound in
\eqref{ncy:gb:eq:atom-decomposition} gives
$\Pbb(M_n=m)\le C a_m=O(\tau^3)$.
For the other tail, let
\[
 m_- =\left\lceil\frac{L-\log(BL)}\tau\right\rceil.
\]
The exact conditioning identity for $\Pbb(M_n\le m_-)$, together
with Lemmas~\ref{ncy:gb:lem:fourierblock} and~\ref{ncy:gb:lem:localmass}, gives
\[
 \Pbb(M_n\le m_-)\le C e^{-S_0(m_-)}
   \le C e^{-BL/2}=O(\tau^{B/2}).
\]
Every $m$ with $\beta_m>BL$ lies below this threshold.

The function $\beta e^{-\beta}$ has maximum $1/e$, at $\beta=1$.
At $m=\lfloor L/\tau\rfloor$, we have
$\beta_m=e^{O(\tau)}$ and
$\beta_me^{-\beta_m}=e^{-1}+O(\tau^2)$.
Equation~\eqref{ncy:gb:eq:local-largest-component-mass} now proves
\eqref{ncy:gb:eq:largest-atom}.

For completeness, the first equality in
\eqref{ncy:gb:eq:continuous-minimax} is an elementary fact about any finitely
supported distribution.  A jump of size $w$ forces a continuous
approximant to have error at least $w/2$: its value at the jump must
be within the claimed error of both one-sided limits.
Conversely, list the support as $y_1<\cdots<y_r$, with masses $w_i$
and cumulative sums $s_i$.  Assign a continuous interpolant the values
\[
 J(y_i)=\frac{s_{i-1}+s_i}{2},\qquad s_0=0,
\]
and interpolate linearly between consecutive support points.
Connect monotonically to zero before $y_1$ and to one after $y_r$.
On $(y_i,y_{i+1})$, the original distribution function equals $s_i$,
whereas the interpolant ranges from $s_i-w_i/2$ to
$s_i+w_{i+1}/2$.  Its error is therefore at most
$\tfrac12\max_iw_i$, everywhere.  This proves the equality and
completes the theorem.
\end{proof}

An increasing bijection preserves every atom size.  Thus the lower bound
in \eqref{ncy:gb:eq:continuous-minimax} also applies after any such
normalization of the largest component.  The comparison class here is
the class of continuous distribution functions.  A formula retaining
the lattice phase, such as Theorem~\ref{ncy:gb:thm:lattice}, can have a smaller
remainder because it records the jumps explicitly.

\section{An explicit optimal continuous calibration}
\label{ncy:gb:sec:optimal-calibration}
\label{ncy:gb:sec:optimal}

Retaining the constant terms in the smooth correction produces a
continuous approximation with the best possible leading error
constant.  Define
\begin{equation}
\label{ncy:gb:eq:full-calibration}
 \begin{split}
 q&=L+x,\qquad \beta=e^{-x},\\
 h_\tau(x)
  &=x+\frac{\tau}{4c}
       \left[q^2-q-1-\beta(q+1)^2\right],\qquad
 J_n(x)=G\bigl(h_\tau(x)\bigr).
 \end{split}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Asymptotically optimal smooth calibration]
\label{ncy:gb:thm:optimal}
For $\tau/c\le1/4$, the map $h_\tau$ is an increasing bijection of
$\R$, and $J_n$ is a continuous distribution function.
Uniformly for $x\in\R$,
\begin{equation}
\label{ncy:gb:eq:calibrated-lattice-expansion}
 F_n(x)=J_n(x)
   +\tau\left(\frac12-
       \left\{\frac{L+x}{\tau}\right\}\right)G'(x)
   +O(\tau/L).
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\begin{equation}
\label{ncy:gb:eq:optimal-calibration-distance}
 \|F_n-J_n\|_\infty
  =\frac{\tau}{2e}+O(\tau/L).
\end{equation}
Equivalently,
\[
 \sup_{z\in\R}
 \left|\Pbb\bigl(h_\tau(Y_n)\le z\bigr)-G(z)\right|
 =\frac{\tau}{2e}+O(\tau/L).
\]
Together with \eqref{ncy:gb:eq:continuous-minimax}, this shows that the
explicit calibration attains the smallest possible leading constant
among continuous approximations.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
First we check monotonicity globally.  Put $a=\tau/c$ and use
$e^{-x}=(c/\tau)e^{-q}$.  Direct differentiation gives
\begin{equation}
\label{ncy:gb:eq:full-calibration-derivative}
 (h_\tau)'(x)
  =1+\frac a4(2q-1)+\frac14e^{-q}(q^2-1).
\end{equation}
For $q\ge1$, both additional terms are nonnegative.
For $-1\le q\le1$, the right side is at least
$1-3a/4-e/4>0$ when $a\le1/4$.
For $-2\le q\le-1$, it is at least $1-5a/4>0$.
Finally, for $q\le-2$, put $t=-q\ge2$.  The inequality
$e^t(t^2-1)\ge2t+1$ follows by evaluating the difference at two
and differentiating it on $[2,\infty)$.  Thus
\eqref{ncy:gb:eq:full-calibration-derivative} is at least
$1+(1-a)(2t+1)/4>0$ in this last region as well.
The limits of $h_\tau(x)$ at $-\infty$ and $+\infty$ are
$-\infty$ and $+\infty$, respectively.  This proves the first claim.

Theorem~\ref{ncy:gb:thm:lattice} gives the uniform lattice expansion with
smooth correction $G'(x)(h_\tau(x)-x)$.
On the central window $\tau^2\le\beta\le16L$, we have
$q\asymp L$ and
\[
 |h_\tau(x)-x|\le C\tau L^2(1+\beta)=o(1).
\]
Taylor's theorem and the exponential decrease of the Gumbel derivatives
give, uniformly on this window,
\[
 G\bigl(h_\tau(x)\bigr)
 =G(x)+G'(x)\bigl(h_\tau(x)-x\bigr)+O(\tau^2L^4).
\]
For example, the second-order remainder is bounded by a constant times
$\tau^2L^4e^{-\beta/2}\beta(1+\beta)^3$, whose supremum is finite.

Here are explicit controls for the complementary tails.  At the right
endpoint $x_+=-2\log\tau$, the correction
$h_\tau(x_+)-x_+$ is eventually positive, and
$1-G(x_+)\le\tau^2$.  Monotonicity therefore gives
$1-J_n(x)\le\tau^2$ for $x\ge x_+$.
On that interval,
\[
 \bigl|G'(x)(h_\tau(x)-x)\bigr|
  \le C\tau e^{-x}\bigl(1+(L+x)^2\bigr)
  \le C\tau^3L^2.
\]
The last inequality follows by differentiating the exponentially
weighted polynomial, which is decreasing there.  Thus the Taylor
replacement costs $O(\tau^2)$ on the right tail.

At the left endpoint $x_-=-\log(16L)$, the correction is
$O(\tau L^3)=o(1)$.  Hence
$J_n(x_-)\le e^{-8L}$ for sufficiently small $\tau$,
and monotonicity extends this bound to $x\le x_-$.
On the same interval $G(x)\le e^{-16L}$, while
\[
 \bigl|G'(x)(h_\tau(x)-x)\bigr|
 \le C\tau e^{-\beta}\beta(1+\beta)
                   \bigl(1+(L-\log\beta)^2\bigr)
 \le C\tau(1+L^2)e^{-8L}.
\]
The polynomial and logarithmic factors in the middle expression are
absorbed by half of its exponential factor.  This proves the required
replacement on the left tail as well.  Since
$\tau^2L^4=o(\tau/L)$,
Theorem~\ref{ncy:gb:thm:lattice} proves
\eqref{ncy:gb:eq:calibrated-lattice-expansion}.

The fractional part belongs to $[0,1)$ and
$\max_xG'(x)=1/e$, so the displayed expansion gives the upper bound
in \eqref{ncy:gb:eq:optimal-calibration-distance}.
At
\[
 x_\tau=\tau\lfloor L/\tau\rfloor-L,
\]
the fractional part is zero, $x_\tau=O(\tau)$, and
$G'(x_\tau)=e^{-1}+O(\tau^2)$.
Substitution gives the matching lower bound.
Finally, the bijection $z=h_\tau(x)$ identifies the supremum
distance for the transformed variable with $\|F_n-J_n\|_\infty$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Uniform central quantiles}

The calibration also locates integer quantiles within a bounded number
of component-size units.  This is finer than the leading Gumbel
normalization: the correction to the largest size is generally of
order $L^2$.

\begin{corollary}[Quantiles with bounded integer error]
\label{ncy:gb:cor:central-quantiles}
Fix $0<\eta<1/2$.  For $p\in[\eta,1-\eta]$, let
\[
 Q_n(p)=\min\{m\in\Z:\Pbb(M_n\le m)\ge p\},\qquad
 x_p=-\log(-\log p),\qquad \beta_p=-\log p.
\]
Uniformly over this interval of probabilities,
\begin{equation}
\label{ncy:gb:eq:central-quantile-expansion}
 \begin{split}
 Q_n(p)={}&\frac{L+x_p}{\tau}\\
 &-\frac{(L+x_p)^2-(L+x_p)-1
              -\beta_p(L+x_p+1)^2}{4c}
   +O_\eta(1).
 \end{split}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Let $y_p=(h_\tau)^{-1}(x_p)$, so $J_n(y_p)=p$.
The numbers $x_p$ lie in a fixed compact interval, uniformly in $p$.
On a slightly larger interval,
\[
 h_\tau(x)-x=O_\eta(\tau L^2),\qquad
 (h_\tau)'(x)=1+O_\eta(\tau L^2).
\]
In particular, $h_\tau(x_p-1)<x_p<h_\tau(x_p+1)$ for all
sufficiently large $n$, uniformly in $p$.
Monotonicity places all $y_p$ in the larger interval, and the derivative
bound then gives $y_p-x_p=O_\eta(\tau L^2)$.
Applying the mean value theorem to $h_\tau(x)-x$ yields
\begin{equation}
\label{ncy:gb:eq:inverse-calibration-quantile}
 y_p=x_p-\frac{\tau}{4c}
  \left[(L+x_p)^2-(L+x_p)-1
                  -\beta_p(L+x_p+1)^2\right]
  +O_\eta(\tau^2L^4).
\end{equation}

Theorem~\ref{ncy:gb:thm:optimal} gives
$\|F_n-J_n\|_\infty\le C\tau$.
On the compact interval just used, $J_n'$ is bounded below by
a positive constant depending only on $\eta$ for all sufficiently
large $n$.  Put $u_p=\tau Q_n(p)-L$.
The defining inequalities for the integer quantile are
\[
 F_n(u_p)\ge p,\qquad F_n(u_p-\tau)<p.
\]
Consequently,
\[
 J_n(u_p)\ge p-C\tau,\qquad
 J_n(u_p-\tau)<p+C\tau.
\]
For large $n$, both $p-C\tau$ and $p+C\tau$ lie in
$[\eta/2,1-\eta/2]$.  Their $J_n$-quantiles remain in a fixed compact
interval on which the same derivative lower bound applies.  Inverting
the two inequalities therefore yields
\[
 J_n^{-1}(p-C\tau)\le u_p
 <\tau+J_n^{-1}(p+C\tau),
\]
and consequently $u_p=y_p+O_\eta(\tau)$, uniformly in $p$.
Divide \eqref{ncy:gb:eq:inverse-calibration-quantile} by $\tau$ and use
$\tau L^4\to0$.  This proves
\eqref{ncy:gb:eq:central-quantile-expansion}, including its bounded integer
error.
\end{proof}

\section{Beyond the lattice scale: the component correction}
\label{ncy:gb:sec:refinement}

The universal lattice formula has error \(O(\tau/L)\).
This scale records the first departure of \(p_k\) from the limiting
constant \(c\). The stronger hypothesis \eqref{ncy:gb:eq:refined-hyp}
allows that departure to be calculated explicitly.

\begin{theorem}[Exponential-integral refinement]\label{ncy:gb:thm:e1}
Assume \eqref{ncy:gb:eq:refined-hyp}. Define
\[
 \widetilde U_n(x)
 =U_n(x)-a_*G(x)E_1(\max\{1,L+x\}).
\]
Then
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:gb:eq:e1-error}
 \norminf{F_n-\widetilde U_n}=O(\tau^{3/2}L^3).
\end{equation}
Moreover, uniformly on \(\R\),
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:gb:eq:sharp-component-profile}
 F_n(x)-U_n(x)
 =-\frac{a_*}{c}\frac{\tau}{L}G'(x)
 +O\left(\frac{\tau}{L^2}+\tau^{3/2}L^3\right).
\end{equation}
Thus, if \(a_*\ne0\),
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:gb:eq:sharp-lattice-error}
 \norminf{F_n-U_n}\sim\frac{|a_*|}{ce}\frac{\tau}{L}.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Work first in \eqref{ncy:gb:eq:central-range}. Monotone sum--integral
comparison gives
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:gb:eq:harmonic-tail}
 \sum_{k>m}\frac{e^{-\tau k}}k
 =E_1(q)+O\left(\frac{\tau^2\beta}{L}\right).
\end{equation}
Indeed, the discrepancy at the lower endpoint \(\tau m\) is
\(O(e^{-\tau m}/m)\), and moving that endpoint to \(q\) costs
\(O(\tau e^{-q}/q)\). Both have the displayed size because
\(q\asymp L\) and \(e^{-q}=\tau\beta/c\).

The residual weight term is bounded by
\begin{align*}
 \sum_{k>m}\frac{\log(k+1)}{k^2}e^{-\tau k}
 &\le C\tau\int_{\tau m}^\infty
 \frac{e^{-u}\log(1+u/\tau)}{u^2}\dd u
 +O\left(\frac{e^{-\tau m}\log(m+1)}{m^2}\right)\\
 &=O(\tau^2\beta/L).
\end{align*}
Here \(\log(1+q/\tau)=O(L)\) on the central range.
Together with \eqref{ncy:gb:eq:exact-geometric-tail}, this proves
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:gb:eq:S0-refined}
 S_0=\beta+\tau\beta(\theta_n(x)-1/2)
       +a_*E_1(q)+O(\tau^2\beta).
\end{equation}
Exponentiate this identity in the tail-moment formula.
The linear contributions are the lattice term in \(U_n\) and
\(-a_*G(x)E_1(q)\). Quadratic products in the exponential are
\(O(\tau^2)\) after multiplication by \(e^{-\beta/2}\).
The replacements of \(S_1,S_2,B_2,B_3\) by
\eqref{ncy:gb:eq:poly-algebra}, and their products with
\eqref{ncy:gb:eq:S0-refined}, cost \(O(\tau^2L^2)\).
All these errors are within
\(O(\tau^{3/2}L^3)\), the error of
Theorem~\ref{ncy:gb:thm:tailmoment}. This proves
\eqref{ncy:gb:eq:e1-error} centrally.

On the right tail \(\beta<\tau^2\), both the true upper tail
and \(1-U_n\) are \(O(\tau^2)\), and
\(G E_1(q)=O(\tau\beta/q)\). On the left tail
\(\beta>16L\), the probability and all explicit polynomial
corrections are negligible, as in Theorem~\ref{ncy:gb:thm:lattice}.
The cutoff at \(1\) bounds the additional \(E_1\) factor in the
remote region where \(q\) is not positive. This proves
\eqref{ncy:gb:eq:e1-error} globally. The cutoff is a convention to
define the approximation there; it has no effect at the claimed
error scale.

Integration by parts gives
\[
 E_1(q)=\frac{e^{-q}}q+O(e^{-q}/q^2).
\]
On the central range \(q=L-\log\beta\ge L/2\), so
\[
 \left|\frac1q-\frac1L\right|
 \le \frac{2|\log\beta|}{L^2}.
\]
Boundedness of \(G\beta|\log\beta|\) therefore yields
\[
 G(x)E_1(q)=\frac{\tau}{cL}G'(x)+O(\tau/L^2).
\]
The same statement holds on the remote tails by exponential
decay and the cutoff convention. Combining it with
\eqref{ncy:gb:eq:e1-error} proves
\eqref{ncy:gb:eq:sharp-component-profile}. Since
\(\norminf{G'}=1/e\) and
\(\tau^{3/2}L^3=o(\tau/L)\), the sharp equivalent follows.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Why the exponential integral is useful}

Repeated integration by parts gives, for each fixed \(r\ge1\),
\[
 E_1(q)=\frac{e^{-q}}q
 \left(\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\frac{(-1)^j j!}{q^j}
       +O_r(q^{-r})\right),\qquad q\to\infty.
\]
Consequently the single \(E_1\) term retains an entire hierarchy
of inverse-logarithmic corrections of sizes
\(\tau/L,\tau/L^2,\ldots\).
For every fixed \(r\),
\(\tau^{3/2}L^3=o(\tau/L^r)\).
This assertion is a comparison of fixed error scales; it does
not assert convergence of the factorial asymptotic series or
uniformity in a truncation order growing with \(n\).

\section{Nested-cycle partitions and explicit normalizations}
\label{ncy:gb:sec:application}

\subsection{The combinatorial meaning}

A permutation whose cycle lengths are distinct has component
EGF
\[
 P(z)=\prod_{k\ge1}(1+z^k/k).
\]
For each cycle length \(k\), either no cycle is selected or one
labelled cycle is selected; a cycle has EGF \(z^k/k\).
A nonempty such permutation is an outer component. Taking a
labelled set of those outer components gives
\(\exp(P(z)-1)\), exactly \eqref{ncy:gb:eq:nested-egf}.
Thus
\[
 p_k=[z^k]P(z),\qquad k!p_k\in\Z_{\ge0},
\]
and \eqref{ncy:gb:eq:assembly-law} is the spectrum of a uniform
nested-cycle partition of an \(n\)-element label set.

The initial total counts are
\[
 1,\ 1,\ 2,\ 9,\ 44,\ 270,\ 2064,\ 17682,\ 171296,\ 1867968,
 \ 22470840,\ldots,
\]
in agreement with A308338 \cite{oeis308338}.
By the credited estimate \eqref{ncy:gb:eq:known-p},
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:gb:eq:cycle-constants}
 c=a_*=e^{-\gamma}=0.561459483566885\ldots,\qquad
 \sqrt c=e^{-\gamma/2}=0.749306001288449\ldots.
\end{equation}
All preceding theorems therefore apply.

\begin{corollary}[Sharp scales for nested-cycle maxima]\label{ncy:gb:cor:nested}
For a uniform nested-cycle partition of size \(n\), let \(M_n\)
be its largest outer component. Then
\begin{align}
 \DK(F_n,G)
 &\sim \frac{e^{\gamma/2}\kappa}{16}
       \frac{\log^2n}{\sqrt n},\label{ncy:gb:eq:nested-raw}\\
 \DK(F_n,J_n)
 &\sim \frac{e^{-\gamma/2}}{2e}\,n^{-1/2},\label{ncy:gb:eq:nested-smooth}\\
 \norminf{F_n-U_n}
 &\sim \frac{2e^{-\gamma/2}}{e}\,
       \frac1{\sqrt n\log n},\label{ncy:gb:eq:nested-lattice}\\
 \norminf{F_n-\widetilde U_n}
 &=O(n^{-3/4}\log^3n).\label{ncy:gb:eq:nested-e1}
\end{align}
The constant in \eqref{ncy:gb:eq:nested-smooth} is asymptotically
optimal among continuous CDF approximations.
\end{corollary}

The largest component has scale \(\sqrt n\log n\), with

\subsection{A two-term explicit saddle}

The exact saddle is simple to compute, but an elementary
normalization is useful for closed formulas.

\begin{proposition}[Explicit saddle to the needed precision]
\label{ncy:gb:prop:explicit-saddle}
Under \eqref{ncy:gb:eq:refined-hyp}, if \(\tau_0=\sqrt{c/n}\), then
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:gb:eq:saddle-expansion}
 \tau=\tau_0+\frac{a_*}{2c}\tau_0^2
       +O(\tau_0^3\log^2(1/\tau_0)).
\end{equation}
For nested cycles this is
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:gb:eq:explicit-cycle-saddle}
 \widehat\tau_n=\sqrt{\frac cn}+\frac{c}{2n},
 \qquad
 \tau-\widehat\tau_n=O(n^{-3/2}\log^2n).
\end{equation}
Using \(\widehat\tau_n\) in place of \(\tau\), and
\(\widehat L_n=\log(c/\widehat\tau_n)\) in place of \(L\),
preserves the sharp continuous conclusion
\eqref{ncy:gb:eq:nested-smooth}, with remainder \(O(\widehat\tau_n/\widehat L_n)\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Summing the refined weight hypothesis gives
\[
 B_1(t)=\frac{c}{t^2}+\frac{a_*}{t}
        +O(\log^2(1/t)).
\]
Indeed, the residual sum is bounded by a constant times
\(\sum_{k\ge1}\log(k+1)e^{-tk}/k=O(\log^2(1/t))\).
Substitute \(t=\tau_0+(a_*/(2c))\tau_0^2\);
the terms of order \(\tau_0^{-1}\) cancel.
The derivative \(-B_2(t)\asymp-\tau_0^{-3}\) near the root,
so the mean value theorem gives \eqref{ncy:gb:eq:saddle-expansion}.

The relative error in \(\tau\) is \(O(\tau_0^2L_0^2)\).
The induced affine change in the normalized coordinate, on the
relevant central range, is \(O(\tau_0^2L_0^3)\).
The explicit smooth function \(G\circ h\) has bounded weighted
derivatives there, and its dependence on \(\tau,L\) costs the
same or less. The two tails are handled by monotonicity and
exponential decay, as before. Since
\(\tau_0^2L_0^3=o(\tau_0/L_0)\), the asserted precision is
preserved.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The phase must remain exact]\label{ncy:gb:rem:phase}
The preceding replacement is justified for continuous
approximations. A fractional part is discontinuous. Even a tiny
error in the saddle can cross an integer cutoff, changing a
lattice approximation by order \(\tau\).
Therefore \eqref{ncy:gb:eq:Un}, \eqref{ncy:gb:eq:e1-error}, and the exact
tail-moment approximation are stated with the exact saddle and
the exact cutoff. One can alternatively implement the
tail-moment approximation directly at a specified integer \(m\),
which avoids evaluating a fractional part numerically.
\end{remark}

\subsection{A second concrete model: forests of paths}

For labelled forests whose connected components are paths,
there is one singleton structure and \(k!/2\) labelled paths on
\(k\ge2\) vertices. Thus
\[
 p_1=1,\qquad p_k=\frac12\quad(k\ge2),\qquad
 C(z):=\sum_{k\ge1}p_kz^k
 =\frac{z}{2(1-z)}+\frac z2.
\]
This is \eqref{ncy:gb:eq:weight-hyp} with \(c=1/2\), and
\eqref{ncy:gb:eq:refined-hyp} with \(a_*=0\). A first-order maximum
law is already known \cite{BMWconf,BMW}.
The universal lattice approximation here has the improved
remainder \(O(\tau^{3/2}L^3)\), because the \(1/k\) coefficient
vanishes. The rational component series also permits numerical
checks at very large \(n\) without enumerating the objects.

\section{Reproducible calculations and their scope}\label{ncy:gb:sec:computation}

The proofs establish the asymptotic statements. The computations
serve two additional purposes: checking the combinatorial model
against exact integers, and checking the signs and sizes of the
probability corrections against independent coefficient calculations.

\subsection{Exact integer and rational checks}

Write \(B_k^{\rm comp}=k!p_k\), an integer for nested cycles.
The exponential formula gives the recurrence
\begin{equation}\label{ncy:gb:eq:count-recurrence}
 A_0=1,\qquad
 A_n=\sum_{k=1}^n\binom{n-1}{k-1}B_k^{\rm comp}A_{n-k}.
\end{equation}
Restricting the sum to \(k\le m\) gives \(A_n^{(\le m)}\), and
\[
 \Pbb(M_n\le m)=A_n^{(\le m)}/A_n
\]
exactly. The supplied code computes \(p_k\) from the finite
product through the needed degree using rational arithmetic,
then uses \eqref{ncy:gb:eq:count-recurrence} with integers.

The first 22 total counts were checked against the displayed
OEIS data. Six exact rational CDF values at \(n=50\) were compared
with a full-circle Fourier calculation; the maximum numerical
difference was \(1.83\times10^{-14}\).
The fractions themselves are retained in
\texttt{data/verification.json}.

\subsection{Numerical Fourier calculations}

For larger \(n\), the code evaluates the coefficient ratio
\eqref{ncy:gb:eq:conditioning} on a saddle circle. Nested-cycle
coefficients are computed by multiplying
\(1+z^k/k\) through a finite degree, and the exponentially small
weighted tail is cut off. A second implementation uses the
rational path-forest generating function
\[
 C_m(z)=C(z)-\frac{z^{m+1}}{2(1-z)}\qquad(m\ge1).
\]
It calculates the same coefficient ratio directly without
nested-cycle coefficients.

Comparisons with continuous CDFs include \emph{both ends} of
each examined lattice step. Looking only at the value just after
a jump can understate the continuous Kolmogorov distance.
For nested cycles every integer threshold in the displayed
central window is examined. The larger path-forest cases use a
dense normalized grid and additional points near the limiting
extrema; their recorded errors are sampled maxima.

\section{Further research questions}\label{ncy:gb:sec:future}

The present estimates settle a quantitative problem for a
specific, broad weight class. They also isolate several
well-defined next steps.

\begin{enumerate}
\item\label{ncy:gb:q:nextconst} \textbf{A second correction to the optimal continuous constant.}
Determine the next term in
\(\DK(F_n,G\circ h_\tau)\). The sawtooth and the location of the
largest atom interact at a smaller scale, so the correction may
depend on a phase subsequence. For nested cycles the
\(1/k\) component term already gives a systematic contribution
of order \(\tau/L\).

\item\label{ncy:gb:q:calibration} \textbf{An optimal nonlinear calibration beyond first order.}
The explicit \(h_\tau\) attains the continuous minimax constant
to first order. Can another explicit smooth transformation
track half the largest jump through a full expansion? The
abstract midpoint interpolant attains the exact minimax error,
but uses the unknown finite distribution.

\item\label{ncy:gb:q:affine} \textbf{Determine the affine minimax constant \(E_*\).}
Theorem~\ref{ncy:gb:thm:affine} reduces this question to a
two-dimensional weighted uniform approximation problem.
A certified equioscillation calculation could identify the
optimal center and scale corrections and provide a rigorous
numerical value of \(E_*\).

\item\label{ncy:gb:q:remainder} \textbf{Improve the discrete remainder.}
The \(O(\tau^{3/2}L^3)\) bound is sufficient to separate the
lattice and component scales. It is not claimed optimal.
Retaining further terms in the paired integral may yield
\(O(\tau^2L^r)\) with an explicit polynomial, or a systematic
fixed-order expansion. Remainders must still be controlled
uniformly on both extreme tails.

\item\label{ncy:gb:q:several} \textbf{Several largest components.}
Develop a joint point-process expansion for the largest
\(r\) components, with fixed \(r\), and identify the
conditioning-induced dependence among their limiting
Poisson points. The one-cutoff tail moments suggest a
finite-dimensional covariance correction.

\item\label{ncy:gb:q:expansive} \textbf{Broader expansive weights.}
For \(p_k\sim c k^{\alpha-1}\), \(\alpha>0\), the first-order
Gumbel law is known \cite{PR}. Determine the analogue of the
signed error profile, the best affine rate, and the
continuous minimax calibration for \(\alpha\ne1\).
Slowly varying factors may compete with conditioning at
different scales.

\item\label{ncy:gb:q:arithmetic} \textbf{Arithmetic support restrictions.}
The present assumption forces eventual positivity at every
integer size and hence gives a particularly direct Fourier
bound. If weights are supported on one or several residue
classes, identify the correct lattice span, admissible sizes,
and phase terms. The atom obstruction should change with
the span.

\item\label{ncy:gb:q:effective} \textbf{Effective finite constants.}
Replace the asymptotic \(O\)-constants by explicit expressions
in \(c\), a bound for \(k|p_k-c|\), and an onset of positivity.
Combined with interval evaluation of the saddle and Fourier
integrals, this would give finite-\(n\) certified probability
bounds.

\item\label{ncy:gb:q:periodic} \textbf{Root-of-unity component oscillations.}
The fuller expansion of \(p_k\) for distinct-cycle-length
permutations contains periodic terms \cite{hybrid}.
At what order do those oscillations enter the largest-component
law, and how do they interact with the outer cutoff phase?

\item\label{ncy:gb:q:quantiles} \textbf{Moment and quantile refinements.}
The proved central quantile formula has bounded error in
integer size. Extend this to moving probabilities \(u=u_n\),
and derive comparably precise mean and variance expansions
for \(M_n\), with integrable error bounds rather than just a
uniform CDF estimate.

\item\label{ncy:gb:q:formal} \textbf{Formalization.}
A useful first formal target is the analytic transfer from
the tail-moment estimate to the explicit profiles and minimax
constants. The longer probabilistic target is the common
Fourier concentration bound and the paired-integral
remainder. Exact count computations should be connected to
their combinatorial semantics by a proved checker.
\end{enumerate}

Symbol & Meaning\\

\(\widetilde U_n\) & Lattice approximation with the \(E_1\) term\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

The scale comparisons used repeatedly are, for any fixed
nonnegative integer \(r\),
\[
 \tau L^r\longrightarrow0,\qquad
 \tau^{1/2}L^r\longrightarrow0.
\]
In particular,
\[
 \tau^{3/2}L^3=o(\tau/L^r),\qquad
 \tau^2L^4=o(\tau/L).
\]
These are asymptotic statements with fixed \(r\). They provide
no uniform assertion when \(r\) grows with \(n\).

The common integration window is
\(|u|\le L\), with \(u=\sqrt V\,\vartheta\).
The common extreme-value window is
\(\tau^2\le e^{-x}\le16L\).
Keeping these two variables separate avoids conflating the
Fourier scale with the extreme-value location.
