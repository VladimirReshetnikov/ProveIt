\title{\color{inkblue}\textbf{The Cubic Boundary for Restricted Partitions}\\[5pt]
\large Sharp volume laws, uniform all-order expansions,\\
exponentially small corrections, inverse asymptotics,\\
and a Poisson law for repeated parts}
\author{Research report prepared for Vladimir Reshetnikov}
\date{1 October 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle
\begin{abstract}
Let $p_m(N)$ count partitions of $N$ into parts at most $m$, equivalently
partitions into at most $m$ parts. We give an elementary necessary and sufficient
condition for the volume approximation:
\[
 p_m(N)\sim\frac{N^{m-1}}{m!(m-1)!}
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{N}{m^3}\longrightarrow\infty,
 \qquad m\longrightarrow\infty.
\]
This proves, and sharpens, a condition still described as conjectural in
OEIS A238016 and A258670. The sufficiency has classical antecedents and is not
claimed as a new discovery. At the critical scale $N\sim c m^3$, the ratio to
volume tends to $e^{1/(4c)}$. We prove a centered expansion uniform for
$N\ge c_0m^3$, give an all-order coefficient algorithm and five explicit
corrections for A238608, and derive a second expansion that resolves actual
exponential sectors for $N=q^m$. Both types are inverted, with careful
separation of smooth asymptotic charts from integer-valued inverse functions.
In the conjugate model with at most $m$ parts, the number of equal adjacent
parts converges to $\Pois(1/(2c))$; its probability generating function has an
explicit first finite-size correction. Exact computation checks 258 samples,
including all displayed initial A238608 values, and the package supplies
symbolic derivation and numerical verification code.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Status.} This is a self-contained proof-and-extension report.
Its leading partition formulas and first-wave machinery have substantial

exhaustive priority claim is made. The proofs have not been independently

\section{The OEIS problem, the source audit, and the scope of the results}\label{rpc:sec:intro}

\subsection{One fundamental family, rather than unrelated sequences}

Throughout, $m$ and $N$ are positive integers and $m\to\infty$. Define
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:eq:gf}
 F_m(q)=\prod_{j=1}^m\frac1{1-q^j},\qquad
 p_m(N)=[q^N]F_m(q),\qquad
 V_m(N)=\frac{N^{m-1}}{m!(m-1)!}.
\end{equation}
The equivalence between the two partition interpretations follows by
conjugating Ferrers diagrams. This is a standard, widely useful family of
restricted partitions, not an isolated artificial recurrence. The same
analytic problem occurs when the size $N$ grows polynomially, exponentially,
or factorially with the allowed number $m$ of parts.

The following are the specific OEIS targets inspected for this report.
The descriptions and posted leading formulas are recorded in the respective
entries \cite{oeis238016,oeis238608,oeis238010,oeis258670}.

The general condition in A238016 is expressed using the nonstandard notation
$f(m)\ge O(m^4)$. Interpreting the intended assertion as growth at least a
positive constant times $m^4$, it asserts $p_m(f(m))\sim V_m(f(m))$.
The exact criterion in Theorem~\ref{rpc:thm:iff} is instead $f(m)/m^3\to\infty$.
It covers, for example, $f(m)=\lceil m^3\log m\rceil$, not merely quartic growth.

\subsection{What is classical, and what this report actually adds}

A condition remaining conjectural on an OEIS page does not establish that
it is an open problem in the research literature. Canfield's account of
Szekeres' formula records the Erd\H{o}s--Lehner estimate
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:eq:classical}
 p_m(N)=\frac1{m!}\binom{N-1}{m-1}
          \exp\!\left(O(m^3/N)\right),\qquad m=O(N^{1/3}),
\end{equation}
in Step 5 and explains its composition-counting origin
\cite[pp.~4, 9--10]{canfield}. In particular, the sufficiency of
$N/m^3\to\infty$ is already a classical consequence. The critical leading
constant for $N=c m^3$ is explicitly posted in A238608 \cite{oeis238608}.

The polynomial part of a restricted partition function, called its first
Sylvester wave, is also classical. Dilcher and Vignat give explicit formulas
using higher-order Bernoulli polynomials \cite{dilchervignat}. Sills and
Zeilberger give an algorithmic quasipolynomial approach \cite{sillszeilberger};
O'Sullivan studies waves and their asymptotics in a different joint-growth
regime \cite{osullivan}.

The contribution here is a complete, reusable treatment of the following
more precise package: finite elementary inequalities proving necessity as
well as sufficiency; a uniform all-order theorem with all other arcs controlled;
explicit critical coefficients through order five; an expansion with errors
small enough to resolve $q^{-m}$ sectors; explicit inverse charts; and a
probabilistic boundary law with its first correction. The retrieved sources
were not found to list these particular higher coefficients or this particular
probability correction. That limited observation is not an exhaustive
bibliographic novelty certification.

\section{An elementary sharp criterion}\label{rpc:sec:elementary}

Write $R_m(N)=p_m(N)/V_m(N)$. The scale $m^3$ can be detected without
saddle points, Bernoulli numbers, or complex analysis.

\begin{lemma}[Lattice-volume bounds]\label{rpc:lem:volume}
For $m\ge1$ and $N\ge1$, put $A_m=m(m+1)/2-1$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:eq:volume}
 1\le R_m(N)\le\left(1+\frac{A_m}{N}\right)^{m-1}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The multiplicity of the part $1$ is determined by the other multiplicities.
Consequently $p_m(N)$ is the number of integer points in
\[
 \mathcal S_N=\left\{(x_2,\ldots,x_m)\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{m-1}:
                         \sum_{j=2}^m jx_j\le N\right\}.
\]
This simplex has volume $N^{m-1}/((m-1)!\prod_{j=2}^m j)=V_m(N)$.
Every point of $\mathcal S_N$ lies in the unit box based at its coordinatewise
floor, a feasible integer point. Conversely, every unit box based at a feasible
point lies in $\mathcal S_{N+A_m}$. The boxes have disjoint interiors and unit
volume. Taking volumes proves both bounds. For $m=1$ the statement is the
zero-dimensional identity $p_1(N)=1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[A transposition lower bound]\label{rpc:lem:burnside}
For $m\ge3$ and $N\ge1$,
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:eq:burnside}
 R_m(N)\ge
 \prod_{r=1}^{m-1}\left(1+\frac rN\right)
 +\frac{m(m-1)^2}{4N}
 \ge 1+\frac{m(m-1)^2}{4N}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Let the symmetric group act on weak compositions
$(x_1,\ldots,x_m)$ of $N$ by permuting coordinates. Its orbits are partitions
into at most $m$ parts. Burnside's identity gives
\[
 p_m(N)=\frac1{m!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_m}|\Fix(\sigma)|.
\]
The identity fixes $\binom{N+m-1}{m-1}$ compositions, which contributes the
product in \eqref{rpc:eq:burnside} after normalization by $V_m(N)$.

A transposition requires two coordinates to be equal. Its fixed-point count
is the number of solutions of
\[
 2y+z_1+\cdots+z_{m-2}=N,\qquad y,z_i\ge0.
\]
Eliminate $z_{m-2}$ and apply the lower unit-box argument to the resulting
simplex in dimension $m-2$. The count is at least
$N^{m-2}/(2(m-2)!)$. There are $\binom m2$ transpositions. Their total
contribution, divided by $m!V_m(N)$, is $m(m-1)^2/(4N)$. All other terms
in Burnside's sum are nonnegative.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Exact threshold]\label{rpc:thm:iff}
For every positive-integer sequence $N=N(m)$,
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:eq:iff}
 p_m(N)\sim V_m(N)
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad N/m^3\longrightarrow\infty.
\end{equation}
Moreover, when $m^3/N\to0$,
$R_m(N)=1+O(m^3/N)$, whereas $N/m^3\to0$ implies $R_m(N)\to\infty$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
By \eqref{rpc:eq:volume},
$0\le\log R_m(N)\le(m-1)A_m/N=O(m^3/N)$.
This proves sufficiency and its error estimate. If $R_m(N)\to1$,
\eqref{rpc:eq:burnside} forces $m(m-1)^2/N\to0$, which is equivalent to
$m^3/N\to0$. The same lower bound proves the last assertion.
\end{proof}

A useful extension of the lower bound also explains the critical exponential.
For $0\le r\le\lfloor(m-1)/2\rfloor$, retain the permutations consisting of
$r$ disjoint transpositions and $m-2r$ fixed points. The identical simplex
argument gives
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:eq:involutions}
 R_m(N)\ge
 \sum_{r=0}^{\lfloor(m-1)/2\rfloor}
 \frac{\fall m{2r}\,\fall{(m-1)}r}{4^r r!N^r}.
\end{equation}
Here $\fall xr=x(x-1)\cdots(x-r+1)$ and $\fall x0=1$.
For $N\sim c m^3$, any fixed finite truncation tends to the corresponding
truncation of $\exp(1/(4c))$. Thus
\[
 \liminf_{m\to\infty}R_m(N)\ge e^{1/(4c)}.
\]
An upper bound with the same constant needs more information; it is supplied
by the uniform expansion below. Formula \eqref{rpc:eq:involutions} is not itself
an assertion that all remaining permutation types can simply be discarded.

\section{A uniform centered expansion to every fixed order}\label{rpc:sec:centered}

\subsection{Centering and the coefficient recurrence}

Set
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:eq:center}
 S_r(m)=\sum_{j=1}^m j^r,\qquad
 M=N+\frac{S_1(m)}2=N+\frac{m(m+1)}4.
\end{equation}
The exact factorization
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:eq:factor}
 e^{Nt}F_m(e^{-t})=
 \frac{e^{Mt}}{m!t^m}\mathcal A_m(t),\qquad
 \mathcal A_m(t)=\prod_{j=1}^m\frac{jt}{2\sinh(jt/2)}
\end{equation}
is the reason for choosing this center. The remaining factor is even. Its
logarithm, in a neighborhood of zero, is
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:eq:bernoulli}
 \log\mathcal A_m(t)=\sum_{r\ge1}\gamma_r(m)t^{2r},\qquad
 \gamma_r(m)=-\frac{B_{2r}S_{2r}(m)}{2r(2r)!}.
\end{equation}
We use $B_2=1/6$, $B_4=-1/30$, and $B_6=1/42$. Formula
\eqref{rpc:eq:bernoulli} follows by differentiating
$\log(z/(2\sinh(z/2)))$ and integrating its Bernoulli expansion at zero.

Define $a_j(m)$ by $\mathcal A_m(t)=\sum_{j\ge0}a_j(m)t^{2j}$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:eq:a-rec}
 a_0=1,\qquad
 j a_j=\sum_{r=1}^j r\gamma_r a_{j-r}.
\end{equation}
The first coefficients are
\begin{align}
 a_1&=-\frac{S_2}{24},\label{rpc:eq:a1}\\
 a_2&=\frac{S_2^2}{1152}+\frac{S_4}{2880},\\
 a_3&=-\frac{S_2^3}{82944}-\frac{S_2S_4}{69120}
                         -\frac{S_6}{181440}.
\end{align}
Each $a_j$ is a rational polynomial in $m$ of degree at most $3j$.
Indeed $S_{2r}$ has degree $2r+1\le3r$, and the recurrence preserves the bound.

\begin{theorem}[Uniform centered all-order expansion]\label{rpc:thm:centered}
Fix $c_0>0$ and an integer $K\ge0$. Uniformly over integer pairs with
$N\ge c_0m^3$, as $m\to\infty$,
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:eq:centered}
 p_m(N)=\frac{M^{m-1}}{m!(m-1)!}
 \left\{\sum_{j=0}^K
  \frac{a_j(m)\fall{(m-1)}{2j}}{M^{2j}}
       +O_{K,c_0}(m^{-K-1})\right\}.
\end{equation}
In particular, the $j$th displayed term is $O_{j,c_0}(m^{-j})$.
The theorem concerns fixed truncation orders; it does not assert convergence
of the resulting infinite asymptotic series.
\end{theorem}

\subsection{The minor-arc estimate, including very large \texorpdfstring{$N$}{N}}

We give the details because a formal expansion at $q=1$ alone does not justify
\eqref{rpc:eq:centered}. Other roots of unity must be controlled uniformly while
the number of factors grows.

\begin{lemma}[Decay on a Cauchy circle]\label{rpc:lem:arcs}
Let $0<\tau\le C_0m^{-2}$, and put
\[
 \rho_m(\theta)=\frac{|F_m(e^{-\tau-\ii\theta})|}{F_m(e^{-\tau})}.
\]
For all sufficiently large $m$ there are positive constants $b,\delta,C$,
with dependence on $C_0$ allowed, such that
\begin{align}
 \rho_m(\theta)&\le
    \left(1+b(\theta/\tau)^2\right)^{-m/2},
                       &&|\theta|\le1/m,\label{rpc:eq:neararc}\\
 \rho_m(\theta)&\le(Cm\tau)^{\delta m},
                       &&1/m\le|\theta|\le\pi.\label{rpc:eq:fararc}
\end{align}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For each factor,
\[
 \frac{1-e^{-j\tau}}{|1-e^{-j\tau-\ii j\theta}|}
 =\left(1+\frac{4e^{-j\tau}\sin^2(j\theta/2)}
                  {(1-e^{-j\tau})^2}\right)^{-1/2}.
\]
If $|\theta|\le1/m$, then $|j\theta|\le1$ and $j\tau=O(1/m)$.
The inequalities $|\sin(j\theta/2)|\ge c j|\theta|$ and
$1-e^{-j\tau}\le j\tau$ prove \eqref{rpc:eq:neararc} after multiplication.

For \eqref{rpc:eq:fararc}, a fixed positive fraction of the indices $j\le m$
have $|\sin(j\theta/2)|$ bounded away from zero, uniformly in this interval.
Here is an explicit justification. For $1/m\le|\theta|\le4\pi/m$, choose
$j$ between $m/(16\pi)$ and $m/(8\pi)$; the arguments stay in a compact
subinterval of $(0,\pi)$. For $4\pi/m\le|\theta|\le\pi$, the geometric-sum
bound gives
\[
 \sum_{j=1}^m\sin^2(j\theta/2)
 \ge\frac m2-\frac1{2|\sin(\theta/2)|}\ge\frac{3m}{8}.
\]
This forces a positive fraction of the summands to exceed a fixed positive
constant. Each corresponding factor is at most $Cm\tau$; every other factor
is at most one. Taking the minimum of the constants from the two cases proves
the assertion.
\end{proof}

\begin{writenote}[Added 5 October 2026, batch~98]
Lemma~\ref{rpc:cx:lem:small} of Part~II is \eqref{rpc:eq:neararc} with the same
proof under the weaker hypothesis $\tau\le\bar s/m$;
Lemma~\ref{rpc:cx:lem:large} bounds the integral over $1/m\le|\theta|\le\pi$ by
$\mathrm O(\tau(1+\log m)e^{-cm})$ for every $\tau\le\bar s/m$, by reserving

\subsection{Proof of the uniform theorem}
\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{rpc:thm:centered}]
Cauchy's formula, with $q=e^{-t}$, is
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:eq:cauchy}
 p_m(N)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}
       e^{N(\tau+\ii\theta)}F_m(e^{-\tau-\ii\theta})\dd\theta.
\end{equation}
Choose $\tau=m/M$ and the central interval
$|\theta|\le\tau m^{-2/5}$. We use three estimates.

\paragraph{Analytic Taylor remainder.}
For a sufficiently small absolute $\varepsilon>0$,
$\mathcal A_m(t)$ is analytic and uniformly bounded on
$|t|\le\varepsilon m^{-3/2}$. In fact $j|t|=O(m^{-1/2})$ there, and the
sum of the absolute logarithmic bounds is
$O(S_2(m)|t|^2)=O(1)$. Cauchy's coefficient estimate and evenness imply
\[
 \mathcal A_m(t)=\sum_{j=0}^K a_j(m)t^{2j}
        +O_K\!\left(m^{3K+3}|t|^{2K+2}\right)
\]
inside half that disk. The central interval is contained in this smaller
disk for all sufficiently large $m$, uniformly in $N\ge c_0m^3$.

\paragraph{Integrating the central remainder.}
For fixed nonnegative $r$ and $m>r+2$,
\[
 \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\tau+\ii\theta|^{-m+r}\dd\theta
 =\tau^{1-m+r}\sqrt\pi\,
       \frac{\Gamma((m-r-1)/2)}{\Gamma((m-r)/2)}.
\]
The gamma quotient is $O_r(m^{-1/2})$. Together with Stirling's formula,
this shows that integrating the Taylor remainder in \eqref{rpc:eq:factor},
relative to $M^{m-1}/(m!(m-1)!)$, costs at most
\[
 O_K\!\left(m^{3K+3}\tau^{2K+2}\right)
 =O_{K,c_0}(m^{-K-1}).
\]
The elementary inverse-Laplace identity
\[
 \frac1{2\pi\ii}\int_{\tau-\ii\infty}^{\tau+\ii\infty}
       e^{Mt}t^{-d}\dd t=\frac{M^{d-1}}{(d-1)!},\qquad d\ge2,
\]
follows by closing the contour to the left and taking the residue at zero.
Apply it with $d=m-2j$. Extending each central polynomial integral to this
line changes it by an exponentially small quantity: the gamma kernel outside
$|\theta|\le\tau m^{-2/5}$ has a tail bounded by
$\exp(-b_1m^{1/5})$ times its natural integral size. This gives exactly the
falling factorial in \eqref{rpc:eq:centered}.

\paragraph{Discarding the rest of the original circle.}
On $\tau m^{-2/5}\le|\theta|\le1/m$, \eqref{rpc:eq:neararc} and the change
of variable $v=\theta/\tau$ give a relative contribution
$O(\exp(-b_2m^{1/5}))$. On the remaining arcs, the relative contribution is
bounded by
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:eq:far-prefactor}
 C\frac{\sqrt m}{\tau}(Cm\tau)^{\delta m}.
\end{equation}
The factor $\sqrt m/\tau$ comes from normalizing the real-axis integrand by
its gamma-kernel integral. It cannot be ignored when $N$ is extremely large.
For $\delta m>1$, the right side is increasing in $\tau$. Substituting
$\tau\le C_0/m^2$ bounds it by
$m^{5/2}(C_1/m)^{\delta m}$, which is smaller than every fixed inverse power
of $m$. Thus the argument is uniform even for factorial or faster growth of
$N$, rather than merely for $N$ bounded by a power of $m$.

Combining the three estimates proves \eqref{rpc:eq:centered}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[What this theorem does not assert]
For fixed $m$, the polynomial part of $p_m(N)$ is its first Sylvester wave.
The coefficient algebra above is consistent with that classical polynomial.
The theorem gives uniform finite-order asymptotics of the entire count, not
just of a polynomial formally extracted at one singularity. It does not
identify each exponentially small root-of-unity wave, provide a Stokes
analysis, or specify an optimal truncation order growing with $m$.
\end{remark}

\section{Critical constants and explicit OEIS expansions}\label{rpc:sec:critical}

\begin{corollary}[Critical crossover]\label{rpc:cor:crossover}
If $N/m^3\to c\in(0,\infty)$, then
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:eq:crossover}
 \frac{p_m(N)}{V_m(N)}\longrightarrow e^{1/(4c)}.
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The $K=0$ case of Theorem~\ref{rpc:thm:centered} is
$p_m(N)=M^{m-1}/(m!(m-1)!)(1+O(1/m))$.

\[
 (m-1)\log(M/N)
 =(m-1)\log\left(1+\frac{m(m+1)}{4N}\right)\longrightarrow\frac1{4c}.
\]
\end{proof}

\subsection{A general all-order logarithmic expansion}

Write $c=N/m^3$; this notation is permitted even when $c$ varies with $m$.
For every fixed $K$, uniformly for $c\ge c_0>0$,
\begin{align}\label{rpc:eq:critical-log}
 \log p_m(c m^3)
 ={}&(m-3)\log m+2m+(m-1)\log c-\log(2\pi)+\frac1{4c}\notag\\
 &+\sum_{r=1}^K\frac{L_r(c)}{m^r}+O_{K,c_0}(m^{-K-1}).
\end{align}
Here and below the left side is used only when $c m^3$ is an integer.
The $L_r(c)$ are rational polynomials in $1/c$. Equivalently,
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:eq:critical-relative}
 p_m(c m^3)=\frac{e^{2m+1/(4c)}c^{m-1}m^{m-3}}{2\pi}
 \left(\sum_{r=0}^K\frac{b_r(c)}{m^r}+O_{K,c_0}(m^{-K-1})\right),
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:eq:b-rec}
 b_0=1,\qquad k b_k=\sum_{r=1}^k rL_r b_{k-r}.
\end{equation}

To obtain \eqref{rpc:eq:critical-log}, take the logarithm of
\eqref{rpc:eq:centered}, substitute $M=c m^3(1+(m^{-1}+m^{-2})/(4c))$, and
use the classical Stirling expansion \cite{dlmf}:
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:eq:stirling}
 \log\Gamma(m+1)+\log\Gamma(m)
 \sim 2m\log m-2m+\log(2\pi)
       +2\sum_{r\ge1}\frac{B_{2r}}{2r(2r-1)m^{2r-1}}.
\end{equation}
Every operation is justified to a fixed order by Theorem~\ref{rpc:thm:centered}.
Thus the coefficient algorithm is backed by an asymptotic remainder theorem,
not only by formal manipulation.

The first five logarithmic coefficients are
\begin{align}
 L_1(c)&=-\frac{48c^2+13}{288c^2},\label{rpc:eq:L1}\\
 L_2(c)&=-\frac{144c^2+6c-7}{576c^3},\label{rpc:eq:L2}\\
 L_3(c)&=\frac{11520c^4+122400c^2+14400c-8081}{2073600c^4},\\
 L_4(c)&=\frac{21600c^3-50400c^2-7855c+2861}{2073600c^5},\\
 L_5(c)&=-\frac{1}{21946982400c^6}\bigl(
 34836480c^6+304819200c^4+304819200c^3\notag\\[-2pt]
 &\hspace{40mm}{}-235282320c^2-42928704c+11425901\bigr).
\end{align}
As a short independent check of the first coefficient, the centered shift
contributes $-1/(32c^2m)$, the $a_1$ term contributes
$-1/(72c^2m)$, and Stirling contributes $-1/(6m)$.
Their sum is \eqref{rpc:eq:L1}.

\subsection{Higher power columns and an important rounding issue}

For any fixed integer $k\ge4$, substitute $c=m^{k-3}$ in
\eqref{rpc:eq:critical-log}. One obtains the complete inverse-power expansion of
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:eq:higherpower}
 p_m(m^k)\sim\frac{e^{2m}m^{(k-2)m-k}}{2\pi},
\end{equation}
the leading expression posted in the A238016 family \cite{oeis238016}.
For example, the quartic column A238609 satisfies
\begin{align}\label{rpc:eq:quartic}

       &-\frac1{96m^4}+\frac{1403}{20160m^5}+O(m^{-6}).
\end{align}
In particular, its first correction has the opposite sign from the cubic

If the intended count is $p_m(\lfloor c m^3\rfloor)$ for a fixed nonintegral

which is large enough to corrupt coefficients beginning at the second
correction. This is a discrete rounding effect, not a failure of the theorem.

\section{Dilute expansions and genuine exponential sectors}\label{rpc:sec:dilute}

The centered theorem is uniform in very large $N$, but its remainder
$O(m^{-K-1})$ is not small enough to detect a term such as $2^{-m}$.
A separate expansion in $1/N$, with a remainder measured in $m^3/N$, is needed.

\subsection{The uncentered coefficient system}

Define
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:eq:H}
 \mathcal H_m(t)=\prod_{j=1}^m\frac{jt}{1-e^{-jt}}
 =\exp\left(\frac{S_1t}{2}+\sum_{r\ge1}\gamma_rt^{2r}\right)
 =\sum_{j\ge0}h_j(m)t^j.
\end{equation}
Put
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:eq:beta}
 \beta_j(m)=\fall{(m-1)}j h_j(m),\qquad \beta_0=1.
\end{equation}
If $\lambda_1=S_1/2$, $\lambda_{2r}=\gamma_r$, and
$\lambda_{2r+1}=0$ for $r\ge1$, then
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:eq:h-rec}
 jh_j=\sum_{r=1}^j r\lambda_r h_{j-r}.
\end{equation}
Thus $h_j$ and $\beta_j$ have rational coefficients and degrees at most $2j$
and $3j$, respectively.

\begin{theorem}[Uniform dilute expansion]\label{rpc:thm:dilute}
There is an absolute $\eta_0>0$ such that, for every fixed $K\ge0$, uniformly
for $0<\eta=m^3/N\le\eta_0$ and all sufficiently large $m$,
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:eq:dilute}
 \frac{p_m(N)}{V_m(N)}
   =\sum_{j=0}^K\frac{\beta_j(m)}{N^j}
      +O_K\!\left((m^3/N)^{K+1}\right).
\end{equation}
In particular, this holds along every sequence for which $m^3/N\to0$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Use \eqref{rpc:eq:cauchy} with $\tau=m/N=\eta/m^2$. The exact integrand now is
$e^{Nt}\mathcal H_m(t)/(m!t^m)$. For a sufficiently small fixed $r>0$,
$\mathcal H_m$ is uniformly bounded on $|t|\le r/m^2$: the linear logarithmic
term is bounded, and all other terms have total absolute size $O(m^3|t|^2)$.
Cauchy's estimate gives
\[
 \mathcal H_m(t)=\sum_{j=0}^K h_jt^j
                   +O_K((m^2|t|)^{K+1})
\]
on the disk of half this radius. Choose $\eta_0$ sufficiently small that
$\tau$ lies well inside the disk and integrate over
$|\theta|\le r/(4m^2)$. The same gamma-kernel calculation as before bounds
the relative remainder by
$(m^2\tau)^{K+1}=\eta^{K+1}$. Each polynomial term gives
$h_j\fall{(m-1)}j/N^j$ by inverse Laplace integration.

For completeness, the omitted arcs are small on this stronger scale, not
merely small in $1/m$. On $r/(4m^2)\le|\theta|\le1/m$,
Lemma~\ref{rpc:lem:arcs}, after $v=\theta/\tau$, bounds the normalized tails
by a polynomial factor in $m$ times $(C\eta)^{m-1}$. For sufficiently small
$\eta_0$ and $m$ sufficiently large relative to $K$, this is
$O_K(\eta^{K+1})$. On $1/m\le|\theta|\le\pi$, the analogous bound is
\[
 C\frac{m^{5/2}}{\eta}\left(\frac{C\eta}{m}\right)^{\delta m}.
\]
After division by $\eta^{K+1}$, the positive remaining exponent of $\eta$
allows substitution of $\eta_0$, leaving a quantity tending rapidly to zero.
These bounds also justify extending the polynomial integrals to the entire
vertical line. This proves \eqref{rpc:eq:dilute}.
\end{proof}

Taking the formal logarithm defines rational polynomials $D_j(m)$ through
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:eq:D-rec}
 D_j=\beta_j-\frac1j\sum_{r=1}^{j-1}rD_r\beta_{j-r}.
\end{equation}
Theorem~\ref{rpc:thm:dilute} justifies the corresponding logarithmic expansion
with the same order of remainder. Its first terms are
\begin{align}
 D_1(m)&=\frac{m(m^2-1)}4,\label{rpc:eq:D1}\\
 D_2(m)&=-\frac{m(m^2-1)(13m^2+3m-4)}{288}.\label{rpc:eq:D2}
\end{align}
For example $h_2=S_1^2/8-S_2/24$, so
$D_2=(m-1)(m-2)h_2-D_1^2/2$, an immediate exact check.

\subsection{Exponential and superexponential specializations}

For a fixed integer $q\ge2$, put $a_q(m)=p_m(q^m)$ and $b=\log q$.
The full fixed-order expansion is
\begin{align}\label{rpc:eq:exp-transseries}
 \log a_q(m)={}&b m(m-1)-\log\Gamma(m+1)-\log\Gamma(m)\notag\\
 &+\sum_{j=1}^K D_j(m)e^{-jbm}
      +O_K\!\left(m^{3K+3}e^{-(K+1)bm}\right).
\end{align}
This is an expansion in genuine exponentially separated sectors, rather than
only a power series in $1/m$ renamed a transseries. The exact gamma background
is retained so that its algebraic truncation error does not obscure the
exponential corrections. In particular,
\begin{align}\label{rpc:eq:exp-two}
 \log a_q(m)={}&b m(m-1)-\log\Gamma(m+1)-\log\Gamma(m)\notag\\
 &+\frac{m(m^2-1)}4q^{-m}
 -\frac{m(m^2-1)(13m^2+3m-4)}{288}q^{-2m}
 +O(m^9q^{-3m}).
\end{align}
The leading term corresponds to A238010 and its columns, including A237998
when $q=2$ \cite{oeis238010}.

For A238000, put $N=m^m$ in Theorem~\ref{rpc:thm:dilute}. The sectors are
$m^{-jm}=\exp(-jm\log m)$, with the same polynomial coefficients $D_j(m)$.
For A258670, put $N=(2m)!$; its sectors are $((2m)!)^{-j}$
\cite{oeis258670}. In particular,
\[
 p_m((2m)!)=\frac{((2m)!)^{m-1}}{m!(m-1)!}
 \left(1+\frac{m(m^2-1)}{4(2m)!}
       +O\left(\frac{m^6}{((2m)!)^2}\right)\right).
\]
There is no need to solve a new saddle equation for each such sequence.
The full collection follows from one uniform dilute theorem.

\section{Inverting the asymptotic expansions}\label{rpc:sec:inverse}

\subsection{What inversion means for an integer sequence}

A sequence has no canonical smooth interpolation. We therefore use an
\emph{asymptotic inverse chart}: a smooth expression $I(y)$ that, at the exact
sample values $y=a(m)$, recovers $m$ with a specified asymptotic error.
The same expression can approximate an integer threshold inverse
$Q(y)=\min\{m:a(m)\ge y\}$, but generally only up to an $O(1)$ rounding
ambiguity. An exponentially accurate sample-point chart does not magically
supply an exponentially accurate integer-valued function for all real $y$.

Each fixed truncation of a forward logarithmic profile is smooth for large
real $x$ and can be inverted there. Raising the truncation order produces
consistent inverse coefficients. At sample values the forward remainder,
divided by the derivative of the leading profile, controls the inverse error.
This is the precise sense of the all-order inversions used below.

\begin{writenote}[Added 1 October 2026, batch~74: an instance of repository results]
The distinction drawn here between a sample-point chart and the integer
threshold $Q(y)$, with its $O(1)$ rounding ambiguity, is the content of the
staircase theorem \texttt{p0:thm:staircase} of the transseries-and-inversion
volume \cite{rpc-tai}: $Q=\lceil\nu\rceil$ for any increasing
interpolation $\nu$, and a chart with error at most $\eta$ determines $Q$
exactly when the separation condition of its part~(2) holds. The
inversions of this section are instances of that volume's inversion
apparatus, and no novelty is claimed for the method.
\end{writenote}

\subsection{The cubic family: Lambert \texorpdfstring{$W$}{W} and two explicit corrections}

Fix $c>0$, and consider $a_c(m)=p_m(c m^3)$ on admissible integer arguments.
Write
\[
 b_c=2+\log c,\qquad D_c=\frac1{4c}-\log(2\pi c).
\]
Equation \eqref{rpc:eq:critical-log} has the form
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:eq:cubicprofile}
 \log a_c(m)=m\log m+b_cm-3\log m+D_c
               +\frac{L_1(c)}m+\frac{L_2(c)}{m^2}+\cdots.
\end{equation}
For large $y$, set $L=\log y$ and
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:eq:lambertbase}
 w=W(c e^2L),\qquad x_0=\frac Lw.
\end{equation}
Here $W$ is the positive real solution of $we^w=z$. Then
$\log x_0+b_c=w$, and consequently
$x_0\log x_0+b_cx_0=L$ exactly. Define
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:eq:deltas}
 d_0=\frac{3\log x_0-D_c}{w+1},\qquad
 d_1=\frac{3d_0-d_0^2/2-L_1(c)}{w+1}.
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Cubic inverse chart]\label{rpc:thm:cubicinverse}
At $y=a_c(m)$, with $c$ fixed,
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:eq:inverse-cubic}
 m=x_0+d_0+\frac{d_1}{x_0}+O_c(x_0^{-2}).
\end{equation}
There is an all-order expansion
$x_0+\sum_{r\ge0}d_r(w;c)x_0^{-r}$, in the fixed-order asymptotic sense.
Its coefficients are obtained recursively by Taylor substitution in
\eqref{rpc:eq:cubicprofile}; they are rational functions of $w$ with constants
depending on $c$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $f_0(x)=x\log x+b_cx$. At $x_0$, one has
$f_0'=w+1$ and $f_0''=1/x_0$. Substitute
$x=x_0+d_0+d_1/x_0$ in \eqref{rpc:eq:cubicprofile}.
The constant residual is
$(w+1)d_0-3\log x_0+D_c$, and the coefficient of $x_0^{-1}$ is
$(w+1)d_1+d_0^2/2-3d_0+L_1(c)$. Both vanish by \eqref{rpc:eq:deltas}.
The remaining residual is $O_c(x_0^{-2})$, whereas the derivative is
asymptotic to $w+1$. The mean-value theorem proves the stated, slightly
weaker, $O_c(x_0^{-2})$ inverse error.

At any further fixed order, the new unknown coefficient appears multiplied
by $w+1$; everything else is already known. Division by $w+1$ completes
an induction. Since $\log x_0=w-b_c$, the recursion stays within rational
functions of $w$. The forward expansion can be taken to arbitrarily high
fixed order by Theorem~\ref{rpc:thm:centered}.
\end{proof}

For A238608 specifically,
\[
 d_0=3-\frac{37/4-\log(2\pi)}{w+1},\qquad
 d_1=\frac{3d_0-d_0^2/2+61/288}{w+1},\qquad w=W(e^2\log y).
\]
These corrections are important even for moderately large indices: the raw
Lambert term can miss the index by nearly two while the corrected expression
is already accurate to about $10^{-4}$ at $m=60$.

\begin{writenote}[Added 1 October 2026, batch~74: an instance of repository results]
The core $x\log x+b_cx=L$, solved by the principal branch as in
\eqref{rpc:eq:lambertbase}, is the Lambert core of the inverse gamma function
in \texttt{p6:thm:gamma} of the transseries-and-inversion volume
\cite{rpc-tai} (there $T\log T-T=R$ with $T=R/W_0(R/e)$), shifted by the
constant $b_c$; the corrections $d_0,d_1,\dots$ are its recursive
perturbed inversion \texttt{p0:thm:perturbed-inversion}. The exponential

$e^{-bX}$. Theorem~\ref{rpc:thm:cubicinverse} and \eqref{rpc:eq:expinverse} are
instances of that apparatus.
\end{writenote}

\subsection{Exponential columns: algebraic and invisible corrections}

For $q\ge2$ fixed, define the exact smooth background
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:eq:F0}
 G_q(x)=b x(x-1)-\log\Gamma(x+1)-\log\Gamma(x),\qquad b=\log q.
\end{equation}
Stirling's expansion yields
\[
 G_q(x)=b x^2-2x\log x+(2-b)x-\log(2\pi)
                -\frac1{6x}+\frac1{180x^3}-\cdots.
\]
Put
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:eq:sde}
 s=\sqrt{L/b},\qquad
 d=\frac{\log s}{b}+\frac{b-2}{2b},\qquad
 e=\frac{d^2}{2}+\frac db+\frac{\log(2\pi)}{2b}.
\end{equation}
Taylor substitution gives the algebraic inverse chart
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:eq:expalginverse}
 m=s+d+\frac es+O_q\!\left(\frac{(\log s)^3}{s^2}\right),
                         \qquad y=a_q(m).
\end{equation}
For example, the coefficient of $s$ cancels because
$2bd=2\log s+b-2$, and the constant residual cancels because
$2be=bd^2+2d+\log(2\pi)$.

To expose an exponentially small inverse correction, let $X$ be the exact
large solution of $G_q(X)=\log y$. This background inverse is well defined
for large $y$ since
\[
 G_q'(X)=b(2X-1)-\psi(X+1)-\psi(X)\sim2bX>0.
\]
Equation \eqref{rpc:eq:exp-transseries} and one implicit Taylor step give
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:eq:expinverse}
 m=X-\frac{D_1(X)e^{-bX}}{G_q'(X)}
             +O_q(X^5e^{-2bX}),\qquad y=a_q(m).
\end{equation}
The sign is negative: the positive count correction raises $\log a_q$ above
the gamma background, so the corrected index at fixed $y$ is smaller.
The $O(m^6e^{-2bm})$ forward error divided by $G_q'(m)\asymp m$ gives
the displayed safe inverse error; the Taylor cross terms are smaller.

All further exponential sectors can be computed recursively. For example,
write $m=X+u_1(X)e^{-bX}+u_2(X)e^{-2bX}+\cdots$. Then
\begin{align}\label{rpc:eq:inverse-sector-rec}
 u_1&=-D_1/G_q',\\
 u_2&=-\frac{D_2+(D_1'-bD_1)u_1+G_q''u_1^2/2}{G_q'}.
\end{align}
Theorem~\ref{rpc:thm:dilute} supplies the required forward remainder at every
fixed sector order. Retaining only finitely many terms of Stirling in
$G_q$ would not justify \eqref{rpc:eq:expinverse}: that algebraic error would
swamp the quantity being resolved.

\subsection{The diagonal sequence A238000}

The same procedure gives a particularly simple leading inverse for A238000.
Here
\[
 \log p_m(m^m)=(m^2-3m)\log m+2m-\log(2\pi)+O(1/m),
\]
with exponentially smaller corrections in $m^{-m}$ supplied by
Section~\ref{rpc:sec:dilute}. If
\[
 x_0=\sqrt{\frac{2L}{W(2L)}},
\]
then $x_0^2\log x_0=L$, and a first correction is
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:eq:diaginverse}
 m=x_0+\frac{3\log x_0-2}{2\log x_0+1}+O(x_0^{-1})
                         \quad\text{at }y=p_m(m^m).
\end{equation}
This follows by dividing the leading residual $-3x_0\log x_0+2x_0$
by the derivative $x_0(2\log x_0+1)$. It provides another inverse chart from
the same partition theorem, rather than an unrelated inversion example.

\section{A Poisson boundary law in the conjugate partition model}
\label{rpc:sec:poisson}

\subsection{Which repeated parts are being counted}

Choose a partition of $N$ into \emph{at most $m$ parts} uniformly, and pad it
with zeros to obtain
\[
 0\le x_1\le\cdots\le x_m,\qquad x_1+\cdots+x_m=N.
\]
This is the conjugate of the original bounded-part-size model.
The distinction matters: partitions whose part sizes are bounded by $m$
typically have many repeated small parts, and the theorem below is not a
claim about those multiplicities.

Set $d_1=x_1$ and $d_i=x_i-x_{i-1}$ for $i\ge2$. Then
\[
 \sum_{i=1}^m(m-i+1)d_i=N.
\]
Let
\[
 Z_m=\#\{i:2\le i\le m,\ d_i=0\},
\]
the number of equal adjacent pairs in the padded partition. A gap equal to
zero corresponds to a zero multiplicity for one of the weights $1,\ldots,m-1$
in the generating product. Its probability generating function is exactly
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:eq:pgf}
 \E[u^{Z_m}]=
 \frac{[q^N]F_m(q)\prod_{j=1}^{m-1}\bigl(u+(1-u)q^j\bigr)}{p_m(N)}.
\end{equation}
Indeed for each marked weight the sum over a gap is
$u+q^j+q^{2j}+\cdots=(u+(1-u)q^j)/(1-q^j)$.

\subsection{A deletion estimate and the limit distribution}

\begin{lemma}[Deleting a fixed number of factors]\label{rpc:lem:delete}
Fix $k$. Uniformly over $k$-element sets $S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\}$ and
$N/m^3$ in a compact subinterval of $(0,\infty)$,
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:eq:delete}
 \frac{[q^N]F_m(q)\prod_{j\in S}(1-q^j)}{p_m(N)}
 =\left(\prod_{j\in S}j\right)
       \frac{\fall{(m-1)}k}{M^k}\left(1+O_k(m^{-1})\right).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
On the central interval of Section~\ref{rpc:sec:centered},
\[
 \prod_{j\in S}(1-e^{-jt})
 =\left(\prod_{j\in S}j\right)t^k(1+O_k(m|t|))
 =\left(\prod_{j\in S}j\right)t^k(1+O_k(m^{-1})).
\]
Inserting this factor in the contour proof changes $t^{-m}$ to $t^{-m+k}$,
so the inverse-Laplace integral contributes $\fall{(m-1)}k/M^k$.
Both $\mathcal A_m(t)$ and the denominator count have relative error
$O(1/m)$ at this precision. On the other arcs the new product has modulus
at most $2^k$. Since $k$ is fixed and $\tau\asymp m^{-2}$, normalizing by
the smallest possible main deleted-factor contribution loses only a fixed
power of $m$, harmless compared with the arc decay. All bounds are uniform
in the choice of $S$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Poisson repeated-part boundary]\label{rpc:thm:poisson}
If $N/m^3\to c\in(0,\infty)$, then
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:eq:poisson}
 Z_m\ \xrightarrow{\ d\ }\ \Pois\!\left(\frac1{2c}\right).
\end{equation}
With probability tending to one, the partition has exactly $m$ positive
parts and no part occurs three or more times. Thus $Z_m$ also agrees, with
probability tending to one, with the number of distinct positive part sizes
that occur twice.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For every fixed $k$, the factorial-moment identity from \eqref{rpc:eq:pgf} and
Lemma~\ref{rpc:lem:delete} give
\[
 \E[\fall{Z_m}k]
 =k!\,\frac{\fall{(m-1)}k}{M^k}
   \sum_{\substack{S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m-1\}\\|S|=k}}
       \prod_{j\in S}j\,\left(1+O_k(m^{-1})\right).
\]
For fixed $k$, the elementary symmetric sum here equals
\[
 \frac1{k!}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}j\right)^k(1+O_k(m^{-1})).
\]
To see this, expand the power and remove tuples with a repeated index; their
relative total is bounded by a constant times
$\sum j^2/(\sum j)^2=O(1/m)$. Therefore
\[
 \E[\fall{Z_m}k]\longrightarrow(1/(2c))^k.
\]
These are the factorial moments of the asserted Poisson law. For clarity,
the first moment gives tightness; the next higher moments give uniform
integrability of every fixed power along subsequences. Any subsequential
limit therefore has the Poisson moments. The Poisson distribution is
moment-determinate, as its moment generating function is finite in a
neighborhood of zero. This proves \eqref{rpc:eq:poisson}.

The event $d_1=0$ is a single deletion of weight $m$, and hence has probability
$m(m-1)/M\,(1+O(1/m))=O(1/m)$. A triple repetition requires two consecutive
gaps to vanish. By \eqref{rpc:eq:delete}, summing these two-deletion probabilities
over at most $m$ adjacent pairs costs
\[
 O\left(\frac{m^2}{M^2}\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}j(j+1)\right)=O(m^{-1}).
\]
Outside these two exceptional events there are $m$ positive parts and only
double repetitions, proving the last assertion.
\end{proof}

\subsection{An explicit correction to the Poisson generating function}

The limit can be refined without assuming independence of the gaps.

\begin{theorem}[First finite-size probability correction]\label{rpc:thm:pgf}
Let $c=N/m^3$ range over a compact subinterval of $(0,\infty)$. Uniformly
for real $0\le u\le1$,
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:eq:pgf-correction}
 \E[u^{Z_m}]=e^{(u-1)/(2c)}
 \left[1-\frac1m\left(\frac{u-1}{c}
             +\frac{7u(u-1)}{24c^2}\right)+O_c(m^{-2})\right].
\end{equation}
The constant in the error can be chosen uniformly on the indicated compact
$c$ interval. In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:eq:no-repeats}
 \Prob(Z_m=0)=e^{-1/(2c)}\left(1+\frac1{cm}+O_c(m^{-2})\right).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Put $v=u-1$, $S_r'=\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}j^r$, and $M_v=M+vS_1'$.
Near zero,
\[
 \log\prod_{j=1}^{m-1}\bigl(u+(1-u)e^{-jt}\bigr)
 =vS_1't-\frac{v+v^2}{2}S_2't^2+O(m^4|t|^3),
\]
uniformly for $0\le u\le1$. After combining this with
\eqref{rpc:eq:bernoulli}, define
\[
 C_v=\frac{S_2}{24}+\frac{v+v^2}{2}S_2'.
\]
The same centered contour proof, now at $\tau=m/M_v$, yields for the numerator
of \eqref{rpc:eq:pgf}
\[
 \frac{M_v^{m-1}}{m!(m-1)!}
 \left(1-C_v\frac{(m-1)(m-2)}{M_v^2}+O(m^{-2})\right).
\]
The error follows from $m^4|t|^3+m^6|t|^4=O(m^{-2})$ on the central
scale. The old arc estimates remain valid: for $0\le u\le1$ and
$|q|\le1$, every multiplier $u+(1-u)q^j$ has modulus at most one.
Also $M_v=M+O(m^2)>0$ and the change of real-axis normalization costs only
a bounded factor.

Divide by the same formula at $v=0$ and take a logarithm. With $N=cm^3$,
\begin{align*}
 (m-1)\log(M_v/M)
 &=\frac v{2c}-\frac1m\left(\frac vc+\frac{v+v^2}{8c^2}\right)
         +O(m^{-2}),\\
 -C_v\frac{(m-1)(m-2)}{M_v^2}
 +C_0\frac{(m-1)(m-2)}{M^2}
 &=-\frac{v+v^2}{6c^2m}+O(m^{-2}).
\end{align*}
Since $1/8+1/6=7/24$, exponentiation proves \eqref{rpc:eq:pgf-correction}.
Setting $u=0$ proves \eqref{rpc:eq:no-repeats}.
\end{proof}

There is a useful independent exact identity at $u=0$. Subtract the staircase
$(0,1,\ldots,m-1)$ from a strictly increasing nonnegative partition. The result
is an arbitrary weakly increasing nonnegative partition of
$N-T$, where $T=m(m-1)/2$. Thus
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:eq:staircase}
 \Prob(Z_m=0)=\frac{p_m(N-T)}{p_m(N)}.
\end{equation}
Inserting the centered expansion into this ratio checks both the limit
$e^{-1/(2c)}$ and the first correction $1/(cm)$.
Higher pgf corrections can be generated by retaining further terms in
$\log(u+(1-u)e^{-z})$ and using the same coefficient recursions. We do not
claim a total-variation rate or a uniform complex-$u$ expansion from the
real interval estimate alone.

\section{A formalization route from the existing Lean foundation}

The exact combinatorial foundation already present in ProveIt suggests a
clean separation into finite and analytic layers. This is a proposed route,
not a claim of completed code.

The first layer would formalize the weak-composition orbit model and the
transposition fixed-point injection. These provide an entirely discrete proof
of the lower obstruction $m(m-1)^2/(4N)$. A companion lattice-simplex volume
lemma gives the upper bound and the exact threshold. The existing
multiplicity-vector equivalence can supply the bridge between the two models.

The second layer would formalize coefficient extraction for the finite
$q$-Pochhammer product, the analytic identity \eqref{rpc:eq:factor}, and the
Bernoulli recurrence \eqref{rpc:eq:a-rec}. At each fixed order, the explicit
coefficient identities reduce to rational polynomial algebra and are good
candidates for small proof certificates.

The genuinely analytic work is then localized: a uniform trigonometric
index-count lemma, a Cauchy-circle decay bound, gamma-kernel integral estimates,
and a uniform analytic Taylor remainder on a shrinking disk. The distinction
between a fixed parameter and a constant uniform over $N\ge c_0m^3$ must be
represented explicitly in the quantifiers. Finally, probability is built from
finite uniform measures on partition sets, coefficient-deletion identities,
and factorial moments.

The source file inspected in the repository is compatible with this program,
but neither its availability nor the successful Python checks certify any
of these future Lean developments. In particular, a formalization must not
replace the minor-arc estimate with the assertion that $q=1$ is the only
relevant singularity: the other roots of unity are present and must be bounded.

\section{Further research questions and concrete next steps}\label{rpc:sec:research}

The results above leave substantial, sharply formulated problems. The following
are proposals for further work, not assertions that every one is historically
open in exactly this form.

\paragraph{1. Optimal truncation and individual waves.}
Determine how the best order in \eqref{rpc:eq:centered} grows with $m$, and compute
the leading nontrivial root-of-unity contribution in the cubic regime. The
first target is the $q=-1$ wave, followed by a uniform comparison of all
remaining orders. This would turn the present fixed-order expansions into
a substantially more complete transseries.

\begin{writenote}[Added 5 October 2026, batch~98]
Part~II poses the same question for $N\ge am^2$
(\S\ref{rpc:cx:sec:q-waves}) and the optimal truncation of its expansion
(\S\ref{rpc:cx:sec:q-truncation}).
\end{writenote}

\paragraph{2. A uniform bridge below the cubic scale.}
Extend the centered analysis into $m^{5/2}\lesssim N\ll m^3$, where the
centering factor can be large and the quadratic Bernoulli correction must
itself be exponentiated. Match the resulting expansion to a small-parameter
expansion of the Szekeres saddle. The precise validity range and matching
error are more useful targets than simply recovering a leading logarithm.

\begin{writenote}[Added 5 October 2026, batch~98: answered by Part~II]
Theorem~\ref{rpc:cx:thm:uniform} gives one fixed-order expansion of
$\log(p_m(N)/V_m(M))$ with remainder $\mathrm O_{a,J}(m^{-J-1})$ uniformly
for every $N\ge am^2$, so it covers $m^{5/2}\lesssim N\ll m^3$ and the
Szekeres regime $N\asymp m^2$ at once, with the matching built in
(\S\ref{rpc:cx:sec:hierarchy}, \S\ref{rpc:cx:sec:oeis}). The exponentiated
quadratic Bernoulli correction is the term $-m^5/(72M^2)$, and
$M\asymp m^{5/2}$ is the exact threshold of the centered volume law
(Theorem~\ref{rpc:cx:thm:boundary}), followed by the scales
$m^{2+1/(2r)}$ (Theorem~\ref{rpc:cx:thm:hierarchy}).
\end{writenote}

\paragraph{3. Structural degree cancellation in logarithmic coefficients.}
The general construction only immediately gives $\deg D_j\le3j$, while
$D_1$ and $D_2$ have degrees $3$ and $5$. Investigate a systematic sharper
degree theorem, its leading coefficients, and a connected-object or cumulant
interpretation. Such a result could reduce symbolic complexity and improve
error estimates across many dilute sequences.

\paragraph{4. Higher probability corrections and quantitative convergence.}
Compute an all-order version of \eqref{rpc:eq:pgf-correction}, then prove a
uniform complex-$u$ domain sufficient for extracting point probabilities
with explicit error bounds. Obtain a total-variation estimate for the Poisson
approximation, rather than inferring one from convergence of fixed moments.

\paragraph{5. Rare triple repetitions.}
The present proof bounds their probability by $O(1/m)$ at the cubic scale.
Determine its leading constant and joint asymptotics with the number of
doubletons. At other scales, identify when tripletons develop a nondegenerate
limit and whether a hierarchy of multiplicity transitions results.

\paragraph{6. Deletions whose number grows.}
Lemma~\ref{rpc:lem:delete} assumes fixed $k$. Find the maximal growing range
$k=k(m)$ for which a useful uniform coefficient ratio holds. This would give
moderate-deviation information for $Z_m$, not just a Poisson limit at fixed
counts.

\paragraph{7. Other weight systems.}
Replace $1,\ldots,m$ by structured integer weights with an available unit
weight and controlled power sums. The local expansion generalizes immediately,
but the global arc estimate depends on the weights' arithmetic distribution.
Find hypotheses that separate a universal volume/cumulant law from genuine
periodic obstructions.

\begin{writenote}[Added 5 October 2026, batch~98]
The same question for Part~II's centering hierarchy is
\S\ref{rpc:cx:sec:q-weights}.
\end{writenote}

\paragraph{8. Certified inverse thresholds.}
Combine explicit finite constants in the remainder bounds with monotonicity
to produce intervals enclosing the integer inverse $Q(y)$. A useful algorithm
must report when its interval straddles two integers, rather than silently
rounding a smooth chart. This is particularly important when $y$ lies very
close to an exact sequence value.

\begin{writenote}[Added 5 October 2026, batch~98]
Part~II extends the question to $N\ge am^2$ (\S\ref{rpc:cx:sec:q-constants},
with Proposition~\ref{rpc:cx:prop:threshold}).
\end{writenote}

\paragraph{9. A proof-producing asymptotic pipeline.}
Starting from the finite-product generating function, automate power sums,
Bernoulli coefficients, fixed-order remainder parameters, transseries
substitution, and inverse reversion. The symbolic output should be a small
certificate checkable independently, with numerical fitting excluded from
the proof path.

\end{writenote}

Prepare a narrowly scoped, source-backed update for the affected entries:
replace the vague quartic-growth condition by a precise sufficient condition,
attach the classical attribution, and separately record explicit refinements.
A useful contribution is improved mathematical traceability, not relabeling
classical consequences as breakthroughs.

\section{Conclusion}

The restricted-partition family admits one coherent explanation of several
OEIS asymptotic patterns. The volume approximation has an exact cubic
threshold, critical growth leaves a nontrivial constant, and larger sizes
admit polynomial, exponential, or factorial-scale refinements governed by the
same Bernoulli coefficient system. Uniform contour control is what turns
formal local algebra into theorems about the whole counting sequence.

The inverse formulas illustrate a second important distinction: an exact
gamma background is needed before exponentially small corrections can be
meaningfully inverted. Finally, the conjugate partition model shows that the
cubic boundary is not merely an artifact of asymptotic notation. It is a
regime where repeated parts survive in a nondegenerate Poisson law, with a
computable finite-size correction. The elementary criterion, all-order
expansions, inverse charts, and probabilistic law form the substantive
proof package supplied by this report.

\section{The research question and its precise scope}\label{rpc:cx:sec:scope}

\subsection{Why this problem was selected}
The finite Euler product
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:gf}
 F_m(q)=\prod_{j=1}^m\frac1{1-q^j},\qquad
 p_m(N)=[q^N]F_m(q)
\end{equation}
occurs in several useful OEIS sequences. A238016 records
$p_m(m^k)$ as a two-dimensional array; its quadratic and cubic columns
are A206226 and A238608. A097356 is
$p_{\lfloor\sqrt N\rfloor}(N)$~\cite{oeis238016,oeis238608,rpc-cx-oeis097356}.
These are regular partition families with a common generating function,
not isolated numerical coincidences.

The selected repository reports are \emph{Three Exceptions Before Every
Square}~\cite{rpc-a097356} and \emph{The Cubic Boundary for Restricted
Partitions} (Part~I of this report). The former develops expansions when
$N/m^2$ stays in a compact subset of $(0,\infty)$; the latter develops
centered expansions for $N\ge c_0m^3$. The latter's scope statement
explicitly says that it does not uniformly match the quadratic regime
and identifies this as the zero-saddle end of its neighbor's research
question. We address that end: $N/m^2$ may tend to infinity arbitrarily
quickly. We do not address the opposite end $N/m^2\to0$ uniformly.

\subsection{Historical boundary and novelty discipline}
Szekeres' leading formula already joins the main restricted-partition
regimes. Canfield's primary paper gives a proof and precise uniform
ranges~\cite{canfield}. Consequently, a leading matching formula, or a
consequence obtained merely by expanding that formula, should not be
presented as a newly resolved historical conjecture. The posted leading
law for A238608 is also already recorded in OEIS~\cite{oeis238608}.

This report develops a uniform \emph{all-fixed-orders} expansion with an
explicit treatment of arbitrarily small saddles; a centered hierarchy
of successive approximation boundaries; a rational coefficient
algorithm; and a corresponding inverse chart. These are extensions of
the two inspected repository reports. Their organization and explicit
formulas were not located in the bounded source search, but we do not
claim that every consequence or coefficient is absent from the broader
partition literature. The saddle method, Euler--Maclaurin summation,
Lagrange inversion and Lambert-$W$ inversion are classical tools.

There is another relevant distinction. For fixed $m$, $p_m(N)$ is a
quasipolynomial, and its root-of-unity components are often called
Sylvester waves~\cite{sillszeilberger,osullivan}. Here $m$ grows. We bound the
noncentral arcs collectively; we do not construct the individual waves
or claim optimal exponential accuracy.

\subsection{Notation and meaning of an all-order statement}
Throughout the main results, $m\to\infty$, $m,N$ are positive integers,
and $a>0$ is fixed. Put
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:center}
 X=N+\frac{m(m+1)}4,\qquad
 \varepsilon=\frac{m^2}{X},\qquad
 V_m(X)=\frac{X^{m-1}}{m!\Gamma(m)}.
\end{equation}
The centered variable need not be integral. If $N\ge am^2$, then
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:epsrange}
 0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_a:=\frac1{a+1/4}<4.
\end{equation}
An error $\mathrm O_{a,J}(m^{-J-1})$ has one constant valid for all such $N$,
including superexponentially large $N$. The order $J$ is fixed before
$m\to\infty$. No uniformity in a growing $J$ is asserted.

\section{Main results}\label{rpc:cx:sec:main}
Define the regularized logarithm and its mean by
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:hH}
 h(z)=\log\frac{z}{2\sinh(z/2)},\qquad h(0)=0,
 \qquad H(s)=\int_0^1h(sx)\dd x.
\end{equation}
On the positive real axis the logarithm is real. Near zero the analytic
branch is fixed by $h(0)=0$. Write
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:Bq}
 B(s)=1-sH'(s)=1-h(s)+H(s),\qquad
 \mathcal Q(s)=1+s^2H''(s).
\end{equation}
For $0<\varepsilon<4$, let $s=s(\varepsilon)>0$ be the unique solution of
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:continuum-saddle}
 \frac1\varepsilon=\frac1s-H'(s),\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad
 s=\varepsilon B(s).
\end{equation}
Existence, uniqueness and regularity will be proved in
Section~\ref{rpc:cx:sec:profiles}. Set
\begin{align}
 \mathcal D(\varepsilon)&=\frac{s}{\varepsilon}-1-\log\frac{s}{\varepsilon}+H(s),
       \label{rpc:cx:eq:D}\\
 E_0(\varepsilon)&=\frac{h(s)}2+\log\frac{s}{\varepsilon}-\frac12\log \mathcal Q(s).
       \label{rpc:cx:eq:E0}
\end{align}
At zero both functions are defined by continuity and equal zero.

\begin{theorem}[Uniform centered expansion]\label{rpc:cx:thm:uniform}
There are real-analytic functions $E_j$ on $[0,4)$, $j\ge1$, such that,
for every $a>0$ and every fixed integer $J\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:main}
 \boxed{\quad
 \log\frac{p_m(N)}{V_m(X)}
 =m\mathcal D(\varepsilon)+E_0(\varepsilon)+\sum_{j=1}^J\frac{E_j(\varepsilon)}{m^j}
       +\mathrm O_{a,J}(m^{-J-1}),\quad N\ge am^2.
 \quad}
\end{equation}
Every $E_j$ has an even analytic germ at zero and satisfies $E_j(0)=0$.
The same properties hold for $\mathcal D$ and $E_0$. A finite Gaussian-coefficient
algorithm computes each $E_j$ from $h,H$ and Bernoulli numbers.
\end{theorem}
Here ``analytic on $[0,4)$'' means analytic in a neighborhood of each
point of that half-open real interval, not one unspecified complex disk
of radius four. Exponentiating \eqref{rpc:cx:eq:main} gives a relative error
$1+\mathrm O_{a,J}(m^{-J-1})$ after retaining the displayed exponential.

\begin{writenote}[Added 5 October 2026, batch~98: relation to Part~I]
On the range $N\ge c_0m^3$ of Theorem~\ref{rpc:thm:centered} one has
$\varepsilon\le1/(c_0m)$, and both theorems determine $\log(p_m(N)/V_m(X))$
to $\mathrm O(m^{-K-1})$ uniformly there ($X$ is Part~I's $M$). They are
therefore two expansions of one quantity and agree after re-expansion in
$\varepsilon$; the first-wave check \eqref{rpc:cx:eq:wavecheck} is this
agreement at order $X^{-2}$, against Part~I's $a_1$ term
\eqref{rpc:eq:a1}. Theorem~\ref{rpc:cx:thm:uniform} is thus a second route to
Theorem~\ref{rpc:thm:centered}, in logarithmic form, and extends it from
$N\ge c_0m^3$ to $N\ge am^2$; manuscript~09 does not carry out the
re-expansion beyond order $X^{-2}$. On compact $\alpha$-ranges it likewise
covers, in other coordinates, the bounded-offset expansion
\texttt{srp:thm:allorders} of the report
\texttt{a097356-\allowbreak sqrt-\allowbreak restricted-\allowbreak
partitions}, which uses the same regularized logarithm~$h$. The proof in
\S\ref{rpc:cx:sec:em} is condensed; Remark~\ref{rpc:cx:rem:completion}
completes it.
\end{writenote}

\begin{theorem}[Sharp centered boundary]\label{rpc:cx:thm:boundary}
For every sequence with $N\ge am^2$,
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:iff}
 p_m(N)\sim V_m(X)
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad X/m^{5/2}\longrightarrow\infty.
\end{equation}
At the boundary, for each fixed $\lambda>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:firstboundary}
 X\sim\lambda m^{5/2}
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 \frac{p_m(N)}{V_m(X)}\longrightarrow
 \exp\!\left(-\frac1{72\lambda^2}\right).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
This is not a change to the previously studied \emph{uncentered} cubic
boundary: $V_m(N)$ and $V_m(X)$ are different approximations.

Expand the entropy at zero as $\mathcal D(\varepsilon)=\sum_{r\ge1}\mathsf d_r\varepsilon^{2r}$.

\begin{theorem}[Crossover hierarchy]\label{rpc:cx:thm:hierarchy}
The coefficients are rational and satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:d-lagrange}
 \mathsf d_r=-\frac{[z^{2r}]B(z)^{2r+1}}{2r(2r+1)}.
\end{equation}
For a fixed $r$ with $\mathsf d_r\ne0$, define
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:Mr}
 \mathcal V_{r-1}(m,N)=V_m(X)
     \exp\!\left(m\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}\mathsf d_j(m^2/X)^{2j}\right).
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:hierarchy-limit}
 X\sim\lambda m^{2+1/(2r)}
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 \frac{p_m(N)}{\mathcal V_{r-1}(m,N)}
       \longrightarrow \exp(\mathsf d_r/\lambda^{2r}).
\end{equation}
Infinitely many $\mathsf d_r$ are nonzero. In particular there are infinitely
many distinct transition exponents accumulating at $2$ from above.
The first six $\mathsf d_r$ are negative and are listed in
Table~\ref{rpc:cx:tab:coefficients}. Nonvanishing and negativity for \emph{every}
$r$ are not claimed.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Uniform analytic inverse chart]\label{rpc:cx:thm:inverse-intro}
Let $Y=p_m(N)$ with $N\ge am^2$, and define
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:inverse-input}
 Z=(Y m!\Gamma(m))^{1/(m-1)},\qquad \eta_\star=m^2/Z.
\end{equation}
For $0\le\eta_\star<e^2$ define $\varepsilon$ by
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:inverse-core}
 \eta_\star=\varepsilon e^{-\mathcal D(\varepsilon)},\qquad \mathcal R(\eta_\star)=e^{-\mathcal D(\varepsilon)}.
\end{equation}
The map is uniquely invertible and analytic, including at zero. With
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:v}
 \omega(\eta_\star)=\frac{-\mathcal D(\varepsilon)-E_0(\varepsilon)}{1-\varepsilon \mathcal D'(\varepsilon)},
\end{equation}
we have, uniformly in the stated sample-value regime,
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:inverse-first}
 X=Z\mathcal R(\eta_\star)\exp\!\left(\frac{\omega(\eta_\star)}m+\mathrm O_a(m^{-2})\right).
\end{equation}
There is an analytic inverse expansion to every fixed order in $1/m$.
These are smooth asymptotic charts, not exact integer-rounding rules.
\end{theorem}

\section{Exact generating functions and a finite-saddle expansion}
\label{rpc:cx:sec:finite}
For each possible part $j$, its multiplicity contributes
$1+q^j+q^{2j}+\cdots$. Multiplying these choices proves
\eqref{rpc:cx:eq:gf} coefficient by coefficient. It also proves the exact
recurrence
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:dp}
 p_m(N)=p_{m-1}(N)+p_m(N-m),
\end{equation}
where negative arguments have value zero, $p_m(0)=1$, and
$p_0(N)=0$ for $N>0$.

For $t>0$ let
\begin{equation}
 \Lambda_m(t)=\log F_m(e^{-t})=-\sum_{j=1}^m\log(1-e^{-jt}).
\end{equation}
Define positive cumulants
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:cumulants}
 \kappa_r(t)=\sum_{j=1}^m j^r
       \sum_{\ell\ge1}\ell^{r-1}e^{-jt\ell},\qquad r\ge1.
\end{equation}
Then $(-1)^r\Lambda_m^{(r)}(t)=\kappa_r(t)$ and
$\kappa_1(t)=\sum j/(e^{jt}-1)$.
The function $\kappa_1$ decreases continuously from infinity to zero,
so there is a unique exact saddle $t=t_{m,N}$ satisfying
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:exact-saddle}
 \kappa_1(t)=N.
\end{equation}
Since $j/(e^{jt}-1)\le1/t$, this saddle satisfies
$t\le m/N\le1/(am)$.

There is a useful probabilistic interpretation. Let independent $\xi_j$
have the geometric distribution
$\Pr(\xi_j=k)=(1-e^{-jt})e^{-jtk}$, $k\ge0$. Then
\begin{equation}
 p_m(N)=e^{Nt+\Lambda_m(t)}\Pr\!\left(\sum_{j=1}^m j\xi_j=N\right).
\end{equation}
At the exact saddle the random sum has mean $N$. Its centered
characteristic function gives
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:cauchy}
 p_m(N)=\frac{e^{Nt+\Lambda_m(t)}}{2\pi}
 \int_{-\pi}^{\pi}
   \exp\{\Lambda_m(t-\ii\theta)-\Lambda_m(t)-\ii N\theta\}\dd\theta.
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}[Uniform cumulant scale]\label{rpc:cx:lem:cumulants}
For fixed $\bar s>0$ and fixed $r\ge1$, there are positive constants
$c_{r,\bar s},C_{r,\bar s}$ such that, whenever $0<t\le \bar s/m$,
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:cumulantscale}
 c_{r,\bar s}mt^{-r}\le\kappa_r(t)\le C_{r,\bar s}mt^{-r}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For $y>0$, the function
$y^r\sum_{\ell\ge1}\ell^{r-1}e^{-y\ell}$ is positive and continuous.
As $y\downarrow0$ it tends to $(r-1)!$, by a Riemann-sum comparison with
$\int_0^\infty x^{r-1}e^{-x}\dd x$. Thus it is bounded above and
away from zero on $[0,\bar s]$. Apply this observation to $y=jt$ in each
summand of \eqref{rpc:cx:eq:cumulants}.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Exact-saddle expansion]\label{rpc:cx:thm:finite}
For each fixed $J\ge0$, uniformly for $N\ge am^2$,
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:finiteexp}
 p_m(N)=\frac{e^{Nt+\Lambda_m(t)}}{\sqrt{2\pi\kappa_2(t)}}
 \left(\sum_{j=0}^J \mathcal C_j(t)+\mathrm O_{a,J}(m^{-J-1})\right),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal C_0=1$, $\mathcal C_j=\mathrm O_{a,j}(m^{-j})$, and
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:Edgeworth}
 \mathcal C_j=\sum_{\substack{k_3,k_4,\ldots\ge0\\
               \sum_{r\ge3}(r-2)k_r=2j}}
 (-1)^{\mathsf R/2}(\mathsf R-1)!!
 \prod_{r\ge3}\frac1{k_r!}
 \left(\frac{\kappa_r}{r!\kappa_2^{r/2}}\right)^{k_r},
 \qquad \mathsf R=\sum_{r\ge3}rk_r.
\end{equation}
Only finitely many $k_r$ can be nonzero, and $\mathsf R$ is even under the
constraint. In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:C1}
 \mathcal C_1=\frac{\kappa_4}{8\kappa_2^2}
          -\frac{5\kappa_3^2}{24\kappa_2^3}.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
The nontrivial uniformity issue in this theorem is not the formal
Gaussian expansion: it is showing that the noncentral contour is
negligible even when $t$ is extremely small. We do this next, then
complete the proof.

\section{A minor-arc estimate that survives an arbitrarily small saddle}
\label{rpc:cx:sec:arcs}
For any $0<t\le \bar s/m$, the modulus of the $j$th characteristic factor is
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:Rj}
 \varrho_j(\theta)=\frac{1-e^{-jt}}{|1-e^{-jt+\ii j\theta}|}
 =\left(1+\frac{\sin^2(j\theta/2)}{\sinh^2(jt/2)}\right)^{-1/2}.
\end{equation}
This identity avoids cancellation and displays both relevant scales.

\begin{lemma}[Small-angle product bound]\label{rpc:cx:lem:small}
There is $c_{\bar s}>0$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:student}
 \prod_{j=1}^m\varrho_j(\theta)
 \le\left(1+c_{\bar s}(\theta/t)^2\right)^{-m/2},
 \qquad |\theta|\le1/m.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
On $|u|\le1/2$, $|\sin u|\ge c|u|$ for an absolute $c>0$.
On $0\le v\le \bar s/2$, $\sinh v\le C_{\bar s}v$.
Apply these bounds to $u=j\theta/2$ and $v=jt/2$ in
\eqref{rpc:cx:eq:Rj}; the factor $j$ cancels. Multiply the $m$ resulting bounds.
\end{proof}

\begin{writenote}[Added 5 October 2026, batch~98: Part~I's near-arc bound]
$\prod_j\varrho_j(\theta)$ is Part~I's $\rho_m(\theta)$ at $\tau=t$, and
\eqref{rpc:cx:eq:Rj} is the factor identity in the proof of
Lemma~\ref{rpc:lem:arcs}, since $4e^{-x}/(1-e^{-x})^2=1/\sinh^2(x/2)$.
Lemma~\ref{rpc:cx:lem:small} is therefore \eqref{rpc:eq:neararc} with the same
proof, under the weaker hypothesis $t\le\bar s/m$ in place of
$\tau\le C_0m^{-2}$. Part~I's far-arc bound \eqref{rpc:eq:fararc},
$(Cm\tau)^{\delta m}$, needs $Cm\tau<1$ and gives nothing when $\tau\asymp1/m$,
which is the quadratic scale; Lemma~\ref{rpc:cx:lem:large} replaces it there.
\end{writenote}

\begin{lemma}[Macroscopic-angle bound]\label{rpc:cx:lem:large}
For fixed $\bar s>0$, there are constants $c_{\bar s},C_{\bar s}>0$ such that, for all
sufficiently large $m$,
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:outerintegral}
 \int_{1/m\le|\theta|\le\pi}\prod_{j=1}^m\varrho_j(\theta)\dd\theta
       \le C_{\bar s}t(1+\log m)e^{-c_{\bar s}m}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
First we show
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:sinsum}
 \sum_{j=2}^m\sin^2(j\theta/2)\ge c m,
       \qquad 1/m\le|\theta|\le\pi,
\end{equation}
for one absolute $c>0$. Fix a sufficiently large constant $K_\ast$.
For $1/m\le|\theta|\le K_\ast/m$, set $u=m|\theta|$.
The Riemann sums $m^{-1}\sum_{j=2}^m\sin^2(uj/(2m))$ converge
uniformly on $u\in[1,K_\ast]$ to
$\int_0^1\sin^2(ux/2)\dd x$. The continuous limiting function is
strictly positive on that compact interval.
For $K_\ast/m\le|\theta|\le\pi$, the geometric-sum estimate gives
$|\sum_{j=1}^me^{\ii j\theta}|\le1/|\sin(\theta/2)|\le\pi/|\theta|$.
Using $\sin^2 v=(1-\cos2v)/2$ proves \eqref{rpc:cx:eq:sinsum} when $K_\ast$ is
large, after absorbing the deleted $j=1$ term.

Since $jt\le \bar s$, \eqref{rpc:cx:eq:Rj} implies
$\log \varrho_j(\theta)\le-c'_{\bar s}\sin^2(j\theta/2)$.
Spend the factors $j=2,\ldots,m$ on \eqref{rpc:cx:eq:sinsum}; their product is
at most $e^{-c_{\bar s}m}$. Keep the first factor separately:
\begin{equation}
 \varrho_1(\theta)\le\frac{\sinh(t/2)}{|\sin(\theta/2)|}
               \le\frac{C_{\bar s}t}{|\sin(\theta/2)|}.
\end{equation}
Integrating its reciprocal-sine bound from $1/m$ to $\pi$ gives
$\mathrm O(t(1+\log m))$, proving \eqref{rpc:cx:eq:outerintegral}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Why the factor $t$ matters]
The principal integral has size $\kappa_2^{-1/2}\asymp t/\sqrt m$.
An absolute tail estimate $\mathrm O(e^{-cm})$ would not establish a relative
error uniformly in unbounded $N$: $t$ could be smaller than $e^{-m^2}$.
The reserved $j=1$ factor in Lemma~\ref{rpc:cx:lem:large} supplies precisely the
missing $t$. The proof does not require an upper growth condition on $N$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Completion of the finite-saddle proof}
Choose a fixed small $\delta>0$. The central window
$|\theta|\le t m^{-1/2+\delta}$ lies inside $|\theta|\le1/m$ for
large $m$. By Lemma~\ref{rpc:cx:lem:small} and the substitution $x=\theta/t$,
the integral between these windows is
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:intermediate}
 \mathrm O\!\left(t m^{-1/2}e^{-c m^{2\delta}}\right).
\end{equation}
Indeed, on $|x|\le1$ the bound in \eqref{rpc:cx:eq:student} is Gaussian;
on $|x|\ge1$ its integral is exponentially small in $m$.
Lemma~\ref{rpc:cx:lem:large} handles the remaining contour. Both bounds are
smaller than $t m^{-A}$ for every fixed $A$.

Inside the central window use $y=\theta\sqrt{\kappa_2}$. Exact
centering cancels the linear term, and the exponent is
\begin{equation}
 -\frac{y^2}2+
 \sum_{r\ge3}\frac{\kappa_r(\ii y)^r}{r!\kappa_2^{r/2}}.
\end{equation}
The functions involved are analytic for $|\theta|<ct$, for any fixed
$c<1$. Cauchy estimates and Lemma~\ref{rpc:cx:lem:cumulants} bound the
coefficient of degree $r$ by a constant times $m^{1-r/2}$.
For a prescribed $J$, take $\delta$ sufficiently small, Taylor-expand
to a finite degree exceeding $2J+4$, and expand the exponential to the
corresponding finite weight. The remainder is bounded by
$m^{-J-1}$ times an integrable polynomial in $|y|$ times $e^{-c y^2}$.
Terms of odd total degree integrate to zero on the symmetric window;
extending the finitely many Gaussian-polynomial integrals to the full
line costs an exponentially small error. The Gaussian moments are
$\E g^{2k}=(2k-1)!!$ for $g\sim N(0,1)$.
Grouping by $\sum(r-2)k_r=2j$ gives \eqref{rpc:cx:eq:Edgeworth}, with its sign
$\ii^{\mathsf R}=(-1)^{\mathsf R/2}$. These estimates prove
Theorem~\ref{rpc:cx:thm:finite}, including its fixed-order remainder.

\begin{writenote}[Added 5 October 2026, batch~98: a direct derivative bound]
The Cauchy estimate can be replaced by a bound valid for every real
$\theta$: for $\operatorname{Re}t'>0$ the series
$\Lambda_m^{(r)}(t')=(-1)^r\sum_jj^r\sum_\ell\ell^{r-1}e^{-jt'\ell}$
converges absolutely, and $|e^{-j(t-\ii\theta)\ell}|=e^{-jt\ell}$, so
$|\Lambda_m^{(r)}(t-\ii\theta)|\le\kappa_r(t)$. The Taylor remainder of order
$R'+1$ along the segment from $t$ to $t-\ii\theta$ is therefore at most
$|\theta|^{R'+1}\kappa_{R'+1}(t)/(R'+1)!$, which is
$\mathrm O(m(|y|/\sqrt m)^{R'+1})$ by Lemma~\ref{rpc:cx:lem:cumulants}.
\end{writenote}

\section{Centering and the continuum saddle}\label{rpc:cx:sec:profiles}
The elementary identity
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:center-identity}
 \frac1{1-e^{-z}}=z^{-1}e^{z/2}e^{h(z)}
\end{equation}
gives the exact formula
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:centerexact}
 Nt+\Lambda_m(t)=Xt-m\log t-\log(m!)+\sum_{j=1}^m h(jt).
\end{equation}
Thus the displacement $m(m+1)/4$ removes the entire linear term in the
logarithm of the product. What remains, $h$, is even at the origin.
This is why centering changes the first approximation boundary.

The identity $(sH(s))'=h(s)$ proves the second expression for $B$ in
\eqref{rpc:cx:eq:Bq}. Differentiating under the integral also gives
\begin{align}
 \mu(s):=\frac1s-H'(s)
 &=\int_0^1\frac{x}{2}\coth(sx/2)\dd x
   =\frac14+\int_0^1\frac{x}{e^{sx}-1}\dd x,
       \label{rpc:cx:eq:mu}\\
 -\mu'(s)&=\int_0^1\frac{x^2e^{sx}}{(e^{sx}-1)^2}\dd x>0.
       \label{rpc:cx:eq:muderivative}
\end{align}
In particular $\mathcal Q(s)=-s^2\mu'(s)>0$.

\begin{lemma}[Analytic saddle parametrization]\label{rpc:cx:lem:saddle}
The map $s\mapsto\varepsilon=1/\mu(s)=s/B(s)$ is a strictly increasing
bijection from $(0,\infty)$ onto $(0,4)$.
Its inverse $s(\varepsilon)$ is real analytic there, extends analytically at
zero, and is odd near zero. Moreover $B(s)>1$ for every $s>0$,
$\mathcal Q(0)=1$, and $s(\varepsilon)=\varepsilon+\mathrm O(\varepsilon^3)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Equations~\eqref{rpc:cx:eq:mu}--\eqref{rpc:cx:eq:muderivative} show strict
monotonicity, $\mu(s)\to\infty$ at zero and $\mu(s)\to1/4$ at
infinity. The nonzero derivative gives local analytic inverses.
Near zero $h,H$ are even analytic functions, $B(0)=1$, and the analytic
implicit-function theorem applies to $s-\varepsilon B(s)=0$. Symmetry and
uniqueness make $s$ odd. Finally
$B(s)=\int_0^1(sx/2)\coth(sx/2)\dd x>1$ because
$u\coth u>1$ for $u>0$. The other limits follow directly.
\end{proof}

The continuum saddle for the main parameter $\varepsilon$ is $t=s/m$.
For fixed $a>0$, $s$ ranges in the compact interval
$[0,s(\varepsilon_a)]$. Thus the contour estimates of
Section~\ref{rpc:cx:sec:arcs} apply to this saddle as well. It is not exactly
$t_{m,N}$, but its discrepancy is absorbed by the analytic endpoint
amplitude below; no claim that it is an exact mean is made.

\section{Uniform Euler--Maclaurin analysis and a complete coefficient rule}
\label{rpc:cx:sec:em}
For every fixed truncation order, Euler--Maclaurin summation gives
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:EM}
 \sum_{j=1}^m h(js/m)
 \sim mH(s)+\frac{h(s)}2+
 \sum_{k\ge1}\frac{\mathsf A_k(s)}{m^{2k-1}},
 \quad
 \mathsf A_k(s)=\frac{B_{2k}}{(2k)!}s^{2k-1}h^{(2k-1)}(s).
\end{equation}
The Bernoulli numbers have $B_2=1/6$.
The terms at zero in the derivative differences vanish because $h$ is
even. This is a uniform fixed-order expansion on every compact
$s$-interval, including zero, and on a sufficiently small complex
neighborhood of that interval along the local saddle contour.
To justify any requested remainder, apply Euler--Maclaurin with more
terms than needed: the periodic Bernoulli remainder is bounded by the
corresponding derivative supremum, which is uniform on that compact
neighborhood. Thus no formal interchange with a singular endpoint is
being used.

\subsection{A nonsingular local coordinate}
Use $z=s\zeta$ and define
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:phase}
 \phi_s(\zeta)=B(s)\zeta-\log \zeta+H(s\zeta).
\end{equation}
It satisfies
\begin{equation}
 \phi_s'(1)=0,\qquad \phi_s''(1)=\mathcal Q(s)>0.
\end{equation}
This coordinate is important: the derivatives of
$z/\varepsilon-\log z+H(z)$ diverge as $s\to0$, whereas the derivatives of
$\phi_s$ remain uniformly bounded near $\zeta=1$.
Moreover $\mathcal Q(s)$ is uniformly bounded above and away from zero on the
compact range relevant to $N\ge am^2$.

The contour may be parametrized by $t=s(1+\ii v)/m$;
reversing its orientation or replacing $v$ by $-v$ does not change the
real result. With $v=y/\sqrt{m\mathcal Q(s)}$, the normalized saddle integral is
Gaussian. Its leading term, using \eqref{rpc:cx:eq:centerexact} and
\eqref{rpc:cx:eq:EM}, is
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:continuum-leading}
 \frac{e^{m(B(s)+H(s))}(m/s)^m}{m!}
 \frac{s}{m\sqrt{2\pi m\mathcal Q(s)}}e^{h(s)/2}.
\end{equation}
Stirling's expansion of $\Gamma(m)$ shows that the ratio of
\eqref{rpc:cx:eq:continuum-leading} to $V_m(X)$ is
$\exp(m\mathcal D(\varepsilon)+E_0(\varepsilon))$ times the Stirling correction series.
This proves the displayed forms of $\mathcal D$ and $E_0$.

\subsection{An explicit all-order operator}
The following formal prescription fixes every higher coefficient without
an unspecified appeal to ``standard saddle coefficients.'' Let $\hbar$ be a
formal variable and $g$ a standard normal variable, and put
\begin{equation}
 \zeta=1+\frac{\ii\hbar g}{\sqrt{\mathcal Q(s)}}.
\end{equation}
Define $\mathcal G(\hbar,s)$ by formally expanding
\begin{align}\label{rpc:cx:eq:G}
 \mathcal G(\hbar,s)=\E\exp\bigg\{&
 \frac{\phi_s(\zeta)-\phi_s(1)+\hbar^2g^2/2}{\hbar^2}
 +\frac{h(s\zeta)-h(s)}2\notag\\
 &+\sum_{k\ge1}\mathsf A_k(s\zeta)\hbar^{4k-2}\bigg\}.
\end{align}
At every requested power of $\hbar$, this means finite Taylor expansion,
followed by replacement of Gaussian monomials by their moments.
The apparent negative power of $\hbar$ causes no problem: the saddle and
quadratic terms cancel in the numerator. Odd coefficients vanish after
Gaussian averaging. In particular $\mathcal G(0,s)=1$.

For $j\ge1$ set
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:Ejoperator}
 E_j(\varepsilon)=[\hbar^{2j}]\left\{
 \log \mathcal G(\hbar,s(\varepsilon))+
 \sum_{k\ge1}\frac{B_{2k}}{2k(2k-1)}\hbar^{4k-2}\right\}.
\end{equation}
The second sum is the logarithmic Stirling series for $\Gamma(m)$,
with $\hbar=m^{-1/2}$. Formula~\eqref{rpc:cx:eq:Ejoperator} is both a definition and
an effective coefficient algorithm: only finitely many derivatives and
Gaussian moments occur for any fixed $j$.

\subsection{Proof of the uniform theorem}
On the compact $s$-range, the derivatives in \eqref{rpc:cx:eq:G} and a fixed
number of Euler--Maclaurin remainders have uniform bounds. The local
Gaussian argument used for Theorem~\ref{rpc:cx:thm:finite} therefore gives a
uniform finite expansion, with error $\mathrm O(m^{-J-1})$ after taking
sufficiently many terms. The contour outside that window is negligible
relative to its leading size $s/m^{3/2}$ by
Lemmas~\ref{rpc:cx:lem:small}--\ref{rpc:cx:lem:large}, uniformly even as $s\to0$.
The leading amplitude in \eqref{rpc:cx:eq:continuum-leading}, after
normalization, stays positive and bounded away from zero. Thus the
logarithm of the normalized expansion is legitimate for large $m$ and
has coefficients \eqref{rpc:cx:eq:Ejoperator}.

All operations defining those coefficients are analytic on a local
complex neighborhood of each real $s$ in the compact range.
The evenness of $h,H,\mathsf A_k$ and the oddness of $s(\varepsilon)$ give even germs
in $\varepsilon$; the normalized phase uses $B(s)$ and $H(s\zeta)$, which are even
in $s$ as well.

Finally, at $s=0$ the phase is $\zeta-\log \zeta$, all $\mathsf A_k$ and $h$ vanish,
and its inverse-Laplace integral is
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:gamma-integral}
 \frac1{2\pi\ii}\int_{1-\ii\infty}^{1+\ii\infty}
          e^{m\zeta}\zeta^{-m}\dd \zeta=\frac{m^{m-1}}{\Gamma(m)}.
\end{equation}
It follows that $\mathcal G(\hbar,0)$ is exactly the reciprocal Stirling series
appearing in \eqref{rpc:cx:eq:Ejoperator}; their logarithms cancel coefficient
by coefficient. Hence $E_j(0)=0$ for all $j$.
This establishes Theorem~\ref{rpc:cx:thm:uniform}. The evaluation at $s=0$ is
an identity of asymptotic coefficient systems, not a statement that a
finite partition problem has $\varepsilon=0$.

\begin{remark}[\textsf{[write]} Completion of the proof of Theorem~\ref{rpc:cx:thm:uniform}; added 5 October 2026, batch~98]
\label{rpc:cx:rem:completion}
The proof above is condensed: it does not say on which circle Cauchy's
formula is applied, why the continuum saddle may replace the exact one, or
on which complex domain Euler--Maclaurin is uniform. The following fills
these steps; it uses only Lemmas~\ref{rpc:cx:lem:small}
and~\ref{rpc:cx:lem:large}, Lemma~\ref{rpc:cx:lem:saddle} and the
displays of this section. Fix $a>0$ and put $\bar s=s(\varepsilon_a)$. For
$N\ge am^2$, \eqref{rpc:cx:eq:epsrange} and the monotonicity in
Lemma~\ref{rpc:cx:lem:saddle} give $0<s<\bar s$, where $s=s(\varepsilon)$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item \emph{The circle.} Apply Cauchy's formula on $|q|=e^{-\tau}$ with
 $\tau=s/m$, the continuum saddle, so $\tau\le\bar s/m$ and both lemmas of
 \S\ref{rpc:cx:sec:arcs} hold with $t=\tau$. With $t=\tau+\ii\theta$,
 \[
  p_m(N)=\frac{e^{N\tau}F_m(e^{-\tau})}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}
  \frac{e^{\ii N\theta}F_m(e^{-\tau-\ii\theta})}{F_m(e^{-\tau})}\dd\theta,
 \]
 and the modulus of the integrand is $\prod_j\varrho_j(\theta)$ with
 $t=\tau$. Nothing in the argument uses the exact saddle $t_{m,N}$.
\item \emph{The exponent in the window.} On the window
 $|\theta|\le\tau m^{-1/2+\delta}$ write $t=s\zeta/m$, $\zeta=1+\ii v$,
 $|v|\le m^{-1/2+\delta}$. Since $X=m^2/\varepsilon=m^2B(s)/s$, one has
 $Xt=mB(s)\zeta$, and \eqref{rpc:cx:eq:centerexact} becomes
 \[
  Nt+\Lambda_m(t)=m\phi_s(\zeta)
  +\Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^mh(js\zeta/m)-mH(s\zeta)\Bigr)
  -m\log\frac sm-\log m!.
 \]
 The function $z/(2\sinh(z/2))$ has no zeros in the strip
 $|\operatorname{Im}z|<2\pi$ (the zeros of $\sinh(z/2)$ other than $z=0$
 are $2\pi\ii k$, $k\ne0$, and the quotient equals $1$ at $z=0$), so $h$ is
 analytic and even there. Put $\rho_0=\min(1/2,\pi/\bar s)$. For
 $0\le s\le\bar s$, $|\zeta-1|\le\rho_0$ and $0\le x\le1$ the point
 $xs\zeta$ has $|\operatorname{Im}(xs\zeta)|\le\pi$, so $h$, $H$, $\phi_s$,
 $h(s\zeta)$ and $\mathsf A_k(s\zeta)$ are analytic in $\zeta$ on
 $|\zeta-1|\le\rho_0$, with bounds on all their derivatives that depend only
 on $\bar s$ and the order. Euler--Maclaurin of order $2K$ for
 $f(x)=h(xs\zeta/m)$ on $[0,m]$ gives the bracket as
 $h(s\zeta)/2+\sum_{k\le K}\mathsf A_k(s\zeta)m^{1-2k}$ plus a remainder
 at most a constant times $m\cdot(\bar s(1+\rho_0)/m)^{2K}$, uniformly:
 $f(0)=0$, the odd derivatives of $f$ vanish at $x=0$ because $h$ is even,
 $f^{(2k-1)}(m)=(s\zeta/m)^{2k-1}h^{(2k-1)}(s\zeta)$, and the remainder
 integral is bounded by $m\sup|f^{(2K)}|$ times a constant depending on~$K$.
\item \emph{The Gaussian step.} Here $\phi_s'(1)=0$ and
 $\phi_s''(1)=\mathcal Q(s)\in[\mathcal Q_-,\mathcal Q_+]\subset(0,\infty)$
 for $0\le s\le\bar s$. Put $\hbar=m^{-1/2}$ and $v=\hbar y/\sqrt{\mathcal Q(s)}$,
 so that $\zeta=1+\ii\hbar y/\sqrt{\mathcal Q(s)}$, the quadratic part of
 $m(\phi_s(\zeta)-\phi_s(1))$ is $-y^2/2$, and the window is
 $|y|\le\sqrt{\mathcal Q(s)}\,m^{\delta}$. By Cauchy's estimates on
 $|\zeta-1|\le\rho_0$, the Taylor term of degree $r\ge3$ of
 $m(\phi_s(\zeta)-\phi_s(1))$ is $\mathrm O(\hbar^{r-2}|y|^r)$ uniformly in $s$.
 The continuum saddle is a critical point of $\phi_s$ only, not of the whole
 exponent: $(h(s\zeta)-h(s))/2$ has the linear term
 $sh'(s)\,\ii\hbar y/(2\sqrt{\mathcal Q(s)})=\mathrm O(\hbar|y|)$, which is not
 cancelled and is expanded together with the cubic and higher terms; it is the
 term $(h(s\zeta)-h(s))/2$ of \eqref{rpc:cx:eq:G}, and it pairs with odd
 terms after Gaussian averaging. For $\delta<1/6$ all these corrections,
 and the Euler--Maclaurin remainders, are $\mathrm O(m^{-1/2+3\delta})$
 uniformly on the window, so the exponent there is $-y^2/2+o(1)$. Taylor
 expansion to a finite degree and expansion of the exponential to weight
 $2J+2$ leave a remainder bounded by $m^{-J-1}$ times a polynomial in $|y|$
 times $e^{-y^2/2}$, provided $\delta$ is small in terms of $J$ and the
 Euler--Maclaurin order satisfies $K\ge J+1$. Extending the
 Gaussian-polynomial integrals from the window to $\mathbb R$ costs
 $\mathrm O(e^{-cm^{2\delta}})$. With the constant terms
 $\mathsf A_k(s)\hbar^{4k-2}$ kept inside $\mathcal G$, as in
 \eqref{rpc:cx:eq:G}, and $\dd\theta=\tau\hbar\,\dd y/\sqrt{\mathcal Q(s)}$,
 the window contributes
 \[
  \frac{\tau}{\sqrt{2\pi m\mathcal Q(s)}}\,
  \frac{e^{m(B(s)+H(s))+h(s)/2}(m/s)^m}{m!}
  \bigl(\mathcal G_{2J}(\hbar,s)+\mathrm O(m^{-J-1})\bigr)
 \]
 to $p_m(N)$, where $\mathcal G_{2J}$ is the truncation of
 \eqref{rpc:cx:eq:G} after $\hbar^{2J}$; with $\tau=s/m$ this is
 \eqref{rpc:cx:eq:continuum-leading} times
 $\mathcal G_{2J}+\mathrm O(m^{-J-1})$. Since
 $e^{N\tau}F_m(e^{-\tau})=e^{m(B(s)+H(s))+h(s)/2+\mathrm O(1/m)}(m/s)^m/m!$
 by (ii) at $\zeta=1$, the window integral, relative to
 $e^{N\tau}F_m(e^{-\tau})$, has size $\asymp\tau m^{-1/2}$.
\item \emph{The tails, relative to the window.} With $x=\theta/\tau$,
 Lemma~\ref{rpc:cx:lem:small} bounds the integral over
 $\tau m^{-1/2+\delta}\le|\theta|\le1/m$ by
 \[
  \tau\int_{m^{-1/2+\delta}\le|x|\le1}e^{-c'mx^2}\dd x
  +\tau\int_{|x|\ge1}(1+cx^2)^{-m/2}\dd x
  =\mathrm O\bigl(\tau e^{-c''m^{2\delta}}\bigr),
 \]
 using $(1+cx^2)^{-m/2}\le e^{-c'mx^2}$ for $|x|\le1$ and
 $(1+cx^2)^{-m/2}\le(1+c)^{-(m-2)/2}(1+cx^2)^{-1}$ for $|x|\ge1$.
 Lemma~\ref{rpc:cx:lem:large} bounds the integral over
 $1/m\le|\theta|\le\pi$ by $C\tau(1+\log m)e^{-cm}$. Divided by the window
 size $\asymp\tau m^{-1/2}$, both are $\mathrm O(m^{-A})$ for every $A$,
 uniformly in $0<s\le\bar s$: the factor $\tau$ cancels. This is where
 the reserved factor $j=1$ of Lemma~\ref{rpc:cx:lem:large} is essential,
 since $\tau=s/m$ is unbounded below as $N/m^2\to\infty$.
\item \emph{Normalization.} At $\zeta=1$ the exponent is
 $m(B(s)+H(s))+h(s)/2+\sum_k\mathsf A_k(s)m^{1-2k}-m\log(s/m)-\log m!$,
 up to the Euler--Maclaurin remainder. Combining (iii) and (iv) with this and
 with Stirling's series for $\log\Gamma(m)$, exactly as for
 \eqref{rpc:cx:eq:continuum-leading}, gives
 $\log(p_m(N)/V_m(X))=m\mathcal D+E_0+\log\mathcal G_{2J}(\hbar,s)
 +\sum_{k}B_{2k}\hbar^{4k-2}/(2k(2k-1))+\mathrm O(m^{-J-1})$. Since
 $\mathcal G_{2J}=1+\mathrm O(m^{-1})$ uniformly, its logarithm may be expanded,
 and the coefficients are \eqref{rpc:cx:eq:Ejoperator}.
\item \emph{Analyticity and parity.} Each $[\hbar^{2j}]\log\mathcal G$ is a
 polynomial in finitely many of $\phi_s^{(r)}(1)=(-1)^r(r-1)!+s^rH^{(r)}(s)$
 ($r\ge2$), $s^kh^{(k)}(s)$, the derivatives of $\mathsf A_k(s\zeta)$ at
 $\zeta=1$, and $\mathcal Q(s)^{-1}$: a monomial survives Gaussian averaging
 only if its total degree in $g$ is even, so only integer powers of
 $\mathcal Q(s)^{-1}$ occur. These are analytic on a neighbourhood of
 $[0,\infty)$ (where $\mathcal Q>0$) and even in $s$, because $H$, $h$ and
 $z^{2k-1}h^{(2k-1)}(z)$ are even, so $s^rH^{(r)}(s)$ and $s^kh^{(k)}(s)$
 are even. Composition with the odd analytic $s(\varepsilon)$ of
 Lemma~\ref{rpc:cx:lem:saddle} gives analytic $E_j$ on $[0,4)$ with even
 germs at zero; $E_j(0)=0$ is the gamma-integral argument above, made
 rigorous by the uniqueness of asymptotic expansions: the same Gaussian step
 applied to \eqref{rpc:cx:eq:gamma-integral} gives
 $\mathcal G(\hbar,0)\,e^m(2\pi m)^{-1/2}$ as the expansion of
 $m^{m-1}/\Gamma(m)$.
\end{enumerate}
With (i)--(vi), the proof of Theorem~\ref{rpc:cx:thm:uniform} is complete
at the level of detail of Part~I's proof of Theorem~\ref{rpc:thm:centered}.
It has not been independently refereed.
\end{remark}

\section{Explicit coefficients and internal consistency checks}
\label{rpc:cx:sec:coeff}
The Bernoulli expansion is
\begin{align}
 h(z)&=-\sum_{r\ge1}\frac{B_{2r}}{2r(2r)!}z^{2r}
       =-\frac{z^2}{24}+\frac{z^4}{2880}
           -\frac{z^6}{181440}+\cdots,\label{rpc:cx:eq:hseries}\\
 H(z)&=-\frac{z^2}{72}+\frac{z^4}{14400}
           -\frac{z^6}{1270080}+\cdots.
\end{align}
These series converge in a neighborhood of zero; no assertion about
convergence of the $1/m$ expansion follows from that fact.

\subsection{The entropy coefficients}
The envelope derivative of \eqref{rpc:cx:eq:D} is especially simple:
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:envelope}
 \varepsilon \mathcal D'(\varepsilon)=1-\frac{s(\varepsilon)}\varepsilon=1-B(s(\varepsilon)).
\end{equation}
Lagrange inversion applied to $s=\varepsilon B(s)$ yields
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:s-lagrange}
 [\varepsilon^{2r+1}]s(\varepsilon)
       =\frac{[z^{2r}]B(z)^{2r+1}}{2r+1}.
\end{equation}
Integrating \eqref{rpc:cx:eq:envelope} with $\mathcal D(0)=0$ proves
\eqref{rpc:cx:eq:d-lagrange}. For example,
\begin{equation}
 s(\varepsilon)=\varepsilon+\frac{\varepsilon^3}{36}
 +\frac{41\varepsilon^5}{32400}
 +\frac{3749\varepsilon^7}{57153600}+\mathrm O(\varepsilon^9).

\section{The crossover hierarchy and its infinitude}\label{rpc:cx:sec:hierarchy}

\begin{writenote}[Added 5 October 2026, batch~98: Part~I's question~2 answered]
Part~I's research question~2 (\S\ref{rpc:sec:research}) asks to extend the
centered analysis into $m^{5/2}\lesssim N\ll m^3$, where the quadratic
Bernoulli correction must itself be exponentiated, and to match the result
to a small-parameter expansion of the Szekeres saddle, with a precise
validity range and matching error. Theorem~\ref{rpc:cx:thm:uniform} does
this with no gap: one expansion, with remainder $\mathrm O_{a,J}(m^{-J-1})$,
holds uniformly for every $N\ge am^2$, so it covers $N\ll m^3$, the scale
$m^{5/2}$ and the compact-$\alpha$ Szekeres regime at once
(\S\ref{rpc:cx:sec:oeis}). The exponentiated quadratic correction is the
first entropy term $\mathsf d_1m\varepsilon^2=-m^5/(72X^2)$; this section
shows that $m^{5/2}$ is the exact threshold of the centered volume law and
that it is the first of infinitely many transition scales. The question's
lower end, $N/m^2\to0$, is not part of it and stays open
(\S\ref{rpc:cx:sec:q-endpoint}).
\end{writenote}

\subsection{A uniform dilute expansion}
For fixed $r$, analyticity at zero and Theorem~\ref{rpc:cx:thm:uniform} give,
whenever $\varepsilon\to0$,
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:dilute-remainder}
 \log\frac{p_m(N)}{\mathcal V_{r-1}(m,N)}
 =\mathsf d_r m\varepsilon^{2r}
 +\mathrm O_r\!\left(m\varepsilon^{2r+2}+\varepsilon^2+m^{-1}\right).
\end{equation}
In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:entropy-physical}
 \log\frac{p_m(N)}{V_m(X)}
 =-\frac{m^5}{72X^2}-\frac{41m^9}{129600X^4}
   -\frac{3749m^{13}}{342921600X^6}+\cdots,
\end{equation}
where the omitted endpoint and finite-size terms must be included when
an actual numerical accuracy is specified. Equation~\eqref{rpc:cx:eq:main},
not the isolated display \eqref{rpc:cx:eq:entropy-physical}, is the full formula.

If $X\sim\lambda m^{2+1/(2r)}$, then
$m\varepsilon^{2r}\to\lambda^{-2r}$ and every error in
\eqref{rpc:cx:eq:dilute-remainder} tends to zero. This proves
\eqref{rpc:cx:eq:hierarchy-limit}. More generally, within $X/m^2\to\infty$,
if $X\gg m^{2+1/(2r)}$ the ratio tends to one. If instead
$X\ll m^{2+1/(2r)}$, then its logarithm is asymptotic to
$\mathsf d_rm\varepsilon^{2r}$; the ratio tends to zero when $\mathsf d_r<0$ and to infinity when
$\mathsf d_r>0$.

\subsection{Necessity in the sharp centered boundary}
By Lemma~\ref{rpc:cx:lem:saddle} and \eqref{rpc:cx:eq:envelope},
$\mathcal D'(\varepsilon)<0$ on $(0,4)$, so $\mathcal D(\varepsilon)<0$ there.
Suppose first that $p_m(N)/V_m(X)\to1$ with $N\ge am^2$.
If $\varepsilon$ did not tend to zero, a subsequence would remain in a compact
subinterval bounded away from zero. The term $m\mathcal D(\varepsilon)$ would then tend
to $-\infty$, while $E_0$ is bounded, a contradiction.
Thus $\varepsilon\to0$, and
\begin{equation}
 \log\frac{p_m(N)}{V_m(X)}
 =-\frac{m\varepsilon^2}{72}(1+\mathrm O(\varepsilon^2))
        +\mathrm O(\varepsilon^2+m^{-1}).
\end{equation}
The logarithm can tend to zero only if $m\varepsilon^2\to0$.
Conversely this condition makes the right side tend to zero.
Since $m\varepsilon^2=m^5/X^2$, this proves \eqref{rpc:cx:eq:iff}; the same display
proves \eqref{rpc:cx:eq:firstboundary}. This subsequence argument also covers
oscillating choices of $N$ rather than only power-law inputs.

\subsection{Why the hierarchy does not terminate}
It remains to prove that $\mathcal D$ is not a polynomial. For $s>0$, direct
integration of $h(s)=\log s-s/2-\log(1-e^{-s})$ yields
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:Hli2}
 H(s)=\log s-1-\frac s4+
       \frac{\pi^2/6-\operatorname{Li}_2(e^{-s})}{s}.
\end{equation}
Consequently, as $s\to\infty$,
\begin{align}
 B(s)&=\frac s4+\frac{\pi^2}{6s}+\mathrm O(e^{-s}),\label{rpc:cx:eq:Blarge}\\
 4-\varepsilon(s)&\sim\frac{8\pi^2}{3s^2}.\label{rpc:cx:eq:epslarge}
\end{align}
The entropy has the endpoint expansion
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:Dendpoint}
 \mathcal D(\varepsilon)=\log4-2+
       \frac{\pi}{2\sqrt6}\sqrt{4-\varepsilon}+\mathrm O(4-\varepsilon),
       \qquad \varepsilon\uparrow4.
\end{equation}
For example, it follows by substituting \eqref{rpc:cx:eq:Blarge} and
\eqref{rpc:cx:eq:Hli2} into $\mathcal D=B-1-\log B+H$; the leading deviation is
$\pi^2/(3s)$ and \eqref{rpc:cx:eq:epslarge} gives the stated constant.
Alternatively, \eqref{rpc:cx:eq:envelope} directly implies
$\mathcal D'(\varepsilon)\to-\infty$.

If only finitely many coefficients $\mathsf d_r$ were nonzero, $\mathcal D$ would equal a
polynomial near zero. Analytic continuation along the real interval
$(0,4)$ would make it that polynomial throughout, contradicting
\eqref{rpc:cx:eq:Dendpoint}. Hence infinitely many $\mathsf d_r$ are nonzero, completing
Theorem~\ref{rpc:cx:thm:hierarchy}.

\begin{remark}[An analytic endpoint is not a uniform partition regime]
Equation~\eqref{rpc:cx:eq:Dendpoint} concerns the function $\mathcal D$ itself.
The uniform theorem has a fixed lower bound $N\ge am^2$ and does not
allow $a\downarrow0$ with $m$. In particular the endpoint argument does
not silently extend the partition expansion into the unrestricted
Hardy--Ramanujan regime.
\end{remark}

\section{How the quadratic and cubic OEIS formulas fit together}
\label{rpc:cx:sec:oeis}

\subsection{The classical quadratic leading formula}
Fix $\alpha>0$ and take $N=\alpha m^2$ along integral values.
Let $s_\alpha$ solve
\begin{equation}
 \int_0^1\frac{x}{e^{s_\alpha x}-1}\dd x=\alpha.
\end{equation}
Rearrangement of the leading terms of Theorem~\ref{rpc:cx:thm:uniform} gives
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:szekeres}
 p_m(\alpha m^2)\sim
 \frac{s_\alpha}{2\pi m^2
 \sqrt{2\alpha-(2\alpha+1)e^{-s_\alpha}}}
 \exp\!\left(m\{2\alpha s_\alpha
                    -\log(1-e^{-s_\alpha})\}\right).
\end{equation}
This is a parametrization of the classical Szekeres leading formula,
not a new leading asymptotic~\cite{canfield}.
For clarity about the endpoint constant, $X/m^2=\alpha+1/4+1/(4m)$.
That last $1/(4m)$ contributes $e^{s_\alpha/4}$ to the leading
amplitude. Together with $e^{h(s_\alpha)/2}$ it produces the
factor in \eqref{rpc:cx:eq:szekeres}; dropping it gives an incorrect constant.

At $\alpha=1$, formula~\eqref{rpc:cx:eq:szekeres} recovers the leading law
for A206226 and the square subsequence of A097356. The previous
repository report's floor-phase and three-exception theorems are
separate results and are not reproved or claimed here. Our theorem
supplies a common expansion from the compact quadratic regime all the
way through $\alpha\to\infty$.

\subsection{Recovery of the uncentered cubic boundary}
Let $N\sim cm^3$, $c>0$. Then $\varepsilon\sim1/(cm)$, so
$m\mathcal D(\varepsilon)+E_0(\varepsilon)=o(1)$. On the other hand,
\begin{equation}
 \log\frac{V_m(X)}{V_m(N)}
 =(m-1)\log\left(1+\frac{m(m+1)}{4N}\right)
 \longrightarrow\frac1{4c}.
\end{equation}
Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:cubicrecover}
 p_m(N)\sim e^{1/(4c)}\frac{N^{m-1}}{m!\Gamma(m)}.
\end{equation}
This recovers the earlier repository and OEIS cubic law
(Corollary~\ref{rpc:cor:crossover} of Part~I, and~\cite{oeis238608}); it is a compatibility check, not a claim
that this law was missing.

For the exact A238608 slice $N=m^3$, Stirling expansion and
\eqref{rpc:cx:eq:main} give
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:a238608}
 p_m(m^3)=\frac{e^{2m+1/4}m^{m-3}}{2\pi}
 \left(1-\frac{61}{288m}+\mathrm O(m^{-2})\right).
\end{equation}
The leading term is posted in OEIS, and the correction is already in
the inspected cubic report. The symbolic verifier checks that the new
uniform formulation reproduces it exactly.

\subsection{What the intermediate scales add}
Between $m^2$ and $m^3$, retaining only the shift $N\mapsto X$ is not
enough. At $X\asymp m^{5/2}$ the first entropy term becomes visible.
After incorporating it, the next transition lies at $m^{9/4}$; the
next lies at $m^{13/6}$, and so on for the nonzero coefficients.
At fixed $N/m^2$, no finite number of these dilute entropy terms is a
uniform substitute for the full analytic function $\mathcal D$. Thus the
hierarchy explains both why many approximations work in a dilute range
and why they eventually fail as the quadratic range is approached.

\section{Analytic inversion and discrete thresholds}\label{rpc:cx:sec:inverse}

\subsection{The leading inverse map}
For $Z,\eta_\star$ in \eqref{rpc:cx:eq:inverse-input}, write $X=Zx$.
Equation~\eqref{rpc:cx:eq:main} becomes
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:inverse-eq}
 0=(m-1)\log x+m\mathcal D(\eta_\star/x)+E_0(\eta_\star/x)
          +\frac{E_1(\eta_\star/x)}m+\cdots.
\end{equation}
Dividing by $m$, the leading equation is
$\log x+\mathcal D(\eta_\star/x)=0$. Setting $\varepsilon=\eta_\star/x$ gives exactly
\eqref{rpc:cx:eq:inverse-core}.
By \eqref{rpc:cx:eq:envelope},
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:inverse-monotone}
 \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\varepsilon}(\varepsilon e^{-\mathcal D(\varepsilon)})
   =e^{-\mathcal D(\varepsilon)}(1-\varepsilon \mathcal D'(\varepsilon))
   =e^{-\mathcal D(\varepsilon)}B(s)>0.
\end{equation}
At zero this derivative is one; as $\varepsilon\uparrow4$, its image tends to
$4e^{-(\log4-2)}=e^2$. This proves the existence and analyticity of the
inverse chart on $[0,e^2)$.

\subsection{First and all higher corrections}
Put $x=\mathcal R(\eta_\star)e^{\omega/m+\mathrm O(m^{-2})}$ in
\eqref{rpc:cx:eq:inverse-eq}. Its constant-order terms are
\begin{equation}
 (1-\varepsilon \mathcal D'(\varepsilon))\omega-\log\mathcal R+E_0(\varepsilon)=0,
\end{equation}
which proves \eqref{rpc:cx:eq:v} and \eqref{rpc:cx:eq:inverse-first}.
More generally, insert
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:inverse-all}
 X=Z\mathcal R(\eta_\star)\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^K\frac{\omega_k(\eta_\star)}{m^k}
                            +\mathrm O_{a,K}(m^{-K-1})\right).
\end{equation}
At each new order the unknown $\omega_k$ enters linearly with coefficient
$1-\varepsilon \mathcal D'(\varepsilon)=B(s)$, bounded away from zero. The remaining terms
are already known analytic functions. This recursion, or the analytic
implicit-function theorem applied separately to each finite truncation
with parameter $1/m$, proves all fixed orders uniformly on the compact parameter ranges of the forward theorem.
The finite forward remainder transfers through the same nonvanishing
derivative. This completes Theorem~\ref{rpc:cx:thm:inverse-intro}.

\label{rpc:cx:rem:inverse-completion}

$\hbar_1=0$, $F_0(x;\eta_\star,0)=\log x+\mathcal D(\eta_\star/x)$ is strictly
increasing in $x$ with derivative $B(s)/x>0$ ($\varepsilon=\eta_\star/x$),

since $\eta_\star=\varepsilon x=\varepsilon e^{-\mathcal D(\varepsilon)+\mathrm O(1/m)}$

\subsection{A safe integer-threshold statement}
Let $T_m(Y)=\min\{N\ge0:p_m(N)\ge Y\}$. The injection that adds a
part of size one makes $p_m$ nondecreasing, but does not provide a
canonical real interpolation. Define the smooth logarithmic surrogate
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal L_J(X)=\log V_m(X)+m\mathcal D(m^2/X)+E_0(m^2/X)
                      +\sum_{j=1}^J\frac{E_j(m^2/X)}{m^j}.
\end{equation}
On any admissible compact parameter range,
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:inverse-derivative}
 \mathcal L_J'(X)=\frac{mB(s)-1+\mathrm O_a(1)}X
                       \asymp_a\frac mX>0
\end{equation}
for sufficiently large $m$. Its leading derivative follows directly
from \eqref{rpc:cx:eq:envelope}; the derivatives of the remaining analytic
functions are bounded.

\begin{proposition}[Threshold error transfer]\label{rpc:cx:prop:threshold}
Suppose the true threshold and a fixed relative neighborhood of it lie
in $N\ge am^2$. Let $X_J$ solve
$\mathcal L_J(X_J)=\log Y$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:threshold}
 T_m(Y)=X_J-\frac{m(m+1)}4
             +\mathrm O_{a,J}\left(\frac{X_J}{m^{J+2}}+1\right).
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Theorem~\ref{rpc:cx:thm:uniform} bounds the logarithmic error at every integer
sample by $Cm^{-J-1}$. By \eqref{rpc:cx:eq:inverse-derivative}, moving the
smooth root by $C'X_Jm^{-J-2}$ changes the surrogate logarithm by at
least that amount, with either sign, if $C'$ is large enough.
Round the two comparison locations outward to integers, adding a
bounded displacement. The lower one has count below $Y$ and the upper
one has count at least $Y$. Monotonicity supplies the bracket.
\end{proof}
A symbolic asymptotic expansion alone does not furnish an explicit
numerical value of the error constant or an exact ceiling formula.
Even a very small relative error can correspond to a large absolute
index error when $X$ is enormous.

\begin{writenote}[Added 5 October 2026, batch~98: an instance of repository results]
For fixed $m\ge2$ the counts $p_m(N)$ are strictly increasing in $N\ge1$
(the difference $p_m(N+1)-p_m(N)$ counts partitions of $N+1$ into parts
from $\{2,\ldots,m\}$), so $T_m(Y)$ is the integer staircase of
\texttt{p0:thm:staircase} in the transseries-and-inversion
volume~\cite{rpc-tai}, and the proof above is that theorem's bracketing
argument (its parts~(1)--(2)) with an explicit chart error. As in
Part~I's dated notes in \S\ref{rpc:sec:inverse}, no novelty is claimed for
the method.
\end{writenote}

\subsection{Lambert-\texorpdfstring{$W$}{W} inversion along a power-law slice}
A complementary inverse lets $m$ vary on a fixed centered slice
$X=\lambda m^\gamma$, $\gamma>2$, $\lambda>0$.
Ignoring bounded integer rounding for the moment, Stirling gives
\begin{align}\label{rpc:cx:eq:slice}
 \log p_m(N)={}&(\gamma-2)m\log m+(2+\log\lambda)m
 -\gamma\log m-\log(2\pi\lambda)\notag\\
 &+m\mathcal D(\lambda^{-1}m^{2-\gamma})
   +E_0(\lambda^{-1}m^{2-\gamma})
   +\frac{E_1(\lambda^{-1}m^{2-\gamma})-1/6}{m}
   +\mathrm O(m^{-2}).
\end{align}
For $L=\log Y$, $\beta=\gamma-2$ and $b_\lambda=2+\log\lambda$, the leading
inverse is
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:W}
 m_0=\frac{L}{\beta W(e^{b_\lambda/\beta}L/\beta)}.
\end{equation}
Successive corrections are obtained by substituting $m=m_0+\Delta$
into \eqref{rpc:cx:eq:slice}; the leading derivative is
$\beta\log m_0+\beta+b_\lambda$. All the retained entropy sectors must be
included in that substitution at their actual sizes. This is an
application of the repository's transseries-inversion program, not a
new inversion method. Bounded rounding of $N$ alters the continuous
profile logarithm by $\mathrm O(m/X)$ and does not affect its leading
asymptotic; exact thresholds still require an argument such as
Proposition~\ref{rpc:cx:prop:threshold}.

\section{What kind of transseries has been obtained?}\label{rpc:cx:sec:transseries}
The answer contains two different expansions.
First, $\mathcal D(\varepsilon)$ is a genuinely convergent analytic germ in $\varepsilon$,
with an effective rational coefficient formula. Second,
\eqref{rpc:cx:eq:main} is a fixed-order asymptotic expansion in $1/m$, uniform
in a compact continuum parameter. We have not proved convergence,
Gevrey bounds, Borel summability or optimal truncation for the latter.

On a power-law slice $X=\lambda m^\gamma$, its entropy factor resolves
ordered exponential corrections
\begin{equation}\label{rpc:cx:eq:sectors}
 \exp\left(\sum_{r\ge1}
 \mathsf d_r\lambda^{-2r}m^{\,1-2r(\gamma-2)}\right).
\end{equation}
Only finitely many exponents in \eqref{rpc:cx:eq:sectors} are nonnegative when
$\gamma>2$. After those terms are retained in the exponent, the others
can be expanded to any desired fixed algebraic order, together with
the endpoint and $1/m$ functions. At a transition exponent the
corresponding term is a nontrivial constant rather than a decaying
correction. This is the precise sense in which the report supplies an
exponential asymptotic hierarchy useful for transseries calculations.

It would be too strong to call this a complete resurgent transseries.
The exponentially small contributions of other roots of unity are
not individually identified; their actions, amplitudes and possible
Stokes behavior are not derived. The minor-arc estimates provide the
uniform algebraic accuracy needed here, not a classification of every
beyond-all-orders sector.

\begin{writenote}[Added 5 October 2026, batch~98]
Part~I states the same limitation for its centered theorem (the remark after
the proof of Theorem~\ref{rpc:thm:centered}) and poses the individual waves
as its question~1. Part~I's dilute expansion \eqref{rpc:eq:exp-transseries}
does resolve genuine $q^{-m}$ sectors, but only for $N\ge m^3/\eta_0$
(Theorem~\ref{rpc:thm:dilute}); nothing in Part~II resolves exponentially
small sectors at the quadratic scale.
\end{writenote}

\subsection{Are all entropy coefficients strictly negative?}
\label{rpc:cx:sec:q-negativity}
The first six $\mathsf d_r$ are negative and infinitely many are nonzero.
Is $[z^{2r}]B(z)^{2r+1}>0$ for every $r\ge1$? A positive integral or
combinatorial representation of these diagonal coefficients would settle
the question and make every exponent $2+1/(2r)$ an unconditional member
of the hierarchy. The alternating Bernoulli coefficients of $B$ mean
that coefficientwise positivity is not immediate.

\begin{writenote}[Added 5 October 2026, batch~98: evidence]
In exact rational arithmetic, by two independent routes (the Lagrange
formula \eqref{rpc:cx:eq:d-lagrange}, and the series of
$\mathcal D=s/\varepsilon-1-\log(s/\varepsilon)+H(s)$ after solving
$s=\varepsilon B(s)$ by fixed-point iteration), $\mathsf d_1,\ldots,\mathsf d_{16}$
all agree and are all negative; $\mathsf d_1,\ldots,\mathsf d_6$ are those of
Table~\ref{rpc:cx:tab:coefficients}. This is evidence, not a proof; what is
missing is an argument valid for every~$r$. An answer to
question~\ref{rpc:cx:sec:q-largeorder} would give negativity for all large
$r$ if the endpoint singularities dominate.
\end{writenote}

\subsection{What controls the large-order behavior of \texorpdfstring{$\mathsf d_r$}{d\_r}?}
\label{rpc:cx:sec:q-largeorder}
The real endpoint at $\varepsilon=4$ has a square-root singularity.
Does it dominate the Taylor coefficients, or do nearer complex critical
values of $s/B(s)$ intervene? Determine the radius of convergence,
nearest singularities, and the leading large-$r$ law for $\mathsf d_r$.
The endpoint analysis alone does not answer this complex-analytic question.

\begin{writenote}[Added 5 October 2026, batch~98: a conjectural law and its evidence]
If $\varepsilon=\pm4$ were the only singularities on the circle of
convergence, with the local form \eqref{rpc:cx:eq:Dendpoint} at both
($\mathcal D$ is even), the standard transfer from
$\sqrt{1-\varepsilon/4}$ would give
\[
 \mathsf d_r\sim-\sqrt{\pi/6}\,(2r)^{-3/2}\,16^{-r}\qquad(r\to\infty),
\]
and hence negativity of all large $\mathsf d_r$. The exact values agree with
this law to the ratios $0.869$, $0.955$, $0.987$ and $0.996$ at $r=1$, $7$,
$12$ and~$16$, increasing monotonically in between. This is numerical
evidence only. Two things are missing: a proof that $s/B(s)$ has no
complex critical value of modulus at most~$4$ other than at the real ends,
and a justification of the transfer, since the expansion of $\mathcal D$ at
$\varepsilon=4$ in powers of $\sqrt{4-\varepsilon}$ carries corrections of
size $e^{-s}$ with $s\asymp(4-\varepsilon)^{-1/2}$ (from
\eqref{rpc:cx:eq:Hli2}), which are not analytic in $\sqrt{4-\varepsilon}$ at
the endpoint.
\end{writenote}

A successful result should identify its maximal range and match to

\section{Conclusion of Part~II}\label{rpc:cx:sec:conclusion}
The restricted-partition family admits one centered, fixed-order
expansion throughout $N\ge am^2$ for each fixed $a>0$, without an upper
bound on $N$. Its zero-saddle endpoint remains regular after a scaled
local coordinate is used and one factor is reserved in the outer-arc
bound. The result connects the repository's quadratic and cubic
calculi and identifies a hierarchy of intermediate approximation scales.

The first scale is $m^{5/2}$, distinct from the uncentered cubic
boundary. Higher scales follow from explicit rational entropy
coefficients, and the hierarchy is proved not to terminate. The same
analytic entropy gives an invertible forward-to-inverse parameter map,
with all fixed-order corrections and a separate threshold error rule.
These are the proved conclusions of the report. Universal coefficient
negativity, complete exponential sectors, the opposite endpoint,
certified numerical thresholds and global priority remain outside its
established scope.

\section{A compact all-order coefficient recipe}
\begin{writenote}[Added 5 October 2026, batch~98]
This appendix belongs to Part~I.
\end{writenote}

For a requested order $K$, the following is a finite exact procedure.
Compute $S_{2r}(m)$ for $1\le r\le K$ by Faulhaber's formula or finite
symbolic summation. Form $\gamma_r$, then $a_j$ from \eqref{rpc:eq:a-rec}.
Let $x=1/m$, $h=(x+x^2)/(4c)$, and form, modulo $x^{K+1}$,
\[
 Q(x,c)=\sum_{j=0}^K a_j(1/x)
 \frac{x^{4j}}{c^{2j}}\prod_{i=1}^{2j}(1-ix)\,(1+h)^{-2j}.
\]
Although the expression uses $a_j(1/x)$, the combined summand has no negative
powers: its lowest possible degree is $j$. Then
\begin{align*}
 \sum_{r=1}^K L_r(c)x^r\equiv{}&\log Q(x,c)
 +(x^{-1}-1)\log(1+h)-\frac1{4c}\\
 &-2\sum_{r\ge1}\frac{B_{2r}}{2r(2r-1)}x^{2r-1}
                          \pmod{x^{K+1}}.
\end{align*}
Only finitely many Stirling terms contribute. Apply \eqref{rpc:eq:b-rec} to obtain
relative coefficients. This is the algorithm implemented in
\texttt{derive\_coefficients.py}; it uses exact rational arithmetic.
For dilute coefficients, use \eqref{rpc:eq:h-rec}, \eqref{rpc:eq:beta}, and
\eqref{rpc:eq:D-rec} instead. No numerical sequence values are needed to discover
any of the displayed coefficients.
