\begin{abstract}
Give a peak at $(a,b)$ of a Dyck path the weight $k+a/b$ and let $p_N(k)$ be the total weight of the paths of semilength $N$ (OEIS A258219). Its falling-factorial (Newton) coefficients $P_{N,m}=[\fall km]p_N(k)$ form the triangle A258220, whose diagonal $a_n=P_{2n,n}$ is A292692. This report prints three manuscripts as Parts~I--III, in the order in which each depends on the one before. Part~I proves the Riccati identity $F-1=kxF+xF^2+2x^2F_x$ for the generating function and its Stieltjes continued fraction, conjectured by Peter Bala on A258219 (July 28, 2022), through an exact identity for all weighted prefixes; it then proves $a_n\sim Cd^nn!/\sqrt n$ with $d=(51\sqrt{17}-107)/4$ and $C=\sqrt{2+2/\sqrt{17}}/\pi^{3/2}$, which establishes the asymptotic form stated numerically by V\'aclav Kot\v{e}\v{s}ovec on A292692 (December 1, 2017) and his conjectured rate (March 17, 2024), with the amplitude in closed form, and adds the first relative correction and a Lambert-$W$ threshold bracket. Part~II proves every fixed order of the normalized Newton ratio $R_{N,m}$ uniformly on every compact subset of $0<m/N<1$, removing Part~I's contraction restriction, with coefficients $\sqrt{1-s^2}$ times rational functions of the saddle parameter, all diagonal coefficients in $\Q(\sqrt{17})$, the second one explicitly, and a Lambert-$W$ inverse at every fixed order. Part~III treats the upper edge $m=N-q$: exact two-sided bounds for every $q\le N/2$, a connected profile uniformly for $q=o(\sqrt N)$ built from the four-regular-map sequence A292186, its expansion to every fixed order, its strict monotonicity, and a ceiling-safe inverse obtained by polynomial reversion, without Lambert~$W$. Asymptotics in the range $\sqrt N\lesssim q=o(N)$ between Parts~II and~III and at the lower edge $m=o(N)$, effective constants and convergence of the expansions remain open. The report is unrefereed, and nothing in it is formalized.
\end{abstract}

\section*{The OEIS statements, and the inverses}\phantomsection\label{wdn:sec:oeis}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{The OEIS statements, and the inverses}
The entries as read by the write on 5~October 2026 (A258219 revision \#30 of August 15, 2022; A292692 revision \#12 of March 17, 2024; A292186 revision \#57 of November 14, 2025):
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A258219} (Alois P. Heinz, May 23, 2015): the square array $A(n,k)$, the sum over Dyck paths of semilength $n$ of the products over peaks of $(x_p+ky_p)/y_p$, that is of $k+x_p/y_p$; so $A(n,k)=p_n(k)$. A comment signed \emph{Peter Bala, Jul 28 2022}, introduced by ``Conjecture'', states that the generating function $G(k,x)$ of the $k$-th column satisfies $2x^2\,dG/dx+1+(kx-1)G+xG^2=0$ ``and hence, by Stokes 1982'', has the Stieltjes continued fraction $1/(1-(k+1)x/(1-3x/(1-(k+3)x/(1-5x/\cdots))))$. The differential equation is \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:riccati} rearranged. Theorem~\ref{wdn:dia:thm:bridge} proves it with $k$ an indeterminate, hence for every column, and Proposition~\ref{wdn:dia:prop:CF} proves the continued fraction directly.
\item \textbf{A292692} (Alois P. Heinz, Sep 20, 2017), $a(n)=\mathrm{A258220}(2n,n)$. A formula signed \emph{Vaclav Kotesovec, Dec 01 2017} states $a(n)\sim c\,d^n\,n!/\sqrt n$ with $d=25.81959672662517201\ldots$ and $c=0.28310309251343879\ldots$, numerically and without proof; a formula signed \emph{Vaclav Kotesovec, Mar 17 2024} states ``Conjecture: $d=(51\sqrt{17}-107)/4$''. Theorem~\ref{wdn:dia:thm:main} proves the form with this $d$ and with $c=C$ in closed form; at 40 digits $C=0.28310309251343879373\ldots$ and $d=25.81959672662517201022\ldots$, agreeing with every stated digit (computed by the write). Part~I's text dates only the rate conjecture to 2024, correctly; the companions' \file{141-diagonal-SOURCES.md} and \file{142-interior-SOURCES.md} date ``the numerical asymptotic form and conjectural algebraic rate'' both to 2024-03-17, which is right for the rate only.
\item \textbf{A292186} (Sasha Kolpakov, Sep 11, 2017): rooted unlabeled connected four-regular maps on a compact closed oriented surface with $n$ vertices; the entry notes that it is Martin and Kearney's $S(4,-6,1)$ sequence with offset~1. A formula block signed \emph{Peter Bala, Aug 22 2023} gives the Riccati equation $4x^2A'=-1+(1-2x)A-xA^2$ and the S-fraction with numerators $3x,5x,7x,\ldots$. Part~III's \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:HRiccati} is this equation rearranged; Section~\ref{wdn:edge:sec:classical} re-derives it and the leading asymptotic, which are prior art, as the manuscript says.
\end{itemize}
\emph{The inverses.} The threshold theorems of Parts~I and~II solve $\Phi(x)=L$ with $\Phi(x)=x(\log x+\log d-1)+c$. Writing $T=L-c$, this is $x(\kappa\log x+d')=T$ with $\kappa=1$ and $d'=\log d-1$, the factorial core \texttt{p0:prop:factorial-core} of the transseries volume \cite{TSvol}, whose solution $\exp(W_0(Te^{d'})-d')$ is $T/W(dT/e)=x_0$; after taking logarithms it is the Lambert core \texttt{p0:thm:lambert-core} with $a=b=1$, in the variable $\log x+d'$ and with right-hand side $\log T+d'$. \textup{(Wording corrected after the independent check of 5~October 2026, recorded at the end of Section~\ref{wdn:sec:further}; the first wording read ``that proposition is the Lambert core \texttt{p0:thm:lambert-core} in the variable $\log x$''.)} The two-ceiling brackets are the separation pattern of \texttt{p0:thm:staircase}\,(1)--(2), proved here directly at the integers without choosing an interpolation of $a_n$. Part~III's inverse has no Lambert~$W$: after squaring, $\frac34F_q^2=q+g_0+g_1q^{-1}+\cdots$ has a linear core (the case $b=0$ of \texttt{p0:thm:lambert-core}), inverted by formal reversion, with the same ceiling pattern. None of the manuscripts claims novelty for the inversion; Part~I calls it ``a standard consequence''.

Give a peak at coordinates $(a,b)$ in a Dyck path the weight $k+a/b$, and
let $p_N(k)$ be the total weight of paths of semilength $N$. We prove the
Riccati identity and Stieltjes continued fraction conjectured for these
polynomials in OEIS A258219. The proof is an exact identity for all weighted
nonnegative prefixes. We then study the falling-factorial coefficient
$a_n=[\fall{k}{n}]p_{2n}(k)$, the sequence A292692, and prove

\section{Definitions and results}
A Dyck path of semilength $N$ starts at $(0,0)$, ends at $(2N,0)$,
uses $U=(1,1)$ and $D=(1,-1)$, and never has negative height. A peak is the
vertex between consecutive steps $UD$. For an indeterminate $k$, define
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:path}
 p_N(k)=\sum_{P}\ \prod_{(a,b)\text{ a peak of }P}\left(k+\frac ab\right),
 \qquad p_0(k)=1.
\end{equation}
The sum ranges over these Dyck paths; every peak has $b\ge1$. This is the
polynomial model of A258219~\cite{A258219}. Although individual path weights
are rational polynomials, we will prove $p_N\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}[k]$.

For any polynomial $P$, its Newton coefficient is
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:newtondef}
 \LL_mP=[\fall{k}{m}]P(k)=\frac{\Delta^mP(0)}{m!}
 =\frac1{m!}\sum_{i=0}^m(-1)^{m-i}\binom mi P(i),
 \quad \fall{k}{m}=k(k-1)\cdots(k-m+1).
\end{equation}
We set $\fall{k}{0}=1$. This normalization is essential. The triangle
$T(N,m)=\LL_mp_N$ is A258220~\cite{A258220}; its diagonal
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:diagonal}
 a_n=T(2n,n),\qquad a_0=1,
\end{equation}
is A292692~\cite{A292692}. Its initial terms are
\[
 1,\ 6,\ 217,\ 13997,\ 1283817,\ 151778430,\ 21902350480.
\]

\begin{theorem}[Exact weighted path identity]\label{wdn:dia:thm:bridge}
The formal series $F(x,k)=\sum_{N\ge0}p_N(k)x^N$ is the unique element of
$\Q[k][[x]]$ with constant term $1$ satisfying
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:riccati}
 F-1=kxF+xF^2+2x^2F_x.
\end{equation}
Equivalently,
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:prec}
 p_N=(2N-2+k)p_{N-1}+\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}p_jp_{N-1-j}\quad(N\ge1).
\end{equation}
Its formal Stieltjes fraction has successive numerators
$(k+1)x,3x,(k+3)x,5x,(k+5)x,7x,\ldots$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Leading diagonal equivalent]\label{wdn:dia:thm:main}
As $n\to\infty$,
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:main}
 a_n\sim C d^n\frac{n!}{\sqrt n},\qquad
 d=\frac{51\sqrt{17}-107}{4},\qquad
 C=\frac{\sqrt{2+2/\sqrt{17}}}{\pi^{3/2}}.
\end{equation}
In particular, $d=25.8195967266\ldots$ and $C=0.283103092513\ldots$.
\end{theorem}

\section{All weighted prefixes and the Riccati equation}\label{wdn:dia:sec:bridge}
Let $H_\ell(y)$ be the total weight of nonnegative prefixes of length
$\ell$ ending at height $y$. Only completed $UD$ peaks carry their weight;
a final up-step alone has not yet completed a peak. Set $H_\ell(y)=0$ for
$y<0$, $H_{-1}(y)=0$, and
\[
 H_0(y)=\mathbf1_{y=0},\qquad H_1(y)=\mathbf1_{y=1}.
\]
The array vanishes unless $0\le y\le\ell$ and $\ell-y$ is even.

\begin{lemma}[Exact last-step recurrence]\label{wdn:dia:lem:prefixrec}
For $\ell\ge1$ and $y\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:prefixrec}
 H_{\ell+1}(y)=H_\ell(y-1)+H_\ell(y+1)
 +\left(k-1+\frac\ell{y+1}\right)H_{\ell-1}(y).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
A final up-step contributes $H_\ell(y-1)$. Provisionally giving every final
down-step weight $1$ contributes $H_\ell(y+1)$. The prefixes in this second
term whose previous step was up are obtained by appending $U$ to a prefix
counted by $H_{\ell-1}(y)$. The final $D$ completes a peak at $(\ell,y+1)$.
Its provisional factor $1$ must be replaced by $k+\ell/(y+1)$, yielding the
correction in \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:prefixrec}. At $y=0$ the first term is zero, and
$y+1$ is positive, so the same argument applies without a boundary exception.
\end{proof}

Independently of paths, define $R_0(k)=1$ and use the right side of
\eqref{wdn:dia:eq:prec} inductively to define $R_N(k)$. The formal series
$G(x)=\sum_{N\ge0}R_N(k)x^N$ exists uniquely and satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:G}
 G-1=kxG+xG^2+2x^2G'.
\end{equation}
There is no circular use of a path identity in this construction.

\begin{proposition}[Stronger prefix identity]\label{wdn:dia:prop:prefix}
For every $n,y\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:prefix}
 H_{2n+y}(y)=[x^n]G(x)^{y+1}.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Multiply \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:G} by $G^y$ and use the formal product rule to obtain
\[
 G^{y+1}-G^y=kxG^{y+1}+xG^{y+2}
 +\frac{2x^2}{y+1}(G^{y+1})'.
\]
Taking the coefficient of $x^n$, with negative indices interpreted as zero,
gives
\begin{align}\label{wdn:dia:eq:powerrec}
 [x^n]G^{y+1}={}&[x^n]G^y+[x^{n-1}]G^{y+2}\notag\\
 &+\left(k+\frac{2n-2}{y+1}\right)[x^{n-1}]G^{y+1}.
\end{align}
Define $C_\ell(y)=0$ for unreachable $(\ell,y)$ and
$C_\ell(y)=[x^{(\ell-y)/2}]G^{y+1}$ otherwise. The arrays $C_0,C_1$ equal
$H_0,H_1$. For a reachable target of length $\ell+1$, set
$n=(\ell+1-y)/2$, so $\ell=2n+y-1$. When $y\ge1$, the first term of
\eqref{wdn:dia:eq:powerrec} is $C_\ell(y-1)$. When $y=0$, reachability at positive
length gives $n\ge1$, so that term is $[x^n]1=0=C_\ell(-1)$. The second term
is $C_\ell(y+1)$, and the last coefficient equals
$k-1+\ell/(y+1)$, as required in \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:prefixrec}. This includes $n=0$
since all coefficients of $x^{-1}$ vanish. At unreachable targets the
recurrence is zero by range or parity. Thus $C$ obeys the same initial
conditions and recurrence as $H$. Induction on $\ell$ proves $C=H$.
\end{proof}

At $y=0$, complete prefixes are exactly Dyck paths. Consequently
$p_N=R_N$, proving the Riccati assertion of Theorem~\ref{wdn:dia:thm:bridge}.
Recurrence \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:prec} also proves the promised nonnegative integral
ordinary coefficients. For example,
\begin{align*}
 p_1(k)&=k+1,\\
 p_2(k)&=k^2+5k+4,\\
 p_3(k)&=k^3+12k^2+36k+25.
\end{align*}
Individual rational peak factors have disappeared from the total polynomial.

\subsection{A bivariate generating function and a boundary check}
The prefix theorem can also be encoded as
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:H}
 H(v,u):=\sum_{\ell,y\ge0}H_\ell(y)v^\ell u^y
 =\frac{F(v^2,k)}{1-vuF(v^2,k)}.
\end{equation}
This is a formal identity; each coefficient of $v^\ell$ is a polynomial in
$u$. For completeness, summing \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:prefixrec}, including its valid
$\ell=0$ version, gives
\begin{multline*}
 [u-v(u^2+1)-v^2(k-1)u]H\
 =u-vH(v,0)+v^2\int_0^u\bigl(vH_v(v,w)+H(v,w)\bigr)\,dw.
\end{multline*}
Formal differentiation yields
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:PDE}
 [u-v(u^2+1)-v^2(k-1)u]H_u-v^3H_v
 +(1-2vu-kv^2)H=1.
\end{equation}
This differentiated equation still determines $H$ uniquely in
$\Q[k,u][[v]]$: its coefficient at $v^\ell$ has leading operator
$u\,d/du+1$ on $[v^\ell]H$, and the remaining terms involve only smaller
indices. The operator multiplies $u^y$ by $y+1$ and is invertible.
If $x=v^2$ and $D=1-vuF(x)$, the right side of \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:H} has derivatives
\[
 H_u=\frac{vF^2}{D^2},\qquad
 H_v=\frac{2vF'+uF^2}{D^2}.
\]
Substitution into \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:PDE} gives numerator
\[
 F-kxF-xF^2-2x^2F'-2vuF+v^2u^2F^2
 =1-2vuF+v^2u^2F^2=D^2.
\]
This independently verifies the zero-height closure in the generating
function presentation. The direct recurrence proof above already establishes
all prefix identities without a PDE uniqueness assumption.

\section{The formal hypergeometric identity and continued fraction}
Put
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:zdef}
 \gamma_N=\frac1{4^N}\binom{2N}{N},\qquad
 z_N(k)=\gamma_N\prod_{i=0}^{N-1}(k+2i+1),\qquad Z(x,k)=\sum_{N\ge0}z_N(k)x^N.
\end{equation}
Empty products and $z_0$ equal $1$. In rising-factorial notation,
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:2F0}
 Z(x,k)={}_2F_0\left(\frac{k+1}{2},\frac12;\,;2x\right)
 =\sum_{N\ge0}\frac{((k+1)/2)_N(1/2)_N}{N!}(2x)^N.
\end{equation}
The symbol ${}_2F_0$ in this report denotes precisely this formal series.

\begin{lemma}[Logarithmic derivative]\label{wdn:dia:lem:logder}
In $\Q[k][[x]]$,
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:logder}
 F=1+2x\frac{Z_x}{Z},\qquad
 2Nz_N=\sum_{j=1}^Np_jz_{N-j}\quad(N\ge1).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The product formula gives
$2Nz_N=(2N-1)(k+2N-1)z_{N-1}$. Coefficient extraction then proves
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:Zode}
 2x^2Z''+((k+4)x-1)Z'+\frac{k+1}{2}Z=0.
\end{equation}
Formal division by $Z$ is legitimate since $Z(0)=1$. Let
$\widetilde F=1+2xZ'/Z$. Writing $q=Z'/Z$ and using \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:Zode},
\[
 2x^2(q'+q^2)+((k+4)x-1)q+(k+1)/2=0
\]
is exactly the Riccati identity for $\widetilde F$ after substitution and
cancellation. Its constant term is $1$, so uniqueness gives
$\widetilde F=F$. Multiplying by $Z$ and extracting $x^N$ proves the second
identity. The $j=0$ term has already canceled, explaining the lower limit $1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Formal Stieltjes fraction]\label{wdn:dia:prop:CF}
One has
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:cf}
 F(x,k)=\cfrac1{1-\cfrac{(k+1)x}{1-\cfrac{3x}{1-\cfrac{(k+3)x}{1-\cfrac{5x}{1-\cfrac{(k+5)x}{1-\ddots}}}}}}.
\end{equation}
The infinite fraction is interpreted coefficientwise: any coefficient is
unchanged after sufficiently many levels.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For formal parameters $a,b,z$, write
$U(a,b;z)=\sum_{N\ge0}(a)_N(b)_Nz^N/N!$. Direct coefficient comparison gives
\[
 U(a,b+1;z)-U(a,b;z)=az\,U(a+1,b+1;z).
\]
If $R(a,b;z)=U(a,b+1;z)/U(a,b;z)$, symmetry in $a,b$ yields
\[
 R(a,b;z)=\frac1{1-azR(b+1,a;z)}.
\]
Iterating this identity gives a fraction with numerators
$az,(b+1)z,(a+1)z,(b+2)z,\ldots$. Every $U$ and every ratio has constant
term $1$, and each level introduces a factor of $z$, so the iteration is
valid in the formal topology. Finally, for $b=1/2$,
\[
 U(a,3/2;z)=U(a,1/2;z)+2z\frac{\partial}{\partial z}U(a,1/2;z).
\]
Taking $a=(k+1)/2$ and $z=2x$, Lemma~\ref{wdn:dia:lem:logder} identifies the ratio
with $F$, and the stated numerators follow.
\end{proof}
This supplies a direct formal proof of the continued fraction, in addition
to the general Riccati continued-fraction theory cited by the OEIS
entry~\cite{Stokes}.

\section{Positive Newton transforms}
For ordinary coefficientwise order, write $P\preceq Q$ when every
coefficient of $Q-P$ is nonnegative. Both $p_N$ and $z_N$ have nonnegative
coefficients, so isolating the $j=N$ term of \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:logder} gives
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:order}
 0\preceq p_N\preceq2Nz_N\quad(N\ge1).
\end{equation}
Newton extraction preserves this order. Indeed,
\[
 k^j=\sum_{m=0}^j\left\{\begin{matrix}j\\m\end{matrix}\right\}\fall{k}{m},
 \qquad \LL_mk^j=\left\{\begin{matrix}j\\m\end{matrix}\right\}\ge0,
\]
where the braces denote Stirling numbers of the second kind. Set
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:PQR}
 P_{N,m}=\LL_mp_N,\quad Q_{N,m}=\LL_mz_N,\quad
 R_{N,m}=\frac{P_{N,m}}{2NQ_{N,m}}\quad(1\le m\le N).
\end{equation}
We will see immediately that $Q_{N,m}>0$ in this range. Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:Rbounds}
 0\le R_{N,m}\le1.
\end{equation}

Define the analytic functions
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:fh}
 f(t)=(1-t)^{-1/2},\qquad h(t)=f(t)-1,\qquad
 C_{N,m}=[t^N]f(t)h(t)^m.
\end{equation}
They are analytic for $|t|<1$, using the branch with $f(0)=1$. All coefficients
of $f$, and all positive-degree coefficients of $h$, are strictly positive.

\begin{lemma}[Exact auxiliary transform]\label{wdn:dia:lem:Q}
With the convention $(-1)!!=1$,
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:Q}
 Q_{N,m}=\frac{(2N-1)!!}{m!}\,C_{N,m}\quad(0\le m\le N).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The product formula gives the coefficientwise identity
\[
 \sum_{N\ge0}\frac{z_N(k)}{(2N-1)!!}t^N
 =(1-t)^{-(k+1)/2}=f(t)(1+h(t))^k.
\]
Expand $(1+h)^k=\sum_{m\ge0}\fall{k}{m}h^m/m!$ and extract $t^N$ and
the Newton coefficient. Only $m\le N$ contributes, so these operations
are finite at each degree. Positivity of $C_{N,m}$ proves the assertion
following \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:PQR}.
\end{proof}
The factor $1/m!$ distinguishes a falling-factorial coefficient from a plain
finite difference. Omitting it would change both the growth scale and the
answer.

\section{A uniform large power local limit estimate}\label{wdn:dia:sec:LLT}
We work on the concrete compact parameter interval
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:interval}
 \frac35\le s\le\frac7{10},\qquad
 \alpha:=\frac mN=\frac{2s^2}{1+s}
 \in I:=\left[\frac9{20},\frac{49}{85}\right],\qquad t=1-s^2.
\end{equation}
The diagonal $m/N=1/2$ lies in the interior. Constants in uniform estimates
may depend on this interval and, when used, a fixed cutoff constant $A$.
The positive saddle satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:muB}
 \mu(t):=\frac{th'(t)}{h(t)}=\frac{1+s}{2s^2}=\frac Nm,
 \qquad B(t):=t\mu'(t)=\frac{t(s+2)}{4s^4}>0.
\end{equation}
Here $t\in[51/100,16/25]$, and $h(t)$ and $B(t)$ are uniformly bounded
above and away from zero.

\begin{lemma}[Uniform local limit with logarithmic shifts]\label{wdn:dia:lem:LLT}
Uniformly for $m/N\in I$,
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:saddle}
 C_{N,m}=\frac{f(t)h(t)^mt^{-N}}{\sqrt{2\pi mB(t)}}(1+o(1)).
\end{equation}
For every fixed $A>0$, uniformly for integers $0\le r\le\ell\le A\log N$,
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:shift}
 \frac{C_{N-\ell,m-r}}{C_{N,m}}
 =t^\ell h(t)^{-r}\left(1+O_A\left(\frac{\log N}{\sqrt N}\right)\right).
\end{equation}
The saddle on the right is the original one from $(N,m)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
At a fixed saddle $t$, define independent integer random variables by
\[
 \Prob(X=j)=\frac{[u^j]h(u)t^j}{h(t)}\quad(j\ge1),\qquad
 \Prob(V=j)=\frac{[u^j]f(u)t^j}{f(t)}\quad(j\ge0).
\]
The variable $X$ has span one since all its positive integer probabilities
are nonzero. Its mean is $\mu=N/m$ and variance is $B$. Both distributions
have exponential moments uniformly on our interval: choose $\eta>0$ so that
$t e^\eta<1$ for every permitted $t$. For a sum $S_M$ of $M$ independent
copies of $X$, the exact coefficient identity is
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:probcoef}
 [u^q]f(u)h(u)^M=f(t)h(t)^Mt^{-q}\Prob(S_M+V=q).
\end{equation}
We claim that, for $\delta=q-M\mu=O(\log M)$,
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:localprob}
 \Prob(S_M+V=q)=\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi MB}}
 \left(1+O\left(\frac{1+|\delta|}{\sqrt M}\right)\right)
\end{equation}
uniformly on the compact saddle interval.

Here are the Fourier details. Let $\phi_t(\theta)=\E e^{i\theta X}$ and
$\psi_t(\theta)=\E e^{i\theta V}$. Uniform exponential moments give, on a
fixed small arc,
\[
 \log\phi_t(\theta)=i\mu\theta-\tfrac12B\theta^2+O(|\theta|^3),
 \qquad \psi_t(\theta)=1+O(|\theta|),
\]
with uniform Taylor constants. After decreasing that arc if necessary,
$|\phi_t(\theta)|\le e^{-c\theta^2}$ there, with $c>0$ independent of $t$.
On a fixed outer arc $\varepsilon\le|\theta|\le\pi$, span one and compactness
give $|\phi_t(\theta)|\le\rho<1$. Fourier inversion splits into
$|\theta|\le M^{-2/5}$, the intermediate arc
$M^{-2/5}<|\theta|<\varepsilon$, and the outer arc. Their latter two
contributions are $O(e^{-cM^{1/5}})$ and $O(\rho^M)$, respectively.

On the central arc put $u=\sqrt M\theta$. The centered characteristic
function becomes $e^{-Bu^2/2}$ with logarithmic remainder
$O(|u|^3/\sqrt M)$; the target phase differs from $1$ by at most
$|\delta u|/\sqrt M$, and the $V$ factor differs from $1$ by
$O(|u|/\sqrt M)$. Since $|u|\le M^{1/10}$, the cubic remainder is small and
can be absorbed into a uniform Gaussian majorant. Integrating the differences
against this majorant bounds the relative error by
$O((1+|\delta|)/\sqrt M)$. The Gaussian integral is $\sqrt{2\pi/B}$;
including the Fourier factor $1/(2\pi\sqrt M)$ proves \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:localprob}.
In particular, the mean of $V$ affects the error, not the leading constant.

Take $(M,q)=(m,N)$ in \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:probcoef} to obtain \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:saddle}.
For $(M,q)=(m-r,N-\ell)$ the mean displacement at the same original saddle is
$\delta=-\ell+r\mu=O_A(\log N)$. Also $M\asymp N$ and
$\sqrt{m/(m-r)}=1+O_A(\log N/N)$. Dividing the two probabilities proves
\eqref{wdn:dia:eq:shift}. This treats both moving indices; no unproved saddle
interchange is needed.
\end{proof}

\section{Endpoint and middle estimates for products}\label{wdn:dia:sec:products}
Define, for $1\le\ell<N$,
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:U}
 U_{N,m,\ell}=\frac{\LL_m(z_\ell z_{N-\ell})}{Q_{N,m}},\qquad
 w_\ell(s)=\gamma_\ell s^{2\ell}.
\end{equation}
Two different estimates are needed: a sharp endpoint estimate with no
polynomial loss, and a rough exponentially decreasing middle bound.

\subsection{Newton multiplication near an endpoint}
The identity $k\fall{k}{m}=\fall{k}{m+1}+m\fall{k}{m}$ implies
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:mult}
 \LL_m((k+c)P)=(m+c)\LL_mP+\LL_{m-1}P.
\end{equation}
Applying it to the $\ell$ factors in $z_\ell$ gives exact coefficients
$c_{\ell,r}(m)$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:cmult}
 \LL_m(z_\ell P)=\sum_{r=0}^\ell c_{\ell,r}(m)\LL_{m-r}P.
\end{equation}
In the endpoint range below $m>\ell$, so no negative Newton indices arise.
All these coefficients are nonnegative.

\begin{lemma}[Uniform endpoint kernel]\label{wdn:dia:lem:endpoint}
For every fixed $A>0$, uniformly for $0\le r\le\ell\le A\log N$,
\begin{align}
 c_{\ell,r}(m)&=\gamma_\ell\binom\ell r m^{\ell-r}
                   (1+O(\ell^2/m)),\label{wdn:dia:eq:capprox}\\
 \frac{Q_{N-\ell,m-r}}{Q_{N,m}}
   &=(2N)^{-\ell}m^r t^\ell h^{-r}(1+O_A(\varepsilon_N)),\label{wdn:dia:eq:Qratio}
\end{align}
where $\varepsilon_N=\log N/\sqrt N+(\log N)^2/N$. Consequently,
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:Uendpoint}
 U_{N,m,\ell}=w_\ell(s)(1+O_A(\varepsilon_N)),
\end{equation}
and the exact nonnegative coefficients
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:kernel}
 b_{N,m,\ell,r}=\frac{N-\ell}{N}\,
 c_{\ell,r}(m)\frac{Q_{N-\ell,m-r}}{Q_{N,m}}
\end{equation}
satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:bapprox}
 b_{N,m,\ell,r}=w_\ell(s)\binom\ell r s^r(1-s)^{\ell-r}
                     (1+O_A(\varepsilon_N)).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
In the iterated multiplication rule, choose which $r$ of the $\ell$ factors
shift the Newton index. There are $\binom\ell r$ paths. Every stay factor
has form $m-q+2i+1$ with $0\le q\le\ell$ and $0\le i<\ell$, hence is
$m+O(\ell)$. If $m\ge4\ell$, the product of the $\ell-r$ stay factors is
$m^{\ell-r}\exp(O(\ell^2/m))$. The estimate is uniform over all paths;
positivity permits summing their relative bounds without a combinatorial
loss. At $r=\ell$ the product is empty and the formula is exact.
This proves \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:capprox}.

By \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:Q}, the required ratio is a product of the coefficient ratio
in \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:shift}, a double-factorial ratio, and $m!/(m-r)!$. Direct finite
products give
\[
 \frac{(2N-2\ell-1)!!}{(2N-1)!!}
 =(2N)^{-\ell}(1+O(\ell^2/N)),\quad
 \frac{m!}{(m-r)!}=m^r(1+O(r^2/m)),
\]
proving \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:Qratio}. Notice the positive power $m^r$.

Multiply these estimates, sum in $r$, and use
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:saddleidentities}
 \frac{\alpha t}{2}=s^2(1-s),\qquad h^{-1}=\frac{s}{1-s}.
\end{equation}
The individual leading summand is
$\gamma_\ell(\alpha t/2)^\ell\binom\ell r h^{-r}$, which equals
$w_\ell(s)\binom\ell r s^r(1-s)^{\ell-r}$. Its sum is $w_\ell(s)$.
All terms are positive, so the uniform relative errors remain uniform on
summation. The factor $(N-\ell)/N=1+O_A(\log N/N)$ gives \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:bapprox}.
\end{proof}
In particular, uniformly throughout this logarithmic endpoint range,
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:Udom}
 U_{N,m,\ell}\le2w_\ell(s)
\end{equation}
for sufficiently large $N$.

\subsection{An integer evaluation controls the middle}
\begin{lemma}[Uniform middle bound]\label{wdn:dia:lem:middle}
There exists a constant $K_0<\infty$ such that, for $m/N\in I$ and
$1\le\ell<N$,
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:middle}
 U_{N,m,\ell}\le K_0N(4/5)^{\min(\ell,N-\ell)}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Choose an integer $K$ nearest to $m/(1-s)$. Uniformly,
\[
 K>m\quad\hbox{eventually},\qquad
 \frac KN=\frac{2s^2}{1-s^2}+O(N^{-1})\le\frac{98}{51}+O(N^{-1})<2.
\]
The polynomial $z_\ell z_{N-\ell}$ has nonnegative ordinary, and therefore
nonnegative Newton, coefficients. At an \emph{integer} $K$, every falling
factorial is nonnegative or zero; degrees larger than $K$ vanish. Thus
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:inteval}
 \LL_m(z_\ell z_{N-\ell})
 \le\frac{z_\ell(K)z_{N-\ell}(K)}{\fall K m}.
\end{equation}
A nonintegral evaluation would not provide this argument.

The normalization from \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:Q} is exactly
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:normalK}
 \frac{z_N(K)}{\fall K m\,Q_{N,m}}
 =\frac{[u^N]f(u)(1+h(u))^K}{\binom Km C_{N,m}}.
\end{equation}
Positive-coefficient evaluation bounds its numerator by
$f(t)(1+h(t))^Kt^{-N}$. Lemma~\ref{wdn:dia:lem:LLT} bounds the denominator from
below by a fixed multiple of
$\binom Km f(t)h(t)^mt^{-N}/\sqrt m$. Set
$p=h/(1+h)=1-s\in[3/10,2/5]$. The resulting ratio is at most
\[
 \frac{C_1\sqrt m}{\binom Km p^m(1-p)^{K-m}}.
\]
Here $m-Kp=O(1)$ and $K\asymp N$. Uniform elementary Stirling estimates for
the central binomial probability give a lower bound $c_1/\sqrt K$ for its
denominator. Hence \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:normalK} is at most $C_2N$. No asymptotic
formula for the numerator is required.

For $\ell\le N/2$, the product definition gives
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:productratio}
 \frac{z_\ell(K)z_{N-\ell}(K)}{z_N(K)}
 =\frac{\gamma_\ell\gamma_{N-\ell}}{\gamma_N}
 \prod_{i=0}^{\ell-1}
 \frac{K+2i+1}{K+2(N-\ell+i)+1}.
\end{equation}
The first factor is bounded by $e^{1/2}$: use $\gamma_\ell\le1$ and
\[
 \log\frac{\gamma_{N-\ell}}{\gamma_N}
 =\sum_{j=N-\ell+1}^N\log\left(1+\frac1{2j-1}\right)
 \le\frac\ell{2(N-\ell)+1}<\frac12.
\]
Each linear ratio increases with $i$ and is at most
\[
 \frac{K+2\ell-1}{K+2N-1}\le\frac{K+N}{K+2N-1}
 \le\frac{3N}{4N-1}\le\frac45
\]
for all sufficiently large $N$. Combine this with \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:inteval} and
\eqref{wdn:dia:eq:normalK}. Symmetry treats $\ell>N/2$, and a larger constant absorbs
the finitely many smaller $N$.
\end{proof}
The factor $N$ in \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:middle} is harmless only away from the endpoints.
It will never replace the sharper bound \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:Udom} near them.

\section{A positive contraction proves the moving ratio limit}\label{wdn:dia:sec:contract}
\begin{theorem}[Local uniform Newton ratio]\label{wdn:dia:thm:uniform}
Uniformly on every compact subinterval $J$ of the interior of $I$,
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:Rlimit}
 R_{N,m}-\sqrt{1-s(m/N)^2}\longrightarrow0
 \quad(N\to\infty,\ m/N\in J),
\end{equation}
where $s(\alpha)$ is the positive solution of
$\alpha=2s^2/(1+s)$. Thus, with $t=1-s^2$,
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:uniformP}
 P_{N,m}\sim
 \frac{2N(2N-1)!!}{m!}
 \frac{\sqrt t\,f(t)h(t)^mt^{-N}}{\sqrt{2\pi mB(t)}}
\end{equation}
uniformly on such $J$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Apply $\LL_m$ to the convolution in \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:logder}, isolate $j=N$, and
write $\ell=N-j$. The exact equation is
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:Rexact}
 R_{N,m}=1-\sum_{\ell=1}^{N-1}
       \frac{\LL_m(p_{N-\ell}z_\ell)}{2NQ_{N,m}}.
\end{equation}
All subtracted terms are nonnegative. Fix $A=20$ and
$L_N=\lfloor A\log N\rfloor$. For large $N$, split the sum into the disjoint
ranges $1\le\ell\le L_N$, $L_N<\ell<N-L_N$, and
$N-L_N\le\ell\le N-1$.

In the middle, \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:order} and Lemma~\ref{wdn:dia:lem:middle} give a total
$O(N^2(4/5)^{L_N})=o(1)$; the chosen $A$ exceeds
$3/(-\log(4/5))$. At the opposite endpoint put $j=N-\ell\le L_N$.
Equations \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:order} and \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:Udom} give a total at most
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:opposite}
 \frac1N\sum_{j=1}^{L_N}jU_{N,m,j}
 \le\frac2N\sum_{j\ge1}j\gamma_js^{2j}=O(N^{-1})
\end{equation}
uniformly. At the principal endpoint, \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:cmult} and
\eqref{wdn:dia:eq:kernel} give exactly
\[
 \frac{\LL_m(p_{N-\ell}z_\ell)}{2NQ_{N,m}}
 =\sum_{r=0}^\ell b_{N,m,\ell,r}R_{N-\ell,m-r}.
\]
Consequently,
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:kernelrec}
 R_{N,m}=1-\sum_{\ell=1}^{L_N}\sum_{r=0}^\ell
 b_{N,m,\ell,r}R_{N-\ell,m-r}-E_{N,m},
 \qquad 0\le E_{N,m}=o(1),
\end{equation}
uniformly on $I$.

The total limiting kernel mass is
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:q0}
 q_0(s)=\sum_{\ell\ge1}w_\ell(s)
 =\frac1{\sqrt{1-s^2}}-1
 \le\frac{10}{\sqrt{51}}-1<1.
\end{equation}
The scalar solution of $r=1-q_0(s)r$ is
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:r0}
 r(\alpha)=\sqrt{1-s(\alpha)^2}.
\end{equation}
Finding this scalar solution does not alone prove convergence. We provide
the necessary moving-index argument.

Positivity, Lemma~\ref{wdn:dia:lem:endpoint}, and the uniformly geometric tail of
$w_\ell$ imply
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:mass}
 \sum_{\ell\le L_N}\sum_{r\le\ell}b_{N,m,\ell,r}
 =q_0(s(m/N))+o(1).
\end{equation}
Every predecessor has ratio displacement
\[
 \left|\frac{m-r}{N-\ell}-\frac mN\right|
 =\frac{|m\ell-rN|}{N(N-\ell)}=O(\log N/N),
\]
so its reference value $r((m-r)/(N-\ell))$ differs from $r(m/N)$ by $o(1)$.
This holds uniformly on a neighborhood of any interior ratio, because $r$
is continuously differentiable there. Subtract \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:r0} from
\eqref{wdn:dia:eq:kernelrec}, add and subtract predecessor reference values, and
use \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:mass}. For $e_{N,m}=|R_{N,m}-r(m/N)|$, this gives
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:errorrec}
 e_{N,m}\le\sum_{\ell\le L_N}\sum_{r\le\ell}
 b_{N,m,\ell,r}e_{N-\ell,m-r}+o(1).
\end{equation}
The error here includes the discarded mass, the kernel-mass error, and the
reference-value shifts; it does not assume convergence of $R$.

Fix an interior ratio $\alpha_0$ and define the precise local envelope
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:envelope}
 M(\alpha_0)=\lim_{\delta\downarrow0}\lim_{N_0\to\infty}
 \sup_{\substack{N\ge N_0\\|m/N-\alpha_0|<\delta}}
 e_{N,m}.
\end{equation}
These monotone limits exist and are finite by \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:Rbounds}. Choose a
sequence tending to $\alpha_0$, with $N\to\infty$, whose errors tend to
$M(\alpha_0)$. Along it, every predecessor in \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:errorrec} has size
$N-O(\log N)\to\infty$ and ratio tending to $\alpha_0$, uniformly. Hence
\[
 \limsup\max_{\ell\le L_N,\ r\le\ell}e_{N-\ell,m-r}\le M(\alpha_0).
\]
If this failed, choose an offending predecessor on each member of a
subsequence; those predecessors themselves contradict the envelope's
definition. Now \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:mass}--\eqref{wdn:dia:eq:errorrec} imply
\[
 M(\alpha_0)\le q_0(s(\alpha_0))M(\alpha_0).
\]
The strict bound in \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:q0} forces $M(\alpha_0)=0$. This proves
convergence along every sequence tending to this ratio. If uniformity
failed on a compact $J$, an offending sequence of ratios would have a
convergent subsequence in $J$, contradicting this conclusion. Equation
\eqref{wdn:dia:eq:uniformP} now follows from Lemmas~\ref{wdn:dia:lem:Q} and \ref{wdn:dia:lem:LLT}.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Why the positivity matters.}
The formal expression $F=1+2x(\log Z)'$ tempts one to expand a logarithm
and discard most of its alternating terms. Such a step needs a separate
uniform remainder argument in the moving Newton regime. The proof above
avoids it. The transformed convolution is positive, its middle is directly
bounded at an integer $K$, and its endpoint mass is genuinely contractive.
The distinction is what permits a rigorous moving-index equivalent.

\section{The diagonal constants}\label{wdn:dia:sec:constants}
At $N=2n$, $m=n$, equation \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:interval} becomes
$4s^2=1+s$. Its positive root and associated parameters are
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:s0}
 s_0=\frac{1+\sqrt{17}}8,\qquad t_0=1-s_0^2,\qquad
 h(t_0)=\frac{1-s_0}{s_0},\qquad B_0=\frac{t_0(s_0+2)}{4s_0^4}.
\end{equation}
For this section write $s,t,B$ for these fixed values. Theorem~\ref{wdn:dia:thm:uniform}
gives, with every normalization retained,
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:diagonalsaddle}
 a_n\sim\frac{4n(4n-1)!!}{n!}
 \frac{h(t)^nt^{-2n}}{s\sqrt{2\pi nB}}\sqrt t.
\end{equation}
In particular, the local-limit variance prefactor contains $m=n$, not $N=2n$.
Using $(4n-1)!!=(4n)!/(2^{2n}(2n)!)$ and Stirling's formula gives
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:stirling}
 \frac{(4n-1)!!}{(n!)^2}\sim\frac{16^n}{\pi\sqrt2\,n}.
\end{equation}
For clarity, the factorial square roots contribute
$\sqrt{8\pi n}/(\sqrt{4\pi n}\,2\pi n)=1/(\pi\sqrt2\,n)$;
the exponential and power terms contribute $16^n$.
Combining \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:diagonalsaddle}--\eqref{wdn:dia:eq:stirling} yields
\begin{align}
 d&=\frac{16h(t)}{t^2}=\frac{16}{s(1-s)(1+s)^2}
   =\frac{51\sqrt{17}-107}{4},\label{wdn:dia:eq:dconstant}\\
 C&=\frac{2\sqrt t}{s\pi\sqrt{\pi B}}
   =\frac{4s}{\pi^{3/2}\sqrt{s+2}}
   =\frac{\sqrt{2+2/\sqrt{17}}}{\pi^{3/2}}.\label{wdn:dia:eq:Cconstant}
\end{align}
All roots are positive. The final simplifications use $4s^2=1+s$.
For example, squaring the last amplitude identity reduces it to
$16s^2/(s+2)=2+2/\sqrt{17}$, an identity in $\Q(\sqrt{17})$.
This completes the proof of Theorem~\ref{wdn:dia:thm:main}. The posted numerical
amplitude is consistent with it, but the proof of the constant is the
exact normalization calculation, not agreement of decimal digits.

\section{The first correction with a uniform remainder}\label{wdn:dia:sec:correction}
We now strengthen the leading result by one relative order. This requires
more than formally expanding the fixed-point equation: both endpoint errors
must be summable, and a stopped contraction must create an $N^{-2}$ bound
from a bounded initial error.

\begin{theorem}[First correction]\label{wdn:dia:thm:correction}
Put $\alpha_*=6-3\sqrt3$. For every compact interval
$J\subset(0,\alpha_*)$, uniformly for integer pairs with $m/N\in J$,
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:Rcorrection}
 R_{N,m}=\sqrt t\left(1+\frac{r_1(s)}N+O_J(N^{-2})\right),\qquad
 r_1(s)=\frac{s^4+6s^3+7s^2+3s+4}
 {4(s-1)(s+1)(s+2)^2},
\end{equation}
where $\alpha=2s^2/(1+s)$ and $t=1-s^2$. In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:acorrection}
 a_n=C d^n\frac{n!}{\sqrt n}
 \left(1+\frac{b_1}{n}+O(n^{-2})\right),\qquad
 b_1=-\frac{989}{2176}-\frac{907\sqrt{17}}{36992}.
\end{equation}
Thus $b_1=-0.555597340031\ldots$. No coefficients beyond this first relative
correction are asserted here.
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Parameter domain and exact split}
Choose nested compact intervals
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:nested}
 J\subset\operatorname{int}J_1\subset\operatorname{int}J_2
 \subset(0,\alpha_*).
\end{equation}
On $J_2$, $s$ is bounded away from $0$ and bounded strictly below
$\sqrt3/2$. This is exactly the range in which $q_0(s)<1$, since
$2(\sqrt3/2)^2/(1+\sqrt3/2)=6-3\sqrt3$.
The local-limit and endpoint arguments above apply without change on
$J_2$: $t,h,B$ and all required derivatives remain uniformly controlled.
For the middle bound the integer $K$ has $K/N$ in a fixed positive bounded
interval, so some $q<1$ bounds $(K+N)/(K+2N-1)$ uniformly for large $N$.
The proof of Lemma~\ref{wdn:dia:lem:middle} therefore gives
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:generalmiddle}
 U_{N,m,\ell}\le C_2Nq^{\min(\ell,N-\ell)}
\end{equation}
on $J_2$. The special value $q=4/5$ is no longer needed.

For valid zero Newton index use $R_{n,0}=p_n(0)/(2nz_n(0))\in[0,1]$.
When an index is negative or exceeds the polynomial degree, its transformed
summand is zero; define the corresponding kernel to be zero and assign the
unused $R$ any bounded value. This avoids undefined $0/0$ expressions.
Such indices never occur in the logarithmic endpoint range for large $N$.

Set $L=\lfloor N/2\rfloor$ and split \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:Rexact} exactly as
\begin{align}
 R_{N,m}&=1-\sum_{\ell=1}^L\sum_{r=0}^{\ell}
 b_{N,m,\ell,r}R_{N-\ell,m-r}-\mathcal B_{N,m},\label{wdn:dia:eq:causal}\\
 \mathcal B_{N,m}&=\sum_{j=1}^{N-L-1}
 \frac{\LL_m(p_jz_{N-j})}{2NQ_{N,m}}.\label{wdn:dia:eq:Bopp}
\end{align}
There is no overlap at the midpoint. Every principal predecessor has size
at least $N/2$, all valid kernels are nonnegative, and
$\sum_r b_{N,m,\ell,r}=(1-\ell/N)U_{N,m,\ell}$.

\subsection{A second order remainder for shifted saddle coefficients}
Write $D=t\,d/dt=-(t/(2s))d/ds$ and put
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:cumulants}
 g_1=D\log f,\quad g_2=D^2f/f,\quad k_3=DB,\quad k_4=D^2B,
 \quad E=g_1-\frac{k_3}{2B}=-\frac{s^3+s+4}{4s^2(s+2)}.
\end{equation}
For $\beta=\ell-\mu r$, define
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:D1}
 D_1(\ell,r)=
 \frac{-g_2/2-g_1\beta+(rB-\beta^2)/2}{B}
 +\frac{(g_1+\beta)k_3}{2B^2}
 +\frac{k_4}{8B^2}-\frac{5k_3^2}{24B^3}.
\end{equation}
For each fixed $A$, there is a finite exponent $D_0$ such that, uniformly
for $0\le r\le\ell\le A\log N$ on $J_2$,
\begin{multline}\label{wdn:dia:eq:shiftsecond}
 C_{N-\ell,m-r}=
 \frac{f(t)h(t)^mt^{-N}t^\ell h(t)^{-r}}{\sqrt{2\pi mB}}
 \\\times\left(1+\frac{D_1(\ell,r)}m
       +O\bigl(m^{-2}(1+\ell+r)^{D_0}\bigr)\right).
\end{multline}
We give the remainder details because fixed-shift expansions do not
by themselves imply this logarithmic-shift assertion.

Set
\[
 \psi(\theta)=\frac{h(te^{i\theta})}{h(t)},\qquad
 g(\theta)=\frac{f(te^{i\theta})}{f(t)}.
\]
After removing the prefactor $f(t)h(t)^mt^{-N}t^\ell h(t)^{-r}$, the exact
Fourier integral is
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:shiftintegral}
 \frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}
 g(\theta)\psi(\theta)^{-r}e^{i\ell\theta}
 \exp\{m(\log\psi(\theta)-i\mu\theta)\}\,d\theta.
\end{equation}
The logarithm is used only near zero. Away from zero use the original
integrand $g(\theta)\psi(\theta)^{m-r}e^{-i(N-\ell)\theta}$.
The uniform span-one and exponential-moment bounds from
Section~\ref{wdn:dia:sec:LLT} apply. Choose $0<\varepsilon<1/6$.
The fixed outer arc is exponentially small. On the intermediate arc
$m^{-1/2+\varepsilon}\le|\theta|\le\theta_0$, the exact power $m-r\ge m/2$
gives $O(e^{-cm^{2\varepsilon}})$, smaller than any fixed inverse power.

On the central arc put $\theta=zu$, $z=m^{-1/2}$, so $|u|\le m^\varepsilon$.
After removing $e^{-Bu^2/2}$, the integrand is
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:centralE}
 g(zu)e^{i\ell zu-r\log\psi(zu)}
 \exp\left\{\sum_{j\ge3}\kappa_j(iu)^jz^{j-2}/j!\right\},
 \qquad \kappa_j=D^j\log h.
\end{equation}
Expand this expression through degree $3$ in $z$. Its fourth derivative,
for $0\le z'\le m^{-1/2}$, is bounded by
\[
 C(1+\ell+r)^{D_0}(1+|u|)^{E_0}e^{\delta u^2},\qquad
 0<\delta<B_{\min}/4,
\]
with fixed finite $D_0,E_0$. To justify this bound, the saddle remainder
exponent is $O(z'|u|^3)$, whose coefficient relative to $u^2$ tends uniformly
to zero. Its fixed derivatives have only polynomial growth in $u$. The
shifted amplitude exponential is bounded by
$\exp\{C(\ell+r)z'|u|+Cr(z'u)^2\}$. Young's inequality absorbs the linear
term into a small multiple of $u^2$ plus $C'(\ell+r)^2/m=o(1)$, and the
quadratic term is likewise absorbed. Fixed derivatives add only polynomial
factors. The analytic functions and their needed derivatives are uniformly
bounded on a common complex neighborhood. Taylor's remainder is therefore
$O(m^{-2}(1+\ell+r)^{D_0})$ after Gaussian integration. Odd powers of $z$
are odd polynomials in $u$ and integrate to zero. Extending the even
polynomial integrals to the real line costs a superpolynomially small
Gaussian tail.

The degree-two polynomial, integrated with Gaussian moments
$\E u^{2j}=(2j-1)!!/B^j$, is exactly \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:D1}. This proves
\eqref{wdn:dia:eq:shiftsecond}. In particular, the unshifted coefficient is
\begin{align}
 c_1(s):=D_1(0,0)
 &=-\frac{D^2f}{2fB}+\frac{(Df)(DB)}{2fB^2}
   +\frac{D^2B}{8B^2}-\frac{5(DB)^2}{24B^3}\notag\\
 &=\frac{s^6-7s^5-5s^4+32s^3+32s^2-9s-8}
 {12(s-1)(s+1)(s+2)^3}.\label{wdn:dia:eq:c1}
\end{align}
Dividing by the unshifted expansion is uniformly legitimate and gives
\begin{multline}\label{wdn:dia:eq:ratiosecond}
 \frac{C_{N-\ell,m-r}}{C_{N,m}}=t^\ell h^{-r}
 \left[1+\frac1m\left(\frac r2-\frac{E\beta}{B}
                         -\frac{\beta^2}{2B}\right)\right.
 \\\left.{}+O\bigl(N^{-2}(1+\ell+r)^{D_0}\bigr)\right].
\end{multline}

\subsection{The principal endpoint through first order}
There is an exact useful version of the Newton multiplication coefficient:
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:cdifference}
 c_{\ell,r}(m)=\frac{\Delta^r z_\ell(m-r)}{r!}.
\end{equation}
Indeed, expand $z_\ell(k)$ in the Newton basis centered at $q=m-r$ and
multiply by $\fall{k}{q}$; the shifted falling factorial becomes
$\fall{k}{q+r}$. Applying this to every Newton term of a polynomial proves
\eqref{wdn:dia:eq:cdifference}.

For a uniform product estimate let $d_0=\ell-r$, let $U$ be a sum of $r$
independent uniform $[0,1]$ variables, and independently choose a uniform
$d_0$-subset $S$ of $\{0,\ldots,\ell-1\}$. Repeated integration of a finite
difference gives the exact identity
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:operatorint}
 c_{\ell,r}(m)=\gamma_\ell\binom\ell r m^{d_0}
 \E\prod_{i\in S}\left(1+\frac{2i+1-r+U}{m}\right).
\end{equation}
The expectation of the sum of offsets is
$d_0(\ell-r/2)$. Each offset has magnitude at most $2\ell$, so the
remainder beyond the linear term is bounded in absolute value by
$(2\ell^2/m)^2e^{2\ell^2/m}/2$. Thus
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:csecond}
 c_{\ell,r}(m)=\gamma_\ell\binom\ell r m^{\ell-r}
 \left[1+\frac{(\ell-r)(\ell-r/2)}m+O(\ell^4/m^2)\right]
\end{equation}
uniformly through logarithmic shifts, including $r=0$ and $r=\ell$.

The remaining finite products have expansions
\begin{align*}
 \frac{(2N-2\ell-1)!!}{(2N-1)!!}
  &=(2N)^{-\ell}\left(1+\frac{\ell^2}{2N}+O((1+\ell)^4/N^2)\right),\\
 \frac{m!}{(m-r)!}
  &=m^r\left(1-\frac{r(r-1)}{2m}+O((1+r)^4/m^2)\right).
\end{align*}
Combine these with \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:ratiosecond}, \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:csecond}, and
$(N-\ell)/N$. With
\[
 b^0_{\ell,r}=\gamma_\ell s^{2\ell}\binom\ell r s^r(1-s)^{\ell-r},
\]
one obtains
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:bsecond}
 b_{N,m,\ell,r}=b^0_{\ell,r}
 \left[1+\frac{\kappa_{\ell,r}}N
 +O\bigl(N^{-2}(1+\ell)^{D_{\rm ker}}\bigr)\right],
\end{equation}
where $D_{\rm ker}$ is a fixed finite exponent and
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:kappa}
 \kappa_{\ell,r}=\frac{\ell^2}{2}-\ell
 +\mu\left[\ell^2-\frac32\ell r+r
 -\frac{E(\ell-\mu r)}B-\frac{(\ell-\mu r)^2}{2B}\right].
\end{equation}
All errors are relative to the positive $b^0$. Consequently, their sum is
bounded by a constant times
$N^{-2}\sum_{\ell\ge1}\gamma_\ell s^{2\ell}(1+\ell)^{D_{\rm ker}}=O_J(N^{-2})$.
There is no leftover power of $\log N$ after summation.

\subsection{The opposite endpoint and degree loss}
We claim
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:Bsecond}
 \mathcal B_{N,m}=\frac{S_0(s)}N+O_J(N^{-2}),\qquad S_0(s)=\frac{s^2}{2t}.
\end{equation}
The recurrence makes every $p_j$ monic. Its top coefficient comes only from
$kp_{j-1}$; all other terms in \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:prec} have smaller degree.
For $0\le d_0\le j\le A\log N$, put
$V_{j,d_0}=\LL_m(k^{d_0}z_{N-j})/Q_{N,m}$. The multiplication coefficient
for $k^{d_0}$ is nonnegative and bounded by
$\binom{d_0}{r}m^{d_0-r}$, since its stay factors do not exceed $m$.
The uniform shifted ratio therefore gives
\begin{align}
 V_{j,d_0}
 &\le C(2N)^{-j}m^{d_0}t^j(1+h^{-1})^{d_0}\notag\\
 &=C s^{2j}M^{d_0-j},\qquad M=m(1+h^{-1})\ge cN.\label{wdn:dia:eq:degreeloss}
\end{align}
For the top degree, the same first-order estimates give
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:topmonomial}
 V_{j,j}=s^{2j}\left(1+O((1+j)^{D_2}/N)\right)
\end{equation}
for a fixed exponent $D_2$.

By \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:order}, the coefficient of $k^{j-a}$ in $p_j$, $a\ge1$, is
at most $2j\gamma_j j^{2a}/a!$. This uses the elementary-symmetric bound
for the positive offsets $1,3,\ldots,2j-1$, whose sum is $j^2$.
Equations \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:degreeloss} and $\gamma_j\le1$ bound the sum of all
lower-degree contributions by
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:lowerdegrees}
 C s^{2j}j\bigl(e^{Cj^2/N}-1\bigr)
 \le C' s^{2j}j^3/N
\end{equation}
uniformly for logarithmic $j$. Dividing
\eqref{wdn:dia:eq:topmonomial}--\eqref{wdn:dia:eq:lowerdegrees} by $2N$ yields
\[
 \frac{\LL_m(p_jz_{N-j})}{2NQ_{N,m}}
 =\frac{s^{2j}}{2N}
 +O\left(\frac{s^{2j}(1+j)^{D_3}}{N^2}\right).
\]
The error sums absolutely and uniformly. For $j>A\log N$ in the opposite
half, positivity bounds its total by $C Nq^{A\log N}$ using
\eqref{wdn:dia:eq:generalmiddle}. Choose $A$ so large that this and all polynomially
weighted geometric tails needed here are $O(N^{-2})$. Summing
$\sum_{j\ge1}s^{2j}=s^2/t$ proves \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:Bsecond}. This argument accounts
for every degree loss and the entire opposite half, not only each fixed $j$.

\subsection{Residual cancellation and its explicit moments}
Write $R_0(\alpha)=\sqrt t$, $R_1(\alpha)=R_0(\alpha)r_1(s)$, and
$H(N,m)=R_0(m/N)+R_1(m/N)/N$. In the short range,
\[
 \frac{m-r}{N-\ell}=\alpha+\frac{\alpha\ell-r}{N}
                    +O((1+\ell)^2/N^2),
\]
and smoothness on nested compact intervals gives
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:Hshift}
 H(N-\ell,m-r)=R_0+\frac{R_1+(\alpha\ell-r)R_0'}N
              +O\bigl(N^{-2}(1+\ell)^{D_4}\bigr).
\end{equation}
The prime here means differentiation in $\alpha$, not in $s$.
The zeroth-order residual in \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:causal} is zero because
$(1+q_0)R_0=1$. For the first order define
\[
 \mathcal K=\sum_{\ell,r}b^0_{\ell,r}\kappa_{\ell,r},\qquad
 \mathcal J=\sum_{\ell,r}b^0_{\ell,r}(\alpha\ell-r).
\]
Its cancellation equation is
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:R1eq}
 (1+q_0)R_1=-R_0\mathcal K-R_0'\mathcal J-S_0.
\end{equation}
All sums converge absolutely. For transparent exact evaluation, put
\begin{align*}
 L_1&=\sum_{\ell\ge1}\ell w_\ell=\frac{s^2}{2t^{3/2}},&
 L_2&=\sum_{\ell\ge1}\ell^2w_\ell=L_1+\frac{3s^4}{4t^{5/2}},\\
 \mathcal J&=(\alpha-s)L_1,&
 R_0'&=-\frac{(1+s)^2}{2(s+2)\sqrt t}.
\end{align*}
Conditionally on $\ell$, the index $r$ is binomial with parameters $(\ell,s)$.
Using its first two moments in \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:kappa} gives
\begin{align}\label{wdn:dia:eq:Kmoments}
 \mathcal K={}&\frac{L_2}2-L_1+
 \mu\left[L_2-\frac32sL_2+sL_1-\frac EB(1-\mu s)L_1\right.\notag\\
 &\left.\hspace{15mm}
 -\frac{(1-\mu s)^2L_2+\mu^2s(1-s)L_1}{2B}\right].
\end{align}
Equivalently, these moments follow by Euler differentiation of
\[
 \sum_{\ell\ge1,r}b^0_{\ell,r}x^\ell y^r
 =\bigl(1-s^2x(1-s+sy)\bigr)^{-1/2}-1.
\]
Substituting \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:cumulants}, \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:Kmoments}, and $S_0$ into
\eqref{wdn:dia:eq:R1eq} gives exactly the rational function $r_1$ in
\eqref{wdn:dia:eq:Rcorrection}. Both the drift term $R_0'\mathcal J$ and the
opposite-endpoint source $S_0$ are essential. The exact companion checks
this rational identity without using the sequence values.

The local polynomial remainders are summable against $w_\ell$, and the
remaining tails were chosen $O(N^{-2})$. Thus $H$ satisfies
\eqref{wdn:dia:eq:causal} with residual $O(N^{-2})$ uniformly on $J_1$. For long
predecessor jumps use bounded smooth extensions of $R_0,R_1$ agreeing with
them near $J_2$; their contributions are bounded by the middle estimate.
No local Taylor assertion is made outside those neighborhoods.

\subsection{Stopped contraction creates the second order bound}
Let $e(N,m)=R_{N,m}-H(N,m)$. Subtract the exact equation and absorb the
long principal tail using boundedness and \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:generalmiddle}. For a
sufficiently large logarithmic cutoff,
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:stoppedrec}
 |e(N,m)|\le\sum_{\ell\le A\log N,r\le\ell}
 b_{N,m,\ell,r}|e(N-\ell,m-r)|+C N^{-2}
\end{equation}
uniformly on $J_1$ for large $N$. The short kernel mass is at most a fixed
$\bar q<1$ by its leading limit and the strict contraction on $J_2$.
Choose $c>2/|\log\bar q|$ and iterate for
$M_N=\lceil c\log N\rceil$ steps.

Each step changes either index by at most $A\log N$. Starting from $J$, all
paths of depth at most $M_N$ satisfy
\[
 N'=N-O((\log N)^2),\quad m'=m-O((\log N)^2),\quad
 \left|\frac{m'}{N'}-\frac mN\right|=O((\log N)^2/N).
\]
The margins in \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:nested} ensure that every such node remains in
$J_1$, has size at least $N/2$, and is above the uniform validity threshold.
Thus \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:stoppedrec} applies at every node. The sum of path weights at
level $j$ is at most $\bar q^j$, independent of the branching count. The
accumulated forcing is at most
$4CN^{-2}\sum_{j<M_N}\bar q^j=O(N^{-2})$. The terminal errors are uniformly
bounded because $0\le R\le1$ and $H$ is bounded; their weighted contribution
is $O(\bar q^{M_N})=O(N^{-2})$. Hence $e(N,m)=O_J(N^{-2})$.
This proves \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:Rcorrection} without assuming beforehand that
$N(R-R_0)$ or $N^2(R-H)$ is bounded.

\subsection{The diagonal coefficient}
At $N=2n,m=n$, the correction from \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:Rcorrection} is
$r_1(s_0)/(2n)$, whereas the saddle correction in \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:shiftsecond} is

\]
It follows that

\end{equation}

\section{Lambert W inversion with integer rounding}\label{wdn:dia:sec:inverse}
Define the integer threshold
\[
 \mathcal N(X)=\min\{n\ge0:a_n\ge X\}.
\]
It exists for large $X$ by Theorem~\ref{wdn:dia:thm:main}. Let $W$ denote the positive
real Lambert function, characterized by $W(z)e^{W(z)}=z$ for $z>0$. Put
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:inverseparams}
 L=\log X,\qquad c=\log(C\sqrt{2\pi}),\qquad
 T=L-c,\qquad x_0(X)=\frac{T}{W(dT/e)}.
\end{equation}
All these expressions are used only for $X$ sufficiently large that $T>0$.

\begin{theorem}[Rounded threshold bracket]\label{wdn:dia:thm:inverse}
There exists a positive function $\epsilon(X)$ satisfying
$\epsilon(X)=o(1/\log\log X)$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:inversebracket}
 \left\lceil x_0(X)-\epsilon(X)\right\rceil
 \le\mathcal N(X)\le
 \left\lceil x_0(X)+\epsilon(X)\right\rceil.
\end{equation}
In particular, $\mathcal N(X)\sim L/W(dL/e)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The leading equivalent and Stirling's formula give
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:logasympt}
 \log a_n=\Phi(n)+e_n,\qquad
 \Phi(x)=x(\log x+\log d-1)+c,\qquad e_n\longrightarrow0.
\end{equation}
The $\tfrac12\log n$ in Stirling cancels the $n^{-1/2}$ in the forward
formula; therefore no $\log n$ term remains in \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:logasympt}.
The equivalent also gives
\[
 \frac{a_{n+1}}{d n a_n}\longrightarrow1,
\]
so $a_n$ is eventually strictly increasing.

Writing $w=W(dT/e)$ gives $x_0=T/w=(e/d)e^w$, hence
$x_0\log(dx_0/e)=T$. Thus $\Phi(x_0)=L$ exactly and
$\Phi'(x)=\log(dx)$. Also $\log x_0\sim\log L$; for example
$W(z)=\log z-\log\log z+o(1)$ follows directly from
$W(z)+\log W(z)=\log z$.

Define the finite tail bound
\[
 \eta(X)=\sup_{n\ge\lfloor x_0/2\rfloor}|e_n|+\frac1{x_0},\qquad
 \epsilon(X)=\frac{2\eta(X)}{\log(dx_0/2)}.
\]
For sufficiently large $X$, the denominator is positive and $\eta(X)\to0$,
so $\epsilon=o(1/\log x_0)=o(1/\log L)$. If an integer
$n\ge x_0/2$ satisfies $n<x_0-\epsilon$, the mean value theorem gives
$\Phi(n)<L-2\eta$, and hence $\log a_n<L$. For
$n_+=\lceil x_0+\epsilon\rceil$, the same derivative lower bound gives
$\Phi(n_+)\ge L+2\eta$, so $\log a_{n_+}>L$.

Integers below $x_0/2$ cause no difficulty: $\Phi(x_0/2)$ differs from
$\Phi(x_0)$ by a quantity tending to infinity, $e_n\to0$, and eventual
monotonicity controls all sufficiently large earlier indices. The finitely
many initial $a_n$ are also below $X$ eventually. Thus every integer less
than $x_0-\epsilon$ fails the threshold, whereas $n_+$ reaches it, proving
\eqref{wdn:dia:eq:inversebracket} without choosing any interpolation of $a_n$.
Finally, replacing $T=L-c$ by $L$ changes only lower-order terms, proving
the leading scale.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[What the error does and does not mean]\label{wdn:dia:rem:inverse}
The $o(1/\log\log X)$ error applies to the \emph{real endpoints before
ceilings}. In general the integer $\mathcal N(X)-x_0(X)$ need not tend to
zero. Near an integer boundary the bracket can contain two adjacent integers.
The function $\epsilon$ above is an existence bound from a qualitative
asymptotic, not an effective numerical certificate for a specified $X$.
A sharper computable threshold would require explicit forward error bounds.
Lambert $W$ inversion itself is a standard consequence, not a separate
novelty claim.
\end{remark}

\begin{corollary}[First corrected threshold bracket]\label{wdn:dia:cor:inversefine}
With $x_0$ as in \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:inverseparams}, put
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:x1}
 \beta_1=b_1+\frac1{12},\qquad
 x_1=x_0-\frac{\beta_1}{x_0\log(dx_0)}.
\end{equation}
There is a fixed constant $M>0$ such that, for all sufficiently large $X$,
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:dia:eq:inversefine}
 \left\lceil x_1-\frac{M}{x_0^2\log(dx_0)}\right\rceil
 \le\mathcal N(X)\le
 \left\lceil x_1+\frac{M}{x_0^2\log(dx_0)}\right\rceil.
\end{equation}
The constant is an existence constant inherited from the forward remainder;
no effective value for a prescribed finite threshold is claimed.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Theorem~\ref{wdn:dia:thm:correction} and Stirling's formula give
\[
 \log a_n=\Psi(n)+O(n^{-2}),\qquad
 \Psi(x)=\Phi(x)+\frac{\beta_1}{x},
\]
where $\Phi$ is defined in \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:logasympt}. In particular, the $1/12$
comes from the factorial, not from $b_1$ alone. By definition
$\Phi(x_0)=L$. The shift $\delta=x_1-x_0$ is
$O(1/(x_0\log x_0))$, and Taylor's theorem yields
\[
 \Psi(x_1)-L=O\bigl(x_0^{-3}/\log x_0\bigr),\qquad
 \Psi'(x)=\log(dx)-\frac{\beta_1}{x^2}.
\]
This derivative is positive and at least a fixed multiple of $\log x_0$
on $[x_0/2,\infty)$ when $X$ is large. Choose $D$ so that the forward
logarithmic error is at most $D/n^2$ for all sufficiently large $n$.
For $n\ge x_0/2$ this is at most $4D/x_0^2$. Increasing $M$ if necessary,
the mean value theorem shows that every integer below
$x_1-M/(x_0^2\log(dx_0))$ in this range has $\log a_n<L$.
The integer ceiling of the upper endpoint has $\log a_n\ge L$.
Earlier integers are below threshold by eventual monotonicity and the
same finite-initial-segment argument as in Theorem~\ref{wdn:dia:thm:inverse}.
These integer witnesses prove \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:inversefine}.
\end{proof}
The real half-width in \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:inversefine} is
$O(x_0^{-2}/\log x_0)$, substantially smaller than the leading-only
existence width. The ceilings remain indispensable.

\paragraph{Higher relative orders.}
The proved first correction suggests continuing in descending powers of
$n$. Further orders need their own uniform coefficient estimates, kernel
equations, and propagated remainders. This report makes no all-orders claim.
A formal symbolic recursion alone would not certify that continuation.

\begin{writenote}[Answered by Part~II, 5~October 2026]
Part~II proves every fixed order: Theorem~\ref{wdn:int:thm:allorders} on every compact $J\subset(0,1)$ and Corollary~\ref{wdn:int:cor:diagonal} on the diagonal, each coefficient produced by the finite algorithm of Section~\ref{wdn:int:sec:algorithm} and backed by a propagated remainder (Section~\ref{wdn:int:sec:fullrange}). Convergence of the series and uniformity for a growing order remain open (Section~\ref{wdn:sec:further}, item~\ref{wdn:q:convergence}).
\end{writenote}

\paragraph{Beyond the contraction interval.}
The elementary kernel mass satisfies $q_0(s)<1$ exactly when
$s<\sqrt3/2$. The first-correction theorem applies on every compact subinterval of this
strict contraction range. Can the same limiting ratio $\sqrt{1-s^2}$ be proved throughout the full
range $0<\alpha<1$, including where this direct absolute-error contraction
fails? A failed sufficient contraction test is not evidence of a phase
transition. Additional structure of the positive convolution may be needed.

\section{Definitions and main results}
For a Dyck path of semilength $N$, weight each peak at coordinates $(a,b)$
by $k+a/b$, and let $p_N(k)$ be the total path weight. The exact bridge
proved in Report 141~\cite{foundation} identifies these polynomials by
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:recurrence}
 p_0(k)=1,\qquad
 p_N(k)=(k+2N-2)p_{N-1}(k)
       +\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}p_i(k)p_{N-1-i}(k).
\end{equation}
Our analytic starting point may equivalently be this recurrence. Write
\begin{gather}
 \LL_m P=[\fall{k}{m}]P(k)=\frac{\Delta^mP(0)}{m!},\qquad
 \gamma_N=4^{-N}\binom{2N}{N},\label{wdn:int:eq:Newton}\\
 z_N(k)=\gamma_N\prod_{i=0}^{N-1}(k+2i+1),\qquad
 Q_{N,m}=\LL_mz_N,\qquad
 R_{N,m}=\frac{\LL_mp_N}{2NQ_{N,m}}.\label{wdn:int:eq:normalization}
\end{gather}
The last definition applies for $N\ge1$ and $0\le m\le N$.
The sequence of interest is
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:diagonaldef}
 a_n=\LL_np_{2n},\qquad a_0=1.
\end{equation}
The factor $1/m!$ in \eqref{wdn:int:eq:Newton} is essential.

Throughout the interior ratio range, set
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:saddleparameters}
 \alpha=\frac mN=\frac{2s^2}{1+s},\quad 0<s<1,\quad
 t=1-s^2,\quad h=\frac{1-s}{s},\quad
 \mu=\frac1\alpha=\frac{1+s}{2s^2},\quad
 B=\frac{t(s+2)}{4s^4}.
\end{equation}
The map $s\mapsto\alpha$ is strictly increasing from $(0,1)$ onto $(0,1)$.
When convenient, a function of $s$ is also regarded as a function of
$\alpha$ through this map.

\begin{theorem}[All fixed orders on the full interior]\label{wdn:int:thm:allorders}
There are unique analytic coefficient functions $R_j$ on $0<s<1$, of the
form
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:rationalform}
 R_j(s)=\sqrt{1-s^2}\,r_j(s),\qquad r_j\in\Q(s),\qquad r_0=1,
\end{equation}
regular throughout this interval, with the following property. For every
fixed integer $K\ge1$ and every compact $J\subset(0,1)$,
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:allorders}
 R_{N,m}=\sum_{j=0}^{K-1}R_j(s(m/N))N^{-j}
                  +O_{J,K}(N^{-K})
\end{equation}
uniformly over integer pairs with $m/N\in J$. The finite algorithm in
Section~\ref{wdn:int:sec:algorithm} determines all coefficients exactly.
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}[The diagonal coefficient field]\label{wdn:int:cor:diagonal}
For every fixed $K\ge1$,
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:diagonal}
 a_n=C d^n\frac{n!}{\sqrt n}
 \left(\sum_{j=0}^{K-1}b_jn^{-j}+O_K(n^{-K})\right),
\end{equation}
where
\begin{gather}
 d=\frac{51\sqrt{17}-107}{4},\qquad
 C=\frac{\sqrt{2+2/\sqrt{17}}}{\pi^{3/2}},\qquad b_0=1,\label{wdn:int:eq:constants}\\
 b_j\in\Q(\sqrt{17})\quad(j\ge0),\qquad
 b_1=-\frac{989}{2176}-\frac{907\sqrt{17}}{36992},\label{wdn:int:eq:b1}\\
 b_2=-\frac{30800095+3912441\sqrt{17}}{80494592}.
 \label{wdn:int:eq:b2}
\end{gather}
\end{corollary}
The first relative correction and the constants were established in
Report 141. The continuation here includes all fixed orders, the entire
interior ratio range, the exact coefficient field, and an integer-safe
inverse at every fixed order. No convergence of the expansion, uniformity
for $K$ growing with $N$, boundary result at $\alpha=0$ or $1$, effective
onset or error constant, or priority claim is made. Finite exact checks
in the companion corroborate algebra; the analytic remainder is proved
below.

\section{Exact transforms and the positive convolution}\label{wdn:int:sec:exact}
Report 141 proves the formal convolution identity
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:convolution}
 2Nz_N=\sum_{j=1}^N p_jz_{N-j}.
\end{equation}
All ordinary coefficients of $p_N,z_N$ are nonnegative. Isolating the
$j=N$ term therefore gives $0\preceq p_N\preceq2Nz_N$ in ordinary
coefficientwise order. Since
$\LL_m k^d=\left\{\begin{smallmatrix}d\\m\end{smallmatrix}\right\}\ge0$,
Newton extraction preserves this order, and hence
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:bounded}
 0\le R_{N,m}\le1.
\end{equation}
Here $Q_{N,m}>0$ throughout the valid range, as the next formula shows.

Define analytic functions on $|z|<1$ by
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:fh}
 f(z)=(1-z)^{-1/2},\qquad h(z)=f(z)-1,\qquad
 C_{N,m}=[z^N]f(z)h(z)^m.
\end{equation}
The product definition gives
\[
 \sum_{N\ge0}\frac{z_N(k)}{(2N-1)!!}z^N
 =f(z)(1+h(z))^k
 =f(z)\sum_{m\ge0}\frac{\fall{k}{m}}{m!}h(z)^m.
\]
Thus, with $(-1)!!=1$,
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:Q}
 Q_{N,m}=\frac{(2N-1)!!}{m!}C_{N,m}.
\end{equation}
All relevant coefficients are positive. The saddle condition
$t h'(t)/h(t)=N/m$ is exactly \eqref{wdn:int:eq:saddleparameters}.

For any polynomial $P$, Newton multiplication gives
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:multiplication}
 \LL_m(z_\ell P)=\sum_{r=0}^{\ell}c_{\ell,r}(m)\LL_{m-r}P,
 \qquad c_{\ell,r}(m)=\frac{\Delta^r z_\ell(m-r)}{r!}.
\end{equation}
Indeed, expand $z_\ell(k)$ in the Newton basis centered at $q=m-r$,
and multiply each term by $\fall{k}{q}$; the identity
$\fall{k}{q}\fall{(k-q)}{r}=\fall{k}{q+r}$ proves the assertion.
Only admissible predecessor indices are included; otherwise the
transformed summand is zero and its kernel is defined to be zero.
For admissible predecessors the coefficients in \eqref{wdn:int:eq:multiplication}
are nonnegative.

Put $L=\lfloor N/2\rfloor$. Splitting \eqref{wdn:int:eq:convolution} into
disjoint halves yields the exact equation
\begin{align}
 R_{N,m}&=1-(\mathcal K_NR)_{N,m}-B_{N,m},\label{wdn:int:eq:causal}\\
 (\mathcal K_NR)_{N,m}
 &=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum_{r=0}^{\ell}
       a_{N,m,\ell,r}R_{N-\ell,m-r},\label{wdn:int:eq:exactkernel}\\
 a_{N,m,\ell,r}
 &=\left(1-\frac\ell N\right)c_{\ell,r}(m)
                 \frac{Q_{N-\ell,m-r}}{Q_{N,m}},\label{wdn:int:eq:kerneldef}\\
 B_{N,m}&=\sum_{j=1}^{N-L-1}
                   \frac{\LL_m(p_jz_{N-j})}{2NQ_{N,m}}.\label{wdn:int:eq:oppositeexact}
\end{align}
The possible even midpoint occurs once. Every principal predecessor has
size at least $N/2$.

\section{A uniform shifted saddle expansion}\label{wdn:int:sec:saddle}
Let $S$ be any compact subinterval of $(0,1)$ in the $s$ variable. Define
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:cumulants}
 \dd=t\frac d{dt}=-\frac{1-s^2}{2s}\frac d{ds},\qquad
 \kappa_j=\dd^j\log h(t),\qquad g_j=\dd^j\log f(t).
\end{equation}
These are rational functions of $s$; in particular $\kappa_1=\mu$ and
$\kappa_2=B>0$.

\begin{lemma}[Shifted expansion with summable remainders]\label{wdn:int:lem:saddle}
Fix $A>0$ and $K\ge1$. Uniformly on $S$ and for
$0\le r\le\ell\le A\log m$,
\begin{align}
 C_{N-\ell,m-r}
 &=\frac{f(t)h(t)^mt^{-N}t^\ell h^{-r}}{\sqrt{2\pi mB}}
 \left(\sum_{j=0}^{K-1}D_j(\ell,r;s)m^{-j}
  +O\bigl(m^{-K}(1+\ell+r)^{M_K}\bigr)\right),\label{wdn:int:eq:shifted}
\end{align}
for some fixed exponent $M_K$. Here $D_0=1$ and
$D_j\in\Q(s)[\ell,r]$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Set $\psi(\theta)=h(te^{i\theta})/h(t)$ and
$g(\theta)=f(te^{i\theta})/f(t)$. After extracting the factors outside the
parentheses in \eqref{wdn:int:eq:shifted}, the Fourier integral before its Gaussian
normalization is
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:Fourier}
 \frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}
 g(\theta)\psi(\theta)^{-r}e^{i\ell\theta}
 \exp\{m(\log\psi(\theta)-i\mu\theta)\}\,d\theta.
\end{equation}
Use the analytic logarithm only near zero. Elsewhere the exact integrand
is
\[
 g(\theta)\psi(\theta)^{m-r}e^{-i(N-\ell)\theta}.
\]
No global logarithm is needed. Moreover $h$ has no zero on $|z|=t>0$,
since $h(z)=0$ would imply $z=0$.

The function $\psi$ is the characteristic function of the integer law
$\Prob(X=j)=[z^j]h(z)t^j/h(t)$, $j\ge1$. Every positive degree has positive
mass, so the law has span one. Its exponential moments are uniform on $S$,
because $t$ stays in a compact subset of $(0,1)$. The mean and variance
are $\mu$ and $B$. Uniform aperiodicity gives $|\psi|\le q_*<1$ on any
fixed outer arc. On a sufficiently small fixed inner arc,
\[
 \Re(\log\psi(\theta)-i\mu\theta)\le-b\theta^2
\]
with $b>0$ uniform on $S$. Choose $0<\eta<1/6$. The outer arc is
exponentially small; the intermediate arc
$m^{-1/2+\eta}\le|\theta|\le\theta_0$ contributes
$O(\exp(-c m^{2\eta}))$. Polynomial logarithmic shifts do not change
these conclusions, since $m-r\ge m/2$ eventually.

On the central arc put $\theta=u/\sqrt m$ and $z=m^{-1/2}$. After
removing $e^{-Bu^2/2}$, the remaining analytic factor is
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:Gaussianexp}
 \exp\left\{
 \sum_{j\ge1}\frac{g_j-r\kappa_j+\ell\mathbf1_{j=1}}{j!}(iu)^jz^j
 +\sum_{j\ge3}\frac{\kappa_j}{j!}(iu)^jz^{j-2}
 \right\}.
\end{equation}
Expand in the real variable $z$ through degree $2K-1$. Fixed derivatives
at zero are polynomials in $u,\ell,r$ over $\Q(s)$. Odd powers of $z$
give odd polynomials in $u$ and integrate to zero.

Here is a uniform Taylor remainder bound. For $0\le z'\le m^{-1/2}$,
$|u|\le m^\eta$, the saddle remainder exponent is
$O(z'|u|^3)=O(m^{-1/2+\eta})u^2$. Each required fixed derivative has
polynomial $u$ growth. The amplitude exponent has modulus bounded by
\[
 C(1+\ell+r)z'|u|+C(1+r)(z'u)^2.
\]
Young's inequality bounds its linear term by
$\delta u^2/2+C'(1+\ell+r)^2/m$, and the second summand is bounded
uniformly in the logarithmic range. The quadratic term is absorbed for
large $m$. Analytic derivatives are uniformly bounded in a fixed complex
neighborhood of the compact saddle set. Thus every derivative required
for the remainder is bounded by
\[
 C_K(1+\ell+r)^{M_K}(1+|u|)^{E_K}e^{\delta u^2},
 \qquad \delta<\tfrac14\min_S B.
\]
Taylor's integral remainder, multiplied by the Gaussian and integrated,
is $O(m^{-K}(1+\ell+r)^{M_K})$. Extending polynomial Gaussian moments
to the whole real line costs a superpolynomially small error. This proves
the asserted expansion and its uniform remainder.
\end{proof}

The proof is also a finite coefficient algorithm: expand
\eqref{wdn:int:eq:Gaussianexp} through $z^{2j}$ and apply
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:Gaussianmoments}
 u^{2a}\longmapsto\frac{(2a-1)!!}{B^a},\qquad
 u^{2a+1}\longmapsto0.
\end{equation}
The coefficient of $z^{2j}$ is $D_j$. Only
$\kappa_1,\ldots,\kappa_{2j+2}$ and $g_1,\ldots,g_{2j}$ are needed.
For later use introduce the normalized ratio series
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:V}
 V_{\ell,r}(x)=
 \frac{\sum_{j\ge0}D_j(\ell,r;s)x^j}
      {\sum_{j\ge0}D_j(0,0;s)x^j}.
\end{equation}
It has polynomial coefficients in $\ell,r$ over $\Q(s)$, satisfies
$V_{0,0}=1$ identically, and has the corresponding relative uniform
remainder when truncated at a fixed order.

Its $D_1(\ell,r;s)$ is Part~I's $D_1(\ell,r)$ of \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:D1}, and $D_1(0,0;s)$ is Part~I's $c_1(s)$ (compare \eqref{wdn:int:eq:D1} with \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:c1}).

\section{A positive estimate away from the endpoints}\label{wdn:int:sec:middle}
Define
\[
 U_{N,m,\ell}=\frac{\LL_m(z_\ell z_{N-\ell})}{Q_{N,m}}.
\]
\begin{lemma}[Uniform middle bound]\label{wdn:int:lem:middle}
On every compact $J\subset(0,1)$ there are constants $C<\infty$ and
$0<q<1$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:middle}
 U_{N,m,\ell}\le CNq^{\min(\ell,N-\ell)}
 \quad(1\le\ell<N,\ m/N\in J).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Choose an integer $H=m/(1-s)+O(1)$. On the compact ratio range,
$H>m$ eventually and $H/N=2s^2/(1-s^2)+O(N^{-1})$ stays in a compact
positive interval. Nonnegative Newton coefficients imply
\[
 \LL_m(z_\ell z_{N-\ell})\le
 \frac{z_\ell(H)z_{N-\ell}(H)}{\fall H m}.
\]
The evaluation must be at an integer: then higher falling factorials
are nonnegative or zero. By \eqref{wdn:int:eq:Q},
\[
 \frac{z_N(H)}{\fall H m\,Q_{N,m}}
 =\frac{[z^N]f(z)(1+h(z))^H}{\binom Hm C_{N,m}}.
\]
Positive-coefficient evaluation bounds the numerator by
$f(t)(1+h)^Ht^{-N}$. Lemma~\ref{wdn:int:lem:saddle}, with no shift and $K=1$,
bounds the denominator below by a constant times
$\binom Hm f(t)h^mt^{-N}/\sqrt m$. Put $p=h/(1+h)=1-s$. The ratio
is therefore at most
\[
 \frac{C\sqrt m}{\binom Hm p^m(1-p)^{H-m}}\le C'N.
\]
The last inequality follows from the uniform central binomial estimate:
$p$ stays away from $0,1$, $m-Hp=O(1)$, and Stirling's formula makes the
binomial probability at least $c/\sqrt H$.

For $\ell\le N/2$, the product formula gives
\[
 \frac{z_\ell(H)z_{N-\ell}(H)}{z_N(H)}
 =\frac{\gamma_\ell\gamma_{N-\ell}}{\gamma_N}
 \prod_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\frac{H+2i+1}{H+2(N-\ell+i)+1}.
\]
The gamma ratio is uniformly bounded: $\gamma_\ell\le1$ and
\[
 \log\frac{\gamma_{N-\ell}}{\gamma_N}
 \le\sum_{j=N-\ell+1}^N\frac1{2j-1}<\frac12.
\]
Each product factor is at most $(H+N)/(H+2N-1)$, which is bounded by
some $q<1$ on our compact range for all sufficiently large $N$. Symmetry
treats $\ell>N/2$, and a larger constant absorbs the finite remaining
sizes. This proves \eqref{wdn:int:eq:middle}.
\end{proof}
In \eqref{wdn:int:eq:exactkernel},
$\sum_r a_{N,m,\ell,r}=(1-\ell/N)U_{N,m,\ell}$.
Also $p_j\preceq2jz_j$ controls \eqref{wdn:int:eq:oppositeexact} by the same
bound. Consequently discarding either endpoint beyond $A\log N$ costs
at most $CN^2q^{A\log N}$. For any prescribed fixed power saving, choose
$A$ large enough. The sharp logarithmic endpoint estimates below, rather
than the extra factor $N$ in \eqref{wdn:int:eq:middle}, control the retained terms.

Lemma~\ref{wdn:int:lem:middle} is Part~I's Lemma~\ref{wdn:dia:lem:middle} and \eqref{wdn:dia:eq:generalmiddle}, by the same integer-evaluation argument, on every compact $J\subset(0,1)$.
\end{writenote}

\section{Polynomial coefficients at both endpoints}\label{wdn:int:sec:endpoints}
\subsection{The Newton multiplication polynomial}
Let $d=\ell-r$, let $U$ be a sum of $r$ independent uniform variables
on $[0,1]$, and let $S_d$ be an independent uniformly chosen $d$-subset
of $\{0,\ldots,\ell-1\}$. Repeated integration of the finite difference
in \eqref{wdn:int:eq:multiplication} gives the exact identity
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:Newtonintegral}
 c_{\ell,r}(m)=\gamma_\ell\binom\ell r m^d
 \E\prod_{i\in S_d}\left(1+\frac{2i+1-r+U}{m}\right).
\end{equation}
Each offset has absolute value at most $2\ell$. Truncating the expectation
after degree $K-1$ in $m^{-1}$ has absolute error at most
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:productremainder}
 \frac{(2\ell^2/m)^K}{K!}\exp(2\ell^2/m).
\end{equation}
Indeed the sum of the absolute elementary products is bounded by the
corresponding exponential series.

Write this expectation as $U_{\ell,r}(x)=\sum_{q\ge0}u_q(\ell,r)x^q$.
For fixed $q$,
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:u}
 u_q(\ell,r)=\frac{\fall{(\ell-r)}q}{\fall\ell q}\,
 \E e_q(1-r+U,3-r+U,\ldots,2\ell-1-r+U).
\end{equation}
Newton identities and sums of powers show that the elementary symmetric
expression is a polynomial in $\ell,r,U$ over $\Q$, of degree at most
$2q$. At $\ell=0,\ldots,q-1$ it vanishes identically in $r,U$.
It is therefore divisible by $\fall\ell q$. Moreover moments of $U$ are
polynomials in $r$, from
$\E e^{zU}=((e^z-1)/z)^r$. Thus $u_q$ is a genuine polynomial in
$\ell,r$. Polynomial continuation, together with $\fall{(\ell-r)}q$,
also gives the correct value when $\ell<q$. For example
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:u12}
 u_0=1,\quad u_1=(\ell-r)(\ell-r/2),\quad
 u_2=\frac{(\ell-r)(\ell-r-1)(12\ell^2-12\ell r-4\ell+3r^2+r-4)}{24}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Top ordinary coefficients and their boundary zeros}
Define $c_a(n)=[k^{n-a}]p_n(k)$ for $n\ge a$, and set $c_a(n)=0$
for $0\le n<a$. This notation is distinct from the two-index Newton
coefficient $c_{\ell,r}(m)$.
\begin{lemma}[Polynomial top coefficients]\label{wdn:int:lem:top}
For every $a\ge0$, $c_a(n)$ extends to a polynomial in $\Q[n]$ of degree
at most $2a$. For $a\ge1$ it is divisible by $\fall n a$. In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:Ca}
 C_a(n):=\frac{c_a(n)}{\fall n a}\in\Q[n],\qquad
 \deg C_a\le a,\qquad C_0=1.
\end{equation}
The quotient is understood polynomially at its apparent zero denominators.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Here $c_0(n)=1$. For $a\ge1$, coefficient extraction from
\eqref{wdn:int:eq:recurrence} gives the constructive recurrence
\begin{align}
 c_a(n)-c_a(n-1)
 &=(2n-2)c_{a-1}(n-1)\notag\\
 &\quad+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\sum_{b=0}^{a-1}
                   c_b(i)c_{a-1-b}(n-1-i),\qquad c_a(0)=0.
 \label{wdn:int:eq:toprec}
\end{align}
Assume the conclusion for smaller $a$. The right side is a polynomial
of degree at most $2a-1$: summing a monomial
$i^v(n-1-i)^w$ has degree at most $v+w+1\le2a-1$.
Its discrete antiderivative with value zero at $0$ is therefore a polynomial
of degree at most $2a$.

For an integer $1\le n<a$, the first term in \eqref{wdn:int:eq:toprec} vanishes.
Both factors of a convolution summand can be nonzero only if
$i\ge b$ and $n-1-i\ge a-1-b$, which would require $n\ge a$.
Every increment at $1\le n<a$ is therefore zero. Together with $c_a(0)=0$
this proves zeros at $0,\ldots,a-1$, and hence divisibility by
$\fall n a$. The same coefficient extraction, including the zero
convention, shows that this polynomial agrees with the actual top
coefficients. The asserted degree of the quotient follows.
\end{proof}
For example $c_1(n)=(3n^2-n)/2$. The boundary zeros in this lemma are
needed for the opposite-endpoint expansion; a degree bound alone would
not justify the coefficient algorithm at small integer indices.

Part~III's Theorem~\ref{wdn:edge:thm:top} bounds the same $c_a(n)$ (its zero convention for $a>n$ is this one) uniformly in both indices: $L_a(n-2a)_+^{2a}\le c_a(n)\le L_a(n+a)^{2a}$. Since $c_a$ is a polynomial of degree at most $2a$ by Lemma~\ref{wdn:int:lem:top} and $c_a(n)/n^{2a}\to L_a$, its coefficient of $n^{2a}$ is Part~III's $L_a$. Both Parts give $c_1(n)=(3n^2-n)/2$.
\end{writenote}

\subsection{The opposite endpoint to every fixed order}
For a monomial $k^d$ and $0\le r\le d$,
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:W}
 \frac{\Delta^r k^d(m-r)}{r!}
 =\binom dr m^{d-r}W_{d,r}(m^{-1}),\qquad
 W_{d,r}(x)=\sum_{q\ge0}\binom{d-r}q\E(-r+U)^q x^q.
\end{equation}
At every fixed order its coefficients are polynomial in $d,r$.
For valid integer indices it is an exact finite sum, with a remainder
bound of the form \eqref{wdn:int:eq:productremainder}. Outside that range below,
only its polynomial continuation multiplied by a vanishing falling
factorial is used.

Put $\eps=N^{-1}$ and define formal series
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:FG}
 F_\ell(\eps)=\prod_{i=0}^{\ell-1}(1-(i+1/2)\eps)^{-1},\qquad
 G_r(x)=\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}(1-ix).
\end{equation}
Each fixed coefficient is polynomial in the indices, by logarithmic
expansion and Faulhaber sums. Their fixed-order remainders, when the
indices are logarithmic in $N$, are a fixed polynomial in $1+\ell$
times the requested power of $N^{-1}$.
Define
\begin{align}
 \beta_{j,r}&=s^{2j}\binom jr s^r(1-s)^{j-r},\label{wdn:int:eq:beta}\\
 Z_{j,r}(\eps)&=\sum_{a\ge0}(\mu\eps)^a C_a(j)
                       \fall{(j-r)}a W_{j-a,r}(\mu\eps).
 \label{wdn:int:eq:Z}
\end{align}
Only finitely many $a$ contribute at a prescribed order. The identity
\[
 c_a(j)\binom{j-a}r=C_a(j)\fall{(j-r)}a\binom jr
\]
justifies \eqref{wdn:int:eq:Z} when $j\ge a$ and $r\le j-a$.
For $j<a$ or $r>j-a$, its right side is zero at the integer indices used
in the sum, so the boundary convention is exact.

The formal expansion of the opposite endpoint is
\begin{align}
 B(\eps,s)&=\frac\eps2\sum_{j\ge1}\sum_{r=0}^j
  \beta_{j,r}F_j(\eps)G_r(\mu\eps)V_{j,r}(\mu\eps)Z_{j,r}(\eps)
   =\sum_{q\ge1}B_q(s)\eps^q.\label{wdn:int:eq:Bseries}
\end{align}
Every $B_q$ is rational in $s$. At fixed order each summand is a polynomial
in $j,r$ over $\Q(s)$, and its moments follow by Euler differentiation of
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:betasymbol}
 \sum_{j\ge1,r}\beta_{j,r}x^jy^r
       =\frac1{1-s^2x(1-s+sy)}-1.
\end{equation}
All moments converge locally uniformly for $0<s<1$. In particular
$B_1=s^2/(2t)$.

We now justify the expansion with a genuine uniform remainder. For
$j\le A\log N$, $0\le d\le j$, and sufficiently large $N$, the
nonnegative monomial Newton coefficient is at most
$\binom dr m^{d-r}$, since $0\le m-r+U\le m$ in the integral formula.
Lemma~\ref{wdn:int:lem:saddle} and the factorial ratios then give
\begin{align}
 T_{j,d}:=\frac{\LL_m(k^dz_{N-j})}{Q_{N,m}}
 &\le C(2N)^{-j}m^dt^j(1+h^{-1})^d
  =Cs^{2j}M^{d-j},\label{wdn:int:eq:monomialbound}\\
 M&=m(1+h^{-1})\ge cN.\notag
\end{align}
Coefficientwise positivity implies
\[
 0\le c_a(j)\le2j\gamma_j e_a(1,3,\ldots,2j-1)
               \le2j\gamma_j\frac{j^{2a}}{a!}.
\]
Hence losses $a\ge H$ contribute at most
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:lossbound}
 \sum_{a=H}^j c_a(j)T_{j,j-a}
 \le Cs^{2j}j\frac{(Cj^2/N)^H}{H!}\exp(Cj^2/N)
 =O\bigl(s^{2j}(1+j)^{2H+1}N^{-H}\bigr).
\end{equation}
For the finitely many retained degree losses, apply
Lemma~\ref{wdn:int:lem:saddle}, \eqref{wdn:int:eq:W}, and the finite-product expansions.
Every error is bounded by the requisite power of $N^{-1}$ times a fixed
polynomial in $j,r$ and the positive weight $\beta_{j,r}$.
All polynomial moments of $s^{2j}$ are uniformly summable on a compact
$s$ interval, so summation loses no logarithm. The extra factor $1/(2N)$
in \eqref{wdn:int:eq:Bseries} is retained. Lemma~\ref{wdn:int:lem:middle} disposes of
$j>A\log N$. Thus for every fixed $K$,
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:Bexpansion}
 B_{N,m}=\sum_{q=1}^{K-1}B_q(s)N^{-q}+O_{J,K}(N^{-K})
\end{equation}
on every compact interior ratio range.

\subsection{The principal endpoint}
Combining \eqref{wdn:int:eq:Newtonintegral}, \eqref{wdn:int:eq:V}, \eqref{wdn:int:eq:FG}, and
\eqref{wdn:int:eq:Q} gives, uniformly for $\ell\le A\log N$,
\begin{align}
 a_{N,m,\ell,r}
 &=a_s(\ell,r)\left(\sum_{q=0}^{K-1}A_q(\ell,r;s)N^{-q}
       +O\bigl(N^{-K}(1+\ell)^{M_K}\bigr)\right),\label{wdn:int:eq:Aexpansion}\\
 a_s(\ell,r)&=\gamma_\ell s^{2\ell}\binom\ell r s^r(1-s)^{\ell-r},
 \label{wdn:int:eq:leadingkernel}\\
 A_{\ell,r}(\eps,s)&=(1-\ell\eps)F_\ell(\eps)
 G_r(\mu\eps)U_{\ell,r}(\mu\eps)V_{\ell,r}(\mu\eps)
     =\sum_{q\ge0}A_q(\ell,r;s)\eps^q.\label{wdn:int:eq:Aseries}
\end{align}
All $A_q$ are in $\Q(s)[\ell,r]$ and $A_0=1$. The normalization includes
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:zerocancellation}
 A_{0,0}(\eps,s)=1,\qquad A_q(0,0;s)=0\quad(q\ge1).
\end{equation}
The remainder in \eqref{wdn:int:eq:Aexpansion} is relative to the positive
$a_s$. Its polynomially weighted sums are uniformly finite, since
$\sum_r a_s(\ell,r)=\gamma_\ell s^{2\ell}$.

\section{The exact coefficient algorithm and rationality}\label{wdn:int:sec:algorithm}
Let $H(\eps,\alpha)=\sum_{q\ge0}R_q(\alpha)\eps^q$.
Insert the endpoint series into the formal equation
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:formalH}
 H(\eps,\alpha)=1-B(\eps,\alpha)
 -\sum_{\ell\ge1,r}a_s(\ell,r)A_{\ell,r}(\eps,s)
 H\left(\frac\eps{1-\ell\eps},
          \frac{\alpha-r\eps}{1-\ell\eps}\right).
\end{equation}
Every coefficient uses finitely many polynomial moments. The leading mass
is
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:mass}
 q_0(s)=\sum_{\ell\ge1,r}a_s(\ell,r)=t^{-1/2}-1.
\end{equation}
Thus $(1+q_0)R_0=1$, giving $R_0=\sqrt t$. At order $q\ge1$, the only
new unknown on the right is $-q_0R_q$. If $P_q^{\rm known}$ denotes
the known order-$q$ principal sum after omitting that term, then
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:Rrec}
 R_q=-\sqrt t\bigl(B_q+P_q^{\rm known}\bigr).
\end{equation}
All other terms use only $R_0,\ldots,R_{q-1}$ and finitely many
$\alpha$ derivatives. The conversion is
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:alphaderivative}
 \partial_\alpha=\frac{(1+s)^2}{2s(s+2)}\partial_s.
\end{equation}
This constructs unique analytic coefficient functions on the whole
interior: all series moments converge locally uniformly and all
necessary denominators $B,t,\alpha,1+q_0$ are positive there.

\begin{proposition}[The zero index removes the radical obstruction]
Every $R_q$ constructed by \eqref{wdn:int:eq:Rrec} belongs to $\sqrt t\,\Q(s)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Suppose this holds for smaller indices. Every $\alpha$ derivative of
$\sqrt t$ times a rational function has the same form. Divide the known
principal residual summand by $\sqrt t$; it becomes a polynomial
$P_q(\ell,r;s)\in\Q(s)[\ell,r]$. At $\ell=r=0$ there is no kernel
correction by \eqref{wdn:int:eq:zerocancellation}, no argument shift, and no
denominator shift. Because the unknown $R_q$ contribution has been
omitted, we have
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:Pzero}
 P_q(0,0;s)=0.
\end{equation}
All moments including $\ell=0$ are Euler derivatives of
\[
 \mathcal G(x,y)=(1-s^2x(1-s+sy))^{-1/2}.
\]
Evaluated at $x=y=1$, each derivative is $t^{-1/2}$ times a rational
function of $s$. Equation~\eqref{wdn:int:eq:Pzero} means that removing $\ell=0$
changes nothing. The principal residual, including its factor $\sqrt t$,
is therefore rational in $s$. The opposite residual $B_q$ is rational
by \eqref{wdn:int:eq:betasymbol}. Equation~\eqref{wdn:int:eq:Rrec} proves the result.
Without \eqref{wdn:int:eq:Pzero}, the removal of the zero index would introduce
an extra radical term, so this cancellation is essential.
\end{proof}

For clarity, the complete finite algorithm at a requested order is:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Form the necessary cumulants and Gaussian coefficients using
\eqref{wdn:int:eq:cumulants}, \eqref{wdn:int:eq:Gaussianexp}, and \eqref{wdn:int:eq:Gaussianmoments};
then divide the two truncated series in \eqref{wdn:int:eq:V}.
\item Generate $U_{\ell,r}$ by Newton identities and uniform-sum moments,
and generate $F_\ell,G_r$ by their finite-product logarithms.
\item Compute the finitely many $C_a$ needed by the discrete
antiderivative recurrence \eqref{wdn:int:eq:toprec}, and form \eqref{wdn:int:eq:Z}.
\item Compute the required principal and opposite moments by Euler
derivatives of $\mathcal G$ and \eqref{wdn:int:eq:betasymbol}.
\item Extract successive coefficients of \eqref{wdn:int:eq:formalH}, dividing
only by $1+q_0=t^{-1/2}$ as in \eqref{wdn:int:eq:Rrec}.
\end{enumerate}
All these operations are exact rational-function algebra and finite
series operations. The coefficients are regular in the full interior;
any apparent rational pole there is removable by the analytic construction.

\section{The uniform remainder on the full interior}\label{wdn:int:sec:fullrange}
The principal mass $q_0$ need not be less than one. We instead use an
exact inverse for a frozen convolution, with an explicit correction for
neighboring saddle changes.

\subsection{The frozen inverse and its moments}
For a fixed $s\in(0,1)$ define operators on bounded functions on $\Z^2$:
\begin{align}
 (\mathcal A_s E)(N,m)
 &=\sum_{\ell\ge1}\sum_{r=0}^{\ell}a_s(\ell,r)E(N-\ell,m-r),
 \label{wdn:int:eq:Aoperator}\\
 (\mathcal B_s E)(N,m)
 &=\sum_{\ell\ge1}\sum_{r=0}^{\ell}b_s(\ell,r)E(N-\ell,m-r),\qquad
 b_s(\ell,r)=\frac{a_s(\ell,r)}{2\ell-1}.
 \label{wdn:int:eq:Boperator}
\end{align}
These $b_s$ are inverse-kernel weights, distinct from the diagonal
coefficients $b_j$. Their two-variable symbols satisfy
\begin{gather}
 1+A_s(u,v)=[1-s^2u(1-s+sv)]^{-1/2},\label{wdn:int:eq:Asymbol}\\
 B_s(u,v)=1-[1-s^2u(1-s+sv)]^{1/2},\label{wdn:int:eq:Bsymbol}\\
 (I-\mathcal B_s)(I+\mathcal A_s)=I.\label{wdn:int:eq:inverseoperator}
\end{gather}
The orientation and signs follow by multiplication of the displayed
symbols; shifts compose as $T_{\beta}T_{\eta}=T_{\beta+\eta}$. Both
kernels are nonnegative, and
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:kernelmasses}
 \|\mathcal A_s\|_1=t^{-1/2}-1,\qquad
 \|\mathcal B_s\|_1=1-\sqrt t<1.
\end{equation}
For bounded $E$, the double sum in the product is dominated by
$\|E\|_\infty\|\mathcal A_s\|_1\|\mathcal B_s\|_1$.
Consequently \eqref{wdn:int:eq:inverseoperator} is an absolutely convergent
identity on the entire lattice, not merely a formal one. Its inverse
norm is at most $1+\|\mathcal B_s\|_1<2$.

On any compact $S\subset(0,1)$ every polynomial moment of both kernels
is uniformly finite. Their first $s$ derivatives also have exponentially
decreasing, polynomially weighted tails. Indeed
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:kernelderivative}
 \partial_s\log a_s(\ell,r)
 =\partial_s\log b_s(\ell,r)
 =\frac{2\ell+r}{s}-\frac{\ell-r}{1-s},
\end{equation}
so $|\partial_s a_s|\le C_S\ell a_s$ and similarly for $b_s$.
Summing in $r$ leaves $\gamma_\ell s^{2\ell}$ or
$\gamma_\ell s^{2\ell}/(2\ell-1)$, both geometrically decreasing
uniformly on $S$. In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:Lipschitz}
 \|\mathcal A_s-\mathcal A_{s'}\|_1\le C_S|s-s'|
\end{equation}
when the intervening interval lies in $S$.

\subsection{A bounded extension and its local residual}
Fix $K$. Choose a compact interval $J_*$ strictly inside $(0,1)$,
containing the desired ratio range and all the nested intervals to be
used below, with additional margin. Put
\[
 H_K(N,m)=\sum_{j=0}^{K-1}R_j(m/N)N^{-j}.
\]
Extend the finitely many coefficient functions smoothly and boundedly
to $[0,1]$, agreeing with their true values on a neighborhood of $J_*$.
At valid states set $E_{N,m}=R_{N,m}-H_K(N,m)$; at every other lattice
state, including $N\le0$, set $E=0$. Equation~\eqref{wdn:int:eq:bounded} shows
that this is a bounded function on $\Z^2$.

The local expansions, the coefficient cancellations in
\eqref{wdn:int:eq:formalH}, and Lemma~\ref{wdn:int:lem:middle} prove the uniform residual
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:localresidual}
 E(x)+(\mathcal K_xE)(x)=\rho(x),\qquad
 |\rho(x)|\le C N^{-K},\qquad x=(N,m),\quad m/N\in J_*.
\end{equation}
Here $\mathcal K_x$ is the finite exact kernel
\eqref{wdn:int:eq:exactkernel}. To justify this subtraction independently of any
remainder theorem, use Taylor expansion only for jumps at most
$A\log N$, whose ratios remain within the extra compact margin. All
coefficient-function derivatives are uniformly bounded there. Their
remainders are a fixed polynomial in the jump times $N^{-K}$, summable
against $a_s$. The long exact jumps act on bounded extensions and are
$O(N^{-K})$ by \eqref{wdn:int:eq:middle}; the formal tails have the same property
by geometric decay. Equation~\eqref{wdn:int:eq:Bexpansion} supplies the opposite
residual. Inadmissible zero-kernel terms are omitted before evaluating
$H_K$. No equation outside the local ratio domain is asserted.

Let $L_N=\lfloor A\log N\rfloor$, with $A$ sufficiently large for all
fixed-order tail bounds. From \eqref{wdn:int:eq:Aexpansion},
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:perturbation}
 |a_{N,m,\ell,r}-a_{s(x)}(\ell,r)|
 \le C N^{-1}(1+\ell)^D a_{s(x)}(\ell,r)
 \quad(\ell\le L_N).
\end{equation}
The exact and frozen tails acting on bounded $E$ are $O(N^{-K})$.
Writing $\mathcal D_x$ for $\mathcal A_{s(x)}-\mathcal K_x$ restricted
to these short shifts, we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:naturalfrozen}
 ((I+\mathcal A_{s(x)})E)(x)
   =\rho(x)+(\mathcal D_xE)(x)+O(N^{-K}).
\end{equation}
The sign of this perturbation is fixed by adding
$\mathcal A_{s(x)}-\mathcal K_x$ to \eqref{wdn:int:eq:localresidual}.

\subsection{A one power improvement}
\begin{lemma}\label{wdn:int:lem:improvement}
Let $J\subset\operatorname{int}J'\subset(0,1)$ be compact intervals,
with a further margin for the residual estimates. If, for an integer
$p\ge0$, the same error $E=R-H_K$ satisfies
\[
 |E_{N,m}|\le C_pN^{-p}\qquad(m/N\in J'),
\]
then it satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:improvement}
 |E_{N,m}|\le C'_pN^{-\min(K,p+1)}\qquad(m/N\in J).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Fix $x=(N,m)$ in the smaller range and freeze $s_0=s(x)$ for the
entire following convolution. Define, at \emph{every} lattice state $y$,
\[
 F(y)=((I+\mathcal A_{s_0})E)(y).
\]
This function is globally bounded, uniformly for $x$ in the fixed compact
range. The exact identity \eqref{wdn:int:eq:inverseoperator} gives
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:frozenatbase}
 E(x)=F(x)-\sum_{b\ge1}\sum_{c=0}^b
                       b_{s_0}(b,c)F(x-(b,c)).
\end{equation}
The inverse tail $b>L_N$ is $O(N^{-K})$ against this bounded function.
For a retained shift $\beta=(b,c)$, including $(0,0)$, set
$y=x-\beta$ and $s_y=s(y)$. Its size is $N_y=N-O(\log N)$ and
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:drift}
 |\alpha_y-\alpha_x|
 =\frac{|\alpha_xb-c|}{N-b}\le C\frac bN,
 \qquad |s_y-s_0|\le C\frac bN.
\end{equation}
For sufficiently large $N$, $y$ and every state reached by a second short
shift remain in $J'$ and have size comparable to $N$.

The natural neighboring equation has parameter $s_y$, not $s_0$.
Retain the difference explicitly:
\begin{align}
 F(y)
 &=((I+\mathcal A_{s_y})E)(y)
                  +((\mathcal A_{s_0}-\mathcal A_{s_y})E)(y)\notag\\
 &=O(N^{-K})+(\mathcal D_yE)(y)
                  +((\mathcal A_{s_0}-\mathcal A_{s_y})E)(y).
 \label{wdn:int:eq:neighbor}
\end{align}
The first short perturbation is $O(N^{-p-1})$ by
\eqref{wdn:int:eq:perturbation}, the induction hypothesis on all short
predecessors, and the finite weighted moments of $a_{s_y}$.

For the parameter-difference term, first discard shifts $\ell>L_N$
in both frozen kernels against globally bounded $E$, at cost
$O(N^{-K})$. On the retained shifts the induction hypothesis applies.
The mean value theorem, \eqref{wdn:int:eq:kernelderivative}, and
\eqref{wdn:int:eq:drift} give
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:neighborbound}
 |((\mathcal A_{s_0}-\mathcal A_{s_y})E)(y)|
 \le C N^{-p}|s_0-s_y|+O(N^{-K})
 \le CbN^{-p-1}+O(N^{-K}).
\end{equation}
The derivative norm is uniform on the full interval between the two
saddles. There is no logarithmic loss in this estimate.
Therefore each retained $F(y)$ is bounded by
\[
 C\{N^{-K}+(1+b)N^{-p-1}\}.
\]
Insert this in \eqref{wdn:int:eq:frozenatbase}. The mass and first $b$ moment of
$\mathcal B_{s_0}$ are uniformly finite, while the discarded inverse tail
is $O(N^{-K})$. This gives
$|E(x)|\le C(N^{-K}+N^{-p-1})$, proving the lemma.
\end{proof}
The proof applies a translation-invariant inverse only after freezing
the parameter at the base state. It neither substitutes the wrong saddle
in a neighboring equation nor takes absolute values of an unproved
signed cancellation.

\subsection{Finite nested compact bootstrap}
Choose $K+1$ compact intervals
\[
 J_0\subset\operatorname{int}J_1\subset\cdots
     \subset\operatorname{int}J_K\subset(0,1),
\]
with the desired compact ratio set inside $J_0$ and the earlier residual
margin outside $J_K$. Use the same $H_K$ and the same bounded extension
throughout. Initially $E=O(1)$ on $J_K$. Apply
Lemma~\ref{wdn:int:lem:improvement} to obtain $O(N^{-1})$ on $J_{K-1}$,
then $O(N^{-2})$ on $J_{K-2}$, and so on, ending with
$E=O(N^{-K})$ on $J_0$. This proves Theorem~\ref{wdn:int:thm:allorders}.
There are only $K$ steps, independent of $N$; no scaled error bound has
been assumed in advance.

Finally, uniqueness as an asymptotic coefficient family follows by
comparing two continuous families successively along integer pairs whose
ratio tends to any prescribed interior value. At the first potentially
different order, multiply by the corresponding power of $N$ and pass
to the limit on a compact neighborhood. The two coefficient values
agree. This proves uniqueness on the whole interior, with no appeal to
a contraction subinterval.

\section{Diagonal normalization and the second correction}\label{wdn:int:sec:diagonal}
On the diagonal $N=2n$, $m=n$, the saddle is
$s_0=(1+\sqrt{17})/8$. From \eqref{wdn:int:eq:Q} and
Theorem~\ref{wdn:int:thm:allorders},
\[
 a_n=4n\frac{(4n-1)!!}{n!}\,
 \frac{f(t)h^nt^{-2n}}{\sqrt{2\pi nB}}\,\sqrt t
 \left(\sum_{j\ge0}D_j(0,0;s_0)n^{-j}\right)
 \left(\sum_{j\ge0}\frac{r_j(s_0)}{(2n)^j}\right),
\]
where each series is truncated at the desired fixed order with its proved
remainder. The factorial normalization is
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:factorialnormal}
 \frac{(4n-1)!!}{(n!)^2}
 =\frac{16^n}{\pi\sqrt2\,n}\,\mathcal S(n^{-1}),\qquad
 \mathcal S(x)=1-\frac3{16}x+\frac9{512}x^2+\cdots.
\end{equation}
The logarithmic series of $\mathcal S$ has coefficient
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:Stirlingseries}
 \frac{\mathsf B_{2v}}{2v(2v-1)}
 \left(4^{-(2v-1)}-2^{-(2v-1)}-2\right)
\end{equation}
at $x^{2v-1}$ and zero at positive even powers, where
$\mathsf B_{2v}$ denotes a Bernoulli number. Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:diagonalalgorithm}
 \sum_{j\ge0}b_jx^j
 =\mathcal S(x)
  \left(\sum_{j\ge0}D_j(0,0;s_0)x^j\right)
  \left(\sum_{j\ge0}r_j(s_0)(x/2)^j\right).
\end{equation}
All its coefficients are in $\Q(s_0)=\Q(\sqrt{17})$.
The leading exponential and amplitude simplify to
\[
 d=\frac{16h}{t^2},\qquad
 C=\frac{2\sqrt t\,f(t)}{\pi^{3/2}\sqrt B},
\]
at $s=s_0$, which are precisely \eqref{wdn:int:eq:constants}.
Finite-order products of the established remainders prove
Corollary~\ref{wdn:int:cor:diagonal}.

For explicit second-order reproduction the algorithm yields
\begin{align}
 r_1(s)&=\frac{s^4+6s^3+7s^2+3s+4}
                    {4(s-1)(s+1)(s+2)^2},\label{wdn:int:eq:r1}\\
 D_1(0,0;s)&=\frac{s^6-7s^5-5s^4+32s^3+32s^2-9s-8}
                    {12(s-1)(s+1)(s+2)^3},\label{wdn:int:eq:D1}\\
 B_2(s)&=\frac{s^4+9s^3+13s^2+3s+4}{4t^2(s+2)^2}.
 \label{wdn:int:eq:B2}
\end{align}
To keep the higher numerators legible, write
\begin{align}
 P_2(s)={}&s^{10}+14s^9+22s^8-218s^7-1295s^6-3068s^5\notag\\
          &-3675s^4-2402s^3-1160s^2-500s-4,\label{wdn:int:eq:P2}\\
 T_2(s)={}&s^{12}-14s^{11}+759s^{10}+1574s^9-1079s^8-4386s^7\notag\\
          &-1346s^6+4410s^5+4128s^4-368s^3-1871s^2-576s+64.
 \label{wdn:int:eq:T2}
\end{align}
Then
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:r2D2}
 r_2(s)=\frac{P_2(s)}{32s\,t^2(s+2)^5},\qquad
 D_2(0,0;s)=\frac{T_2(s)}{288t^2(s+2)^6}.
\end{equation}
These formulas can be checked directly from the finite algorithm without
inserting the proposed answers. One convenient Gaussian implementation
uses $\exp(\sum_{j\ge1}F_jz^j)=\sum_{n\ge0}E_nz^n$ with
\[
 F_j=\frac{g_j-r\kappa_j+\ell\mathbf1_{j=1}}{j!}(iu)^j
       +\frac{\kappa_{j+2}}{(j+2)!}(iu)^{j+2},\qquad
 E_0=1,\quad E_n=\frac1n\sum_{j=1}^n jF_jE_{n-j}.
\]
Apply \eqref{wdn:int:eq:Gaussianmoments} to $E_2,E_4$ and then divide by the
unshifted series. This independently generates $D_1,D_2$ and the ratio
coefficients needed in \eqref{wdn:int:eq:Aseries}.

For a direct check of the residual bookkeeping, set
$\delta=\alpha\ell-r$, $g=(\partial_\alpha\sqrt t)/\sqrt t$, and
\[
 \mathcal M(P)=\sqrt t\sum_{\ell\ge1,r}a_s(\ell,r)P(\ell,r).
\]
The known shifted terms, divided by the current $R_0$, are
\begin{align*}
 H_1&=r_1+g\delta,\\
 H_2&=\ell r_1+\delta(\partial_\alpha r_1+gr_1)
       +\ell g\delta+\frac{\delta^2}{2}(\partial_\alpha g+g^2).
\end{align*}
Thus the first two residual equations are
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:explicitresiduals}
 r_1=-\mathcal M(A_1+g\delta)-B_1,\qquad
 r_2=-\mathcal M(H_2+A_1H_1+A_2)-B_2.
\end{equation}
This retains the derivatives of both $R_0$ and $r_1$, the change in
$1/(N-\ell)$, the cross term, and the opposite endpoint.

Finally, the second diagonal coefficient is
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:b2product}
 b_2=\left[
 \frac9{512}+D_2+\frac{r_2}{4}-\frac3{16}D_1
       +\left(-\frac3{16}+D_1\right)\frac{r_1}{2}
 \right]_{s=s_0},
\end{equation}
which simplifies to \eqref{wdn:int:eq:b2}. Its decimal value is
$-0.583039199664459265278390709755\ldots$.
The saddle coefficients are in powers of $m^{-1}$, whereas $r_j$ are
in powers of $N^{-1}$; the factors $1/2$ and $1/4$ on the diagonal are
therefore necessary. The companion regenerates these identities exactly.

\section{All orders of the integer safe inverse}\label{wdn:int:sec:inverse}
Set
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:Phi}
 c=\log(C\sqrt{2\pi}),\qquad
 \Phi(x)=x(\log(dx)-1)+c.
\end{equation}
Define $\lambda_j$ by the formal identity
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:lambda}
 \sum_{j\ge1}\lambda_jx^{-j}
 =\log\left(\sum_{j\ge0}b_jx^{-j}\right)
       +\sum_{v\ge1}\frac{\mathsf B_{2v}}{2v(2v-1)}x^{-(2v-1)}.
\end{equation}
Each $\lambda_j\in\Q(\sqrt{17})$; specifically,
\begin{align}
 \lambda_1&=b_1+\frac1{12}
       =-\frac{2423}{6528}-\frac{907\sqrt{17}}{36992},\label{wdn:int:eq:lambda1}\\
 \lambda_2&=b_2-\frac{b_1^2}{2}
       =-\frac{4940681+601183\sqrt{17}}{10061824}.
 \label{wdn:int:eq:lambda2}
\end{align}
The forward theorem and Stirling's expansion imply, for every fixed $K$,
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:loginteger}
 \log a_n=\Phi(n)+\sum_{j=1}^{K-1}\lambda_jn^{-j}+O(n^{-K}).
\end{equation}
The $\tfrac12\log n$ from $\log n!$ cancels the original
$n^{-1/2}$ factor.

For large $X$ put $L=\log X$, $T=L-c$, and
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:x0}
 x_0=\frac{T}{W(dT/e)},\qquad
 \Psi_K(x)=\Phi(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{K-1}\lambda_jx^{-j},
\end{equation}
using the positive real Lambert $W$ branch. Thus $\Phi(x_0)=L$ and
$x_0\sim\log X/\log\log X$.

\begin{theorem}[All orders with the uncertainty inside the ceilings]\label{wdn:int:thm:inverse}
For every fixed $K\ge1$, the equation $\Psi_K(x)=L$ has a unique root
$x_K$ in $[x_0/2,2x_0]$ for sufficiently large $X$, and
$x_K-x_0=O((x_0\log x_0)^{-1})$. There is a constant $M_K>0$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:ceilingbracket}
 \left\lceil x_K-\frac{M_K}{x_0^K\log(dx_0)}\right\rceil
 \le N(X):=\min\{n:a_n\ge X\}
 \le\left\lceil x_K+\frac{M_K}{x_0^K\log(dx_0)}\right\rceil
\end{equation}
for all sufficiently large $X$.
Starting at $y_0=x_0$, finitely many explicit Newton steps
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:Newtoninverse}
 y_{v+1}=y_v-\frac{\Psi_K(y_v)-L}{\Psi_K'(y_v)}
\end{equation}
may replace $x_K$ in \eqref{wdn:int:eq:ceilingbracket}, after increasing $M_K$,
whenever $2^{v+1}-1\ge K$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
On $[x_0/2,2x_0]$, $\Psi_K'(x)\sim\log x_0$ uniformly, and its endpoints
bracket $L$ for large $X$. This proves existence and uniqueness there.
The value $\Psi_K(x_0)-L$ is $O(x_0^{-1})$, giving the claimed root
displacement by the mean value theorem.

No interpolation of the sequence is needed for integer rounding.
For integers in this interval, \eqref{wdn:int:eq:loginteger} has absolute error
at most $C_Kx_0^{-K}$. Moving a real argument by
$M_Kx_0^{-K}/\log(dx_0)$ changes $\Psi_K$ by more than this error when
$M_K$ is large enough. Every integer below the lower witness in
\eqref{wdn:int:eq:ceilingbracket} therefore has $a_n<X$, while the ceiling of the
upper witness has $a_n\ge X$. The forward equivalent gives
$a_{n+1}/a_n\sim dn$, so the sequence is eventually strictly increasing.
All earlier integers, including the finite initial prefix, are below
$X$ when $X$ is sufficiently large. These observations prove the bracket.

Throughout a neighborhood of $x_0$,
$|\Psi_K''/\Psi_K'|=O((x_0\log x_0)^{-1})$.
The initial error is $O((x_0\log x_0)^{-1})$, and the Newton error
recurrence therefore gives, for each fixed $v$,
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:int:eq:Newtonerror}
 |y_v-x_K|=O\left((x_0\log x_0)^{-(2^{v+1}-1)}\right).
\end{equation}
These iterates remain in the required neighborhood for sufficiently large
$X$. If $2^{v+1}-1\ge K$, their error is
$O(x_0^{-K}/\log x_0)$ and is absorbed by increasing the witness constant.
For example, $v=\lceil\log_2(K+1)\rceil$ always suffices.
\end{proof}
The constants and onset here are existence statements inherited from the
asymptotic remainder, not effective numerical certificates. A real error
tending to zero cannot be removed from the ceilings: arbitrarily close
approach to an integer prevents an unrounded $o(1)$ formula for the integer
threshold from following from these estimates.

\begin{partabstract}
We study the upper edge $m=N-q$ of the falling-factorial triangle
$P_{N,m}=[\fall{k}{m}]p_N(k)$ defined by the nonlinear recurrence
$p_0=1$ and
$p_N=(k+2N-2)p_{N-1}+\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}p_ip_{N-1-i}$.
This is the weighted-Dyck triangle A258220, whose proportional diagonal
$P_{2n,n}$ is A292692. A simultaneous comparison for every top-coefficient
index $a$ and size $n$ gives exact positive finite-index sandwiches for
$P_{N,N-q}$ and its hypergeometric auxiliary $Q_{N,N-q}$.
Consequently the normalized connected ratio has a profile $F_q$ uniformly
for every growing deficit $q=o(\sqrt N)$, with explicit exponential errors.
The profile is a binomial smoothing of the classical four-regular-map
sequence A292186. We prove
$F_q=2\sqrt{q/3}\{1-7/(16q)+O(q^{-2})\}$ with controlled convolution
and binomial tails. The first correction is resolved by the joint bound
when $q=o(N^{1/3})$. Finite rational recursions give every fixed order
of the profile, with proved remainders, exact monotonicity, and a
ceiling-safe threshold inverse. Classical map and generalized-Stirling asymptotics
are credited separately from the nonlinear comparison. All boundary
conventions and remainder ranges are explicit; finite computations are
provided as reproducible checks rather than substitutes for proof.
\end{partabstract}
\section{The model and the contribution}
A Dyck path of semilength $N$ has up-steps $(1,1)$ and down-steps $(1,-1)$,
starts at $(0,0)$, ends at $(2N,0)$, and stays at nonnegative height.
Give each peak at coordinates $(a,b)$ the weight $k+a/b$ and multiply
the peak weights of a path. Their sum is the polynomial $p_N(k)$ of
OEIS A258219~\cite{A258219}. Report~141~\cite{foundation} proves the exact
weighted-prefix identity that yields the recurrence
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:recurrence}
 p_0(k)=1,\qquad
 p_N(k)=(k+2N-2)p_{N-1}(k)
       +\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}p_i(k)p_{N-1-i}(k)\quad(N\ge1).
\end{equation}
The present article takes \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:recurrence} as its starting definition,
and proves its upper-edge results directly from it. An unchanged copy of
Report~141 accompanies the source archive for the path interpretation.
No analytic estimate from that report is needed in the edge proof.

For a polynomial $f$, use the falling-factorial normalization
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:newton}
 [\fall{k}{m}]f(k)=\frac{\Delta^m f(0)}{m!},\qquad
 \fall{k}{m}=k(k-1)\cdots(k-m+1),\qquad \fall{k}{0}=1.
\end{equation}
Define
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:PQ}
 \begin{split}
 P_{N,m}&=[\fall{k}{m}]p_N(k),\\
 \gamma_N&=4^{-N}\binom{2N}{N},\qquad
 z_N(k)=\gamma_N\prod_{i=0}^{N-1}(k+2i+1),\\
 Q_{N,m}&=[\fall{k}{m}]z_N(k),\qquad
 R_{N,m}=\frac{P_{N,m}}{2NQ_{N,m}}\quad(N\ge1).
 \end{split}
\end{equation}
Empty products are one. The triangle $P_{N,m}$ is A258220~\cite{A258220}.
Its Newton diagonal $P_{2n,n}$ is A292692~\cite{A292692}; the ordinary
parameter diagonal $p_n(n)$ is the different sequence A292693.
Throughout this article the integer $q=N-m$ denotes the \emph{deficit}.
In particular the growing upper edge has $m/N\to1$; it is not the fixed
ratio $m/N=1/2$ of A292692.

The principal result is an all-$a,n$ nonlinear coefficient comparison,
followed by exact $P/Q$ sandwiches and a genuinely joint limiting theorem.
It is stronger than proving a polynomial leading term separately for each
fixed deficit and then taking a second limit of its coefficient: such
iterated limits give no control over the error as $q$ grows with $N$.

\subsection{Prior work and claim boundary}
The sequence that determines our edge constants is classical A292186,
the rooted connected four-regular-map sequence~\cite{A292186,CK}.
Its recurrence belongs to the Martin--Kearney self-convolutive family
$S(4,-6,1)$ with an index shift~\cite{MK}; its Riccati equation,
hypergeometric logarithmic derivative, and factorial leading asymptotic
are prior art. We rederive the quantitative estimates needed below to
make the proof self-contained and fix the normalization.

The auxiliary coefficients are classical generalized Stirling numbers.
In the Hsu--Shiue convention,
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:HS}
 \frac{Q_{N,m}}{\gamma_N}=S(N,m;-2,1,1).
\end{equation}
Indeed their connection identity between generalized factorials, with
$z=k+1$, becomes
$\prod_{i=0}^{N-1}(k+2i+1)=\sum_m S(N,m;-2,1,1)\fall{k}{m}$.
Hsu--Shiue's Theorem~5 and fixed-$r$ specialization~\cite[Section~5.2]{HS}
cover a near-diagonal asymptotic regime with deficit $o(\sqrt m)$.
Thus the leading $Q$ estimate by itself is not a new asymptotic phenomenon.
Ordinary Stirling estimates in this range have a long history; effective
error bounds and discussion of the Moser--Wyman expansions appear in
Arratia--DeSalvo~\cite{AD}. Neither a single factorial connection
polynomial nor a fixed-parameter self-convolutive asymptotic supplies the
nonlinear all-$a,n$ comparison proved here.

A bounded primary-source check did not locate that comparison or an
equivalent quantified growing-deficit theorem for \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:recurrence}.
This article makes no exhaustive worldwide priority claim. Its substantive
content is the explicit simultaneous comparison and its consequences;
the classical sequence and the auxiliary asymptotic are identified as such.

\section{Exact bounds and the connected profile}\label{wdn:edge:sec:results}
Define positive rational constants $L_a$ by
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:L}
 L_0=1,\qquad
 L_a=\frac{L_{a-1}}a+
 \sum_{b+c=a-1}L_bL_c\frac{(2b)!(2c)!}{(2a)!}\quad(a\ge1).
\end{equation}
Then $V_a=(2a)!L_a$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:V}
 V_0=1,\qquad
 V_a=(4a-2)V_{a-1}+\sum_{b+c=a-1}V_bV_c,
\end{equation}
so $V_a$ begins $1,3,24,297,4896,100278,\ldots$ and is A292186.
Set
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:profile}
 K_q=\sum_{a=0}^{q}\frac{L_a}{2^{q-a}(q-a)!},\qquad
 C_q=\frac{(3/2)^q}{q!},\qquad
 F_q=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2}\frac{K_q}{C_q}.
\end{equation}
For example,
\[
 K_0=1,\qquad K_1=2,\qquad K_2=\frac{15}{8},\qquad
 K_3=\frac{269}{240},\qquad K_4=\frac{6539}{13440}.
\]

\begin{theorem}[Simultaneous top-coefficient comparison]\label{wdn:edge:thm:top}
Let $c_a(n)=[k^{n-a}]p_n(k)$ for $0\le a\le n$, and set $c_a(n)=0$
for $a>n$. For every pair of integers $a\ge1$ and $n\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:top}
 L_a(n-2a)_+^{2a}\ \le\ c_a(n)\ \le\ L_a(n+a)^{2a},
 \qquad x_+=\max(x,0).
\end{equation}
For $a=0$, both bounding quantities are defined separately to be one,
and $c_0(n)=1$ exactly for every $n\ge0$.
\end{theorem}
This is one assertion quantified over both indices. Its proof does not
use a fixed-$a$ asymptotic remainder with an unspecified dependence on $a$.

\begin{writenote}[Relation to Part~II, 5~October 2026]
Part~II's Lemma~\ref{wdn:int:lem:top} proves that $c_a(n)$ is, for each fixed $a$, a polynomial in $n$ of degree at most $2a$ divisible by $\fall na$; with the theorem above its leading coefficient is $L_a$. The two results are independent and complementary: polynomiality at fixed $a$ (used by Part~II's opposite endpoint) and bounds uniform in $a$ (used here).
\end{writenote}

\begin{theorem}[Exact joint sandwiches]\label{wdn:edge:thm:sandwich}
For every integer $N\ge1$ and $1\le q\le\lfloor N/2\rfloor$,
\begin{align}
 K_q(N-2q)^{2q}&\le P_{N,N-q}\le K_q(N+q)^{2q},\label{wdn:edge:eq:Psand}\\
 \gamma_N C_q(N-2q)^{2q}&\le Q_{N,N-q}
                       \le\gamma_N C_qN^{2q}.\label{wdn:edge:eq:Qsand}
\end{align}
At $q=0$ the separate exact statements are $P_{N,N}=1$ and
$Q_{N,N}=\gamma_N$. When $N=2q\ge2$, the lower bounds are zero with
strictly positive exponent. No expression $0^0$ is used.
\end{theorem}

For $q=0$ define $A_{N,0}=B_{N,0}=1$. For $q\ge1$ put
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:ABd}
 A_{N,q}=\left(1-\frac{2q}{N}\right)^{2q},\qquad
 B_{N,q}=\left(1+\frac qN\right)^{2q},\qquad
 d_N=\frac1{\sqrt{\pi N}\gamma_N}.
\end{equation}
\begin{corollary}[Explicit relative errors]\label{wdn:edge:cor:uniform}
For $0\le q<N/2$,
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:ratioexact}
 d_N A_{N,q}\ \le\ \frac{\sqrt N R_{N,N-q}}{F_q}
                 \ \le\ d_N\frac{B_{N,q}}{A_{N,q}}.
\end{equation}
For $0\le q\le N/4$ this implies
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:exponential}
 \exp\left(-\frac{8q^2}{N}\right)
 \le\frac{\sqrt N R_{N,N-q}}{F_q}
 \le\exp\left(\frac{10q^2+1/8}{N}\right).
\end{equation}
Consequently, simultaneously along every integer sequence
$q=q(N)\ge0$ with $q=o(\sqrt N)$,
\begin{align}
 P_{N,N-q}&=K_qN^{2q}\{1+O(q^2/N)\},\label{wdn:edge:eq:Puniform}\\
 Q_{N,N-q}&=\gamma_N C_qN^{2q}\{1+O(q^2/N)\},\label{wdn:edge:eq:Quniform}\\
 \sqrt N R_{N,N-q}&=F_q\{1+O((q+1)^2/N)\}.\label{wdn:edge:eq:Runiform}
\end{align}
The first two statements are exact at $q=0$, interpreting their leading
scales as one and $\gamma_N$. The implied constants can be chosen absolute
on, for example, $(q+1)^2/N\le1$. More precisely the absolute relative
error in \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:Runiform} is at most
$\exp((10q^2+1/8)/N)-1$ whenever $q\le N/4$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{theorem}[First profile correction and overlap]\label{wdn:edge:thm:matching}
As $q\to\infty$,
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:Ffirst}
 F_q=2\sqrt{q/3}\left\{1-\frac7{16q}+O(q^{-2})\right\}.
\end{equation}
Hence, jointly for $q\to\infty$ and $q=o(\sqrt N)$,
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:Rfirst}
 R_{N,N-q}=2\sqrt{\frac q{3N}}
 \left\{1-\frac7{16q}
 +O\left(q^{-2}+\frac{(q+1)^2}{N}\right)\right\}.
\end{equation}
The displayed correction is resolved as a genuine first correction by
this estimate in the narrower range $q=o(N^{1/3})$, where the remainder
is $o(q^{-1})$.
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Why this is a connected ratio}\label{wdn:edge:sub:connected}
Write $G(x,k)=\sum_{N\ge0}p_N(k)x^N$ and
$Z(x,k)=\sum_{N\ge0}z_N(k)x^N$, both as formal series.
Recurrence~\eqref{wdn:edge:eq:recurrence} is equivalent to
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:GRiccati}
 G-1=kxG+xG^2+2x^2G_x.
\end{equation}
The product definition of $z_N$ gives
$2Nz_N=(2N-1)(k+2N-1)z_{N-1}$, and therefore
\[
 2x^2Z_{xx}+((k+4)x-1)Z_x+\frac{k+1}{2}Z=0.
\]
Substitution of $1+2xZ_x/Z$ in \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:GRiccati}, followed by the unique
coefficientwise solution with constant term one, proves
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:connected}
 G=1+2x\frac{Z_x}{Z},\qquad
 2Nz_N=\sum_{j=1}^Np_jz_{N-j}.
\end{equation}
Here $Z(0,k)=1$ makes formal division legitimate. The generally factorially
divergent series are never evaluated at nonzero $x$.
All ordinary coefficients of $p_N,z_N$ are nonnegative. Isolating the
$j=N$ term yields $0\preceq p_N\preceq2Nz_N$ coefficientwise.
Since $[\fall{k}{m}]k^j=\stir jm\ge0$, Newton extraction preserves
this order. Thus $0<R_{N,m}\le1$ for $0\le m\le N$, $N\ge1$.
The logarithmic derivative explains the connected normalization used here.

\section{A nonlinear bound uniform in both indices}\label{wdn:edge:sec:topproof}
Extracting the coefficient of $k^{n-a}$ from
\eqref{wdn:edge:eq:recurrence} gives, for $a\ge1$ and $n\ge1$,
\[
 c_a(n)-c_a(n-1)=2(n-1)c_{a-1}(n-1)
       +\sum_{b+c=a-1}\ \sum_{i+j=n-1}c_b(i)c_c(j).
\]
The zero convention for indices above the degree makes this identity
valid also when $a>n$. Telescoping from $c_a(0)=0$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:ctelescope}
 c_a(n)=2\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}i\,c_{a-1}(i)
 +\sum_{b+c=a-1}\ \sum_{\substack{i,j\ge0\\i+j\le n-1}}c_b(i)c_c(j).
\end{equation}
Empty sums are zero. All coefficient indices on the right are below $a$.
For $a>n$, a nonzero convolution term would require
$a-1=b+c\le i+j\le n-1$, which is impossible. The derivative terms
vanish for the same degree reason. Thus the out-of-range convention is
consistent with \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:ctelescope}, rather than an extra assumption.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{wdn:edge:thm:top}]
Induct on $a$, proving the bounds for \emph{every} $n\ge0$ at each stage.
The base $a=0$ is the exact monic coefficient $c_0(n)=1$.

For the upper bound, suppose $a\ge1$ and $n\ge1$. For the derivative
term of \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:ctelescope}, the induction hypothesis and
$i\le i+a-1$ give
\begin{align}\label{wdn:edge:eq:upperderivative}
 2\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}i\,c_{a-1}(i)
 &\le2L_{a-1}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(i+a-1)^{2a-1}\notag\\
 &\le2L_{a-1}\int_0^n(x+a-1)^{2a-1}\,dx
 \le\frac{L_{a-1}}a(n+a)^{2a}.
\end{align}
At $a=1$, the induction factor $c_0(i)$ is one, so the first step is
simply $i\le i$; the argument remains valid at $i=0$ without any
zero-th power. All integrands in this step have positive exponent.
The integral bound follows from monotonicity on each unit interval.

For one convolution term, use
$c_b(i)\le L_b(i+b)^{2b}$ and
$c_c(j)\le L_c(j+c)^{2c}$, interpreting an index-zero factor as the
constant one. On the unit square $[i,i+1]\times[j,j+1]$ the increasing
integrand $(x+b)^{2b}(y+c)^{2c}$ dominates its value at $(i,j)$.
The squares with $i+j\le n-1$ have disjoint interiors and lie in
$x,y\ge0$, $x+y\le n+1$. Shift $u=x+b$, $v=y+c$.
As $b+c=a-1$, the shifted union lies inside
$u,v\ge0$, $u+v\le n+a$. Enlarging the domain, with nonnegative
integrand, gives
\begin{align}\label{wdn:edge:eq:upperconvolution}
 \sum_{\substack{i,j\ge0\\i+j\le n-1}}c_b(i)c_c(j)
 &\le L_bL_c\int_{\substack{u,v\ge0\\u+v\le n+a}}
                       u^{2b}v^{2c}\,du\,dv\notag\\
 &=L_bL_c\frac{(2b)!(2c)!}{(2a)!}(n+a)^{2a}.
\end{align}
The simplex integral is a beta integral; $2b+2c+2=2a$ is its total
homogeneous degree including the two measures. Summing
\eqref{wdn:edge:eq:upperderivative} and \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:upperconvolution} gives the
upper bound by \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:L}. At $n=0$, $c_a(0)=0$ makes it immediate.

For the lower bound, positivity suffices if $n\le2a$. Assume $n>2a$.
The induction hypothesis gives
\[
 i\,c_{a-1}(i)\ge L_{a-1}(i-2a+2)_+^{2a-1}.
\]
Where the positive part vanishes this follows from positivity; elsewhere
use $i\ge i-2a+2$. At $a=1$ it is exactly $i c_0(i)=i$.
An increasing function sampled at the right endpoints dominates its
integral over $[0,n-1]$, so
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:lowerderivative}
 2\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}i\,c_{a-1}(i)
 \ge\frac{L_{a-1}}a(n-2a+1)^{2a}
 \ge\frac{L_{a-1}}a(n-2a)^{2a}.
\end{equation}

For the convolution, integrate the lower-bound functions on the triangle
$x,y\ge0$, $x+y\le n-2$. Away from the coordinate integer lines, set
$i=\lceil x\rceil$, $j=\lceil y\rceil$. Then
$i+j<x+y+2\le n$, whence the integer $i+j\le n-1$.
Each assigned region lies in a distinct ceiling cell of area at most one.
Monotonicity makes the lower-bound integrand there no greater than
$c_b(i)c_c(j)/(L_bL_c)$. Integrating over these cells therefore gives a
lower bound for the discrete sum in \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:ctelescope}.
Restrict further to $x\ge2b$, $y\ge2c$ and set
$u=x-2b$, $v=y-2c$. The resulting triangle is
$u,v\ge0$, $u+v\le n-2a$, since $b+c=a-1$. Hence
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:lowerconvolution}
 \sum_{\substack{i,j\ge0\\i+j\le n-1}}c_b(i)c_c(j)
 \ge L_bL_c\frac{(2b)!(2c)!}{(2a)!}(n-2a)^{2a}.
\end{equation}
For $b=0$ or $c=0$ its lower-bound function is identically one and its
coordinate restriction is unchanged. These endpoint terms are therefore
included, and measure-zero cell boundaries cause no problem.
Adding \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:lowerderivative} and \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:lowerconvolution} and
using \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:L} proves the lower bound. The induction is complete.
\end{proof}

For fixed $a$, division by $n^{2a}$ shows
$c_a(n)/n^{2a}\to L_a$. For orientation,
$c_1(n)=n(3n-1)/2$ and $c_2(n)=n^2(n-1)^2$.
Polynomiality for fixed $a$ is consistent with the theorem but is not
needed in its proof. In particular no growing index is substituted into
an uncontrolled fixed-degree remainder.

\section{Positive bounds for the Newton conversion}\label{wdn:edge:sec:stirling}
We record elementary estimates with constants convenient for common
scales in the final sum. Their role is transparent and does not require
a deep asymptotic theorem for Stirling numbers.

\begin{lemma}[Near-diagonal Stirling bounds]\label{wdn:edge:lem:stirling}
For integers $M\ge2j$ and $j\ge1$,
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:stirling}
 \frac{\fall M{2j}}{2^j j!}
 \le\stir M{M-j}\le\frac{M^{2j}}{2^j j!}.
\end{equation}
In particular its lower bound is at least
$(M-2j)^{2j}/(2^j j!)$. For $j=0$, both bounding quantities and
$\stir MM$ are defined separately as one, including $M=0$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The lower bound counts partitions with exactly $j$ doubleton blocks and
all remaining blocks singletons; their number is
$M!/((M-2j)!2^j j!)$.
For the upper bound, associate to any set partition the graph joining
the least element of each block to every other element of that block.
This is a forest of exactly $j$ edges. Its connected components recover
the partition, so the map is injective into the $j$-subsets of the
$\binom M2$ possible edges. Therefore
\[
 \stir M{M-j}\le\binom{\binom M2}{j}
 \le\frac{\binom M2^j}{j!}\le\frac{M^{2j}}{2^j j!}.
\]
Each of the $2j$ falling-factorial factors is at least $M-2j$.
At $M=2j$ this last lower scale is zero with positive exponent.
\end{proof}

Let $E_a(N)=e_a(1,3,\ldots,2N-1)$, so
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:Edef}
 \frac{z_N(k)}{\gamma_N}=\sum_{a=0}^{N}E_a(N)k^{N-a}.
\end{equation}
\begin{lemma}[Odd-weight elementary symmetric bounds]\label{wdn:edge:lem:E}
For $1\le a\le N/2$,
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:Ebounds}
 \frac{(N-2a)^{2a}}{a!}\le E_a(N)\le\frac{N^{2a}}{a!}.
\end{equation}
For $a=0$, $E_0(N)=1$ separately for all $N\ge0$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The sum of the odd weights is $N^2$. Allowing repeated indices in the
ordered weighted sample gives $a!E_a(N)\le(N^2)^a$.
After any $r$ distinct choices, the remaining total weight is at least
$N^2-r(2N-1)$. Successive conditional summation of the positive weights
therefore yields
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:Eproduct}
 a!E_a(N)\ge\prod_{r=0}^{a-1}\{N^2-r(2N-1)\}
 \ge\{N(N-2a)\}^a\ge(N-2a)^{2a}.
\end{equation}
All factors are nonnegative in the stated range. The boundary $N=2a$
is harmless, since $2a>0$.
\end{proof}

\section{Assembly and explicit error bounds}\label{wdn:edge:sec:assembly}
\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{wdn:edge:thm:sandwich}]
Since Newton extraction of a monomial is a Stirling number,
\begin{align}
 P_{N,N-q}&=\sum_{a=0}^{q}c_a(N)\stir{N-a}{N-q},\label{wdn:edge:eq:Psum}\\
 \frac{Q_{N,N-q}}{\gamma_N}
 &=\sum_{a=0}^{q}E_a(N)\stir{N-a}{N-q}.\label{wdn:edge:eq:Qsum}
\end{align}
Put $j=q-a$ and $M=N-a$. If $q\le N/2$, then $M\ge2j$.
Assume first $1\le q<N/2$, so $N-2q>0$.
The lower bases in Theorem~\ref{wdn:edge:thm:top} and
Lemma~\ref{wdn:edge:lem:stirling} are respectively $N-2a$ and
$M-2j=N-2q+a$, both at least $N-2q$. Their exponents add to $2q$.
Each summand of \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:Psum} is thus at least
\[
 \frac{L_a}{2^j j!}(N-2q)^{2q}.
\]
For the upper bound both bases $N+a$ and $M$ are at most $N+q$,
so the same coefficient multiplies the common upper scale $(N+q)^{2q}$.
When $a=0$ or $j=0$ the associated factor has exact value one; this
argument still applies. Summation gives \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:Psand} by
\eqref{wdn:edge:eq:profile}.

Use Lemma~\ref{wdn:edge:lem:E} instead in \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:Qsum}. The lower scale is
again $(N-2q)^{2q}$ and the upper scale is $N^{2q}$. The coefficients sum to
\[
 \sum_{a=0}^{q}\frac1{a!2^{q-a}(q-a)!}
 =\frac{(3/2)^q}{q!}=C_q,
\]
proving \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:Qsand}. At $N=2q\ge2$ the lower claim follows directly
from positivity: one need not multiply any zero-scale endpoint factors.
The upper argument is unchanged. At $q=0$, monicity gives the separate
exact assertions. Thus no step requires a convention for $0^0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Corollary~\ref{wdn:edge:cor:uniform}]
The definitions give the exact normalization
\[
 \frac{\sqrt N R_{N,N-q}}{F_q}
 =d_N\frac{P_{N,N-q}/(K_qN^{2q})}
              {Q_{N,N-q}/(\gamma_N C_qN^{2q})},
\]
where at $q=0$ the powers mean the separate constant scales specified
above. Theorem~\ref{wdn:edge:thm:sandwich} immediately implies
\eqref{wdn:edge:eq:ratioexact}; $q<N/2$ makes its denominator positive.

Stirling's formula gives $d_N\to1$. Directly from $\gamma_N$,
\[
 \frac{d_{N+1}^2}{d_N^2}=1-\frac1{(2N+1)^2}.
\]
Thus $d_N\ge1$ and, using $-\log(1-x)\le x/(1-x)$,
\begin{align}\label{wdn:edge:eq:dbound}
 0\le\log d_N
 &=-\frac12\sum_{j=N}^{\infty}\log\left(1-\frac1{(2j+1)^2}\right)\notag\\
 &\le\frac12\sum_{j=N}^{\infty}\frac1{(2j+1)^2-1}
 =\frac1{8N}.
\end{align}
The last sum telescopes because $(2j+1)^2-1=4j(j+1)$.
For $q/N\le1/4$, the elementary logarithm inequalities imply
\begin{align*}
 2q\log(1-2q/N)&\ge-8q^2/N,\\
 2q\{\log(1+q/N)-\log(1-2q/N)\}&\le10q^2/N.
\end{align*}
Together with \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:dbound}, these prove \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:exponential}.
The larger upper exponential error dominates
$1-\exp(-8q^2/N)$, so it bounds the absolute relative error.
The same logarithm bounds on \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:Psand} and \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:Qsand}
prove \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:Puniform}--\eqref{wdn:edge:eq:Runiform}. If $(q+1)^2/N\le1$,
then $N\ge4q$ because $(q+1)^2\ge4q$; the constants converting small
exponents to relative errors are consequently absolute. The statements
along every $q=o(\sqrt N)$ follow, including bounded and zero deficits.
\end{proof}

\section{The classical sequence with a controlled remainder}\label{wdn:edge:sec:classical}
The leading factorial estimate below agrees with
Martin--Kearney~\cite[Section~2.4, Theorem~3]{MK} and
Ciobanu--Kolpakov~\cite[Theorem~4.1 and Examples~4.4, 5.3]{CK}.
The proof here includes the remainder needed for binomial smoothing.

\begin{writenote}[Prior art re-derived, 5~October 2026]
Equation~\eqref{wdn:edge:eq:HRiccati} is Bala's Riccati equation for A292186 (August~22, 2023), $4x^2A'=-1+(1-2x)A-xA^2$, rearranged; \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:WV} is the hypergeometric logarithmic derivative, the mechanism of Part~I's Lemma~\ref{wdn:dia:lem:logder}; and the leading term of \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:Vfirst} is the classical factorial asymptotic. The manuscript presents the first correction $1-3/(16n)$ of Lemma~\ref{wdn:edge:lem:tail} and the remainder control as its own; the write did not search the literature for them.
\end{writenote}
Define
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:w}
 w_n=\frac{4^n(1/4)_n(3/4)_n}{n!},\qquad
 W(x)=\sum_{n\ge0}w_nx^n,\qquad H(x)=\sum_{n\ge0}V_nx^n,
\end{equation}
where $(u)_n$ is the rising factorial. These are formal series.
From \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:V},
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:HRiccati}
 H-1=2xH+xH^2+4x^2H'.
\end{equation}
The coefficient ratio
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:r}
 r_n:=\frac{w_n}{w_{n-1}}=4n-4+\frac3{4n}
\end{equation}
gives $4x^2W''+(8x-1)W'+(3/4)W=0$ coefficientwise.
Substitution and uniqueness in \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:HRiccati} yield
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:WV}
 H=1+4x\frac{W'}W,\qquad
 4n w_n=\sum_{j=1}^{n}V_jw_{n-j}.
\end{equation}
All terms are positive; hence, for $n\ge1$,
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:vdef}
 v_n:=\frac{V_n}{4nw_n}\quad\hbox{satisfies}\quad0<v_n\le1.
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}[Convolution tail and first correction]\label{wdn:edge:lem:tail}
As $n\to\infty$,
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:vfirst}
 v_n=1-\frac3{16n}+O(n^{-2}).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Put $U_{n,\ell}=w_\ell w_{n-\ell}/w_n$. Its adjacent ratio is
\[
 \frac{U_{n,\ell+1}}{U_{n,\ell}}
 =\frac{r_{\ell+1}}{r_{n-\ell}}.
\]
The sequence $r_j$ is strictly increasing, since
$r_{j+1}-r_j=4-3/(4j(j+1))>0$. Thus $U$ decreases up to its midpoint.
For $n\ge8$ and $1\le\ell\le\lfloor n/4\rfloor$,
\[
 \frac{r_{\ell+1}}{r_{n-\ell}}
 \le\frac{4\ell+1}{4(n-\ell-1)}
 \le\frac{n+1}{3n-4}\le\frac23.
\]
Moreover $w_1=3/4$, $w_2=105/32$, so
\[
 U_{n,1}=\frac3{16n}+O(n^{-2}),\qquad U_{n,2}=O(n^{-2}).
\]
The sum from $\ell=2$ through the first quarter is bounded by a geometric
series beginning at $O(n^{-2})$. The remaining part up to the midpoint
has at most $n$ terms, each bounded by an exponentially diminished
quarter endpoint. By symmetry,
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:Utail}
 \sum_{\ell=2}^{n-2}U_{n,\ell}=O(n^{-2}).
\end{equation}
All sufficiently small exceptional $n$ can be absorbed in a fixed constant.

Dividing \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:WV} by $4nw_n$ and putting $\ell=n-j$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:vrec}
 v_n=1-\sum_{\ell=1}^{n-1}
 \left(1-\frac\ell n\right)U_{n,\ell}v_{n-\ell}.
\end{equation}
First $0<v_j\le1$, the two endpoint estimates, and \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:Utail}
show $v_n=1+O(n^{-1})$. Retain the $\ell=1$ term in a second use of the
identity: its factor $(1-1/n)v_{n-1}=1+O(n^{-1})$.
The opposite endpoint $\ell=n-1$ has the extra factor $1/n$ and is
$O(n^{-2})$; the middle is \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:Utail}. This proves
\eqref{wdn:edge:eq:vfirst} with its stated sign and coefficient.
\end{proof}

The gamma representation of $w_n$, fixed-parameter Stirling expansion,
and $\Gamma(1/4)\Gamma(3/4)=\pi\sqrt2$ give
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:wfirst}
 w_n=\frac{4^n n!}{\pi\sqrt2\,n}
       \left\{1-\frac3{16n}+O(n^{-2})\right\}.
\end{equation}
Combining \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:vfirst} and \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:wfirst}, then dividing by
$(2n)!$ and using the central binomial expansion, gives
\begin{align}
 V_n&=\frac{2\sqrt2}{\pi}4^nn!
          \left\{1-\frac3{8n}+O(n^{-2})\right\},\label{wdn:edge:eq:Vfirst}\\
 h_n:=n!L_n&=2\sqrt{\frac2\pi}\sqrt n
          \left\{1-\frac1{4n}+O(n^{-2})\right\}.
          \label{wdn:edge:eq:hfirst}
\end{align}
These are ordinary single-sequence asymptotics with absolute remainder
constants. Absorbing finitely many small indices gives a global bound
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:hglobal}
 0<h_n\le C\sqrt{n+1}\qquad(n\ge0),\qquad h_0=1.
\end{equation}

\section{Binomial smoothing and the profile correction}\label{wdn:edge:sec:smoothing}
Let $A$ have the binomial distribution with parameters $q$ and $2/3$.
An exact rewriting of \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:profile} is
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:binomial}
 q!K_q=(3/2)^q\E[h_A],\qquad
 F_q=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2}\E[h_A].
\end{equation}
This positive identity is the mechanism for passing from the classical
sequence to the profile. Set $\mu=2q/3$ and consider
$\mathcal C=\{|A-\mu|\le\mu/2\}$.
The generating function
$\E e^{tA}=(1/3+(2/3)e^t)^q$ and Markov's inequality, with fixed
positive and negative $t$, give
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:bintail}
 \Prob(\mathcal C^c)\le2e^{-cq}
\end{equation}
for an absolute $c>0$. For example the usual fixed-fraction Chernoff
bound permits a positive constant independent of $q$.
By \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:hglobal} and $A\le q$, the contribution of this complement
to $\E h_A$ is $O(\sqrt{q+1}\,e^{-cq})$.
In particular $A=0$ has probability $3^{-q}$ and value $h_0=1$;
no negative power is evaluated there.

On $\mathcal C$, equation~\eqref{wdn:edge:eq:hfirst} holds with a uniform constant:
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:hcentral}
 h_A=2\sqrt{2/\pi}\left\{\sqrt A-\frac14 A^{-1/2}
                              +O(A^{-3/2})\right\}.
\end{equation}
Taylor-expand $\sqrt A$ about $\mu$ through the cubic term.
The fourth-derivative remainder on $\mathcal C$ has absolute value at most
$C\mu^{-7/2}(A-\mu)^4$. The exact central moments are
\begin{align}\label{wdn:edge:eq:moments}
 \E(A-\mu)&=0,&\E(A-\mu)^2&=\mu/3,\notag\\
 \E(A-\mu)^3&=-\mu/9,&
 \E(A-\mu)^4&=\mu^2/3-\mu/9.
\end{align}
Replacing central-event moments by full moments costs only a polynomial
in $q$ times $e^{-cq}$, since $|A-\mu|\le q$.
The cubic contribution and the fourth-derivative remainder both have
relative order $q^{-2}$, whereas the quadratic contribution is
$-1/(24\mu)=-1/(16q)$. Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:sqrtmean}
 \E\sqrt A=\sqrt\mu\left\{1-\frac1{16q}+O(q^{-2})\right\}.
\end{equation}
The square root at zero is harmless in this equation.
For the negative power, evaluated only at $A\ge1$, the same central
expansion gives
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:invrootmean}
 \E[A^{-1/2};A\ge1]=\mu^{-1/2}\{1+O(q^{-1})\}.
\end{equation}
Outside $\mathcal C$ that integrand is at most one, so its tail is
exponentially small. The remainder in \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:hcentral} contributes
$O(\mu^{-3/2})$ on $\mathcal C$; the complement for $h_A$ is handled
by the separate global bound, not by a large-index asymptotic there.
Inserting these estimates into \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:binomial} gives
\begin{align*}
 \E h_A
 &=2\sqrt{2/\pi}\sqrt\mu
     \left\{1-\frac1{16q}-\frac1{4\mu}+O(q^{-2})\right\}\\
 &=2\sqrt{2/\pi}\sqrt{2q/3}
     \left\{1-\frac7{16q}+O(q^{-2})\right\}.
\end{align*}
This proves \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:Ffirst}. Multiplying by the exact joint error
of Corollary~\ref{wdn:edge:cor:uniform} proves \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:Rfirst}.
Finally $(q+1)^2/N=o(q^{-1})$ for growing $q$ precisely under the
sufficient scale $q^3/N\to0$. This establishes the stated
correction-resolving range and completes Theorem~\ref{wdn:edge:thm:matching}.

\section{All fixed orders of the edge profile}\label{wdn:edge:sec:allorders}
The profile alone admits a complete fixed-order expansion. This section
refines the one-variable analysis, while leaving the finite-$N$ sandwich
and its error unchanged.

\begin{theorem}[Constructive fixed-order profile expansion]\label{wdn:edge:thm:allorders}
For every fixed integer $K\ge1$ there are explicitly constructible
rational coefficients $f_0,\ldots,f_{K-1}$, with $f_0=1$, such that
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:Fallorders}
 F_q=2\sqrt{q/3}\left\{\sum_{r=0}^{K-1}f_rq^{-r}+O_K(q^{-K})\right\}
 \qquad(q\to\infty).
\end{equation}
The first coefficients are
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:fvalues}
 \begin{split}
 f_0&=1,\qquad f_1=-\frac7{16},\qquad f_2=-\frac{455}{512},\qquad
 f_3=-\frac{34717}{8192},\\
 f_4&=-\frac{16688901}{524288},\qquad
 f_5=-\frac{2712316809}{8388608}.
 \end{split}
\end{equation}
Consequently, for every fixed $K$, jointly for $q\to\infty$ and
$q=o(\sqrt N)$,
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:Rallorders}
 R_{N,N-q}=2\sqrt{\frac q{3N}}
 \left\{\sum_{r=0}^{K-1}f_rq^{-r}
       +O_K\left(q^{-K}+\frac{(q+1)^2}{N}\right)\right\}.
\end{equation}
For $K\ge2$, the sufficient scale $q=o(N^{1/(K+1)})$ makes this
remainder $o(q^{-(K-1)})$ and separates the last displayed correction.
No convergence of the series or uniformity for growing $K$ is asserted.
Equation~\eqref{wdn:edge:eq:Rallorders} is not an all-orders finite-$N$ expansion.
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Fixed-order control of the connected normalization}
Write $\ell_0=\lfloor n/2\rfloor$. Splitting the exact positive
identity~\eqref{wdn:edge:eq:vrec} into disjoint halves gives
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:vsplit}
 v_n+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\ell_0}
 \left(1-\frac\ell n\right)U_{n,\ell}v_{n-\ell}
 =1-\sum_{j=1}^{n-\ell_0-1}\frac{V_jw_{n-j}}{4nw_n}.
\end{equation}
For every fixed $s\ge1$ and $d\ge0$, the ratio proof of
Lemma~\ref{wdn:edge:lem:tail} gives the stronger bound
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:fixedtail}
 \sum_{\ell=s}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}(1+\ell)^d U_{n,\ell}
 =O_{s,d}(n^{-s}).
\end{equation}
To see every part of the claim, $U_{n,s}=O_s(n^{-s})$ for fixed $s$.
On the first quarter the adjacent ratio is at most $2/3$, so the
polynomially weighted geometric sum starting at $s$ has this order.
On the remaining part up to the midpoint, monotonicity bounds each term
by an exponentially small quarter-endpoint value. Any fixed polynomial
in $n$ is absorbed by that exponential. For fixed $s$ this applies once
$n$ is sufficiently large; only the constant changes for finite
exceptions. This proves \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:fixedtail}.
Furthermore
\[
 \frac{V_jw_{n-j}}{4nw_n}\le\frac jn U_{n,j},
\]
so the source tail starting at fixed $s$ is $O_s(n^{-s-1})$.
The entire source is $O(n^{-2})$, whereas the principal kernel mass
in \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:vsplit} is $O(n^{-1})$.

Put $h=1/n$ and define rational products
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:D}
 D_\ell(h)=\prod_{i=0}^{\ell-1}
 \frac{1-ih}{(1-(i+1/4)h)(1-(i+3/4)h)}.
\end{equation}
The exact ratio of hypergeometric coefficients is
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:wshift}
 \frac{w_{n-\ell}}{w_n}=4^{-\ell}h^\ell D_\ell(h).
\end{equation}
A formal series $v(h)=\sum_{r\ge0}\nu_rh^r$ is defined by
\begin{multline}\label{wdn:edge:eq:vformal}
 v(h)+\sum_{\ell\ge1}w_\ell4^{-\ell}h^\ell(1-\ell h)D_\ell(h)
               v\left(\frac h{1-\ell h}\right)\\
 +\frac h4\sum_{j\ge1}V_j4^{-j}h^jD_j(h)=1.
\end{multline}
Every coefficient involves only finitely many terms. Set $\nu_0=1$.
At order $h^r$, only $\ell\le r$ and $j\le r-1$ contribute, and
occurrences of $\nu_i$ in the first sum have $i\le r-\ell<r$.
Thus the finite recursion is
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:nurec}
 \begin{split}
 \nu_r=-[h^r]\Bigg\{&\sum_{\ell=1}^{r}w_\ell4^{-\ell}h^\ell
 (1-\ell h)D_\ell(h)v_{<r}\left(\frac h{1-\ell h}\right)\\
 &+\frac h4\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}V_j4^{-j}h^jD_j(h)\Bigg\},\qquad
 v_{<r}(h)=\sum_{i=0}^{r-1}\nu_i h^i.
 \end{split}
\end{equation}
It uses rational arithmetic and the finite exact recurrence for $V_j$.
For example $\nu_1=-3/16$, $\nu_2=-183/512$, and
$\nu_3=-8313/8192$.

The formal calculation has a genuine remainder. Fix $K$ and put
$H_K(n)=\sum_{r=0}^{K-1}\nu_rn^{-r}$. Insert $H_K$ in
\eqref{wdn:edge:eq:vsplit}, retaining principal indices $\ell<K$ and source
indices $j<K-1$. These are finitely many fixed shifts; Taylor expansion
of \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:wshift} and $H_K(n-\ell)$ has remainder $O_K(n^{-K})$,
and \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:vformal} cancels all lower coefficients. The remaining
principal tail is $O_K(n^{-K})$ by \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:fixedtail}, since $H_K$
is bounded on positive integers. For $K\ge2$, the source tail begins
at $K-1$ and has order $O_K(n^{-K})$ from its extra $1/n$ factor.
For $K=1$, the whole source is $O(n^{-2})$, which is smaller still.
Thus the residual of this approximation is $O_K(n^{-K})$.
For $E_n=v_n-H_K(n)$, subtraction yields
\[
 |E_n|\le C_Kn^{-K}
 +\sum_{\ell=1}^{\ell_0}(1-\ell/n)U_{n,\ell}|E_{n-\ell}|.
\]
Each predecessor is at least $n/2$. The principal kernel mass is
$O(1/n)$, so for sufficiently large $n$,
\[
 \sum_{\ell=1}^{\ell_0}(1-\ell/n)U_{n,\ell}
                  \left(\frac n{n-\ell}\right)^K\le\frac12.
\]
Strong induction, with $M_K$ chosen to cover the finite initial indices
and at least $2C_K$, proves $|E_n|\le M_Kn^{-K}$. We have proved
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:vallorders}
 v_n=\sum_{r=0}^{K-1}\nu_rn^{-r}+O_K(n^{-K}).
\end{equation}

\subsection{Gamma normalization and central-moment coefficients}
Let $c=2\sqrt{2/\pi}$. The exact gamma expression for
$4nw_n n!/(2n)!$, with fixed-shift Stirling expansion~\cite[Eq.~5.11.8]{DLMF}, gives
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:hexp}
 h_n=c\sqrt n\,
 \exp\left(\sum_{r\ge1}\lambda_rn^{-r}\right)v_n
\end{equation}
as a fixed-order asymptotic identity, where
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:lambda}
 \lambda_r=\frac{(-1)^{r+1}}{r(r+1)}
 \left\{\mathsf B_{r+1}(1/4)+\mathsf B_{r+1}(3/4)
       -\mathsf B_{r+1}(1/2)-\mathsf B_{r+1}(1)\right\}.
\end{equation}
Here $\mathsf B_j(x)$ is the Bernoulli polynomial, specified by
$t e^{xt}/(e^t-1)=\sum_{j\ge0}\mathsf B_j(x)t^j/j!$.
All even $\lambda_r$ vanish; the first odd ones are
$\lambda_1=-1/16$, $\lambda_3=1/1536$, $\lambda_5=-1/20480$.
Define $\tau_r$ by the formal product
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:tau}
 \sum_{r\ge0}\tau_rh^r
 =\exp\left(\sum_{r\ge1}\lambda_rh^r\right)
          \sum_{r\ge0}\nu_rh^r.
\end{equation}
This is an explicit finite algorithm at any prescribed order: set
$e_0=1$ and
\[
 n e_n=\sum_{r=1}^n r\lambda_re_{n-r},\qquad
 \tau_n=\sum_{i=0}^n\nu_i e_{n-i}.
\]
Combining \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:vallorders} with the ordinary fixed-order Stirling
remainder proves
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:hallorders}
 h_n=c\sqrt n\left\{\sum_{r=0}^{K-1}\tau_rn^{-r}+O_K(n^{-K})\right\}.
\end{equation}
The first $\tau$ values are
\[
 1,\quad-\frac14,\quad-\frac{11}{32},\quad-\frac{127}{128},\quad
 -\frac{9021}{2048},\quad-\frac{215895}{8192}.
\]
The global bound~\eqref{wdn:edge:eq:hglobal} remains available for all tail terms.

Put $p=2/3$ and $M_d(q)=\E(A-pq)^d$ for $A\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(q,p)$.
These are exact rational polynomials in $q$, with
$M_0=1$, $M_1=0$, and $\deg M_d\le\lfloor d/2\rfloor$ for $d\ge2$.
Indeed their exponential generating function is
\[
 \sum_{d\ge0}M_d(q)\frac{z^d}{d!}
 =\exp\{q[\log(1-p+pe^z)-pz]\}.
\]
A finite recurrence is obtained from the Bernoulli cumulants
$\kappa_1(p)=p$ and
$\kappa_{j+1}(p)=p(1-p)\,d\kappa_j(p)/dp$:
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:momentrec}
 M_d(q)=\sum_{j=2}^d\binom{d-1}{j-1}q\kappa_j(2/3)M_{d-j}(q)
 \qquad(d\ge2).
\end{equation}
The absence of the first cumulant is responsible for the degree bound.
The profile coefficients have the finite rational formula
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:falgorithm}
 f_r=[h^r]\sum_{j=0}^{r}\tau_jp^{-j}h^j
       \sum_{d=0}^{2(r-j)}\binom{1/2-j}{d}p^{-d}h^dM_d(1/h).
\end{equation}
The smallest possible degree of the $j,d$ term is
$j+\lceil d/2\rceil$. Consequently no $d>2(r-j)$ can contribute,
and only $\tau_0,\ldots,\tau_r$ and moments through $2r$ are needed.
At $r=1$, the two contributions are $\tau_1/p=-3/8$ and $-1/16$,
recovering $f_1=-7/16$. Applying the same exact recursion yields
\eqref{wdn:edge:eq:fvalues}.

\subsection{A smoothing remainder at every fixed order}
\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{wdn:edge:thm:allorders}]
Fix $K$ and use the same central event
$\mathcal C=\{|A-\mu|\le\mu/2\}$, $\mu=pq$, as in
Section~\ref{wdn:edge:sec:smoothing}. The global bound for $h_A$ and
\eqref{wdn:edge:eq:bintail} make the noncentral contribution
$O(\sqrt{q+1}\,e^{-cq})$; $A=0$ is again handled by its exact value.
On $\mathcal C$, the large-$n$ expansion \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:hallorders} is uniform:
\[
 h_A=c\sum_{j=0}^{K-1}\tau_j A^{1/2-j}+O_K(q^{1/2-K}).
\]
For the $j$th retained term, Taylor-expand about $\mu$ through degree
$2(K-j)-1$. Its remainder is bounded by
\[
 C_{K,j}\mu^{1/2-j-2(K-j)}|A-\mu|^{2(K-j)}.
\]
By \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:momentrec}, the even central moment of order $2(K-j)$
is $O_K(q^{K-j})$. After expectation and division by $\sqrt\mu$, this
remainder is $O_K(q^{-K})$. Replacing central-event moments by full
moments costs an exponentially small quantity times a fixed power of
$q$, since $|A-\mu|\le q$. The Taylor polynomials produce exactly
\eqref{wdn:edge:eq:falgorithm}, and any additional terms have powers at least
$q^{-K}$. Inserting them in \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:binomial} proves
\eqref{wdn:edge:eq:Fallorders} with a genuine remainder. The fixed-$n$ expansion
has only been used where $A$ is a positive fixed fraction of $q$;
the global bound controls the entire small-$A$ tail.
Finally Corollary~\ref{wdn:edge:cor:uniform} gives \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:Rallorders}.
The condition $q^{K+1}/N\to0$ separates the last correction as stated.
\end{proof}

\section{An increasing profile and a ceiling-safe inverse}\label{wdn:edge:sec:inverse}
\begin{theorem}[Exact monotonicity and fixed-order inverse]\label{wdn:edge:thm:inverse}
The sequence $F_q$ is strictly increasing for all integers $q\ge0$.
For real $Y$, define
\[
 \mathcal Q(Y)=\min\{q\ge0:F_q\ge Y\}.
\]
It exists for every $Y$. For every fixed integer $M\ge0$ there is a
rational inverse polynomial
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:J}
 J_M(x)=x+\sum_{r=0}^M\xi_r x^{-r},\qquad x=3Y^2/4,
\end{equation}
and constants $C_M,Y_M>0$ such that, for $Y\ge Y_M$,
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:ceilinverse}
 \left\lceil J_M(x)-C_Mx^{-M-1}\right\rceil
 \le\mathcal Q(Y)\le
 \left\lceil J_M(x)+C_Mx^{-M-1}\right\rceil.
\end{equation}
The first coefficients are
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:xivalues}
 \xi_0=\frac78,\qquad \xi_1=\frac{203}{128},\qquad
 \xi_2=\frac{3231}{512}.
\end{equation}
All higher coefficients are specified by the finite reversion below.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Multiply \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:V} by $n!/(2n)!$. Since
$(4n-2)n!/(2n)!=(n-1)!/(2n-2)!$, one obtains the exact identity
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:hmonotone}
 h_n=h_{n-1}+\frac{n!}{(2n)!}\sum_{b+c=n-1}V_bV_c>h_{n-1}
 \qquad(n\ge1).
\end{equation}
Couple $A_q\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(q,2/3)$ to an independent
Bernoulli$(2/3)$ variable $B$ by $A_{q+1}=A_q+B$.
Strict increase of $h_n$ gives $\E h_{A_{q+1}}>\E h_{A_q}$,
and \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:binomial} proves strict increase of $F_q$.
As $F_q\to\infty$, $\mathcal Q(Y)$ exists for every real $Y$;
for $Y\le F_0=\sqrt\pi/2$ it equals zero.

Square the formal profile expansion and write
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:Greversion}
 \begin{split}
 \mathcal G(z)&=z\left(\sum_{r\ge0}f_rz^{-r}\right)^2
      =z+g_0+g_1z^{-1}+g_2z^{-2}+\cdots,\\
 g_r&=\sum_{a+b=r+1}f_af_b.
 \end{split}
\end{equation}
In particular $g_0=-7/8$, $g_1=-203/128$, and $g_2=-7883/1024$.
Define $\xi_0,\ldots,\xi_M$ successively by
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:reversion}
 \mathcal G\left(x+\sum_{r=0}^M\xi_r x^{-r}\right)
      =x+O(x^{-M-1})
\end{equation}
as a formal Laurent identity to the stated order. To compute $\xi_r$,
substitute the preceding coefficients with $\xi_r$ provisionally zero,
and negate the coefficient of $x^{-r}$. The missing $\xi_r$ enters that
coefficient with multiplier one; every $g_jz^{-j}$ term with $j\ge1$
changes only at higher order. This gives a finite and unique rational
algorithm and yields \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:xivalues}.

Take $K=M+2$ in \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:Fallorders}. Squaring the estimate gives,
for large integer $q$,
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:squareerror}
 \frac34F_q^2=\mathcal G_M(q)+O_M(q^{-M-1}),\qquad
 \mathcal G_M(z)=z+g_0+\sum_{r=1}^M g_rz^{-r}.
\end{equation}
Choose a constant that bounds this remainder. For all sufficiently large
positive $z$, the two real envelopes
$\mathcal G_M(z)\pm C z^{-M-1}$ have positive derivative tending to one.
Their roots at level $x$ are within $O_M(x^{-M-1})$ of $J_M(x)$,
by \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:reversion} and the mean value theorem.
The upper envelope has the smaller root $y_-$, and the lower envelope
the larger root $y_+$. At an integer $q<\lceil y_-\rceil$, one has
$q<y_-$, so the upper envelope is below $x$ and hence $F_q<Y$.
At $q=\lceil y_+\rceil$ the lower envelope is at least $x$, hence
$F_q\ge Y$. These implications use $Y>0$, valid in the stated large-$Y$
range, so comparison of squares is equivalent to comparison with $Y$.
The finitely many indices below the envelope domain also satisfy
$F_q<Y$ once $Y$ is large. Thus
$\lceil y_-\rceil\le\mathcal Q(Y)\le\lceil y_+\rceil$.
Replacing the two roots by their bounds around $J_M(x)$ proves
\eqref{wdn:edge:eq:ceilinverse} after increasing $C_M$.
\end{proof}

For $M=0$, the continuous center is $3Y^2/4+7/8$ with
$O(Y^{-2})$ uncertainty \emph{inside} the two ceilings. Including one
more term gives
\[
 \frac{3Y^2}{4}+\frac78+\frac{203}{96Y^2}
\]
with $O(Y^{-4})$ uncertainty inside the ceilings. There is no unrounded
$O(Y^{-2})$ approximation to the integer-valued threshold in this
statement: closeness to an integer can change a ceiling.
The constants and sufficiently large onset in this inverse are existence
statements, not certified numerical witnesses at a specified $Y$.
This inverse concerns only the one-variable profile $F_q$; it is not
an inverse theorem for freely varying $(N,q)$.

\section{Comparison with the compact interior}\label{wdn:edge:sec:comparison}
Report~142~\cite{interior} gives a compact-interior expansion in the
coordinates
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:interiorcoord}
 \alpha=\frac mN=\frac{2s^2}{1+s},\qquad 0<s<1,
\end{equation}
with leading connected ratio $\sqrt{1-s^2}$ and first relative coefficient
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:r1}
 r_1(s)=\frac{s^4+6s^3+7s^2+3s+4}{4(s-1)(s+1)(s+2)^2}.
\end{equation}
Only these explicit functions are used in this section. An unchanged
source/PDF pair of Report~142 is included for the provenance of this
comparison; none of its companion code is needed here.

At $\alpha=1-q/N$, elementary expansion of \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:interiorcoord}
at $s=1$ gives
\begin{align}
 1-s&=\frac{2q}{3N}+O((q/N)^2),\label{wdn:edge:eq:sedge}\\
 \sqrt{1-s^2}&=2\sqrt{q/(3N)}\{1+O(q/N)\}.\label{wdn:edge:eq:leadmatch}
\end{align}
The numerator of \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:r1} equals $21$ at $s=1$, while its
denominator is $72(s-1)$ to first order. Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{wdn:edge:eq:correctionmatch}
 \frac{r_1(s)}N=-\frac7{16q}+O(N^{-1}).
\end{equation}
This matches the coefficient in \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:Rfirst}.
These are algebraic endpoint expansions of stated functions, not an
application of the compact-interior theorem at a moving boundary.
That theorem's remainder constants are only uniform on fixed compact
subsets of $0<\alpha<1$ and cannot be carried to $s=1$ by fiat.
The actual error for the growing-deficit transition here comes from
Theorems~\ref{wdn:edge:thm:top}--\ref{wdn:edge:thm:sandwich} and the smoothing proof.

\section{Further questions and precise limitations}\label{wdn:edge:sec:future}
The estimates isolate several concrete questions without settling them.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The scale $q\asymp\sqrt N$.}
The exponential bounds remain informative there but their relative
errors need not tend to zero. Is there a nontrivial two-parameter
correction to $K_qN^{2q}$, or to the connected profile, on this scale?
The present theorem neither identifies that limit nor asserts failure
of a stronger approximation.
\item \textbf{Sharper nonlinear comparison.}
The shifts $n-2a$ and $n+a$ were selected for a uniform positive proof.
A more precise expansion of the discrete convolution, with bounds
uniform in $a$, could reveal a first $q^2/N$ correction and enlarge the
range in which profile corrections are separated. Fixed-$a$ polynomial
formulas alone would not justify such a conclusion. In particular, could
first finite-$N$ corrections in $P$ and $Q$ cancel in their connected
ratio enough to improve the present $O(q^2/N)$ relative error? Such
cancellation and any resulting larger range remain unproved here.
\item \textbf{A combinatorial meaning for $K_q$.}
The exact binomial smoothing of A292186 gives a positive algebraic
construction. An interpretation directly in terms of weighted paths
or connected structures would clarify why the particular probability
$2/3$ appears in the edge profile. No new counting interpretation is
claimed here.
\item \textbf{A bridge across all moving ratios.}
A uniform theorem linking this boundary range with the compact interior
would require control of the remainder as the saddle approaches the
boundary. Agreement of the displayed coefficients is evidence of
compatibility, not a proof of that global statement.
\item \textbf{Effective onset constants.}
The finite-index sandwich constants are explicit. Constants and onset
indices in the asymptotic profile remainders are existence statements
from the tail estimates. Computing usable, certified numerical onset
bounds is a separate problem; finite tests do not provide them.
\end{enumerate}

All five remain open after the merge. Items~1 and~4 are Section~\ref{wdn:sec:further}, items~\ref{wdn:q:gap} and~\ref{wdn:q:bridge}; item~2 is item~\ref{wdn:q:sharper}; item~3 is item~\ref{wdn:q:comb}; item~5 is item~\ref{wdn:q:effective}.
\end{writenote}

\begin{remark}[\wtag\ The scale $q\asymp\sqrt N$: order of magnitude, 5~October 2026]\label{wdn:rem:sqrtscale}
There are absolute constants $0<a_*<b_*$ such that for every $c>0$, all $N\ge1$ and all integers $0\le q\le\min(c\sqrt N,N/4)$,
\[
 a_*e^{-8c^2}\sqrt{\frac{q+1}N}\ \le\ R_{N,N-q}\ \le\ b_*e^{10c^2+1/8}\sqrt{\frac{q+1}N}.
\]
\end{remark}
\begin{proof}
By \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:profile} every $F_q$ is positive, since every $L_a$ in \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:L} is, and $F_0=\sqrt\pi/2$; by Theorem~\ref{wdn:edge:thm:matching} $F_q/\sqrt{q+1}\to2/\sqrt3$. \textup{(Trimmed after the independent check of 5~October 2026, recorded at the end of this section; the first wording read ``By Theorem~\ref{wdn:edge:thm:inverse} the profile $F_q$ is strictly increasing, with $F_0=\sqrt\pi/2>0$'', but monotonicity is not needed, only positivity.)} A sequence of positive numbers with a positive limit is bounded above and below by positive constants, so $a_*\sqrt{q+1}\le F_q\le b_*\sqrt{q+1}$ for all $q\ge0$ with absolute $0<a_*<b_*$. For $0\le q\le N/4$, \eqref{wdn:edge:eq:exponential} gives $e^{-8q^2/N}F_q\le\sqrt NR_{N,N-q}\le e^{(10q^2+1/8)/N}F_q$, and $q^2/N\le c^2$, $1/(8N)\le1/8$. Dividing by $\sqrt N$ proves the claim.
\end{proof}
The remark settles the order of magnitude of $R_{N,N-q}$ on the whole scale $q=O(\sqrt N)$, which Part~III's item~1 calls informative but does not state; it does not identify a limit there, and it says nothing for $q\gg\sqrt N$, where the exponential factors are unbounded.
