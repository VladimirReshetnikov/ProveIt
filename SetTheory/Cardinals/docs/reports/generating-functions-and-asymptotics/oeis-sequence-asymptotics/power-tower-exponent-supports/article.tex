\begin{abstract}
For a positive exponent $a$, write the $N$th derivative of $x^{x^a}$ as $x^{x^a}$ times a finite sum of monomials $x^{a\ell-N}(\log x)^k$, and let $A_a(N)$ count the nonzero ones.

\emph{Integer exponents (Part~\ref{pte:part:integer}).} For every fixed positive integer $a$ we prove $A_a(N)=N^2/2+o_a(N^2)$. An exact coefficient transformation gives positivity throughout the nonnegative power columns. In the remaining tail, circles in the coordinate $z=\tanh(t/2)$ and horizontal lines provide globally dominant saddles; a convex-envelope argument proves complete contour coverage; nonzero phase curvature, an integer-triangle estimate and compact exhaustion give density one of nonzero tail coefficients. Every negative-column coefficient at depths one and two is classified, and outside a countable algebraic set of real exponents the count is exactly $N(N+3)/2$. For $a=1$ this is the asymptotic $a(N)\sim N^2/2$ of OEIS A293239, equivalently density zero for the zeros of the first-kind Lehmer--Comtet triangle; for $a=2$ it is $\lim a(N)/N^2=1/2$ for OEIS A290268. Both were also proved separately, with more explicit detail at those exponents, and are printed as second routes.

\emph{The half exponent (Part~\ref{pte:part:half}).} For $x^{\sqrt x}$ the same leading asymptotic holds, by a circle-only saddle selection, and two infinite families of cancellations lie off the reflection line.

\emph{Fixed defects (Part~\ref{pte:part:defects}).} On a fixed factor defect $r=N-\ell$: for rational $a\ne1$ and $r\ge4$ only finitely many coefficients vanish off an explicit reflection line (Schur, Siegel, and two certified quartics); for algebraic $a$ of degree $D$ the same holds on $r\ge4D+2$; for irrational $a$ there are at most $(2\lfloor r/2\rfloor)^2-1$ cancellation cells, effectively, at most one on each of defects two and three, and none for transcendental $a$; for rational $1/2<a<1$ the nonreflection cancellations on defects two and three are counted exactly to $O(1)$ by explicit square-root laws.

All asymptotic statements are ineffective: no power-saving remainder or numerical order threshold is asserted, exact noncancellation at arbitrary depth is not proved, and no exponent grows with $N$. The proofs are conventional mathematics, not proof-assistant formalizations or refereed publications.
\end{abstract}

\subsection{What was merged, and where the merge had to choose}\label{pte:sec:merged}
No source cites another, except that 20 cites 20r as its companion. Apart from the coefficient setup shared by 20r, 20 and 16, the duplication between them is mathematical, not textual.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{One theorem, three proofs.} Source 28 proves $A_a(N)=N^2/2+o_a(N^2)$ for every fixed positive integer $a$ (Theorem~\ref{pte:ga:main}). Source 31 proved the case $a=1$, OEIS A293239, and source 34 the case $a=2$, OEIS A290268, half an hour and an hour earlier, by the same contour method (tanh circles, horizontal lines, the kernel $U(s,y)$, the integer-triangle lemma and compact exhaustion). The general theorem is stated once. The statements of 31 and 34 are printed as Corollaries~\ref{pte:lc:main} and~\ref{pte:as:main}, in the forms their sources gave them, each followed by its source's complete proof as a second route. Those proofs are kept because their steps are genuinely more explicit at the two exponents: source 31 classifies every critical point and identifies the real fold $\ee^{T_*}=1+T_*+T_*^2$, $\rho_*\approx1.29843$; source 34 proves real-saddle positivity on the whole strict real region and gives a rational curvature certificate at $T=14/5$. All three proofs are credited at each statement.
\item \textbf{One lemma, four proofs.} The integer-triangle lemma is proved with the same argument in sources 28, 31, 34 and 26. It is printed once, as Lemma~\ref{pte:ga:lattice}, whose proof includes the two details that only 31 and 34 spell out; the four labels of the sources all point to it.
\item \textbf{One coefficient setup.} Sources 20r, 20 and 16 open with the same centered coefficient identity, in nearly the same words. It is printed once, in Section~\ref{pte:sec:shared}, in source 20r's words, together with the degree lemma that only source 16 contains.
\item \textbf{A finiteness theorem implied twice.} Source 20's Theorem~\ref{pte:ac:algebraic} (finitely many nonreflection cancellations on every fixed defect $r\ge4D+2$, for algebraic $a$ of degree $D$) is implied by 20r's Theorem~\ref{pte:rf:main} for rational $a$ and by 16's Theorem~\ref{pte:ir:main} for irrational $a$. Its proof, by field degrees and Siegel's theorem over $\mathbb Q(a)$, is a different route and is printed as such. Source 20's counting theorem and its window corollary are new and are printed in full.
\item \textbf{One pair of families, two derivations.} Source 26's two infinite zero families at $a=1/2$ (Theorem~\ref{pte:he:families}) are 20r's residual formulas~\eqref{pte:rf:low} specialized to $a=1/2$, and 20's endpoint remark counts them. The families are stated once, as Theorem~\ref{pte:he:families} with 26's zero lists and Bell-block proof; the specialization is recorded where 20r's formulas are printed.
\item \textbf{A clause implied by a corollary.} The transcendental clause of 16's Theorem~\ref{pte:ir:main} also follows from 28's Corollary~\ref{pte:ga:generic}.
\item \textbf{Re-derivations of the host.} Several statements re-derive results already proved in \texttt{power-\allowbreak{}tower-\allowbreak{}derivative-\allowbreak{}term-\allowbreak{}counts}. They are kept, and the host statement is cited next to each, as a second route and never as new: 28's Proposition~\ref{pte:ga:bulk} at $a=2$, Theorem~\ref{pte:ga:depthtwo} at $a=1,2$ and~\eqref{pte:ga:folded} at $a=2$; 31's expansion and count; 34's coefficient model, its tanh coefficient formula and its upper bound, which is weaker than the host's.
\end{itemize}
The merge chose 28 as the base because it is the most general of the three proofs, and printed the a=1 and a=2 material after it, rather than splitting 31 and 34 into the host report, which would have printed the a=1, 2 theorem twice in the collection. It chose to print 20's algebraic-degree theorem in full although it is implied, because its proof is the only one of the three that works uniformly over $\mathbb Q(a)$. It renamed symbols only where two meanings would meet in one statement, and lists every renaming in Section~\ref{pte:sec:conventions}.

\section{Statement and exact coefficient coordinates \src{28}}\label{pte:sec:ga-coordinates}
For $a\in\mathbb Z_{\ge1}$, write
\[
 \frac{d^N}{dx^N}x^{x^a}=x^{x^a}
 \sum_{j\in\mathbb Z,\,k\ge0}c_{a,N}(j,k)x^j(\log x)^k,
\]
with like monomials collected, and let $A_a(N)$ count the nonzero coefficients.

\begin{theorem}\label{pte:ga:main}
For every fixed positive integer $a$,
\begin{equation}\label{pte:ga:mainlimit}
 A_a(N)=\frac{N^2}{2}+o_a(N^2)\qquad(N\to\infty).
\end{equation}
More precisely, only $o_a(N^2)$ candidate coefficients cancel in the negative-column tail specified below. The theorem does not classify individual extra zeros, provide a global power-saving remainder, or assert uniformity when $a$ grows with $N$.
\end{theorem}

\mergenote{Theorem~\ref{pte:ga:main} is proved by source 28 for every fixed positive integer $a$ (Sections~\ref{pte:sec:ga-coordinates}--\ref{pte:sec:ga-density}). \begin{proposition}[The exact positive bulk]\label{pte:ga:bulk}
Every candidate with $j\ge0$ is strictly positive. The $j=-1$ boundary is strictly positive when $d\ge1$ and structurally zero when $d=0$.
\end{proposition}

\end{proof}

\section{A uniform angular-selection mechanism \src{28}}\label{pte:sec:ga-selection}
\subsection{Tanh circles, including circles through numerator zeros}
The roots of $C_a$ are purely imaginary:
\begin{equation}\label{pte:ga:factorization}
 C_a(z)=\prod_{j=1}^{\lfloor(a-1)/2\rfloor}
       (1+z^2/\lambda_j),\qquad
 \lambda_j=\tan^2(\pi j/a).
\end{equation}
An empty product equals one. On $|z|=R<1$, let $\alpha=(1-R^2)/(1+R^2)$. In the favored quadrant the image in the $t$ plane satisfies
\[
 \cos y=\alpha\cosh s,\qquad y'=-\tanh s\cot y<0.
\]
Write $C=\cosh s$, $v_j=R^2/\lambda_j$. The exact geometry yields
\begin{gather}
 \cos^2(\arg z)=\frac{1-C^{-2}}{1-\alpha^2},\notag\\
 |1+z^2/\lambda_j|^2=A_j-B_j/C^2,\quad
 B_j=\frac{4v_j}{1-\alpha^2}>0,\quad A_j=(1-v_j)^2+B_j\ge B_j.\label{pte:ga:factorcircle}
\end{gather}
Hence
\[
 \frac d{ds}\log|1+z^2/\lambda_j|
 =\tanh s\,\frac{B_j}{A_jC^2-B_j}.
\]
The other $\kappa$-dependent power of $|z|$ is constant on the circle. Direct differentiation of the full action gives
\begin{equation}\label{pte:ga:circleprofile}
 \frac d{ds}\operatorname{Re}L_c(-s-iy(s))
 =\tanh s\left[U(s,y(s))-V(s)
       +\kappa\sum_j\frac{B_j}{A_jC^2-B_j}\right].
\end{equation}
Every term in the bracket decreases strictly in $s$: $U_s+U_y y'<0$, $-V'<0$, and each rational summand decreases. The derivative is positive near the imaginary endpoint. If $R^2=\lambda_j$, the logarithm tends to $-\infty$ at that endpoint and its derivative diverges positively; the maximum remains away from the zero. A zero-weight factor when $\kappa=0$ is simply omitted.

It follows that each favored quadrant has one maximum, either a strict interior maximum or its real endpoint. Reflection to the other half-contour lowers the real action by $2e|\operatorname{Re}t|$. In particular every off-axis saddle of height less than $\pi/2$ is globally selected on its tanh circle, with nonzero angular curvature. This includes $a\ge5$, when some numerator zeros lie inside the unit disk.

\subsection{Horizontal lines and a finite Jensen inequality}
Fix $\pi/2\le y\le\pi$ and write $C=\cosh s$, $S=\sinh s$, $c=\cos y\le0$ and $D=s^2+y^2$. For $u(s,y)=\operatorname{Re}L_c(-s-iy)$, the multiplication formula for $\coth$ gives
\begin{align}
 u_s\frac{C-c}{S}&=B_1(s)+eB_2(s)-\frac\omega2+\kappa W_a(C,c),\label{pte:ga:horizontalprofile}\\
 B_1&=\frac{s(C-c)}{DS},\qquad B_2=\frac{C-c}{S},\notag\\
 W_a(C,c)&=\frac12\sum_{j=0}^{a-1}\frac{c_j-c}{C-c_j},\qquad
 c_j=\cos(y+2\pi j/a).\notag
\end{align}
Both $B_1$ and $B_2$ strictly decrease, because
\[
 B_2'=(C c-1)/S^2<0,\qquad
 \frac1{B_1}=2\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}
 \frac{s^2+y^2}{s^2+(y+2\pi n)^2}.
\]
All summands in the second identity are nondecreasing and some strictly increase; the differentiated series converges locally uniformly.

For $a\ge2$, the numbers $c_j$ average to zero. With
\[
 g(x)=\frac{x-c}{(C-x)^2},\qquad
 g''(x)=\frac{4C+2x-6c}{(C-x)^4}>0\quad(-1\le x\le1),
\]
Jensen's inequality gives $\sum_jg(c_j)\ge ag(0)=-ac/C^2\ge0$. Thus $W_a$ is nonincreasing in $C$. Equality throughout is possible at $a=2$, $y=\pi/2$, but the other terms still decrease strictly. For $a=1$, $W_a=0$ identically, so the same conclusion holds.

The bracket in~\eqref{pte:ga:horizontalprofile} runs from $+\infty$ to $-h$. It therefore gives a unique favored-side maximum $s(y)>0$, with $u_{ss}<0$. Reflection strictly suppresses the opposite side. The resulting horizontal envelope $J(y)=u(s(y),y)$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{pte:ga:horizontalconvex}
 J''=u_{yy}-u_{sy}^2/u_{ss}
     =-u_{ss}-u_{sy}^2/u_{ss}>0,
\end{equation}
by harmonicity at the maximum. At $y=\pi$ all the hyperbolic logarithmic factors have zero $y$ derivative for every integer $a$, and therefore
\begin{equation}\label{pte:ga:endpoint}
 J'(\pi)=\frac{\pi}{s(\pi)^2+\pi^2}>0.
\end{equation}
The horizontal tails satisfy $\operatorname{Re}L_c\le\log(1+s)-hs+O(1)$. Closing the strip encloses only the original pole at zero, and the vertical contributions vanish. Thus every high favored saddle is globally selected. These assertions are locally uniform for $\kappa>0$, $\eta>0$, $\eta\ne a$.

\section{Complete coverage from two convex envelopes \src{28}}\label{pte:sec:ga-coverage}
Let $\mathcal M(\xi)$ be the maximum of $\operatorname{Re}L_a$ on $|z|=\ee^\xi$, $\xi<0$. Up to a real additive constant, the modulus is
\begin{equation}\label{pte:ga:subharmonic}
 \log|\operatorname{arctanh}z|+\kappa\log|C_a(z)|
 +a\log|1+z|+\eta\log|1-z|-\nu\log|z|,
 \quad \nu=(a-1)\kappa+a+\eta.
\end{equation}

Conjugate maxima have the same radial derivative. At an interior complex maximum, harmonicity and its strict angular curvature give strictly positive envelope second derivative. \section{Integer points and full tail density \src{28}}\label{pte:sec:ga-density}
We state the elementary lattice estimate with its proof.
\begin{lemma}[Integer-triangle estimate; sources 28, 31, 34, 26]\label{pte:ga:lattice}\label{pte:lc:lattice}\label{pte:as:lattice}\label{pte:he:lattice}
Let $f\in C^2(J)$, where $J$ has length $O(d)$, and suppose $f''$ has one sign with $m/d\le|f''|\le M/d$. For fixed $A,m,M>0$, only $O(d^{2/3})$ integers $k\in J$ satisfy $\operatorname{dist}(f(k),\mathbb Z)\le A/d$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Split $J$ into blocks of length $H=c d^{1/3}$. Round candidate values to integers within $A/d$. The integer determinant of any three rounded points in a block has absolute value at most $MH^3/(2d)+4AH/d<1$ for small fixed $c$ and large $d$, so it is zero. All candidates in the block are collinear. After changing sign, assume $f''\ge m/d$. For a middle candidate $x_i$ between the extreme candidates $x_1,x_n$, the chord inequality gives
\[
 \frac m{2d}(x_i-x_1)(x_n-x_i)\le\frac{2A}d.
\]
Integer spacing implies $\lfloor(n-1)^2/4\rfloor\le4A/m$, hence $n\le2+4\sqrt{A/m}$. Each block contains only a constant number, and there are $O(d^{2/3})$ blocks.
\end{proof}

\[
 \left(\frac d{N-1},\frac k{N-1}\right)
 =\left(\frac1{a(\kappa+1)+\eta},
        \frac\kappa{a(\kappa+1)+\eta}\right),
\]
with Jacobian $[a(\kappa+1)+\eta]^{-3}$. Its full integral is
\begin{equation}\label{pte:ga:area}
 \int_0^\infty\int_0^\infty
 \frac{d\eta\,d\kappa}{[a(\kappa+1)+\eta]^3}
 =\frac1{2a^2}.
\end{equation}
This equals the leading candidate area in~\eqref{pte:ga:tailcount}. Compact exhaustion therefore leaves an arbitrarily small normalized number of omitted candidates. The whole tail has only $o_a(N^2)$ zeros. Adding~\eqref{pte:ga:bulkcount} and using the candidate upper bound proves Theorem~\ref{pte:ga:main}.

\section{An elementary exact count for generic real parameters \src{28}}\label{pte:sec:ga-generic}
The integer-exponent theorem contrasts with a simple algebraic genericity statement. Allow a real parameter $\alpha\ne0$ and write
\[
 \frac{d^N}{dx^N}x^{x^\alpha}
 =x^{x^\alpha}\sum_{\ell,k}p_{N,\ell,k}(\alpha)
                 x^{\alpha\ell-N}(\log x)^k.
\]
Differentiation gives the polynomial recurrence
\begin{align}\label{pte:ga:parameterrecurrence}
 p_{N+1,\ell,k}(\alpha)={}&(\alpha\ell-N)p_{N,\ell,k}(\alpha)
 +(k+1)p_{N,\ell,k+1}(\alpha)\notag\\
 &+p_{N,\ell-1,k}(\alpha)
 +\alpha p_{N,\ell-1,k-1}(\alpha),
\end{align}
starting from $p_{0,0,0}=1$ and with out-of-range entries zero. Thus every coefficient belongs to $\mathbb Z[\alpha]$. For $N\ge1$, the potential cells are $1\le\ell\le N$, $0\le k\le\ell$. None of these coefficient polynomials is identically zero: choose an integer $a>N$, for which every such cell lies in the strictly positive bulk of Proposition~\ref{pte:ga:bulk}.

\begin{corollary}\label{pte:ga:generic}
There is a countable set $E$ of real algebraic numbers such that every real $\alpha\notin E$ has, for every $N\ge1$, exactly
\[
 \frac{N(N+3)}2
\]
nonzero derivative monomials. In particular this exact count holds for every transcendental real $\alpha$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Take $E$ to contain zero and all roots of the nonzero integer polynomials $p_{N,\ell,k}$. It is countable and algebraic. For $\alpha\ne0$, distinct $(\ell,k)$ give distinct monomials: after $x=\ee^t$, finite sums of the functions $\ee^{(\alpha\ell-N)t}t^k$ with distinct exponents are linearly independent. All candidate coefficients are nonzero outside $E$, and their number is $\sum_{\ell=1}^N(\ell+1)=N(N+3)/2$.
\end{proof}
This corollary does not classify the exceptional algebraic parameters. Such exceptions occur even at order two, where $p_{2,1,0}(\alpha)=2\alpha-1$ and $p_{2,1,1}(\alpha)=\alpha(\alpha-1)$. Positive noninteger algebraic parameters therefore remain a meaningful separate frontier.

\mergenote{That frontier is the subject of Part~\ref{pte:part:defects}, on fixed defects $r=N-\ell$. Both exceptions displayed above lie on defect one, where the residual is $R_{\alpha,1,N}=-K/2$ (equation~\eqref{pte:rf:low}): at $N=2$ the reflection form~\eqref{pte:rf:center} is $K=1-2\alpha$ for $k=0$ and $K=1-\alpha$ for $k=1$, so $p_{2,1,0}$ vanishes at $\alpha=1/2$ and $p_{2,1,1}$ at $\alpha=1$, both on the reflection line. \section{An exact classification at the first two depths \src{28}}\label{pte:sec:ga-depths}
The branch cut of the original coefficient formula gives an additional exact result. For $d\ge1$, $k,q\ge0$, set
\[
 \mathcal R_a(x)=x^a-(-1)^a,\qquad P_d(\tau)=\operatorname{Im}(\tau+i\pi)^d.
\]
Deforming the coefficient contour to $w=-1-x$ is justified by its $O(w^{-q-2}\log^d w)$ decay and integrability at the branch endpoint. Folding $x\leftrightarrow1/x$ yields
\begin{align}
 c_{a,N}(-q-1,k)
 ={}&\frac{(-1)^qN!}{\pi k!d!}\int_1^\infty
 \frac{\mathcal R_a(x)^kP_d(\log x)}{(1+x)^{N+1}}\notag\\
 &\hspace{17mm}\cdot\left[x^{ad}+(-1)^{(a-1)k+d-1}x^q\right]dx.
 \label{pte:ga:folded}
\end{align}
The signs follow from $\mathcal R_a(1/x)=(-1)^{a-1}x^{-a}\mathcal R_a(x)$ and $P_d(-\tau)=(-1)^{d-1}P_d(\tau)$. In particular the reflection line is $q=ad$, with a forced zero when $(a-1)k+d$ is even.

\begin{theorem}\label{pte:ga:depthtwo}
For every integer $a\ge1$, all $k,q\ge0$, and $d\in\{1,2\}$:
\begin{itemize}
\item if $(a-1)k+d$ is odd, the coefficient is nonzero with sign $(-1)^q$;
\item if $(a-1)k+d$ is even, it vanishes exactly at $q=ad$, and otherwise has sign $(-1)^q\operatorname{sgn}(ad-q)$.
\end{itemize}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For $x>1$, $\mathcal R_a(x)>0$. Also $P_1(\log x)=\pi>0$ and $P_2(\log x)=2\pi\log x>0$. The remaining factor in~\eqref{pte:ga:folded} is either a strictly positive sum or a difference of the fixed strict sign stated in the theorem.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The count and the theorem}
Let $a(N)$ be the number of distinct nonzero terms after collecting like monomials in
\[
 \frac{d^N}{dx^N}x^x=x^x\sum_{h,j\ge0}c_N(h,j)x^{-h}(\log x)^j.
\]
Put $\Phi(w)=(1+w)\log(1+w)$ and define the first-kind Lehmer--Comtet numbers by
\begin{equation}\label{pte:lc:definition}
 b(M,d)=\frac{M!}{d!}[w^M]\Phi(w)^d,\qquad 0\le d\le M.
\end{equation}
Here $b(0,0)=1$ and $b(M,0)=0$ for $M>0$. These classical numbers are OEIS A008296; their relation to derivatives of $x^x$ is recorded in the primary literature and in the inspected repository~\cite{pte-jeong,pte-a008296,pte-host}.

\begin{corollary}[Source 31; Theorem~\ref{pte:ga:main} at $a=1$]\label{pte:lc:main}
The zeros of the Lehmer--Comtet triangle satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{pte:lc:zerodensity}
 Z(N):=\#\{(M,d):1\le d\le M\le N,\ b(M,d)=0\}=o(N^2).
\end{equation}
Consequently the derivative-term sequence OEIS A293239 satisfies
\[
 a(N)=\frac{N^2}{2}+o(N^2).
\]
This is an asymptotic statement; no global power-saving remainder or effective order threshold is asserted.
\end{corollary}

\mergenote{By~\eqref{pte:lc:count} below, the corollary is equivalent to Theorem~\ref{pte:ga:main} at $a=1$, with the same (ineffective) strength. Its proofs: source 28's, in Sections~\ref{pte:sec:ga-coordinates}--\ref{pte:sec:ga-density}, which at $a=1$ reduce to the endpoint paragraph of Section~\ref{pte:sec:ga-real}; and source 31's, which is the rest of this section.}

To obtain the exact counting bridge, expand at a fixed $x>0$:
\[
 \frac{(x+u)^{x+u}}{x^x}
 =\exp(u\log x)\exp\bigl(x\Phi(u/x)\bigr).
\]
Coefficient extraction gives
\begin{equation}\label{pte:lc:expansion}
 \frac{d^N}{dx^N}x^x
 =x^x\sum_{M=0}^N\binom NM(\log x)^{N-M}
                   \sum_{d=0}^M b(M,d)x^{d-M}.
\end{equation}
The pair of powers $(N-M,d-M)$ determines $(M,d)$ uniquely. Thus
\begin{equation}\label{pte:lc:count}
 a(N)=1+\frac{N(N+1)}2-Z(N).
\end{equation}
It remains to prove~\eqref{pte:lc:zerodensity}. The known reflection zeros $b(4j+1,2j)=0$ and the sporadic zero $b(8,5)=0$ are fully compatible with it. We do not prove these are the only zeros.

\subsection{The integral and the complete critical-point classification}\label{pte:sec:lc-critical}
Fix $d\ge1$ and $M>d$, and write $\rho=M/d>1$. The change of variable $w=\ee^t-1$ in~\eqref{pte:lc:definition} gives
\begin{align}
 b(M,d)&=\frac{M!}{d!}\frac1{2\pi i}\oint \exp(dL_\rho(t))A(t)\,dt,\label{pte:lc:integral}\\
 L_\rho(t)&=\log t+t-\rho\log(\ee^t-1)\notag\\
 &=\log t+(1-\rho/2)t-\rho\log\sinh(t/2)-\rho\log2,\label{pte:lc:action}\\
 A(t)&=\frac{\ee^t}{\ee^t-1}.\notag
\end{align}
The full integer-parameter integrand is single-valued; its poles other than zero lie at $2\pi i\mathbb Z\setminus\{0\}$. The saddle equation is
\begin{equation}\label{pte:lc:F}
 F(t):=(1+1/t)(1-\ee^{-t})=\rho.
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{A positive kernel}
For the modulus put
\[
 \omega=\rho,\qquad e=\frac{|\rho-2|}{2},\qquad
 h=\frac\omega2-e=\min(1,\rho-1)>0.
\]
Here $e$ is a tilt magnitude; Euler's number is denoted $\ee$. Reflect the real coordinate when $1<\rho<2$, so the favored direction is left. Up to a constant phase the reflected action is
\[
 L_c(t)=\log t-et-\omega\log\sinh(t/2)-\omega\log2.
\]
For $t=-s-iy$, $s>0$, $0<y<\pi$, define
\begin{equation}\label{pte:lc:UV}
 U(s,y)=\frac{s\coth s-y\cot y}{s^2+y^2},\qquad
 V(s)=\frac\omega2-e\coth s=h-\frac{2e}{\ee^{2s}-1}.
\end{equation}
The classical partial-fraction expansions of $\coth$ and $\cot$~\cite{pte-dlmf4} give
\begin{equation}\label{pte:lc:kernel}
 U(s,y)=2\sum_{n\ge1}
 \frac{(n\pi)^2}{(s^2+(n\pi)^2)((n\pi)^2-y^2)}.
\end{equation}
In particular $U_s<0$, $U_y>0$, and $U(s,0)$ decreases from $1/3$ to zero. If $\rho\ne2$, then $V'>0$ and $V$ increases from $-\infty$ to $h$. There is a unique $s_0>0$ such that $U(s_0,0)=V(s_0)$. For every $s>s_0$ there is a unique $y(s)\in(0,\pi)$ satisfying $U(s,y(s))=V(s)$. It is strictly increasing and tends to $\pi$.

Introduce the auxiliary critical map
\begin{equation}\label{pte:lc:G}
 G(t)=\frac{\sinh t}{t}-\frac\omega2(1+\cosh t)-e\sinh t,
 \qquad L_c'(t)=\frac{G(t)}{\sinh t}.
\end{equation}
Direct algebra gives
\[
 \operatorname{Im}G(-s-iy)=\sinh s\sin y\,[U(s,y)-V(s)].
\]
Along the curve $U=V$, the Cauchy--Riemann equations imply
\begin{align}
 \operatorname{Re}G'(-s-iy)&=-\sinh s\sin y\,U_y<0,\notag\\
 \frac d{ds}\operatorname{Re}G(-s-iy(s))
 &=-\operatorname{Re}G'(-s-iy(s))(1+y'(s)^2)>0.\label{pte:lc:criticalcurve}
\end{align}
The real critical value tends to $+\infty$: indeed $U=V\to h$ implies $y\to\pi$, and substitution into~\eqref{pte:lc:G} gives $\ee^{-s}G(-s-iy(s))\to h/2$. Thus the critical curve runs increasingly from $G(-s_0)$ to infinity, and all its roots are simple.

\subsubsection{The single real fold}
For $\rho>2$ one has
\[
 G(-s)=\frac{\sinh s}{s}-\sinh s-\frac\rho2(1+\ee^{-s})
 \le\frac{\sinh s}{s}-(1+\cosh s)<0,
\]
since $\tanh(s/2)<s$. Hence a unique favored complex pair exists for every $\rho>2$.

For $1<\rho<2$, the original favored side is positive real. On this axis
\begin{equation}\label{pte:lc:realmap}
 G(-T)=\frac{\ee^T+1}{2}\bigl(F(T)-\rho\bigr),\qquad
 F'(T)=\frac{\ee^{-T}(T^2+T+1)-1}{T^2}.
\end{equation}
The equation
\begin{equation}\label{pte:lc:fold}
 \ee^{T_*}=1+T_*+T_*^2
\end{equation}
has exactly one positive solution. To see this, differentiate $\ee^T-1-T-T^2$ twice: its second derivative changes sign once, forcing first a decrease and then a single crossing back to positive values. The inequalities $\ee<3$ and $\ee^2>7$ show $1<T_*<2$. Thus $F$ increases from $1$ to
\[
 \rho_*:=F(T_*)=\frac{(T_*+1)^2}{T_*^2+T_*+1}\in(1,2)
\]
and decreases to $1$. Also $dG(-T)/dT=\sinh T[U(T,0)-V(T)]$, so $G(-T)$ has its unique maximum at $s_0$. Its sign at that maximum is the sign of $\rho_*-\rho$, by~\eqref{pte:lc:realmap}. Combining this fact with~\eqref{pte:lc:criticalcurve} proves the classification:
\begin{itemize}
\item if $1<\rho<\rho_*$, there are two positive real saddles, and the smaller one satisfies $T<s_0$;
\item if $\rho>\rho_*$ and $\rho\ne2$, there is exactly one favored complex pair in $0<|\operatorname{Im}t|<\pi$;
\item at $\rho=\rho_*$ there is a real double saddle.
\end{itemize}
At $\rho=2$, the lower simple saddle is $t=-iT$, where
\begin{equation}\label{pte:lc:center}
 \tan(T/2)=T,\qquad \pi/2<T<\pi.
\end{equation}
The positive solution is unique because $\tan(T/2)/T$ is strictly increasing there, begins below one, and diverges. Moreover
\begin{equation}\label{pte:lc:branchsign}
 L_2''(-iT)=\frac{1-T^2}{2T^2}<0,\qquad
 \operatorname{Re}\left.\frac{dt}{d\rho}\right|_{\rho=2}
 =\frac1{2L_2''(-iT)}<0.
\end{equation}
The implicit continuation is therefore on the favored side for both signs of $\rho-2$. Uniqueness identifies it with the preceding branches. The complex-saddle domain is the connected interval $(\rho_*,\infty)$.

\subsection{Global selection by explicit contours}
The classification alone does not identify a Cauchy contribution. We next provide contours on which the selected saddles are the only global modulus maxima.

\subsubsection{The tanh coordinate}
Set $z=\tanh(t/2)$, so $t=2\operatorname{arctanh}z$ is analytic and univalent on $|z|<1$. Exactly,
\begin{equation}\label{pte:lc:differential}
 A(t)\,dt=\frac{dz}{z(1-z)}.
\end{equation}
Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{pte:lc:tanhcoefficient}
 b(M,d)=\frac{M!}{d!}\,2^{d-M}[z^M]
 (\operatorname{arctanh}z)^d(1+z)^d(1-z)^{M-d-1}.
\end{equation}
Every circle $|z|=R<1$ represents the exact original Cauchy cycle. Its image is a simple contour around zero in $|\operatorname{Im}t|<\pi/2$.

On a favored quadrant write $t=-s-iy$. Since
\[
 |\tanh(t/2)|^2=\frac{\cosh s-\cos y}{\cosh s+\cos y},
\]
the circle is described by $\cos y/\cosh s=(1-R^2)/(1+R^2)>0$. Along its arc,
\[
 y'(s)=-\tanh s\cot y<0.
\]
Direct differentiation, without imposing stationarity, gives
\begin{equation}\label{pte:lc:bent}
 \frac d{ds}\operatorname{Re}L_c(-s-iy(s))
 =\tanh s\,[U(s,y(s))-V(s)].
\end{equation}
The bracket strictly decreases, because its derivative is $U_s+U_y y'-V'<0$. The left side tends to $e>0$ at the imaginary endpoint.

For any favored complex saddle with height below $\pi/2$, choose $R=|\tanh(t_s/2)|$. Its equation $U=V$ places it at the unique strict maximum of the favored quadrant. At that point the second derivative in~\eqref{pte:lc:bent} is strictly negative. Reflection to the other half-contour lowers the real action by $2e|\operatorname{Re}t|$. The two conjugate favored saddles are therefore the only global maxima, with nonzero Gaussian curvature.

For the smaller positive real saddle $T<s_0$, choose $R=\tanh(T/2)$. Every interior point of the favored arc satisfies
\[
 U(s,y)>U(s,0)>V(s),\qquad s<T.
\]
Thus the arc is strictly increasing up to $T$, which is the unique global maximum on the full contour. Its angular curvature is positive, since $F'(T)>0$.

\subsubsection{Horizontal contours for high saddles}
Suppose $\pi/2\le y<\pi$. On the line $t=-s-iy$, put $C=\cosh s$, $S=\sinh s$, $c=\cos y$ and $D=s^2+y^2$. For $J(s)=\operatorname{Re}L_c(-s-iy)$,
\begin{equation}\label{pte:lc:horizontal}
 J'(s)\frac{C-c}{S}=B_1(s)+eB_2(s)-\frac\omega2,
 \quad B_1=\frac{s(C-c)}{DS},\quad B_2=\frac{C-c}{S}.
\end{equation}
Both $B_1$ and $B_2$ strictly decrease. Indeed
\[
 B_2'(s)=\frac{C c-1}{S^2}<0,
\]
and the periodized partial-fraction identity gives
\begin{equation}\label{pte:lc:B1}
 \frac1{B_1(s)}=2\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}
 \frac{s^2+y^2}{s^2+(y+2\pi n)^2}.
\end{equation}
Every summand with $n\ne0$ strictly increases, because $|y+2\pi n|>y$. The series and its differentiated series converge locally uniformly. The right side of~\eqref{pte:lc:horizontal} decreases from $+\infty$ to $-h$. It has a unique zero, the favored saddle, which is the unique favored-side maximum. Reflection strictly suppresses the other side.

Close the horizontal strip by vertical segments and let their real coordinates tend to infinity. Its only enclosed pole is zero. The estimate
\[
 \operatorname{Re}L_c(t)\le \log(1+|\operatorname{Re}t|)
                       -h|\operatorname{Re}t|+O(1)
\]
and boundedness of $A$ on the two horizontal lines justify the limit and exponential tail control. Thus the lower and upper favored saddles are the only contributing maxima. Their horizontal Gaussian curvature has positive real part. All these bounds are uniform on compact parameter intervals away from the real fold, $\rho=2$, and, if desired, the contour-switch points where $|\operatorname{Im}t_s|=\pi/2$.

\subsection{The additive cosine law and its phase curvature}
Use the original, unreflected lower saddle $t_s$, and write $S(\rho)=L_\rho(t_s)$.
On a tanh circle define
\begin{equation}\label{pte:lc:circleprefactor}
 z_s=\tanh(t_s/2),\quad C_s=\sinh^2(t_s)L_\rho''(t_s),\quad
 P_c=\frac1{(1-z_s)\sqrt{C_s}},
\end{equation}
where the square root has positive real part. The curvature satisfies $\operatorname{Re}C_s>0$. Parameterizing $z=R\ee^{i\theta}$ in~\eqref{pte:lc:differential} gives the Cauchy amplitude $1/(1-z)$, which explains the entire factor in~\eqref{pte:lc:circleprefactor}. On a horizontal contour the lower-saddle factor is instead
\begin{equation}\label{pte:lc:horizontalprefactor}
 P_h=-i\frac{A(t_s)}{\sqrt{-L_\rho''(t_s)}}.
\end{equation}
The lower boundary is traversed left to right, accounting for $-i$. Consistent local branches give the same saddle contribution when the contours can be compared.

\begin{proposition}\label{pte:lc:cosine}
On every compact interval in the complex-saddle region admitting one of the preceding contour choices,
\begin{equation}\label{pte:lc:cosinelaw}
 b(M,d)=\frac{2M!}{d!}\frac{\ee^{d\operatorname{Re}S}|P|}{\sqrt{2\pi d}}
 \left\{\cos\bigl(d\operatorname{Im}S+\arg P\bigr)+O(d^{-1})\right\},
\end{equation}
where $P=P_c$ or $P_h$. The error is uniform and additive, including at cosine zeros. The stationary action and the nonzero prefactor are locally analytic in $\rho$.
For a compact interval in $1<\rho<\rho_*$, the selected real saddle gives
\begin{equation}\label{pte:lc:reallaw}
 b(M,d)=\frac{M!}{d!}\frac{\ee^{dL_\rho(T)}}
 {(1-\tanh(T/2))\sqrt{2\pi d C_T}}
 \{1+O(d^{-1})\}>0
\end{equation}
for all sufficiently large $d$, where $C_T=\sinh^2(T)L_\rho^{\prime\prime}(T)>0$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The strict contour maxima provide a uniform gap off fixed local saddle neighborhoods. Inside a neighborhood the action is analytic with nonzero Gaussian curvature and the amplitude is analytic. Taylor expansion under the rescaling $\theta=d^{-1/2}u$ gives the leading Gaussian integral. Odd terms integrate to zero; the first remaining error is $O(d^{-1})$ relative to the single-saddle amplitude. The far arcs and horizontal tails are exponentially small. Summing the conjugate contributions gives~\eqref{pte:lc:cosinelaw}; no division by its cosine occurs. For a real saddle there is one contribution and all factors in~\eqref{pte:lc:reallaw} are positive.
\end{proof}

Stationarity and the affine dependence of $L$ on $\rho$ give
\begin{equation}\label{pte:lc:curvature}
 S'(\rho)=-\log(\ee^{t_s}-1),\qquad
 S''(\rho)=-\frac{\ee^{t_s}}{(\ee^{t_s}-1)F'(t_s)}.
\end{equation}
At $\rho=2$, relation~\eqref{pte:lc:center} implies
\[
 \ee^{-iT}=\frac{1-iT}{1+iT},\qquad
 F'(-iT)=\frac{1-T^2}{T(T+i)},\qquad
 S''(2)=\frac12+i\frac{T}{T^2-1}.
\]
Thus $\operatorname{Im}S''(2)>0$. The real-analytic function $\operatorname{Im}S''$ is not identically zero on the connected complex interval $(\rho_*,\infty)$; its zeros are discrete. The same holds for the artificial height-switch set, since the height near $\rho=2$ is strictly greater than $\pi/2$.

\subsection{Sparse integer points and exhaustion of the triangle}
We include the elementary counting argument so that the density conclusion does not depend on a presumed equidistribution theorem.

\mergenote{Source 31 states and proves here, as its Lemma~5.1, the integer-triangle estimate printed once as Lemma~\ref{pte:ga:lattice}; its proof, with the two details only source 31 spells out, is given there.}

On a compact ratio interval where $|\operatorname{Im}S''|$ is bounded below, define for each fixed $d$
\[
 f_d(M)=\frac{d\operatorname{Im}S(M/d)+\arg P(M/d)}\pi-\frac12.
\]
An exact zero in~\eqref{pte:lc:cosinelaw} satisfies $\operatorname{dist}(f_d(M),\mathbb Z)\le C/d$. Also
\[
 f_d''(M)=\frac{\operatorname{Im}S''(M/d)}{\pi d}+O(d^{-2}),
\]
so Lemma~\ref{pte:lc:lattice} applies. The number of zero cells with such ratios is $O(d^{2/3})$ per depth and $O(N^{5/3})$ after summing over $d\le N$. Compact intervals in the real-saddle region have no zeros for large $d$.

Fix a compact subinterval of $(1,\infty)$. First remove arbitrarily small neighborhoods of the real fold $\rho_*$ and of $\rho=2$. The remaining complex portions are compactly contained in $(\rho_*,\infty)$, so they contain only finitely many curvature or contour-switch zeros. Cover these by additional intervals, with the total removed length less than $\varepsilon$. The lattice count in the removed intervals is $O(\varepsilon N^2)+O_\varepsilon(N)$. Cover the remaining compact set by finitely many intervals to which the preceding estimates apply. Taking $N\to\infty$ and then $\varepsilon\downarrow0$ shows that the zero cells have density zero on every compact ratio interval.

Finally restrict the full candidate triangle to
\[
 \varepsilon N\le d\le M\le N,\qquad M/d\ge1+\varepsilon.
\]
The ratios now lie in $[1+\varepsilon,1/\varepsilon]$. The excluded candidates have area $O(\varepsilon)$ after scaling by $N$, and their boundary contributes only $O(N)$. The compact-ratio zero count is $o(N^2)$. Taking $N\to\infty$ first and $\varepsilon\downarrow0$ afterward proves~\eqref{pte:lc:zerodensity}. Equation~\eqref{pte:lc:count} completes the proof of Corollary~\ref{pte:lc:main}.

\subsection{An exact contour and the favored critical points}
Put
\[
 \kappa=k/d,\qquad \eta=q/d,\qquad \delta=\eta-2,\qquad \omega=\eta+2.
\]
The substitution $w=\ee^t-1$ in~\eqref{pte:as:H} gives exactly
\begin{equation}\label{pte:as:contour}
 H_{d,k,q}=\frac{M!}{d!}\frac1{2\pi i}
       \oint\frac{\exp(dL(t))}{4\sinh^2(t/2)}\,dt,
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}\label{pte:as:action}
 L(t)=\log t-\frac\delta2t+\kappa\log\cosh(t/2)
       -(\kappa+\omega)\log\sinh(t/2)-\omega\log2.
\end{equation}
For example, the unsimplified integrand is
$2^{-(M-k)-1}t^d\ee^{-(q-2d)t/2}\cosh^k(t/2)/\sinh^{M+1}(t/2)$.
The extra powers in the denominator include the Cauchy differential. The original integer powers make the integrand single-valued despite local logarithm choices; its other poles are at $2\pi i\mathbb Z\setminus\{0\}$.

We first work off the line $\eta=2$. Write $\ee$ for Euler's number, and let
\[
 e=|\eta-2|/2>0,\qquad h=\omega/2-e=\min(2,\eta)>0.
\]
The left half-plane is favored in modulus when $\eta>2$, and the right half-plane when $0<\eta<2$. Reflection of the real coordinate reduces both cases to
\begin{equation}\label{pte:as:centered}
 L_c(t)=\log t-et+\kappa\log\cosh(t/2)
                 -(\kappa+\omega)\log\sinh(t/2)-\omega\log2.
\end{equation}
For $0<\eta<2$, the reflected parameters are $h=\eta$, $\omega=\eta+2$, $e=(2-\eta)/2$. Reflection preserves real action values and sends a lower-left critical point of the favored model to the original lower-right point.

On the lower favored quadrant write $t=-s-iy$, $s>0$, $0<y<\pi$. The classical partial fractions~\cite{pte-dlmf4} give
\begin{equation}\label{pte:as:U}
 U(s,y)=\frac{s\coth s-y\cot y}{s^2+y^2}
 =2\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{(n\pi)^2}
 {(s^2+(n\pi)^2)((n\pi)^2-y^2)}.
\end{equation}
The series and its derivatives converge locally uniformly. Hence $U_s<0$, $U_y>0$, $U(s,0)$ decreases from $1/3$ to zero, and $U(s,y)\to\infty$ as $y\uparrow\pi$.

The critical equation is $F_{\omega,e}(t)=\kappa$, with
\[
 F_{\omega,e}(t)=\frac{\sinh t}{t}-\frac\omega2(1+\cosh t)-e\sinh t.
\]
Its imaginary part is
\begin{equation}\label{pte:as:imaginary}
 \operatorname{Im}F_{\omega,e}(-s-iy)
 =\sinh s\sin y\{U(s,y)-V(s)\},\qquad
 V(s)=h-\frac{2e}{\ee^{2s}-1}.
\end{equation}
The function $V$ increases from $-\infty$ to $h>0$. There is one $s_0>0$ with $U(s_0,0)=V(s_0)$, and for $s>s_0$ one curve $y(s)\in(0,\pi)$ solving $U(s,y)=V(s)$. It increases strictly from zero to $\pi$.

\begin{lemma}[Monotone favored critical curve]\label{pte:as:criticalcurve}
Along this curve, the real critical value
$\kappa(s)=\operatorname{Re}F_{\omega,e}(-s-iy(s))$ increases strictly to infinity. Every interior critical point on it is simple.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
At a zero of the imaginary part,
\[
 \operatorname{Re}F_{\omega,e}'(-s-iy)=-\sinh s\sin y\,U_y<0.
\]
The Cauchy--Riemann equations along the real-value curve imply
\[
 \kappa'(s)=-\operatorname{Re}F_{\omega,e}'(-s-iy)(1+y'(s)^2)>0.
\]
As $s\to\infty$, the height tends to $\pi$ and the real critical value has a positive term of leading size $h\ee^s/2$; the term $\sinh t/t$ is smaller by $O(1/s)$. Thus $\kappa(s)\to\infty$. The strict derivative also makes $F_{\omega,e}'$ nonzero. Since $L_c'=(F_{\omega,e}-\kappa)/\sinh t$ in the open strip, the saddle is simple.
\end{proof}

For $\eta>2$, every $\kappa\ge0$ has a favored complex saddle of height greater than $\pi/2$. Here $h=2$. With $C=\cosh s$, $c=\cos y$, $D=s^2+y^2$, eliminating $e$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{pte:as:elimination}
 \frac\kappa{C+c}
 =\ee^s\left\{\frac{s+y(\ee^{-s}-c)/\sin y}{D}-2\right\}.
\end{equation}
At $y=\pi/2$, the numerator after subtracting $2D$ decreases with $s$, because its derivative is
$1-(y/\sin y)\ee^{-s}-4s<1-\ee^{-s}-s<0$.
Its value at zero is $y-2y^2<0$. Thus the height-$\pi/2$ crossing has negative $\kappa$, and monotonicity proves the assertion.

\subsection{Horizontal contours for the high complex saddles}
\begin{lemma}[Global horizontal selection]\label{pte:as:horizontal}
Every favored complex saddle of height $\pi/2\le y<\pi$ is the unique global modulus maximum on its lower horizontal line. The upper line has the conjugate maximum, and both horizontal Hessians are nondegenerate.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
In the reflected favored model, put $J_y(s)=\operatorname{Re}L_c(-s-iy)$, $C=\cosh s$, $S=\sinh s$, and $c=\cos y\le0$. Differentiation yields
\[
 J_y'(s)\frac{C-c}{S}
 =B_1(s)+eB_2(s)-\frac\omega2-\frac{\kappa c}{C+c},
\]
where
\[
 B_1(s)=\frac{s(C-c)}{(s^2+y^2)S},\qquad B_2(s)=\frac{C-c}{S}.
\]
The partial-fraction identity
\[
 \frac1{B_1(s)}=2\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}
                  \frac{s^2+y^2}{s^2+(y+2\pi n)^2}
\]
makes $B_1$ strictly decreasing: the $n=0$ term is constant and every other term increases. Also $B_2'(s)=(C\cos y-1)/S^2<0$, while the last term is nonincreasing. The entire right side therefore decreases strictly from $+\infty$ to $e-\omega/2=-h<0$. Its unique zero is the classified saddle, and $J_y''<0$ there.

At the reflected point on the other half-line, the real action is smaller by $2es$. Thus the selected point is the unique maximum on the whole line. In original coordinates this gives $\operatorname{Re}(-L''(t_s))>0$.
\end{proof}

The small contour~\eqref{pte:as:contour} deforms to the boundary of $|\operatorname{Im}t|\le y$, with vertical sides tending to infinity. No other pole is enclosed. Uniformly on compact parameter sets, the real action on the ends is at most $\log(1+|\operatorname{Re}t|)-h|\operatorname{Re}t|+C$, so the vertical integrals vanish and the tails are negligible. The unique maxima and their Hessians give a uniform gap away from the two local saddle arcs.

For the original lower favored saddle define
\begin{equation}\label{pte:as:horizontalprefactor}
 P_h=\frac{-i}{4\sinh^2(t_s/2)\sqrt{-L''(t_s)}},\qquad
 \Theta_h=d\operatorname{Im}L(t_s)+\arg P_h.
\end{equation}
The square root has positive real part. The resulting compact-uniform asymptotic is
\begin{equation}\label{pte:as:horizontalcosine}
 H_{d,k,q}=\frac{2M!}{d!\sqrt{2\pi d}}
  \ee^{d\operatorname{Re}L(t_s)}|P_h|
  \{\cos\Theta_h+O(d^{-1})\}.
\end{equation}
The lower horizontal integral is traversed left to right, the upper right to left. They are conjugates, so division by $2\pi i$ extracts the lower imaginary part and produces the factor $-i$. Expanding the analytic exponent through its cubic term and the prefactor through its linear term, odd Gaussian terms cancel; the remaining normalized moments are $O(d^{-1})$. The error in~\eqref{pte:as:horizontalcosine} is additive, including at cosine zeros.

\subsection{Tanh circles select the low-height saddles}
\label{pte:as:tanhsection}
The remaining contour is particularly simple after one more change of
coordinate.  It is a circle in the variable $z=\tanh(t/2)$, although its
image in the $t$-plane is bent.

\begin{lemma}[Exact tanh-coordinate integral]\label{pte:as:tanhidentity}
Let $\widehat L(z)=L(2\operatorname{arctanh}z)$.  For every $0<R<1$,
\begin{equation}\label{pte:as:tanhintegral}
 H=\frac{M!}{d!}\frac1{2\pi i}
       \int_{|z|=R}\frac{\ee^{d\widehat L(z)}}{2z^2}\,dz.
\end{equation}
Equivalently,
\begin{equation}\label{pte:as:tanhcoefficient}
 H=\frac{M!}{d!}\,2^{-d-q-1}
       [z^M](1+z)^{2d}(1-z)^q
                 \operatorname{arctanh}(z)^d.
\end{equation}
The full integrand in \eqref{pte:as:tanhintegral} is single-valued at the
integer parameters.  The image of the circle is a bounded simple
positively oriented contour around zero in $|\operatorname{Im}t|<\pi/2$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The inverse map $t=2\operatorname{arctanh}z
=\log((1+z)/(1-z))$ is analytic and univalent on the unit disk: the
fraction in the logarithm maps that disk onto the right half-plane.
In particular its image lies in $|\operatorname{Im}t|<\pi/2$.  Directly,
\begin{equation}\label{pte:as:tanhdifferential}
 \frac{dt}{4\sinh^2(t/2)}=\frac{dz}{2z^2}.
\end{equation}
Furthermore $\omega d=2d+q$ and $\delta d=q-2d$, so
\[
 \ee^{d\widehat L(z)}
 =\frac{(2\operatorname{arctanh}z)^d
             (1+z)^{2d}(1-z)^q}
            {2^{2d+q}z^{k+2d+q}}.
\]
Substitution in the exact $t$-integral proves both identities, including the
coefficient index $M=k+2d+q+1$.  Inside the disk the only singularity of
the full integrand is its pole at zero, so each circle represents the
original residue contour.
\end{proof}

\mergenote{The coefficient identity~\eqref{pte:as:tanhcoefficient} is source 28's~\eqref{pte:ga:tanh} at $a=2$, and it is the host's transformed coefficient formula (its Proposition~3.6, \texttt{xxb:prop:mobius}), which source 34 does not cite, written in depth coordinates ($k_{\mathrm{host}}=k+d$, $j_{\mathrm{host}}=k$, $m=d$). The batch-72A intake checked the host's formula and~\eqref{pte:as:tanhcoefficient} against the exact coefficients for every $n\le16$ (for the latter, on all 168 cells with $q\ge0$, $d\ge1$), with no mismatch; that check is not shipped.}

For the modulus use the reflected favored model
\begin{gather*}
 L_c(t)=\log t-e t+\kappa\log\cosh(t/2)
                 -(\kappa+\omega)\log\sinh(t/2)-\omega\log2,\\
 e=|\delta|/2,\quad h=\omega/2-e>0.
\end{gather*}
Here the favored half-plane is the left half-plane.  Reflection changes
only phase conventions, not the real-action comparison; the final
normalizations below are always in the original coordinate.

\begin{lemma}[The bent-arc derivative]\label{pte:as:bentderivative}
On the lower favored arc of $|\tanh(t/2)|=R<1$, put $t=-s-iy$.
There is an $\alpha\in(0,1)$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{pte:as:bentgeometry}
 \frac{\cos y}{\cosh s}=\alpha=\frac{1-R^2}{1+R^2},\qquad
 0\le s\le s_{\rm end}:=\operatorname{arcosh}(1/\alpha),\qquad
 y'(s)=-\tanh s\cot y.
\end{equation}
For $J(s)=\operatorname{Re}L_c(-s-iy(s))$, one has the exact identity
\begin{equation}\label{pte:as:bentderivativeidentity}
 J'(s)=\tanh s\,[U(s,y(s))-V(s)],
 \qquad V(s)=\frac\omega2-e\coth s
            =h-\frac{2e}{\exp(2s)-1}.
\end{equation}
If $e>0$, the bracket is strictly decreasing on the open arc.  Thus its
modulus profile either increases throughout or has exactly one critical
point, a strict global maximum.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Writing $C=\cosh s$, $S=\sinh s$, and $c=\cos y$, the identity
\[
 |\tanh((-s-iy)/2)|^2=\frac{C-c}{C+c}
\]
gives \eqref{pte:as:bentgeometry}.  For
$u(s,y)=\operatorname{Re}L_c(-s-iy)$, differentiation gives
\begin{align*}
 u_s&=\frac{s}{s^2+y^2}+e
       +\frac\kappa2\frac{S}{C+c}
       -\frac{\kappa+\omega}{2}\frac{S}{C-c},\\
 u_y&=\frac{y}{s^2+y^2}
       -\frac\kappa2\frac{\sin y}{C+c}
       -\frac{\kappa+\omega}{2}\frac{\sin y}{C-c}.
\end{align*}
In $u_s+y'u_y$, all $\kappa$ terms cancel.  What remains is
\[
 \tanh s\left[
   \frac{s\coth s-y\cot y}{s^2+y^2}-\frac\omega2+e\coth s
 \right],
\]
which proves \eqref{pte:as:bentderivativeidentity}.  The kernel signs in
\eqref{pte:as:U} give $U_s<0$ and $U_y>0$; also $V'(s)=e\operatorname{csch}^2s>0$.
Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{pte:as:bentstrict}
 \frac d{ds}[U(s,y(s))-V(s)]
       =U_s+U_y y'-V'<0.
\end{equation}
At the initial endpoint $J'(s)\to e>0$.  The assertion follows.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Global selection below height $\pi/2$]
\label{pte:as:lowheightselection}
Suppose $e>0$, $h>0$, and a favored off-axis saddle has height strictly
between $0$ and $\pi/2$.  Its circle in the $z$-coordinate selects it and
its conjugate as the only global maxima of the leading modulus.  The
angular Gaussian curvature has strictly positive real part at each.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
In reflected coordinates write the lower saddle as $t_s=-s_s-iy_s$ and
choose $R=|\tanh(t_s/2)|<1$.  Its imaginary critical equation is
$U(s_s,y_s)=V(s_s)$, by \eqref{pte:as:imaginary}.  Lemma~\ref{pte:as:bentderivative}
therefore makes it the unique maximum on the lower favored arc, with
\[
 J''(s_s)=\tanh(s_s)[U_s+U_y y'-V']<0.
\]
The arc parameter is regular at this interior point, so the same strict
sign holds for the real part of the angular second derivative.  The
upper favored arc is its conjugate.  Both axis endpoints are strictly
below the two interior maxima.  At every point in the other open
half-contour, reflection to the favored side increases the real action
by $2e|\operatorname{Re}t|$.  No further global maximum is possible.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Low-height two-arc normalization]\label{pte:as:tanhcosine}
In the original, unreflected coordinate let $t_s$ be the lower favored
saddle from Theorem~\ref{pte:as:lowheightselection}, and put
\begin{align}
 z_s&=\tanh(t_s/2),&
 C_s&=z_s^2\widehat L''(z_s)
       =\sinh(t_s)^2L''(t_s),\label{pte:as:tanhcurvature}\\
 P_c&=\frac1{2z_s\sqrt{C_s}},&
 \Theta_c&=d\operatorname{Im}L(t_s)+\arg P_c.
\end{align}
The square root has positive real part.  Uniformly on every compact
family of such saddles,
\begin{equation}\label{pte:as:tanhcosinelaw}
 H=A_c\{\cos\Theta_c+O(d^{-1})\},\qquad
 A_c=\frac{2(M!/d!)\ee^{d\operatorname{Re}L(t_s)}|P_c|}
               {\sqrt{2\pi d}}>0.
\end{equation}
This is an additive error estimate, including at the cosine zeros.
The stationary action and the nonzero prefactor are locally analytic
functions of the parameters.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Parameterize the $z$-circle by its positive angle.  The residual
amplitude in \eqref{pte:as:tanhintegral} is then $1/(2z)$.  At a saddle,
$\widehat L'=0$ and $z\,dt/dz=\sinh t$, proving
\eqref{pte:as:tanhcurvature}.  The angular second derivative is $-C_s$, and
Theorem~\ref{pte:as:lowheightselection} gives $\operatorname{Re}C_s>0$.

On a fixed compact parameter family, all relevant derivatives are
bounded, the local negative quadratic bound is uniform, and the strict
modulus gap off the two saddle neighborhoods is uniform.  Taylor
expansion on symmetric angular neighborhoods of radius $d^{-2/5}$,
followed by Gaussian integration, has relative error $O(d^{-1})$ for
each single saddle contribution: the terms of order $d^{-1/2}$ are odd
and integrate to zero.  The other portions of those neighborhoods are
$O(\exp(-c d^{1/5}))$ on the leading scale, and the arcs outside them
have a fixed exponential gap.  The lower contribution is
\[
 \frac{M!}{d!}\frac{\ee^{dL(t_s)}P_c}{\sqrt{2\pi d}}
       \{1+O(d^{-1})\}.
\]
The upper contribution is its conjugate.  Their sum proves
\eqref{pte:as:tanhcosinelaw} without division by the leading cosine.
Local analyticity follows from the simple critical-point equation and
from the nonvanishing factors in $P_c$.
\end{proof}

Where the horizontal and bent contours are both admissible, these
are the same local Gaussian contributions.  With consistent lower-saddle
branches, $\sqrt{C_s}=i\sinh(t_s)\sqrt{-L''(t_s)}$, and hence
\[
 P_c=\frac{-i}{4\sinh^2(t_s/2)\sqrt{-L''(t_s)}}.
\]
This is exactly the horizontal prefactor.  Local branch choices suffice
for all phase-counting arguments.

\subsection{The entire strict real-saddle region}
\label{pte:as:realsection}
For $0<\eta<2$ the favored direction in the original coordinate is the
positive real direction.  Set
\begin{equation}\label{pte:as:realmap}
 f_\eta(T)=\frac{\sinh T}{T}-\ee^{-T}-1
                    -\frac\eta2(1+\ee^T).
\end{equation}
The real saddle equation is $f_\eta(T)=\kappa$.  Here
$e=(2-\eta)/2$, $\omega=\eta+2$, and $h=\eta$ in the reflected modulus model.

\begin{lemma}[A unique real turning point]\label{pte:as:realturning}
There is exactly one $s_0=s_0(\eta)>0$ with
$U(s_0,0)=V(s_0)$.  The function $f_\eta$ increases strictly on
$(0,s_0)$ and decreases strictly on $(s_0,\infty)$.  Its maximum
\begin{equation}\label{pte:as:realmaximum}
 \kappa_0(\eta)=f_\eta(s_0(\eta))
\end{equation}
is analytic for $0<\eta<2$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The function $U(T,0)$ decreases from $1/3$ to zero, whereas $V(T)$
increases from $-\infty$ to $\eta>0$.  Direct differentiation of
\eqref{pte:as:realmap} gives
\begin{equation}\label{pte:as:realderivative}
 f_\eta'(T)=\sinh T\,[U(T,0)-V(T)].
\end{equation}
This proves the unique turning point and the derivative signs.
The strict derivative of $U(T,0)-V(T)$ also proves analytic dependence
by the implicit function theorem.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Unrestricted smaller-real-saddle positivity]
\label{pte:as:realpositivity}
If $0<\eta<2$ and $0\le\kappa<\kappa_0(\eta)$, the smaller positive
solution $T$ of $f_\eta(T)=\kappa$ is selected by its tanh circle as the
unique global modulus maximum.  Put
\[
 C_T=\sinh(T)^2L''(T)=\sinh(T)f_\eta'(T)>0.
\]
Uniformly on every compact subset of this strict parameter region,
\begin{equation}\label{pte:as:realasymptotic}
 H=\frac{(M!/d!)\ee^{dL(T)}}
          {2\tanh(T/2)\sqrt{2\pi d C_T}}
          \{1+O(d^{-1})\}>0
\end{equation}
for sufficiently large $d$.  No upper bound on $T$ is required.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Since $f_\eta(0)=-1-\eta<0$ and $f_\eta(T)\to-\infty$, the assumed
strict inequality gives a unique smaller root $T\in(0,s_0)$.
Choose $R=\tanh(T/2)$.  On the favored bent arc ending at $T$, every
interior point has $s<T$ and
\[
 U(s,y(s))>U(s,0)>V(s).
\]
Thus the profile in Lemma~\ref{pte:as:bentderivative} is strictly increasing
up to its real endpoint.  Reflection strictly suppresses the unfavored
half-contour, making $T$ the unique global maximum.  The curvature
identity follows from $L'=(f_\eta-\kappa)/\sinh T$ at the real saddle.
It is positive by \eqref{pte:as:realderivative}.  The one-arc Gaussian
calculation is the same as in Proposition~\ref{pte:as:tanhcosine}, with
positive amplitude $1/(2R)$ and with no factor of two.  This proves
\eqref{pte:as:realasymptotic}; all of its leading factors are positive.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Coverage and connected phase regions}
\label{pte:as:coverage}
For $0<\eta<2$, the favored off-axis critical curve starts at
$(s_0,0)$ and its real critical value increases strictly from
$\kappa_0(\eta)$ to infinity, by Lemma~\ref{pte:as:criticalcurve}.
Consequently every $\kappa\ge0$ strictly below the turning value is
covered by Theorem~\ref{pte:as:realpositivity}; every $\kappa$ strictly above
it has one favored conjugate pair.  Theorem~\ref{pte:as:lowheightselection}
selects that pair below height $\pi/2$, and the horizontal theorem
selects it at larger height.

At height exactly $\pi/2$, the horizontal proof also remains strict:
the term proportional to $\cos y$ vanishes while the other derivative
terms remain strictly negative.  Alternatively this contour-switch
curve can be omitted from a density exhaustion, as it has area zero.
For $\eta>2$ the earlier horizontal result already selects every favored
saddle.  The lines $\eta=2$, $\eta=0$, and $\kappa=0$, together with the
real turning curve, also have area zero.


\subsection{Compact density away from the analytic exceptional sets}\label{pte:sec:as-density}
The two complex-saddle regions are
\begin{align*}
 \mathcal C_-&=\{0<\eta<2,\ \kappa>0,\ \kappa>\kappa_0(\eta)\},\\
 \mathcal C_+&=\{\eta>2,\ \kappa>0\}.
\end{align*}
Both are connected. For $\mathcal C_-$, raise $\kappa$ above the maximum of the continuous $\kappa_0$ on the compact interval between any two chosen $\eta$ values, move across that interval, and lower it again. The second region is immediate. On each, the favored saddle and its stationary action
$S(\kappa,\eta)=L(t_s(\kappa,\eta))$ are analytic.

The directional phase curvature relevant to a fixed derivative order is
\begin{equation}\label{pte:as:curvature}
 \operatorname{Im}D^2S(\kappa,\eta)=(\partial_\kappa-2\partial_\eta)^2\operatorname{Im}S,\qquad D=\partial_\kappa-2\partial_\eta.
\end{equation}
It is not identically zero in either connected region. To check this without numerical evidence, put
\[
 \mathcal K(T)=\frac{\sin T}{T}-2\cos T-2,\qquad
 \mathcal F(T)=(2T^2-1)\sin T+T\cos T.
\]
At $T=14/5$, alternating Taylor bounds give $\mathcal K(T)>1/250$ and $\mathcal F(T)>2$. Thus $\eta=2$, $\kappa=\mathcal K(T)$ has a nondegenerate saddle $t_s=-iT$ with $L''(t_s)<0$. Its continuation enters the favored side as $\eta\to2^\pm$, since
$\operatorname{Re}(\partial_\eta t_s)=1/(2L''(t_s))<0$.

The action is affine in $\kappa,\eta$, and
$L_{t\kappa}-2L_{t\eta}=1+\coth t$. Stationary differentiation gives
\[
 (\partial_\kappa-2\partial_\eta)^2 S
 =-\frac{(1+\coth t_s)^2}{L''(t_s)}.
\]
At the displayed base point its imaginary part is
$2T^2\cos T/\mathcal F(T)<0$. Therefore~\eqref{pte:as:curvature} is a nonzero real-analytic function on each complex region.

On any compact complex patch with $\operatorname{Im}D^2S$ of constant sign bounded away from zero, the selected contour gives
\[
 H=\mathcal A\{\cos(d\operatorname{Im}S+\arg P)+O(d^{-1})\},
 \qquad \mathcal A>0,
\]
with analytic nonzero prefactor $P$. At fixed $N,d$ let
\[
 \eta=\frac{N-1-2d-2k}{d},\qquad
 f_{d,N}(X)=\frac1\pi\left[d\operatorname{Im}S
 \left(\frac Xd,\frac{N-1-2d-2X}{d}\right)+\arg P\right]-\frac12.
\]
Its second derivative is bounded between fixed positive multiples of $1/d$ in one sign. An exact zero forces $\operatorname{dist}(f_{d,N}(k),\mathbb Z)=O(d^{-1})$, by the additive cosine law. Lemma~\ref{pte:as:lattice} bounds zeros by $O(d^{2/3})$ per row. Over a fixed compact parameter patch, $d\asymp N$ and there are $O(N)$ rows, yielding $O(N^{5/3})$ zeros.

The remaining exceptional sets have area zero: the line $\eta=2$, the real turning curve $\kappa=\kappa_0(\eta)$, the contour-switch curve $\operatorname{Im}t_s=-\pi/2$, and the zero set of~\eqref{pte:as:curvature}. The first two are explicit analytic curves. The latter two are zero sets of nonzero real-analytic functions on connected complex regions; nontriviality of the height condition follows from the same regular limit. A nonzero real-analytic function has an area-zero zero set: at each zero a finite-order directional derivative is nonzero, and Rolle's theorem followed by Fubini's theorem proves the assertion locally. A countable cover completes the proof.

Now fix a compact rectangle $\Omega$ inside $\kappa>0$, $\eta>0$. Cover the exceptional set in its scaled lattice image by finitely many open boxes of total area less than $\epsilon$. If necessary first cover the turning and switch boundaries, then the remaining compact analytic zero sets. Outside those boxes there is a finite cover by complex patches of nonzero curvature and real-saddle patches. The latter have no zero coefficients once $N$ is large, uniformly on each compact patch. The former have $O_\epsilon(N^{5/3})$ zeros. The boxes contain at most $\epsilon(N-1)^2+O_\epsilon(N)$ candidates. Taking $N\to\infty$ first and then $\epsilon\downarrow0$ proves that every compact $\Omega$ has only $o(N^2)$ exact cancellations.

\subsection{Exhausting the full tail and completing the count}
The ratio-to-lattice map is
\begin{equation}\label{pte:as:Jacobian}
 x=\frac d{N-1}=\frac1{2+2\kappa+\eta},\qquad
 y=\frac k{N-1}=\frac\kappa{2+2\kappa+\eta},\qquad
 \left|\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(\kappa,\eta)}\right|
 =(2+2\kappa+\eta)^{-3}.
\end{equation}
The image of a fixed compact rectangle has piecewise smooth boundary. Its candidate count is its area times $(N-1)^2$, with $O(N)$ boundary error. Exhaust the parameter cone by $\Omega_m=[1/m,m]^2$. The areas increase to
\begin{equation}\label{pte:as:tailarea}
 \int_0^\infty\int_0^\infty
       (2+2\kappa+\eta)^{-3}\,d\eta\,d\kappa=\frac18.
\end{equation}
All analytic constants are fixed before $N$ tends to infinity; there is no global uniform power-saving error claim.

The complete outside-bulk candidate list has $d\ge1$, $k\ge0$, $q\ge1$ and $N=2k+2d+q+1$. Let $m_N=\lfloor(N-2)/2\rfloor$. Its exact size is
\begin{equation}\label{pte:as:tailcount}
 \sum_{s=1}^{m_N}s=\frac{m_N(m_N+1)}2
                  =\frac{N^2}{8}+O(N).
\end{equation}
For each fixed $m$, the compact patch $\Omega_m$ has only $o(N^2)$ zeros. The number of candidates outside it, divided by $N^2$, has limsup at most $1/8-I_m$, where $I_m$ is its area under~\eqref{pte:as:Jacobian}. Letting $m\to\infty$ proves that the entire tail candidate set has only $o(N^2)$ zeros. Boundary rows such as $k=0$ are absorbed by this exhaustion and its exact total count.

The nonzero tail therefore has $N^2/8+o(N^2)$ cells. These give distinct monomials by~\eqref{pte:as:bridge}, all with $j\le-2$, so they are disjoint from~\eqref{pte:as:bulk}. Hence
\[
 a(N)\ge\beta(N)+\frac{N^2}{8}+o(N^2)
        =\frac{N^2}{2}+o(N^2).
\]
Together with the recurrence upper bound~\eqref{pte:as:upper}, this proves Corollary~\ref{pte:as:main}.

\subsection{The theorem and an exact reciprocal coefficient}
For $x>0$, define coefficients by
\begin{equation}\label{pte:he:expansion}
 \frac{d^N}{dx^N}x^{\sqrt{x}}
 =x^{\sqrt{x}}\sum_{\ell,k}p_{N,\ell,k}
             x^{\ell/2-N}(\log x)^k.
\end{equation}
For $N\ge1$, the potential cells are $1\le\ell\le N$, $0\le k\le\ell$, with distinct monomials. Let $A(N)$ be the number of nonzero coefficients.

\begin{theorem}\label{pte:he:main}
The number of zero candidates in~\eqref{pte:he:expansion} is $o(N^2)$. Consequently
\[
 A(N)=\frac{N^2}{2}+o(N^2).
\]
No global power-saving remainder or effective order threshold is asserted. The exact cancellation families in Section~\ref{pte:he:diagonals} show that reflection is not the only zero mechanism.
\end{theorem}

Put $d=\ell-k\ge0$. Taylor expansion gives
\begin{equation}\label{pte:he:taylor}
 p_{N,\ell,k}=\frac{N!}{k!d!}[w^N]
 (\sqrt{1+w}-1)^k(1+w)^{d/2}\log^d(1+w).
\end{equation}
Use the reciprocal coordinate
\[
 \sqrt{1+w}=\frac{1+z}{1-z},\qquad
 w=\frac{4z}{(1-z)^2},\qquad
 dw=\frac{4(1+z)}{(1-z)^3}\,dz.
\]
Then $\sqrt{1+w}-1=2z/(1-z)$ and $\log(1+w)=4\operatorname{arctanh}z$. The exact result is
\begin{equation}\label{pte:he:coefficient}
 p_{N,\ell,k}=\frac{N!}{k!d!}\,2^k4^{d-N}
 [z^{N-k}](\operatorname{arctanh}z)^d
 (1+z)^{d+1}(1-z)^{2N-k-d-1}.
\end{equation}
The map $4z/(1-z)^2$ is univalent from the unit disk onto the plane cut along $(-\infty,-1]$, with the displayed inverse and branches. Every circle $|z|=R<1$ is therefore an exact coefficient cycle.

For the interior $d>0$, introduce
\begin{equation}\label{pte:he:ratios}
 \nu=\frac{N-k}{d}>1,\qquad \kappa=\frac{k}{d}>0,\qquad
 \hat\eta=2\nu+\kappa-1>1.
\end{equation}
Writing
\begin{equation}\label{pte:he:Psi}
 \Psi(z)=\log\operatorname{arctanh}z+\log(1+z)
                  +\hat\eta\log(1-z)-\nu\log z,
\end{equation}
the residual differential in~\eqref{pte:he:coefficient} is
\begin{equation}\label{pte:he:differential}
 \frac{1+z}{z(1-z)}\,dz.
\end{equation}
All outside constants in~\eqref{pte:he:coefficient} are positive. The original integer powers make the full integrand single-valued despite local logarithm choices.

\subsection{Circle-only global saddle selection}
Set $t=2\operatorname{arctanh}z$. Up to the real constant $-\log2$, the action is
\begin{equation}\label{pte:he:taction}
 L(t)=\log t-\frac{\hat\eta-1}{2}t
           +(\nu-\hat\eta-1)\log\cosh(t/2)-\nu\log\sinh(t/2).
\end{equation}
The effective $\cosh$ exponent is $\nu-\hat\eta-1=-\nu-\kappa<0$. Thus no integer-exponent horizontal monotonicity theorem is being assumed.

On $|z|=R<1$, the favored side is left. Write $t=-s-iy$ in its lower quadrant. The circle satisfies
\[
 \frac{\cos y}{\cosh s}=\frac{1-R^2}{1+R^2}>0,
 \qquad y'(s)=-\tanh s\cot y<0.
\]
Define
\[
 U(s,y)=\frac{s\coth s-y\cot y}{s^2+y^2},\qquad
 V(s)=\frac{\hat\eta+1}{2}-\frac{\hat\eta-1}{2}\coth s.
\]
The partial-fraction identity~\cite{pte-dlmf4}
\[
 U(s,y)=2\sum_{n\ge1}
 \frac{(n\pi)^2}{(s^2+(n\pi)^2)((n\pi)^2-y^2)}
\]
implies $U_s<0$, $U_y>0$ and $V'>0$. The radial power in~\eqref{pte:he:Psi} has constant modulus on the circle. Direct differentiation gives
\begin{equation}\label{pte:he:angular}
 \frac d{ds}\operatorname{Re}L(-s-iy(s))
      =\tanh s\,[U(s,y(s))-V(s)].
\end{equation}
The bracket strictly decreases because $U_s+U_y y'-V'<0$. The derivative is positive near the imaginary endpoint. Thus each favored quadrant has one maximum, either a strict interior maximum or its negative real endpoint. Reflection lowers the real action by $(\hat\eta-1)|\operatorname{Re}t|$, so the opposite side is strictly suppressed. Every selected complex saddle has nonzero angular curvature.

\subsubsection{The positive terminal derivative}
Let $\mathcal M(\xi)$ be the maximum of $\operatorname{Re}\Psi$ on $|z|=\ee^\xi$, $\xi<0$. The modulus in~\eqref{pte:he:Psi} is subharmonic on annuli; comparison with a harmonic affine function of $\log|z|$ proves convexity of $\mathcal M$. Its initial slope is $1-\nu<0$.

As $R\uparrow1$, the maxima remain at bounded $s$, because the favored-end action is at most $\log(1+s)-s+O(1)$. The limiting circle is the line $y=\pi/2$. On that line $U(s,\pi/2)-V(s)$ strictly decreases from $+\infty$ to $-1$, giving a unique limiting horizontal maximum. The circle maxima converge to it.

For $u(s,y)=\operatorname{Re}L(-s-iy)$, differentiation of~\eqref{pte:he:taction} at $y=\pi/2$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{pte:he:terminal}
 u_y=\frac{\pi}{2(s^2+\pi^2/4)}
 -\frac{2(\nu-\hat\eta-1)+\hat\eta+1}{2\cosh s}
 =\frac{\pi}{2(s^2+\pi^2/4)}+\frac\kappa{2\cosh s}>0.
\end{equation}
Since $dt/d\xi=\sinh t=-i\cosh s$ at this line, the envelope has strictly positive terminal derivative. Convexity now forces a stationary minimum strictly inside $R<1$.

This minimum is unique. At a complex angular maximum, harmonicity gives strictly positive radial envelope curvature. A flat zero-slope interval could therefore contain only real stationary maxima, which would make the analytic action derivative vanish on an interval and hence identically, contradicting its pole at zero. Conjugate angular maxima have the same radial derivative, so the envelope is differentiable even when the maximum changes between the real endpoint and a conjugate pair.

We have obtained a selected real saddle or conjugate pair for every interior physical parameter using exact circles only. Except at degenerate real saddles, the selected saddle is simple, the global gap is strict, and the geometry persists uniformly on compact parameter patches.

\subsection{A real threshold and one connected complex region}\label{pte:sec:he-real}
Write the favored real point as $z=-\sigma $, $0<\sigma <1$. Its logarithmic radial derivative is
\begin{align}
 &\frac{\sigma }{(1-\sigma ^2)\operatorname{arctanh}\sigma }
       -\frac \sigma {1-\sigma }+\frac{\hat\eta \sigma }{1+\sigma }-\nu\notag\\
 &\hspace{12mm}=\frac{1-\sigma }{1+\sigma }\,[E(\sigma ;\kappa)-\nu],\label{pte:he:realderivative}
\end{align}
where
\begin{equation}\label{pte:he:realmap}
 E(\sigma ;\kappa)=f(\sigma )+\kappa g(\sigma ),\quad
 f(\sigma )=\frac \sigma {(1-\sigma )^2}\left(\frac1{\operatorname{arctanh}\sigma }-2\right),\quad
 g(\sigma )=\frac \sigma {1-\sigma }.
\end{equation}
One has $f(\sigma )<1$, since $\sigma /\operatorname{arctanh}\sigma <1$. Also
\[
 f(\sigma )=1-\frac43\sigma ^2+O(\sigma ^3),\qquad
 E(\sigma ;\kappa)\longrightarrow-\infty\quad(\sigma \uparrow1).
\]
For each $\kappa>0$, the linear term $\kappa \sigma $ makes $E>1$ near zero. Hence
\begin{equation}\label{pte:he:threshold}
 M(\kappa)=\max_{0<\sigma <1}E(\sigma ;\kappa)>1
\end{equation}
exists, is continuous and nondecreasing, and tends to one as $\kappa\downarrow0$. The dominant negative $(1-\sigma )^{-2}$ term confines maximizers away from $\sigma =1$ on bounded $\kappa$ intervals. On compact subintervals, $f<1$ and $\kappa g\to0$ uniformly; together with the limit $f(0)=1$, this proves the last assertion and continuity at zero.

When $1<\nu<M(\kappa)$, a first simple real crossing has $E_\sigma >0$. Equation~\eqref{pte:he:realderivative} gives positive radial curvature and hence a local angular maximum. The uniqueness proved in~\eqref{pte:he:angular} makes it the selected global real maximum. For $\nu>M(\kappa)$ there is no favored real stationary point, so the selected saddle is complex. This complex region is the connected epigraph above $M$.

The degenerate real locus has area zero. The equations $E=\nu$ and $E_\sigma =0$ solve affinely for $(\kappa,\nu)$ because $g'(\sigma )=(1-\sigma )^{-2}>0$; their solutions form an analytic curve image. On compact parameter rectangles away from $\nu=1$, $\kappa=0$, real stationary points stay away from both $\sigma =0$ and $\sigma =1$, so the exceptional image is compact there. Nongeneric tangencies do not produce an additional open complex component, by the stability of a selected complex saddle and the density of parameters with simple first real crossings.

A selected negative-real saddle contributes a single nonzero term. Its sign is $(-1)^{N-k-d}$, coming from $(\operatorname{arctanh}z)^d z^{-(N-k)}$; positivity is not required for the density argument.

\subsection{A compact-uniform cosine law and its curvature seed}
Let $z_s$ be the selected lower complex saddle and put
\begin{equation}\label{pte:he:prefactor}
 S=\Psi(z_s),\qquad C_s=z_s^2\Psi''(z_s),\qquad
 P=\frac{1+z_s}{(1-z_s)\sqrt{C_s}}.
\end{equation}
The square root is fixed by the positively oriented Gaussian circle, with $\operatorname{Re}C_s>0$. The ratio $(1+z_s)/(1-z_s)$ is the full amplitude obtained from~\eqref{pte:he:differential}.

\begin{proposition}\label{pte:he:cosine}
On every compact simple complex-saddle patch,
\begin{align}\label{pte:he:cosinelaw}
 p_{N,\ell,k}
 ={}&\frac{N!}{k!d!}\,2^k4^{d-N}
 \frac{2\ee^{d\operatorname{Re}S}|P|}{\sqrt{2\pi d}}\notag\\
 &\cdot\left\{\cos\bigl(d\operatorname{Im}S+\arg P\bigr)+O(d^{-1})\right\}.
\end{align}
The error is additive and compact-uniform. On a compact simple real-saddle patch, the analogous one-saddle expansion has a nonzero leading term.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The selected circle has a strict global modulus gap and nonzero local Gaussian curvature. Taylor expansion under the $d^{-1/2}$ rescaling and cancellation of odd Gaussian terms give a relative $O(d^{-1})$ error for each individual saddle. The complementary arcs are exponentially smaller. Summing the conjugate contributions gives~\eqref{pte:he:cosinelaw}; the error is never divided by the cosine.
\end{proof}

At fixed $N,d$, increasing $k$ follows $D=\partial_\kappa-\partial_\nu$. Affine parameter dependence and stationarity imply
\begin{equation}\label{pte:he:phasecurvature}
 DS=\log\frac{z_s}{1-z_s},\qquad
 D^2S=-\frac1{z_s^2(1-z_s)^2\Psi''(z_s)}.
\end{equation}
We give an explicit boundary expansion to prove this curvature is not identically zero.

Set $\kappa=0$, $\nu=1+\varepsilon$, $\hat\eta=1+2\varepsilon$. The saddle equation is exactly
\begin{equation}\label{pte:he:F}
 F(z,\varepsilon)=\frac z{\operatorname{arctanh}z}-1-\varepsilon
                   -2\varepsilon z-(1+\varepsilon)z^2=0,
 \qquad \Psi_z=\frac F{z(1-z^2)}.
\end{equation}
Let $\varepsilon=h^2$. Since
\[
 \frac{F(hv,h^2)}{h^2}=-1-\frac43v^2-2hv+O(h^2),
\]
the analytic implicit-function theorem at the lower root gives
\begin{equation}\label{pte:he:saddleseed}
 z=-i\frac{\sqrt3}{2}h-\frac34h^2+O(h^3).
\end{equation}
Its negative real part identifies the favored side. At $\kappa=0$, the inequality $f<1$ excludes a real saddle for every $\nu>1$, so uniqueness identifies this local branch with the selected complex saddle.

At that saddle,~\eqref{pte:he:phasecurvature} simplifies to
\[
 D^2S=-\frac{1+z}{z(1-z)F_z}.
\]
If $v_0=-i\sqrt3/2$, then $F_z=-(8/3)hv_0+O(h^3)$: the $h^2$ term cancels because the next coefficient in~\eqref{pte:he:saddleseed} is $-3/4$. Substitution gives
\begin{equation}\label{pte:he:seedcurvature}
 D^2S=-\frac1{2\varepsilon}
           +i\frac{\sqrt3}{4\sqrt\varepsilon}+O(1).
\end{equation}
Its imaginary part is positive for sufficiently small fixed $\varepsilon>0$. It persists at small positive $\kappa$ because $M(\kappa)\to1$. Connectedness of the complex region and real analyticity now show that $\operatorname{Im}D^2S$ has a zero set of area zero.

\subsection{Density in the full candidate triangle}
We recall the elementary integer-triangle estimate in the form needed here.

\mergenote{Source 26 states and proves here, as its Lemma~5.1, the integer-triangle estimate printed once as Lemma~\ref{pte:ga:lattice}.}

On a compact patch avoiding the phase-curvature zero set, an exact zero in~\eqref{pte:he:cosinelaw} lies within $O(1/d)$ of a half-integer normalized phase. Its second derivative in integer $k$, with $d,N$ fixed, is
\[
 \frac{\operatorname{Im}D^2S}{\pi d}+O(d^{-2}).
\]
Lemma~\ref{pte:he:lattice} gives $O(d^{2/3})$ zeros per row and $O(N^{5/3})$ on the compact patch. Simple real-saddle patches contain no zeros for large $d$.

First remove neighborhoods of the boundaries $\nu=1$ and $\kappa=0$, and restrict to compact parameter rectangles. Remove a small-area neighborhood of the compact real-degenerate locus before taking finite small-area covers of the remaining analytic exceptional set. The complement has a finite simple-saddle cover. Taking $N\to\infty$ first, then the removed area to zero, proves zero density on every compact physical rectangle.

The ratio-to-lattice map is
\[
 \left(\frac dN,\frac kN\right)
 =\left(\frac1{\nu+\kappa},\frac\kappa{\nu+\kappa}\right),
\]
with Jacobian $(\nu+\kappa)^{-3}$. Its full area is
\[
 \int_1^\infty\int_0^\infty\frac{d\kappa\,d\nu}{(\nu+\kappa)^3}
 =\frac12.
\]
This is exactly the interior area of $1\le\ell\le N$, $0\le k\le\ell$, with $\ell=k+d$. The boundaries $d=0$, $k=0$, and $\ell=N$ contain only $O(N)$ cells. Compact exhaustion therefore gives $o(N^2)$ zero candidates in the full triangle. Since its exact size is $N(N+3)/2$, Theorem~\ref{pte:he:main} follows.

\subsection{Two infinite nonreflection zero families}\label{pte:he:diagonals}
The defect $r=N-\ell$ counts the loss in the number of exponent factors. It is different from the depth $d=\ell-k$. Let $g(x)=\sqrt{x}\log x$ and $L=\log x$. The required low derivatives are
\begin{align*}
 g'&=x^{-1/2}(1+L/2),&
 g''&=x^{-3/2}(-L/4),\\
 g'''&=x^{-5/2}(3L/8-1/4),&
 g''''&=x^{-7/2}(1-15L/16).
\end{align*}
At defect one, the exponential Bell expansion contains one $g''$ and $N-2$ copies of $g'$. Thus, for $N\ge2$, the unique zero on $\ell=N-1$ is $k=0$. This is the reciprocal-reflection family.

\begin{theorem}\label{pte:he:families}
For every $N\ge3$ and $0\le k\le N-2$,
\begin{equation}\label{pte:he:defecttwo}
 p_{N,N-2,k}=\frac{2^{-k}N(N-1)}{96}\binom{N-2}k
                         (3k^2+13k-4N+8).
\end{equation}
Its zeros are exactly
\begin{equation}\label{pte:he:familytwo}
 N=\frac{3k^2+13k+8}{4},\qquad
 k\ge1,\quad k\equiv0,1\pmod4.
\end{equation}
For every $N\ge4$ and $0\le k\le N-3$,
\begin{equation}\label{pte:he:defectthree}
 p_{N,N-3,k}=-\frac{2^{-k}N(N-1)(N-2)}{384}\binom{N-3}k
                   (k+4)(k^2+9k-4N+12).
\end{equation}
Its zeros are exactly
\begin{equation}\label{pte:he:familythree}
 N=\frac{k^2+9k+12}{4},\qquad
 k\ge3,\quad k\equiv0,3\pmod4.
\end{equation}
Both families are infinite and lie outside the reflection line.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For defect two the block-size patterns are $3$ and $2+2$. With $u=L/2$, the corresponding logarithmic polynomial is
\[
 \binom N3\frac{(1+u)^{N-4}}{16}
       \bigl(3(N+1)u^2+8u-4\bigr).
\]
Coefficient extraction and cancellation of the binomial factors give~\eqref{pte:he:defecttwo}. The same identity holds directly at $N=3$, using $g'''$, so no division at a vanishing binomial denominator is used.

For defect three the patterns are $4$, $3+2$, and $2+2+2$. Their multiplicities are $\binom N4$, $10\binom N5$, and $15\binom N6$, respectively. The logarithmic polynomial becomes
\[
 \binom N4\frac{(1+u)^{N-6}}{16}
 \bigl(16+2(2N-7)u-4(2N+3)u^2-(N+1)(N+2)u^3\bigr).
\]
Extracting coefficients gives~\eqref{pte:he:defectthree}; $N=4,5$ follow directly from the available block patterns.

The zero equations reduce to the quadratics displayed in~\eqref{pte:he:familytwo} and~\eqref{pte:he:familythree}. Reduction modulo four gives their congruence classes. At defect two, $k=0$ formally gives $N=2$, outside the $N\ge3$ domain; every listed $k\ge1$ is admissible. At defect three, $k=0$ gives $N=3$, outside $N\ge4$; the listed $k\ge3$ are all admissible. The reflection condition is $N=(k+2d)/2+1$, which at defect $r$ reads $r+k/2=1$. It has no admissible point at defects two or three.
\end{proof}

The first members of~\eqref{pte:he:familytwo} are $(N,k)=(6,1),(27,4),(37,5),(76,8)$; those of~\eqref{pte:he:familythree} are $(12,3),(16,4),(31,7),(37,8)$. These sparse families approach the boundary corner $(\kappa,\nu)=(0,1)$, so their existence is consistent with the compact zero-density proof.

\section{The centered coefficient identity \texorpdfstring{\textup{(sources 20r, 20, 16)}}{(sources 20r, 20, 16)}}\label{pte:sec:shared}
\mergenote{Sources 20r, 20 and 16 each open with this setup in nearly the same words. It is printed once, in source 20r's words, with the sentences only source 20 or only source 16 contains. Source 16 writes the residual as $R_r(a;N,k)$, a polynomial in $(a,N,k)$, and the reflection form as $K_a$; in this section, including source 16's Lemma~\ref{pte:ir:degrees}, they are $R_{a,r,N}(k)$ and $K$, and Section~\ref{pte:sec:ir} keeps source 16's notation. Ten equation labels of sources 20 and 16 name equations of this section and were merged into source 20r's labels (the README lists them).}

For fixed $a>0$, write
\begin{equation}\label{pte:rf:derivative}
 \frac{d^N}{dx^N}x^{x^a}
 =x^{x^a}\sum_{\ell,k}p_{N,\ell,k}(a)
                      x^{a\ell-N}(\log x)^k.
\end{equation}
The factor defect is $r=N-\ell$. Throughout a physical cell means
\begin{equation}\label{pte:rf:physical}
 N\ge r+1,\qquad 0\le k\le N-r,\qquad N,k\in\mathbb Z.
\end{equation}
Distinct pairs $(\ell,k)$ represent distinct monomials because $a>0$.

Set
\[
 A_a(z)=\frac{(1+z)^a\log(1+z)}z,\qquad
 B_a(z)=\frac{(1+z)^a-1}{az}.
\]
Both power series have constant term one. Taylor expansion of $x^a\log x$ and the partial Bell-polynomial formula give
\begin{align}
 p_{N,N-r,k}(a)
 &=a^k\frac{N!}{(N-r)!}\binom{N-r}k R_{a,r,N}(k),\label{pte:rf:bridge}\\
 R_{a,r,N}(k)&=[z^r]A_a(z)^{N-r-k}B_a(z)^k.\label{pte:rf:residual}
\end{align}
The prefactor in~\eqref{pte:rf:bridge} is positive throughout~\eqref{pte:rf:physical}. At each fixed $r$, the residual is a polynomial in $N,k$ with rational coefficients when $a$ is rational. \src{16}~The coefficients of $A_a$ and $B_a$ are polynomials in $a$; the apparent singularity of $B_a$ at zero is removable coefficient by coefficient. Thus $R_{a,r,N}(k)\in\mathbb Q[a,N,k]$.

Write
\begin{equation}\label{pte:rf:center}
 K=ak-(2a-1)N+2ar-1,\qquad c=(a-1)(2a+1).
\end{equation}
\mergenote{Source 20r defines $K$ in its Theorem~1.1 and $c$ in its Section~3; source 20 defines both together, as here, and source 16 writes $K_a$ for $K$. On an odd defect, $K=0$ is the \emph{reflection line}.}

The substitution $t=\log(1+z)$ in the coefficient integral, including its differential, gives
\begin{equation}\label{pte:rf:even}
 R_{a,r,N}(k)=[t^r]\exp(-Kt/2)
 \left(\frac{\sinh(at/2)}{at/2}\right)^k
 \left(\frac{t/2}{\sinh(t/2)}\right)^{N+1}.
\end{equation}
Indeed,
\[
 A_a=e^{(a-1/2)t}\frac{t/2}{\sinh(t/2)},\qquad
 B_a=e^{(a-1)t/2}\frac{\sinh(at/2)}{a\sinh(t/2)},
\]
and the Cauchy differential supplies $e^{(1-r)t/2}(t/(2\sinh(t/2)))^{r+1}$. The exponential factors combine to $e^{-Kt/2}$, and the remaining factors combine as displayed.

Both hyperbolic ratios in~\eqref{pte:rf:even} are even functions. Thus the residual vanishes at $K=0$ whenever $r$ is odd. After expressing $k$ linearly in $N,K$, this is an exact polynomial factor. Equivalently, the reciprocal substitution $z\mapsto-z/(1+z)$ multiplies $A_a$ by $(1+z)^{1-2a}$ and $B_a$ by $(1+z)^{1-a}$, while the Cauchy differential contributes $(-1)^r(1+z)^{r-1}$. The combined power of $1+z$ is exactly $K$.

\subsection{Degrees of the residual \src{16}}
\begin{lemma}\label{pte:ir:degrees}
As a polynomial in $(N,k)$ at fixed $a$, $R_{a,r,N}(k)$ has total degree $r$, with leading homogeneous part
\begin{equation}\label{pte:ir:line}
 \frac{\Lambda_a(N,k)^r}{r!},\qquad
 \Lambda_a(N,k)=(a-1/2)N-ak/2.
\end{equation}
At every physical cell, $R_{a,r,N}(k)$ is a nonzero polynomial in $a$ of exact degree $r$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The first coefficients of $\log A_a$ and $\log B_a$ are respectively $a-1/2$ and $(a-1)/2$. In
\[
 [z^r]\exp\bigl((N-r-k)\log A_a+k\log B_a\bigr),
\]
parameter degree $r$ can come only from $r$ linear-in-$z$ factors. The constant shift $-r$ lowers parameter degree. This proves~\eqref{pte:ir:line}; the line is nonzero for every $a$.

Moreover $[z^j]A_a$ has degree $j$ in $a$ with leading coefficient $1/j!$, and $[z^j]B_a$ has degree $j$ with leading coefficient $1/(j+1)!$. Put $d=N-r-k\ge0$. The coefficient of $a^r$ in~\eqref{pte:rf:residual} is exactly
\begin{equation}\label{pte:ir:positiveleading}
 [w^r]e^{dw}\left(\frac{e^w-1}{w}\right)^k>0.
\end{equation}
Both series have nonnegative coefficients. Since $d+k=N-r\ge1$, at least one factor has strictly positive coefficients in every degree. This proves strict positivity and exact degree.
\end{proof}
\mergenote{In this proof $d=N-r-k=\ell-k$ is the depth, as everywhere in this report, and $e$ is Euler's number. Source 16's leading line is written $\Lambda_a$ here; source 28's $L_a(t)$ is an action.}

\section{Rational exponents \texorpdfstring{$a\ne1$}{a<>1} \src{20r}}\label{pte:sec:rf}
\mergenote{This section is source 20r, \emph{Reflection and Finite Exceptions: fixed defects in derivatives of $x^{x^a}$ for positive rational $a\ne1$}, which arrived only inside archive 20 as its companion. Its coefficient setup is Section~\ref{pte:sec:shared}; its closing section is printed in Sections~\ref{pte:sec:questions} and~\ref{pte:sec:verification}.}

\begin{theorem}\label{pte:rf:main}
Fix $a\in\mathbb Q_{>0}\setminus\{1\}$ and an integer $r\ge4$. With $K$ as in~\eqref{pte:rf:center}, only finitely many physical cells have $p_{N,N-r,k}(a)=0$, after excluding $K=0$ when $r$ is odd. No exclusion is needed for even $r$. In particular each fixed defect is eventually free of nonreflection cancellations. Every odd defect has the exact reflection factor $K$.
\end{theorem}

Here ``eventually'' allows a threshold depending on both $a$ and $r$. The proof provides no numerical threshold or complete list of the finite exceptions. It does not establish a support-density theorem when the defect grows with $N$.

\mergenote{Source 20r states the theorem in its Section~1 with the definition~\eqref{pte:rf:center} of $K$ inside it. Source 20's Theorem~\ref{pte:ac:algebraic} (defects $r\ge4D+2$, so $r\ge6$ at $D=1$) is weaker for rational $a$, and source 20 says so; source 16's Theorem~\ref{pte:ir:main} covers irrational $a$.}

\subsection{The weighted edge and defects at least six}
Put
\[
 k=\frac{K+(2a-1)N-2ar+1}{a},
\]
with $c=(a-1)(2a+1)\ne0$ as in~\eqref{pte:rf:center}. Assign weight two to $N$ and weight one to $K$. In the exponent of~\eqref{pte:rf:even}, the only terms whose parameter weight equals their $t$ degree are
\[
 -Kt/2+cNt^2/24.
\]
This follows from $\log(\sinh(at/2)/(at/2))=a^2t^2/24+O(t^4)$ and $\log((t/2)/\sinh(t/2))=-t^2/24+O(t^4)$. Hence the top weighted part of the centered residual is
\[
 [t^r]\exp(-Kt/2+cNt^2/24).
\]
For $r=2m$ use the whole centered residual as $F_r(N,K)$; for $r=2m+1$ divide it by its exact factor $K$. Its top weighted part is respectively
\begin{align}
 E_{2m}(N,K)&=\frac{m!}{(2m)!}\left(\frac{cN}{6}\right)^m
       L_m^{(-1/2)}\left(-\frac{3K^2}{2cN}\right),\label{pte:rf:edgeeven}\\
 E_{2m+1}(N,K)&=-\frac{m!}{2(2m+1)!}\left(\frac{cN}{6}\right)^m
       L_m^{(1/2)}\left(-\frac{3K^2}{2cN}\right).\label{pte:rf:edgeodd}
\end{align}
These identities follow from the standard Hermite--Laguerre relations~\cite{pte-dlmf18}. Both expressions are polynomials, of weighted degree $2m$ and degree $m$ in $N$, with nonzero constant leading $N$ coefficient.

Schur's theorem states that $L_m^{(-1/2)}$ and $L_m^{(1/2)}$ are irreducible over $\mathbb Q$ for every $m\ge1$; a primary research account explicitly records the theorem and its original references~\cite[pp.~1--2]{pte-schur}. Rational reciprocal rescaling shows that $E_r(v,1)$ is irreducible in $\mathbb Q[v]$. After extending to the purely transcendental field $\mathbb Q(K)$ and replacing $v$ by $N/K^2$, it remains irreducible. The constant leading $N$ coefficient makes it primitive over $\mathbb Q[K]$, so Gauss's lemma gives irreducibility in $\mathbb Q[N,K]$.

The top weighted parts of any two polynomial factors of $F_r$ would multiply to $E_r$. Since every nonconstant polynomial has positive weighted degree, irreducibility of $E_r$ forces one such factor to be constant. Therefore $F_r$ is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$.

The Laguerre roots are positive and simple. The roots of $E_r(v,1)$ are thus distinct and nonzero, whether $c$ is positive or negative. Write them as $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_m$. With $t=1/K$, $N=v/t^2$, the expression
\[
 t^{2m}F_r(v/t^2,1/t)
\]
is analytic at $t=0$ and specializes to $E_r(v,1)$. The implicit-function theorem gives $m$ distinct branches
\begin{equation}\label{pte:rf:branches}
 v=v_i(t),\quad v_i(0)=\alpha_i,\qquad K=t^{-1},\quad N=t^{-2}v_i(t).
\end{equation}
They are distinct points on the smooth projective normalization, and $K$ has a simple pole at each. They exhaust the points above $K=\infty$ because the degree in $N$ is $m$ with constant leading coefficient.

To finish for $r\ge6$, first suppose $F_r$ is not absolutely irreducible. Its absolute components are Galois conjugate. A rational point lies on every conjugate of any component containing it, so it belongs to a finite intersection of distinct components. Thus there are only finitely many rational affine points in this case.

Otherwise take the smooth projective normalization $C$. Its affine singular points form a finite set, and nonsingular rational points lift uniquely to $C(\mathbb Q)$. Write $a=\fp/\fq$ in lowest positive terms. At an integer cell,
\[
 \fq K=\fp k-(2\fp-\fq)N+2\fp r-\fq\in\mathbb Z.
\]
By~\eqref{pte:rf:branches}, the rational function $\fq K$ on $C$ has $m\ge3$ distinct geometric poles. The three-pole form of Siegel's theorem~\cite[Theorem 1.1]{pte-levin} makes the set of rational points with $\fq K$ integral finite, regardless of genus. The linear change $(N,k)\mapsto(N,K)$ is injective. This proves Theorem~\ref{pte:rf:main} for every $r\ge6$.


\subsection{Parameterized quartics at defects four and five}
Expansion of~\eqref{pte:rf:even} through degree five uses
\[
 U=a^2k-N-1=aK+cN+C_r,\quad C_r=-2a^2r+a-1,\qquad
 V=N+1-a^4k.
\]
Introduce the rational functions
\begin{equation}\label{pte:rf:parameters}
 b=-\frac{2a^3+a^2+a+1}{2a+1},\qquad
 e=\frac{a(a+1)}{2a+1},\qquad
 f_r=-\frac{2a(a+1)((r+1)a-1)}{2a+1}.
\end{equation}
Direct substitution gives $V=bU+eK+f_r$. Coefficient extraction is finite algebra:
\begin{align}
 5760R_{a,4,N}&=15K^4+30K^2U+5U^2+2V,\label{pte:rf:r4}\\
 11520R_{a,5,N}&=-K(3K^4+10K^2U+5U^2+2V).\label{pte:rf:r5}
\end{align}
Set $Y_4=5U+15K^2+b$ and $Y_5=5U+5K^2+b$. Away from $K=0$ in the odd case, the cancellation equations become
\begin{align}
 Y_4^2&=150K^4+30bK^2-10eK+b^2-10f_4,\label{pte:rf:q4}\\
 Y_5^2&=10K^4+10bK^2-10eK+b^2-10f_5.\label{pte:rf:q5}
\end{align}
Since $c\ne0$, each correspondence is injective: from $K,Y_r$, recover $U$ and then $N=(U-aK-C_r)/c$, followed by $k$ from~\eqref{pte:rf:center}.

\begin{lemma}\label{pte:rf:smooth}
Both quartics in~\eqref{pte:rf:q4}--\eqref{pte:rf:q5} are squarefree for every positive rational $a$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Let $Q_r(a,K)$ be the right-hand quartic multiplied by $(2a+1)^2$, so $Q_r\in\mathbb Z[a,K]$. Exact expansion gives
\begin{align}
 \operatorname{disc}_K Q_4&=27000000(2a+1)^6D_4(a),\label{pte:rf:d4}\\
 \operatorname{disc}_K Q_5&=8000(2a+1)^6D_5(a),\label{pte:rf:d5}
\end{align}
where $D_4,D_5$ are degree-$18$ integer polynomials. The definitions~\eqref{pte:rf:parameters}--\eqref{pte:rf:d5} specify them exactly; their full coefficient lists and an exact discriminant verifier accompany this note.

Here is a short certificate that neither has a rational root. The leading coefficient of $D_4$ is $512$, congruent to $2$ modulo $3$, and
\[
 D_4(a)\equiv2\pmod{(3,\ a^3-a)}.
\]
The leading coefficient of $D_5$ is $4608$, congruent to $10$ modulo $11$. Its remainder modulo $11$ and $a^{11}-a$ is
\begin{align*}
 &-2a^{10}-a^9+3a^8+3a^7+5a^6+5a^5\\
 &\hspace{20mm}-4a^4+4a^3+2a^2+4a-5.
\end{align*}
At $a=0,1,\ldots,10$ its successive values modulo $11$ are
\[
 6,3,8,8,8,8,3,2,9,7,6,
\]
all nonzero. Suppose a reduced rational number $u/v$ were a root. If the selected prime divides $v$, the homogenized identity contradicts the nonzero leading coefficient modulo that prime. Otherwise $u/v$ reduces to a field element, contradicting the displayed root-free values. Thus both $D_r$ have no rational root. Finally $2a+1\ne0$ for $a>0$, so neither discriminant vanishes.
\end{proof}
\mergenote{The coefficient lists of $D_4$ and $D_5$ and the exact verifier that ``accompany this note'' are shipped with this report as \texttt{data/\allowbreak{}20r-\allowbreak{}rational-\allowbreak{}certificates.\allowbreak{}json} and \texttt{code/\allowbreak{}20r-\allowbreak{}rational-\allowbreak{}verify.\allowbreak{}py}, with the independent replay \texttt{code/\allowbreak{}20r-\allowbreak{}rational-\allowbreak{}replay\_\allowbreak{}independent.\allowbreak{}py} and its output (Section~\ref{pte:sec:verification}).}

A squarefree degree-four equation $Y^2=Q(K)$ has a genus-one smooth projective completion: the double cover of the $K$-line has four simple branch points. Siegel's positive-genus theorem~\cite[Theorem 4]{pte-lang}, applied to the rational function $\fq K$, therefore makes the rational points with $\fq K\in\mathbb Z$ finite. The injective correspondence proves Theorem~\ref{pte:rf:main} for $r=4,5$. This completes its proof in all cases.

The modular certificates rule out exceptional \emph{rational parameters}; they are not searches over parameter values. They do not exclude singular quartics at every irrational algebraic parameter.

\subsection{Reflection lines and the first three defects}
For $a=\fp/\fq$ in lowest positive terms, solving $K=0$ over integers gives exactly
\begin{equation}\label{pte:rf:line}
 N=1+\fp h,\qquad k=(2\fp-\fq)h-2(r-1),\qquad h\in\mathbb Z,
\end{equation}
subject to~\eqref{pte:rf:physical}. Indeed, reduction of $K=0$ modulo $\fp$ gives $N\equiv1\pmod \fp$.

If $1/2<a<1$, then $0<2\fp-\fq<\fp$. On every fixed odd defect, all sufficiently large positive $h$ in~\eqref{pte:rf:line} are physical. These reflection cells have cumulative count $X/\fp+O_{a,r}(1)$. At $a=1/2$, the line is $k=-2(r-1)$, so only $r=1$ supplies a physical infinite family. For $a<1/2$ or $a>1$, the line eventually lies outside the physical range.

The first three exact residuals are
\begin{equation}\label{pte:rf:low}
 R_{a,1,N}=-K/2,\qquad
 R_{a,2,N}=(3K^2+U)/24,\qquad
 R_{a,3,N}=-K(K^2+U)/48,
\end{equation}
where $U=aK+cN-2a^2r+a-1$ uses the corresponding defect. In particular, the nonreflection zero equations at defects two and three are
\begin{align}
 N&=-\frac{3K^2+aK-4a^2+a-1}{c},\label{pte:rf:parabola2}\\
 N&=-\frac{K^2+aK-6a^2+a-1}{c}.\label{pte:rf:parabola3}
\end{align}
\mergenote{At $a=1/2$ these residuals are source 26's exact diagonal formulas (Theorem~\ref{pte:he:families}; the computation is recorded after it). Source 26 derives them by a different route, from the exponential Bell blocks of $g(x)=\sqrt x\log x$.}

\begin{corollary}\label{pte:rf:infinite}
For every rational $1/2<a<1$, defects two and three each have infinitely many physical nonreflection cancellations. On every fixed defect $r\ge4$, all sufficiently late cancellations are precisely the physical reflection cells when $r$ is odd, and there are no cancellations when $r$ is even.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Each parabola contains the structural rational point $(N,K)=(r,r-1)$ with $k=0$, outside the physical domain. Set $K=r-1+Mh$. Because the formulas for $N$ and recovered $k$ are rational polynomials in $h$ with integer constant terms, a sufficiently divisible positive integer $M$ makes both integer for every integer $h$. When $1/2<a<1$, $c<0$, so $N\to+\infty$ as $|h|\to\infty$, while
\[
 \frac{k}{N}\longrightarrow2-\frac1a\in(0,1).
\]
Consequently the cells are physical and $K\ne0$ for all sufficiently large $|h|$. The last assertion follows from Theorem~\ref{pte:rf:main} and the exact odd factor.
\end{proof}
\mergenote{Source 20's Theorem~\ref{pte:ac:counts} counts the families of the first assertion exactly, up to $O(1)$. They are not reflection zeros, so the host's observation that every cancellation it proves is a parity zero (Section~\ref{pte:sec:host}) does not extend to $x^{x^a}$ for these $a$. In the proof, $M$ is a positive integer and $h$ an integer parameter, as in~\eqref{pte:rf:line}.}

There is also an elementary description outside that interval. Uniformly for $0\le k\le N-r$ at fixed $a,r$,
\begin{equation}\label{pte:rf:ordinary}
 R_{a,r,N}(k)=\frac{[N(a-1/2)-ak/2]^r}{r!}
                    +O_{a,r}(N^{r-1}).
\end{equation}
This is the highest ordinary-degree part of~\eqref{pte:rf:residual}; the fixed shift $r$ affects only lower degree. For $a>1$, the bracket is at least $(a-1)N/2$, so every fixed defect is eventually positive. For $0<a<1/2$, it is at most $-(1/2-a)N$, so the eventual sign is $(-1)^r$. These statements concern each fixed defect, not the whole derivative support.
\mergenote{The bracket in~\eqref{pte:rf:ordinary} is source 16's leading line $\Lambda_a(N,k)$ of Lemma~\ref{pte:ir:degrees}.}

The exception $a=1$ is structural. Then $c=0$, $K=k-N+2r-1$, and~\eqref{pte:rf:residual} becomes
\[
 [z^r]\left(\frac{(1+z)\log(1+z)}z\right)^{r-1-K},
\]
independent of $N$ after centering. Its possible integer roots produce entire coefficient lines, so the preceding finite-exception proof does not apply.
\mergenote{At $a=1$ a fixed defect is a fixed Lehmer--Comtet offset. The batch-72A intake's exact scan (Section~\ref{pte:sec:questions}) shows such a line: the host's sporadic zero $b(8,5)=0$ recurs at every $k$, at the cells $(N,\ell,k)=(k+8,k+5,k)$.}

\subsection{An algebraic-degree bound}
\begin{theorem}\label{pte:ac:algebraic}
Let $a>0$, $a\ne1$ be algebraic of degree $D=[\mathbb Q(a):\mathbb Q]$. Fix a defect $r$ with
\[
 \left\lfloor\frac r2\right\rfloor>2D,
 \quad\text{equivalently}\quad r\ge4D+2.
\]
Only finitely many physical cells have $p_{N,N-r,k}(a)=0$ after excluding $K=0$ for odd $r$. If $a$ is irrational, no physical cell lies on that reflection line, so all coefficients on the fixed defect are nonzero for sufficiently large $N$.
\end{theorem}

\mergenote{Theorem~\ref{pte:ac:algebraic} is implied by the other two fixed-defect theorems: for rational $a$ ($D=1$, $r\ge6$) by source 20r's Theorem~\ref{pte:rf:main} ($r\ge4$), and for irrational $a$ by source 16's Theorem~\ref{pte:ir:main}, which bounds the cancellation cells by $(2\lfloor r/2\rfloor)^2-1$ on every defect $r\ge2$, effectively. Source 20 says the first (after the proof below); source 16 calls the theorem ``a prior sufficient algebraic-degree threshold'' without naming it. The proof below, by field degrees and Siegel's theorem over $\mathbb Q(a)$, is a second route and is printed as such.}

\begin{proof}[Proof \src{20}]
Put $\mathbb L=\mathbb Q(a)$ and $m=\lfloor r/2\rfloor$. Express $k=(K+(2a-1)N-2ar+1)/a$ in~\eqref{pte:rf:even}. Let $F_r(N,K)$ be this centered residual for even $r$, and its quotient by $K$ for odd $r$. It is a polynomial over $\mathbb L$.

Assign weights two and one to $N$ and $K$. Its top weighted part $E_r$ has weight $2m$ and is given by~\eqref{pte:rf:edgeeven}--\eqref{pte:rf:edgeodd}. Indeed the highest-weight terms of the exponent in~\eqref{pte:rf:even} are $-Kt/2+cNt^2/24$, and the Hermite--Laguerre relations give those formulas. The assumption $a>0$, $a\ne1$ makes $c\ne0$.

Schur's theorem makes each Laguerre polynomial in~\eqref{pte:rf:edgeeven}--\eqref{pte:rf:edgeodd} irreducible of degree $m$ over $\mathbb Q$~\cite{pte-schur}. If $\theta$ is one of its roots, the field-degree inequality gives
\[
 [\mathbb L(\theta):\mathbb L]\ge\frac{[\mathbb Q(\theta):\mathbb Q]}{[\mathbb L:\mathbb Q]}
 =\frac mD.
\]
The roots of $E_r(v,1)$ have the form $-3/(2c\theta)$, so their degrees over $\mathbb L$ satisfy the same bound.

Factor $E_r(v,1)$ over $\mathbb L$ into monic irreducibles $f_i(v)$ of degrees $d_i$. Each $d_i\ge\lceil m/D\rceil\ge3$. Its corresponding homogeneous factor is
\[
 H_i(N,K)=K^{2d_i}f_i(N/K^2).
\]
Purely transcendental extension and Gauss's lemma show that $H_i$ is irreducible in $\mathbb L[N,K]$. The edge is squarefree because the Laguerre roots are simple.

Now factor $F_r$ into irreducibles over $\mathbb L$. The top weighted part of each nonconstant factor is a nonempty product of distinct $H_i$. There is no nonconstant factor of degree zero in $N$, since the leading $N$ coefficient of $F_r$ is a nonzero constant. Thus each factor has degree at least $\lceil m/D\rceil$ in $N$. More explicitly, if its edge has weight $2n$, it contains a nonzero $N^n$ term; weighted degree bounds its $N$ degree by $n$, so that degree is exactly $n$.

Each such factor has distinct infinity branches. Setting $t=1/K$, $N=v/t^2$ and multiplying by $t^{2n}$ gives an analytic equation whose specialization at $t=0$ has $n$ simple roots. The implicit-function theorem gives $n$ distinct normalization points, with local coordinate $t$, at which $K=t^{-1}$ has simple poles. Every component therefore has at least three geometric poles of $K$.

If an $\mathbb L$-irreducible factor is not absolutely irreducible, its $\mathbb L$-rational points lie in finite intersections of distinct Galois-conjugate absolute components. Otherwise, apart from finitely many singular points, they lift to the smooth projective normalization. Choose a fixed positive integer $b$ with $ba$ an algebraic integer. At every integer pair $(N,k)$,
\[
 bK=(ba)(k-2N+2r)+b(N-1)\in\mathcal O_{\mathbb L}.
\]
The function $bK$ has at least three poles. Siegel's theorem over $\mathbb L$~\cite[Theorem 1.1]{pte-levin} therefore gives only finitely many $\mathbb L$-rational points where $bK$ is integral. Taking the finite union over all components proves finiteness of cancellation cells off the discarded factor.

Finally, if $a$ is irrational, the equation
\[
 a(k-2N+2r)+(N-1)=0
\]
with integer variables forces $N=1$ and $k=2-2r$. This contradicts~\eqref{pte:rf:physical} for $r\ge1$, proving the last assertion.
\end{proof}
\mergenote{Source 20 displays the edge formulas again in its proof, under the label \texttt{ac:edges}; they are source 20r's~\eqref{pte:rf:edgeeven}--\eqref{pte:rf:edgeodd} and are cited instead.}

The degree threshold is sufficient, not claimed optimal. For rational $a\ne1$, the companion theorem (Theorem~\ref{pte:rf:main}) improves the threshold from six to four using two explicit genus-one quartics. The present argument does not classify every smaller defect at an arbitrary algebraic exponent.

\subsection{Exact low-defect constants for rational exponents}
Fix a reduced rational $a=\fp/\fq$ with $1/2<a<1$. Put
\[
 C=(\fq-\fp)(2\fp+\fq)>0,\qquad \widetilde K=\fq K.
\]
For $r=2$ set $t_r=3$, and for $r=3$ set $t_r=1$. Direct expansion of~\eqref{pte:rf:even} gives the nonreflection zero equations
\begin{align}
 N&=\frac{t_r\widetilde K^2+\fp\widetilde K-2\fp^2r+\fp\fq-\fq^2}{C},\label{pte:ac:parabola}\\
 k&=\frac{\widetilde K+(2\fp-\fq)N-2\fp r+\fq}{\fp}.\label{pte:ac:recover}
\end{align}
For instance the centered residuals are $(3K^2+U)/24$ and $-K(K^2+U)/48$, with $U=aK+cN-2a^2r+a-1$.

Let $S_r$ be the number of classes $\widetilde K\pmod{\fp C}$ making both expressions~\eqref{pte:ac:parabola}--\eqref{pte:ac:recover} integral. This is an explicit finite congruence count.

\begin{theorem}\label{pte:ac:counts}
The number of physical nonreflection cancellation cells on defect $r\in\{2,3\}$ through order $X$ is
\begin{equation}\label{pte:ac:sqrt}
 \frac{2S_r}{\fp\sqrt{Ct_r}}\sqrt X+O_{a,r}(1).
\end{equation}
Moreover $S_r>0$, and it is the product of the number of roots of
\[
 t_r\widetilde K^2+\fp\widetilde K-2\fp^2r+\fp\fq-\fq^2\equiv0\pmod C
\]
and the number of roots of
\[
 t_ry^2-y-2\equiv0\pmod \fp.
\]
The latter polynomials factor as $(3y+2)(y-1)$ for $r=2$, and $(y-2)(y+1)$ for $r=3$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Since $C\equiv\fq^2\pmod \fp$ and $\gcd(\fp,\fq)=1$, also $\gcd(\fp,C)=1$. The first integrality condition is exactly the stated congruence modulo $C$. Modulo $\fp$, it gives
\[
 N\equiv t_r(\widetilde K/\fq)^2-1.
\]
Substituting in~\eqref{pte:ac:recover} makes the second integrality condition precisely $t_ry^2-y-2\equiv0$, where $y=\widetilde K/\fq$ modulo $\fp$. Multiplication by $\fq$ is a bijection modulo $\fp$, and the Chinese remainder theorem proves the product formula. The structural point $\widetilde K=\fq(r-1)$, $N=r$, $k=0$ proves that at least one class exists; it is used only to establish solvability, not as a physical cell.

As $|\widetilde K|\to\infty$, the first equation gives $N\sim t_r\widetilde K^2/C$ and the second gives $k/N\to2-1/a\in(0,1)$. Thus both tails are physical, after deleting finitely many initial points. The constraint $N\le X$ gives a real interval of length
\[
 2\sqrt{C/t_r}\sqrt X+O_{a,r}(1)
\]
in the variable $\widetilde K$. Each admissible class modulo $\fp C$ contributes its interval length divided by $\fp C$, up to $O(1)$. Summing the $S_r$ classes proves~\eqref{pte:ac:sqrt}. The coordinate $\widetilde K$ determines $(N,k)$ injectively. Omitting $\widetilde K=0$ in the odd case affects at most a bounded term.
\end{proof}

For example, $a=3/4$ gives $C=10$ and $S_2=S_3=4$. Its two square-root constants are
\[
 \frac8{3\sqrt{30}},\qquad \frac8{3\sqrt{10}}.
\]
At the endpoint $a=1/2$, only the positive $\widetilde K$ tail is physical. The same congruence calculation has $C=4$, $S_2=S_3=2$, and halving~\eqref{pte:ac:sqrt} recovers the constants $1/\sqrt3$ and $1$.
\mergenote{The endpoint constants count source 26's two families of Theorem~\ref{pte:he:families}: through order $X$ they have $\sqrt{X/3}+O(1)$ and $\sqrt X+O(1)$ members. For $a=3/4$ the batch-72A intake enumerated the two families to $X=10^8$ and found $4{,}866$ and $8{,}430$ members, against $4{,}868.6$ and $8{,}432.7$ from~\eqref{pte:ac:sqrt}; that check is not shipped.}

\begin{corollary}\label{pte:ac:window}
For a fixed rational $1/2<a=\fp/\fq<1$ and fixed $R\ge3$, the total number of cancellation cells on defects $0\le r\le R$ through derivative order $X$ is
\begin{equation}\label{pte:ac:windowformula}
 \frac{\lceil R/2\rceil}{\fp}X
 +\frac2{\fp\sqrt C}\left(\frac{S_2}{\sqrt3}+S_3\right)\sqrt X
 +O_{a,R}(1).
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
On each odd defect, the exact reflection family is
\[
 N=1+\fp h,\qquad k=(2\fp-\fq)h-2(r-1),
\]
which contributes $X/\fp+O_{a,r}(1)$ physical cells. There are $\lceil R/2\rceil$ odd defects in the window. Defect zero has none, and defect one has only reflection. Theorem~\ref{pte:ac:counts} supplies the two nonreflection square-root terms. The rational fixed-defect theorem (Theorem~\ref{pte:rf:main}) says every remaining fixed defect contributes only finitely many nonreflection exceptions; their finite sum is $O_{a,R}(1)$. All cells on different defects are disjoint.
\end{proof}

\subsection{A uniform finite bound by conjugates}
Let
\[
 s_r=2\lfloor r/2\rfloor.
\]
\begin{theorem}\label{pte:ir:main}
For every positive irrational $a$ and every fixed $r\ge2$, at most $s_r^2-1$ physical cells satisfy $p_{N,N-r,k}(a)=0$. There are no physical cancellations when $r=1$.

If $a$ is transcendental, there are no cancellations on any fixed defect. If $a$ is algebraic of degree $D>s_r$, there are no cancellations on defect $r$ either.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The transcendental assertion follows immediately from Lemma~\ref{pte:ir:degrees}. Suppose now that $a$ is irrational algebraic, and choose a distinct conjugate $b$.

For even $r$, put $F_a=R_r(a;N,k)$. For odd $r\ge3$, put $F_a=R_r(a;N,k)/K_a$. This quotient is polynomial, and its leading homogeneous part is
\[
 -\frac{\Lambda_a(N,k)^{r-1}}{2r!},
\]
because the top homogeneous part of $K_a$ is $-2\Lambda_a$. Thus in either parity $F_a$ has total degree $s_r$ and leading form a nonzero constant times $\Lambda_a^{s_r}$. The same holds at $b$.

At an integer cell the equation $K_a=0$ would read
\[
 a(k-2N+2r)+(N-1)=0.
\]
Irrationality forces $N=1$ and $k=2-2r$, contradicting~\eqref{pte:rf:physical}. Hence a physical zero for odd $r$ is a zero of $F_a$. Applying the embedding sending $a$ to $b$ shows it is also a zero of $F_b$.

The two leading lines are distinct, since
\begin{equation}\label{pte:ir:det}
 \det\begin{pmatrix}a-1/2&-a/2\\ b-1/2&-b/2\end{pmatrix}
 =\frac{b-a}{4}\ne0.
\end{equation}
A common nonconstant factor of $F_a,F_b$ would have a leading homogeneous part dividing both $\Lambda_a^{s_r}$ and $\Lambda_b^{s_r}$, impossible. The two curves are therefore coprime. Their projective closures have no common point at infinity, again by~\eqref{pte:ir:det}. B\'ezout's theorem~\cite[I, Theorem 1.18]{pte-milne} bounds their affine common points by $s_r^2$, counting multiplicity.

The structural point $(N,k)=(r,0)$ is always common: $R_r(a;r,0)=[z^r]1=0$. In the odd case $K_a(r,0)=r-1\ne0$, so it survives division. This point lies outside~\eqref{pte:rf:physical}; removing at least this one intersection leaves at most $s_r^2-1$ physical zeros.

For the degree restriction, the even case is Lemma~\ref{pte:ir:degrees}. At a physical cell with odd $r$, $K_a$ as a polynomial in $a$ has nonzero leading coefficient
\[
 k-2(N-r)\le-(N-r)<0.
\]
The reduced coefficient polynomial consequently has exact degree $r-1=s_r$ in $a$, with rational coefficients. An algebraic number of degree greater than $s_r$ cannot be its root. Finally, $R_1=-K_a/2$, so the absence of irrational reflection cells settles $r=1$.
\end{proof}

In particular, every fixed defect is entirely nonzero beyond some derivative order. The bound is independent of the algebraic degree and does not invoke an integral-point finiteness theorem. It does not by itself control the union of defects growing with $N$.

\mergenote{The transcendental clause also follows from source 28's Corollary~\ref{pte:ga:generic}, which gives every transcendental exponent the full count $N(N+3)/2$ at every order; both rest on the coefficient polynomials not vanishing identically. For irrational $a$ the theorem implies source 20's Theorem~\ref{pte:ac:algebraic}, effectively and on every defect $r\ge2$.}

\subsection{Sharp bounds on defects two and three}
\begin{corollary}\label{pte:ir:onecell}
For each fixed positive irrational exponent, there is at most one physical cancellation cell on defect two, and at most one on defect three. Both bounds are attained.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Theorem~\ref{pte:ir:main} excludes degrees greater than two, so only quadratic irrational exponents need consideration. Put $u=N-r\ge1$, $\delta=2u-k$, and
\[
 \varpi=1/u>0,\qquad \tau=\delta/u\in[1,2].
\]
For defect two, direct expansion gives
\[
 24R_2=(3\delta^2+k)a^2-6(u+1)\delta a+u(3u+5).
\]
Normalize its positive constant term to one, writing the quadratic as $Pa^2-Sa+1$. Then
\[
 S=\frac{6\tau(1+\varpi)}{3+5\varpi},\qquad
 P=\frac{3\tau^2+(2-\tau)\varpi}{3+5\varpi}.
\]
A common irrational root forces two rational quadratics to be proportional, so their normalized pairs $(S,P)$ agree. Along a fixed $S$, the function $\tau=S(3+5\varpi)/(6(1+\varpi))$ increases with $\varpi$. Direct differentiation gives
\[
 \left.\frac{dP}{d\varpi}\right|_S
 =-\frac{15\varpi\tau^2+5\varpi\tau-6\varpi+3\tau^2+3\tau-6}{(\varpi+1)(5\varpi+3)^2}<0.
\]
The numerator equals $14\varpi>0$ at $\tau=1$ and increases with $\tau\ge1$. Between two admissible values of $\varpi$, the increasing $\tau(\varpi)$ remains in $[1,2]$. Thus $P$ is strictly decreasing along that interval and determines $\varpi$ uniquely. It then determines $\tau$, hence $u,\delta,N,k$.

For defect three, remove the nonvanishing irrational reflection factor $K_a$. The exact quadratic is
\[
 -48R_3/K_a=(\delta^2+k)a^2-2(u+2)\delta a+u(u+3).
\]
Its corresponding pair is
\[
 S=\frac{2\tau(1+2\varpi)}{1+3\varpi},\qquad
 P=\frac{\tau^2+(2-\tau)\varpi}{1+3\varpi},
\]
with $\tau=S(1+3\varpi)/(2(1+2\varpi))$ again increasing. Here
\[
 \left.\frac{dP}{d\varpi}\right|_S
 =-\frac{6\varpi\tau^2+3\varpi\tau-4\varpi+\tau^2+\tau-2}{(2\varpi+1)(3\varpi+1)^2}<0.
\]
The numerator is $5\varpi$ at $\tau=1$ and increases thereafter, proving uniqueness in the same way. The defect-two example in Section~\ref{pte:ir:example} attains one cell. At defect three, either $a=(3\pm\sqrt5)/2$ gives the physical cell $(N,k)=(4,0)$, so that bound is also sharp.
\end{proof}
\mergenote{In this proof $P$ and $S$ are the normalized coefficients of a quadratic, not a prefactor or an action, and $R_2$, $R_3$ abbreviate $R_2(a;N,k)$, $R_3(a;N,k)$.}

\subsection{An explicit order bound and exact elimination}\label{pte:sec:ir-order}
There is also a direct upper bound on the order of an algebraic cancellation. Use the compared residual $F_a$ from Theorem~\ref{pte:ir:main}, with $s_r\ge2$, and write
\[
 F_a=c_r\Lambda_a^{s_r}+Q_a,\qquad \deg Q_a\le s_r-1,
 \qquad
 c_r=\begin{cases}1/r!,&r\text{ even},\\-1/(2r!),&r\text{ odd}.\end{cases}
\]
Let $\Sigma_a$ be the sum of the absolute values of all coefficients of $Q_a$, and put $\Gamma_a=(\Sigma_a/|c_r|)^{1/s_r}$. Define $\Sigma_b,\Gamma_b$ for any distinct conjugate $b$ in the same way. At a physical zero, $N\ge1$ and $0\le k\le N$ imply
\[
 |Q_a(N,k)|\le\Sigma_aN^{s_r-1},\qquad
 |\Lambda_a(N,k)|\le\Gamma_aN^{1-1/s_r},
\]
and similarly for $b$. Since $b\Lambda_a-a\Lambda_b=(a-b)N/2$, every physical zero satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{pte:ir:orderbound}
 \boxed{\displaystyle
 N\le\left(\frac{2(|b|\Gamma_a+|a|\Gamma_b)}{|a-b|}\right)^{s_r}.}
\end{equation}
All quantities are algebraic and computable from exact algebraic input. Rational upper bounds for the numerator and a positive rational lower bound for $|a-b|$ give an explicit finite search rectangle. The case $r=1$ has no zeros and needs no such bound.

Suppose $a$ is specified by an exact irreducible minimal polynomial $P(A)\in\mathbb Q[A]$ of degree $D\ge2$, together with an embedding selecting a positive real root. Fix $r$. Reduce the raw residual modulo $P$:
\begin{equation}\label{pte:ir:remainders}
 R_r(A;N,k)\equiv\sum_{j=0}^{D-1}A^jf_j(N,k)\pmod{P(A)},
 \qquad f_j\in\mathbb Q[N,k].
\end{equation}
For integer $N,k$, cancellation is equivalent to $f_j(N,k)=0$ for every $j$, by linear independence of $1,a,\ldots,a^{D-1}$ over $\mathbb Q$.

The common complex variety of the $f_j$ is contained in the intersection of the raw conjugate curves $R_r(a;N,k)=R_r(b;N,k)=0$. The leading-line argument of~\eqref{pte:ir:det}, applied before any odd division, makes this intersection finite. Thus the rational polynomial system~\eqref{pte:ir:remainders} is zero-dimensional or empty.

Exact Gr\"obner elimination or resultants therefore compute a finite candidate set. Concretely, a nonzero univariate elimination polynomial in $N$ supplies all possible integer $N$ by rational-root extraction. At each such value, the remaining polynomials in $k$ supply all possible integer $k$. Check every candidate in all $f_j$ and apply~\eqref{pte:rf:physical}. Equivalently, univariate elimination polynomials in both coordinates give explicit rational Cauchy bounds, followed by a finite exact integer enumeration. A unit ideal means there are no candidates.

This is a terminating exact procedure for the complete physical zero list. One plus its largest derivative order is an explicit eventual threshold; if the list is empty, the entire physical range is already nonzero. The statement does not promise a small computational cost. It requires exact algebraic input, and does not provide an algorithm to recognize transcendence from arbitrary numerical data.

\subsection{A complete quadratic example}\label{pte:ir:example}
Take either
\[
 a=1\pm\frac1{\sqrt3}>0,\qquad P(A)=3A^2-6A+2,
\]
and fix $r=2$. At $(N,k)=(3,0)$,
\[
 R_2(a;3,0)=a^2/2-a+1/3=0.
\]
Thus an irrational exponent can have a genuine physical cancellation.

Reducing the full residual modulo $P$ and computing a rational Gr\"obner basis gives the equivalent equations
\begin{align}
 &(N-2)(N-3)(9N^2-N-2)=0,\label{pte:ir:exampleN}\\
 &k=-\frac34N^3+\frac{37}{12}N^2-\frac76N-4.\label{pte:ir:examplek}
\end{align}
The only integer roots of~\eqref{pte:ir:exampleN} are $N=2,3$, since the remaining quadratic has nonsquare discriminant $73$. Both yield $k=0$. The point $(2,0)$ is structural and lies outside $N\ge3$. Therefore, for each of the two exponents, the complete physical defect-two zero list is
\[
 \boxed{\{(N,k)=(3,0)\}.}
\]
Every coefficient on that defect is nonzero at every order $N\ge4$. The accompanying exact checker verifies both directions of the ideal equivalence in~\eqref{pte:ir:exampleN}--\eqref{pte:ir:examplek}, so this is a complete elimination rather than a bounded numerical search.

There is no uniform derivative-order cutoff as the irrational exponent varies, even at defect two. For an integer $u\ge1$, direct substitution gives
\[
 R_2(a;u+2,0)=\frac{u}{24}
       \bigl(12ua^2-12(u+1)a+3u+5\bigr).
\]
Its two roots are
\[
 a=\frac{u+1}{2u}\pm\frac{\sqrt{3(u+3)}}{6u}.
\]
Both are positive, and they are quadratic irrational whenever $3(u+3)$ is nonsquare. Taking $u=3h^2$ with integers $h\ge1$ supplies such exponents with unbounded cancellation order $N=u+2$. Thus the cell-count bound is uniform, while an eventual order threshold genuinely depends on the exponent.
\mergenote{In this example $P(A)$ is a minimal polynomial in the variable $A$, not the series $A_a$. The two exponents $1\pm1/\sqrt3$ are the roots of source 28's $p_{3,1,0}(\alpha)=3\alpha^2-6\alpha+2$; the batch-72A intake also checked that $p_{4,1,0}(\alpha)=2(2\alpha-3)(\alpha^2-3\alpha+1)$, whose quadratic factor has the roots $(3\pm\sqrt5)/2$ of the defect-three example. ``The accompanying exact checker'' is shipped as \texttt{code/\allowbreak{}16-\allowbreak{}irrational-\allowbreak{}verify.\allowbreak{}py}.}
