\begin{abstract}
For a partition $\lambda$ into distinct parts, let $\normp(\lambda)$ be the
product of its parts. We analyze the fixed positive norm moments
$a_\alpha(n)=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n\text{ distinct}}\normp(\lambda)^\alpha$.
A direct argument proves the logarithmic growth formulas still marked
conjectural in five OEIS entries. With $K=\sqrt{2n}$, $r=\log K$, and
$c=\pi^2/(6\alpha^2)$, we then prove, to every fixed order,
\[
 \log a_\alpha(n)=\alpha K\left(r-1+\frac c r+\frac c{r^2}
 +\frac{c-c^2/2}{r^3}+\cdots\right).
\]
An explicit inverse-function construction generates all coefficients; seven are
printed. A separate local-limit argument gives multiplicatively accurate
exact-saddle asymptotics and all fixed Edgeworth orders. This separation matters:
exponentiating a fixed truncation of the logarithmic expansion does not give a
relative asymptotic equivalent. We invert the dominant block using the principal
Lambert function and obtain asymptotics for the first index attaining a large
value. Under the norm-biased measure, the occupation boundary has a logistic
profile, while the largest part has a Gumbel limit and is asymptotic to
$(1+1/\alpha)\sqrt{2n}$. Finally, we prove that the first Fourier resonances have
logarithmic modulus of order $-K/(\alpha^3(\log K)^3)$, explaining a striking
finite-size limitation visible in the exact computations. Reproducible scripts,
source-status notes, and further research questions accompany the proofs.

This report merges two manuscripts that proved these theorems independently:
source 40 (the base text, five OEIS sequences, real $\alpha>0$) and source 48
(A022629 and the same real family, written $\prod(1+k^sq^k)$). Every shared
theorem is printed once and credited to both, with identical coefficients.
Source 48 adds a second, elementary proof of the upper bound, a second
derivation of all coefficients by an explicit differential operator, explicit
centring of the Gumbel law in $\log\sqrt{2n}$, the radius of convergence of
$\log A_\alpha$ at the origin, the logarithm of the largest single weight, the
entropy factor $\log(a_\alpha(n)/\max\normp^\alpha)\sim\pi^2K/(6\alpha r)$,
exact data to $n=6400$ and its own research questions.
\end{abstract}

\section{The problem, its significance, and the source audit}
\subsection{Five conjectures in one natural family}
Let $\mathcal D_n$ be the set of partitions of $n$ into distinct positive parts.
The empty partition has norm $1$. For fixed $\alpha>0$, define
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:def}
 \normp(\lambda)=\prod_{k\in\lambda}k,
 \qquad a_\alpha(n)=\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal D_n}\normp(\lambda)^\alpha.
\end{equation}
Choosing whether each part occurs gives the locally uniformly convergent product
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:product}
 A_\alpha(q)=\sum_{n\ge0}a_\alpha(n)q^n
 =\prod_{k\ge1}(1+k^\alpha q^k),\qquad |q|<1.
\end{equation}
For integral $\alpha$ the coefficients are integers. One may interpret $k^\alpha$
as the number of colors available for a part of size $k$, with at most one part
of each \emph{size}, regardless of color. This is not the product
$\prod_k(1+q^k)^{k^\alpha}$, which allows several differently colored copies of
the same size.

The OEIS entries consulted on October 1, 2026 have the following status
\cite{oeis1,oeis2,oeis3,oeis4,oeis5}.

\midrule

All five formulas are attributed there to V\'aclav Kote\v{s}ovec and have the
unified form
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:conjecture}
 \log a_\alpha(n)\sim
 \alpha\sqrt{\frac n2}\bigl(\log(2n)-2\bigr).
\end{equation}
The full array of nonnegative integral moments is \OEIS{A292189}; its columns
$0,1,\ldots,5$ are identified in that entry \cite{oeisarray}. The zeroth column
is the ordinary distinct-partition function, but $\alpha=0$ is a different
asymptotic regime and is excluded from our fixed-positive-parameter theorems.

Source 48, which treats A022629 and the real family, makes two further points
\cite{oeis1}.  Its use of ``currently'' refers to the entry as retrieved on
October 1, 2026, not to an exhaustive determination of the publication
history.  And a logarithmic equivalent does not determine a multiplicative
equivalent: even the constant $-2$ inside the parentheses of
\eqref{dpn:eq:conjecture} is not specified by the relation $\sim$, since
changing it leaves the same equivalence.  The additive statement that the
corresponding $-\sqrt{2n}$ term is correct is sharper, and
Proposition~\ref{dpn:prop:elementary} proves it.  For non-integral $\alpha$,
\eqref{dpn:eq:product} is a positive weighted enumeration, and the product
converges normally on compact subsets of $|q|<1$ \src{48}.

These sequences are not arbitrary numerical constructions. They simultaneously
encode weighted enumeration, moments of a fundamental multiplicative partition
statistic, and a conditioned model of independent binary occupancies. Their
common structure makes a family theorem more informative than five separate
fits to numerical data.

\subsection{What is established here, and what is not a priority claim}
The conjectural labels in the consulted entries are verifiable facts; the claim
that no proof has ever appeared anywhere would be much stronger and is not made.
This report supplies self-contained proofs of the displayed formulas, followed
by explicit refinements and probabilistic consequences. Novelty of the
refinements should be assessed against the broader literature before publication.

Schneider and Sills survey partition norms, including extremal norms over
distinct partitions and their connection with triangular numbers
\cite{schneider}. Bridges and Craig study norm distributions and moments for
\emph{unrestricted} partitions \cite{bridges}; repeated parts change the
analytic problem substantially. Rana, Kaur, and Kumar give recurrences and
related norm identities for distinct partitions \cite{rana}. None of those
results is used as an unproved asymptotic input here.

A particularly relevant recent source is Gikunda's March 2026 dissertation
\cite{gikunda}. Its Chapter 4 uses
$\prod_k(1+u^{\log k}q^k)^{b_k}$ and proves log-norm central limit theorems.
For $b_k=1$ and $u=e^\alpha$ this is our generating family. However, the
coefficient estimate in Proposition 4.2.5 and the surrounding analysis use a
\emph{shrinking} tilt $\log u=O(t^\delta)$ as the saddle parameter $t$ tends
to zero. Our tilt $\alpha>0$ stays fixed. Thus that estimate does not directly
supply the fixed-tilt results below. This is a distinction between asymptotic
regimes, not a claim that the underlying family has not previously been studied.

Source 48 places the method in the classical saddle-point and probabilistic
tradition of asymptotic enumeration; standard background is in Flajolet and
Sedgewick \cite{FS}.  Granovsky and Stark \cite{GS} study a broad product
framework and, in their Section 3, the related products
$\prod(1+k^{-q}z^k)$ with $q>0$.  Their main theorem assumes parameters
$0<a_k\le1$, so substituting $a_k=k^\alpha>1$ is not a direct application of
that theorem.  This observation is not a claim that no other general theorem
can cover the present case \src{48}.

\section{Main results and the hierarchy of scales}
Throughout the paper, $\alpha>0$ is fixed. Constants in $O$-terms may depend on
$\alpha$ and on the indicated truncation order. Put
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:Kr}
 K=\sqrt{2n},\qquad r=\log K,\qquad c=\frac{\pi^2}{6\alpha^2}.
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[All-orders logarithmic expansion; sources 40 and 48]\label{dpn:thm:log}
There are explicit polynomials $P_j(c)\in\mathbb Q[c]$ such that, for every
fixed integer $R\ge1$,
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:logall}
 \log a_\alpha(n)=\alpha K\left(r-1+
       \sum_{j=1}^R\frac{P_j(c)}{r^j}
       +O_{\alpha,R}(r^{-R-1})\right).
\end{equation}
The first seven are
\begin{align}
 P_1(c)&=c,&P_2(c)&=c,\notag\\
 P_3(c)&=c-\tfrac12c^2,&
 P_4(c)&=c+\tfrac9{10}c^2,\notag\\
 P_5(c)&=c+\tfrac{41}5c^2+\tfrac12c^3,\label{dpn:eq:P}\\
 P_6(c)&=c+27c^2+\tfrac{1973}{210}c^3,\notag\\
 P_7(c)&=c+\tfrac{129}2c^2+\tfrac{8109}{70}c^3-\tfrac58c^4.\notag
\end{align}
Equations \eqref{dpn:eq:dformal}--\eqref{dpn:eq:formalH} below generate every $P_j$.
\end{theorem}
In particular,
\begin{align}\label{dpn:eq:firstterms}
 \log a_\alpha(n)
 ={}&\alpha K(r-1)+\frac{\pi^2K}{6\alpha r}
     +\frac{\pi^2K}{6\alpha r^2}\notag\\
 &+\left(\frac{\pi^2}{6\alpha}-\frac{\pi^4}{72\alpha^3}\right)
       \frac K{r^3}+O_\alpha(K/r^4).
\end{align}
This proves \eqref{dpn:eq:conjecture} for all five OEIS sequences, and more strongly
identifies the $-\alpha K$ term with an error $O_\alpha(K/r)$.

Source 48 proves Theorem~\ref{dpn:thm:log} independently (its Theorem~1.1,
with $P_1,\dots,P_6$ written $c_1(A),\dots,c_6(A)$), and adds three remarks.
First, its constants are uniform when $\alpha$ ranges over a fixed compact
subinterval of $(0,\infty)$; they are \emph{not} claimed to be uniform as
$\alpha\downarrow0$.  Second, the case $R=0$ states
$\log a_\alpha(n)=\alpha K(r-1)+O_\alpha(K/r)$, which already proves more than
the displayed OEIS conjecture, and the first nonzero correction is positive:
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:48:eq:firstlimit}
 \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{r}{K}
       \bigl(\log a_\alpha(n)-\alpha K(r-1)\bigr)=\frac{\pi^2}{6\alpha}.
\end{equation}
Third, all-order statements in this report mean: \emph{for each fixed
truncation order, the asserted remainder bound holds}.  No convergence of the
infinite inverse-logarithmic series is assumed \src{48}.

For a different level of precision, define
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:F}
 F(t)=\sum_{k\ge1}\log(1+k^\alpha e^{-tk}),\qquad t>0,
\end{equation}
and let $t=t_n$ be the unique solution of $-F'(t)=n$. Set
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:phase}
 s=-W_{-1}(-t/\alpha),\qquad M=e^s,
 \qquad t=\alpha s/M,\qquad H=\frac M{\alpha(s-1)}.
\end{equation}
These definitions use the large real branch $s>1$, valid for all sufficiently
large $n$. Let $B=F''(t)$ and $\kappa_j=(-1)^jF^{(j)}(t)$.

\begin{theorem}[Exact-saddle coefficient expansion; sources 40 and 48]\label{dpn:thm:saddle}
As $n\to\infty$,
\[
 M\sim K,\quad s\sim r,\quad B\sim\frac{M^3}{\alpha s},
 \quad
 a_\alpha(n)\sim\frac{\exp(F(t_n)+nt_n)}{\sqrt{2\pi B}}.
\]
More precisely, for every fixed $J\ge1$,
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:edgeworth}
 a_\alpha(n)=\frac{\exp(F(t_n)+nt_n)}{\sqrt{2\pi B}}
 \left(1+\sum_{\ell=1}^{J-1}\mathcal E_\ell
       +O_{\alpha,J}((s/M)^J)\right),
\end{equation}
where each $\mathcal E_\ell=O_{\alpha,\ell}((s/M)^\ell)$ is given explicitly
in \eqref{dpn:eq:edgepoly}. The first correction is
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:E1}
 \mathcal E_1=\frac{\kappa_4}{8B^2}
                    -\frac{5\kappa_3^2}{24B^3}.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Asymptotic inversion; sources 40 and 48]\label{dpn:thm:inverse}
For $Y\to\infty$, let $N_\alpha(Y)$ be the least integer $n$ such that
$a_\alpha(n)\ge e^Y$. Define
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:Wlead}
 w=W_0\left(\frac{Y}{\alpha e}\right),\qquad R_0=1+w,
 \qquad K_0=\frac{Y}{\alpha w}.
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:inverselead}
 N_\alpha(Y)=\frac{K_0^2}{2}\left(
 1-\frac{2c}{R_0^2}-\frac{2c}{R_0^3}
 +\frac{4c^2-2c}{R_0^4}+O_\alpha(R_0^{-5})\right).
\end{equation}
A formal recurrence in Section~\ref{dpn:sec:inverse} gives all inverse-logarithmic
orders, with a remainder at every fixed order.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Logistic boundary and Gumbel extreme; sources 40 and 48]\label{dpn:thm:geometry}
Give $\mathcal D_n$ the probability law
$\Prob_n(\lambda)=\normp(\lambda)^\alpha/a_\alpha(n)$.
For each fixed $x\in\R$, a part near $M+xH$ has limiting occupation probability
$(1+e^x)^{-1}$; any fixed finite collection of distinct such sites is
asymptotically independent. If $L_n$ is the largest part, define
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:bdef}
 b=b(t_n)>\alpha/t_n,\qquad
 \frac{b^\alpha e^{-t_nb}}{t_n}=1.
\end{equation}
Then, for every fixed real $x$,
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:gumbel}
 \Prob_n\{t_n(L_n-b)\le x\}\longrightarrow \exp(-e^{-x}),
 \qquad \frac{L_n}{\sqrt{2n}}\longrightarrow 1+\frac1\alpha
 \quad\text{in probability}.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\paragraph{A precision warning.}
The error in \eqref{dpn:eq:logall} is $O(K/r^{R+1})$, which still grows without
bound for fixed $R$. Consequently, exponentiating a fixed truncation need not
approximate $a_\alpha(n)$ to relative error $o(1)$. Formula
\eqref{dpn:eq:edgeworth} does, because it retains the \emph{exact} saddle and the
\emph{exact} infinite sums. There is also a smaller exponential scale from
noncentral Fourier arcs; Section~\ref{dpn:sec:resonance} identifies it. We do not
conflate these three statements into an unjustified single explicit
coefficient transseries.

Source 48 draws the same line: the inverse-logarithmic expansion determines
the complete block on the scales $Kr,K,K/r,K/r^2,\ldots$; it does \emph{not}
resolve every smaller block or every exponentially small contribution, and a
finite truncation of this block generally leaves a diverging error in
$\log a_\alpha(n)$.  A multiplicative equivalent is instead obtained from the
exact saddle and exact convergent sums.  Its numerical routines are
independent checks, not substitutes for the proofs; they use
arbitrary-precision arithmetic but not directed-rounding interval
certification \src{48}.

\section{A short proof of all five posted conjectures}
This section already proves the formulas that are explicitly conjectural in the
OEIS entries. Later sections supply the stronger refinements.

\begin{lemma}[A useful continuum estimate]\label{dpn:lem:rough}
For $s\to\infty$, $M=e^s$, and $t=\alpha s/M$,
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:roughF}
 F(t)=\alpha M(s/2-1)+O_\alpha(M/s).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The function $f_t(x)=\log(1+x^\alpha e^{-tx})$ is nonnegative and unimodal
on $(0,\infty)$, tends to zero at both ends, and has maximum $O_\alpha(s)$.
For a nonnegative unimodal function, comparison on unit intervals gives
\[
 \left|\sum_{k\ge1}f_t(k)-\int_0^\infty f_t(x)\dd x\right|
 \le 2\sup_x f_t(x)=O_\alpha(s).
\]
With $x=Mu$, the exponent is $\alpha[s(1-u)+\log u]$. Replacing
$\log(1+e^z)$ by $\max(z,0)$ changes the integral by $O_\alpha(M/s)$:
in a fixed neighborhood of $u=1$, the derivative of $s(u-1)-\log u$
is comparable with $s$, and
$\int_\R\log(1+e^{-|v|})\dd v<\infty$.
Outside that neighborhood, split off $0<u<e^{-s/2}$; its integral is
exponentially small times a polynomial in $s$. On the remaining region
away from $1$, the absolute exponent grows linearly in $s$, except for the
negligible small lower crossing. Thus the remaining error is smaller than
$M/s$. The lower crossing changes the following signed baseline by $O(1)$:
\[
 \alpha M\int_0^1\bigl(s(1-u)+\log u\bigr)\dd u
       =\alpha M(s/2-1).
\]
The lattice error $O(s)$ is also $O(M/s)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[sources 40 and 48]\label{dpn:prop:elementary}
For every fixed $\alpha>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:twoterms}
 \log a_\alpha(n)=\alpha\sqrt{2n}\bigl(\log\sqrt{2n}-1\bigr)
                   +O_\alpha\left(\frac{\sqrt n}{\log n}\right).
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $m$ be maximal with $m(m+1)/2\le n$, and write
$d=n-m(m+1)/2$, so $0\le d\le m$. The distinct partition
\[
 1+2+\cdots+(m-1)+(m+d)
\]
has norm at least $m!$. Therefore
\[
 \log a_\alpha(n)\ge\alpha\log(m!)
   =\alpha K(r-1)+O_\alpha(r),
\]
where $m=K+O(1)$ and the usual integral bounds, or Stirling's formula, give
$\log(m!)=m\log m-m+O(\log m)$.

For the upper bound, nonnegative coefficients give
$a_\alpha(n)\le\exp(F(t)+nt)$ for every $t>0$.
Choose $t=\alpha r/K$, that is, $M=K$ and $s=r$ in
Lemma~\ref{dpn:lem:rough}. Since $n=K^2/2$,
\[
 F(t)+nt=\alpha K(r/2-1)+\alpha Kr/2+O_\alpha(K/r)
        =\alpha K(r-1)+O_\alpha(K/r).
\]
The two bounds prove \eqref{dpn:eq:twoterms}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The lower-bound partition need not maximize the norm. An exact maximizer is
unnecessary. Also, a bare asymptotic equivalence to $\alpha K(r-1)$ would not
by itself certify $-\alpha K$ as the second term; the error in
\eqref{dpn:eq:twoterms} does certify it.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Two structural facts and a second proof \src{48}}\label{dpn:48:sec:elementary}

\paragraph{Why an expansion at the origin cannot simply be used at the saddle.}
\begin{proposition}[source 48]\label{dpn:48:prop:zeros}
The Taylor series of the analytic logarithm of $A_\alpha$ at $q=0$ has radius
of convergence $3^{-\alpha/3}$, although $A_\alpha$ is analytic throughout
$|q|<1$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The zeros of $1+k^\alpha q^k$ have modulus $k^{-\alpha/k}$. Among positive
integers $k$, the maximum of $\log k/k$ occurs at $k=3$: the function
decreases for $k>e$, and $\log3/3>\log2/2$. Hence the closest zeros of the
product have modulus $3^{-\alpha/3}$. The remaining factors converge normally
and cannot remove a zero. Inside this circle all factors are nonzero, so the
product has a single-valued analytic logarithm with value zero at the origin.
At a closest zero its logarithm cannot be analytic. This proves the assertion.
\end{proof}
Consequently,
\[
 \log A_\alpha(q)=\sum_{k\ge1}\sum_{j\ge1}
          \frac{(-1)^{j+1}}{j}k^{\alpha j}q^{kj}
\]
is useful as a \emph{formal} identity, but this expansion is not convergent at
the positive saddles approaching $q=1$. Treating it as a convergent expansion
there would lose the transition that controls this problem.

\paragraph{The largest individual weight.}
Let
\[
 \Pi_\alpha(n)=\max_{\lambda\in\mathcal D_n}\normp(\lambda)^\alpha .
\]
\begin{proposition}[source 48]\label{dpn:48:prop:maxweight}
For fixed $\alpha>0$, with $K=\sqrt{2n}$ and $r=\log K$,
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:48:eq:maxweight}
 \log\Pi_\alpha(n)=\alpha K(r-1)+O_\alpha(r).
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Write $n=m(m+1)/2+d$ with $0\le d\le m$. The distinct partition
$(1,2,\ldots,m-1,m+d)$ has weight $((m-1)!(m+d))^\alpha$. Since $m=K+O(1)$,
Stirling's estimate yields the required lower bound, with error $O_\alpha(r)$.

For the upper bound set $t=\alpha r/K$. Any distinct partition $\lambda$ of $n$
satisfies
\[
 \alpha\sum_{k\in\lambda}\log k
 \le tn+\sum_{k\ge1}(\alpha\log k-tk)_+,
 \qquad u_+=\max(u,0).
\]
For sufficiently large $K$, the positive terms are exactly those with
$2\le k<K$, apart from a possible zero term at an integer endpoint. Summing
$\log k$ by Stirling's formula and $k$ by the arithmetic progression formula
gives
\[
 \sum_{k\ge1}(\alpha\log k-tk)_+
       =\alpha K(r/2-1)+O_\alpha(r).
\]
Adding $tn=\alpha Kr/2$ proves the upper bound.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[transition width; source 48]\label{dpn:48:lem:width}
Let $M=e^s$, $t=\alpha s/M$, and $s\to\infty$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:48:eq:smoothingbound}
 \sum_{k\ge1}\exp\bigl(-|\alpha\log k-tk|\bigr)=O_\alpha(M/s).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Put $\psi(u)=\alpha\log u-tu$. On $k\ge M/2$, the derivative of $\psi$ is at
most $-t/2$ once $s\ge4$, and $\psi(M)=0$. Thus this part of the sum is
bounded by a two-sided geometric sum of scale $1/t=O_\alpha(M/s)$.

For $1\le k\le M^{1/4}$, use the bound $1$, giving $O(M^{1/4})$. On
$[M^{1/4},M/2]$, concavity of $\psi$ implies that its minimum is at an
endpoint. For large $s$, both endpoint values exceed $c_\alpha s$ for a
constant $c_\alpha>0$. This range therefore contributes $O_\alpha(M^{1-c_\alpha})$.
Both estimates are $o_\alpha(M/s)$. Adjusting integer endpoints changes
nothing.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Second proof of Proposition~\ref{dpn:prop:elementary} \src{48}]
The lower bound follows from $a_\alpha(n)\ge\Pi_\alpha(n)$ and
Proposition~\ref{dpn:48:prop:maxweight} (the same partition as in the first
proof). For the upper bound, positivity gives
$a_\alpha(n)\le e^{tn}A_\alpha(e^{-t})$. Use $t=\alpha r/K$ and the exact
identity
\[
 \log(1+e^u)=u_++\log(1+e^{-|u|}).
\]
The positive-part sum was evaluated in the proof of
Proposition~\ref{dpn:48:prop:maxweight}; the second sum is bounded by
Lemma~\ref{dpn:48:lem:width} with $M=K$, $s=r$. We obtain
\[
 tn+F(t)=\alpha K(r-1)+O_\alpha(K/r+r),
\]
and $r=o(K/r)$. This proves \eqref{dpn:eq:twoterms} without any local limit
theorem, continuum integral or formal asymptotic manipulation.
\end{proof}
This argument is a separate, elementary proof of the OEIS conjecture; the
remaining sections determine what lies inside its error term \src{48}.

\section{The exact probabilistic saddle and its scales}\label{dpn:sec:saddle}
For fixed $t>0$, let $X_k$ be independent Bernoulli random variables with
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:pk}
 p_k=\Prob_t(X_k=1)=\frac{k^\alpha e^{-tk}}{1+k^\alpha e^{-tk}},
 \qquad S=\sum_{k\ge1}kX_k.
\end{equation}
The sum is finite almost surely because $\sum p_k<\infty$. Multiplying the
Bernoulli probabilities gives the exact identity
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:tiltidentity}
 \Prob_t(S=n)=a_\alpha(n)e^{-nt-F(t)}.
\end{equation}
Differentiation under the absolutely convergent sums shows
\[
 \E_tS=-F'(t)=\sum k p_k,\qquad
 \Var_t S=F''(t)=\sum k^2p_k(1-p_k)>0.
\]
The mean is continuous and strictly decreasing from $\infty$ to $0$ as $t$
increases from $0$ to $\infty$. Hence the saddle equation has a unique solution
for every $n>0$.

The large crossing $k^\alpha e^{-tk}=1$ occurs at $k=M$ in
\eqref{dpn:eq:phase}. Its logarithmic derivative there is
\[
 \frac\alpha M-t=-\frac{\alpha(s-1)}M=-H^{-1}.
\]
Thus $H$ is the width of the transition from predominantly occupied to
predominantly unoccupied sites.

\begin{lemma}[Transition estimates]\label{dpn:lem:scales}
For fixed $\alpha>0$, as $s\to\infty$,
\begin{align}
 \sum_{k\ge1}kp_k&\sim M^2/2,\label{dpn:eq:meanleading}\\
 \sum_{k\ge1}k^2p_k(1-p_k)&\sim M^2H
                    \sim M^3/(\alpha s).\label{dpn:eq:varianceleading}
\end{align}
For each fixed integer $j\ge2$,
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:mombound}
 \sum_{k\ge1}k^j p_k(1-p_k)=O_{\alpha,j}(M^{j+1}/s).
\end{equation}
Moreover, for every fixed nonnegative integer $h$,
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:profilemoment}
 \frac1{H^{h+1}}\sum_{k\ge1}|k-M|^h p_k(1-p_k)
 \longrightarrow
 \int_\R |x|^h\frac{e^x}{(1+e^x)^2}\dd x.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For $k=M+xH$ with $x$ in a fixed bounded interval,
\[
 \alpha\log k-tk
 =\alpha\log(1+xH/M)-\alpha s xH/M
 =-x+O_{\alpha,x}(s^{-2}).
\]
Here and below rounding $k$ to an integer changes the scaled coordinate by
$O(H^{-1})=o(1)$. Thus $p_k$ converges to $(1+e^x)^{-1}$.
Since $H\to\infty$, sums over any fixed $x$-interval are Riemann sums.
The limiting variance density has integral $1$.

We record a tail justification so that this argument is not just a local
heuristic. On $k/M\in[1/2,3/2]$, the derivative of the log odds is
negative and comparable to $-1/H$ for large $s$; the variance density is
therefore bounded by $C_\alpha e^{-c_\alpha|k-M|/H}$.
On $k\ge3M/2$, the odds themselves are exponentially small in $s$ and then
in $tk$. On $k\le M/2$, use
$p_k(1-p_k)\le\min(1,k^{-\alpha}e^{tk})$, and split at $M/s$.
The range $M/s<k\le M/2$ is exponentially small in $s$ after scaling by
any of the powers of $M$ above. On $k\le M/s$, the crude bound is
$O_\alpha(k^{-\alpha})$; its contribution to \eqref{dpn:eq:profilemoment},
even with $|k-M|^h\le M^h$, is $o(H^{h+1})$.
Indeed the resulting ratio is bounded by a fixed power of $s$ times
$M^{-\min(\alpha,1)}$, with an extra logarithm when $\alpha=1$.
These bounds establish uniform integrability for every fixed moment.
They also prove \eqref{dpn:eq:mombound} and \eqref{dpn:eq:varianceleading}.

Finally, $p_{\lfloor Mu\rfloor}\to1$ for $0<u<1$ and tends to zero for
$u>1$. The same tail bounds and a Riemann sum give
$M^{-2}\sum k p_k\to\int_0^1u\dd u=1/2$.
\end{proof}

Source 48 sets up the same Bernoulli representation (its
\eqref{dpn:eq:tiltidentity} is identical, with the total written $X_t$ and the
variance $V$), and the same transition coordinate $s=-W_{-1}(-t/\alpha)$, which
it calls the \emph{upper transition coordinate}; the lower real branch is
necessary because $s\to\infty$, and the branch conventions agree with the
DLMF \cite{DLMFW}.  Its version of Lemma~\ref{dpn:lem:scales} (its
Lemma~3.1) states $-F'(t)\sim M^2/2$, $B\sim M^3/(\alpha s)$ and
$|\kappa_j|=O_{\alpha,j}(M^{j+1}/s)$ for $j\ge2$, with the following proof.

\begin{proof}[Second proof of \eqref{dpn:eq:meanleading}--\eqref{dpn:eq:mombound} \src{48}]
In the scaled variable $k=Mu$, the log odds equal $\alpha s(1-u)+\alpha\log u$.
For $0<u<1$ this tends to $+\infty$, and for $u>1$ it tends to $-\infty$.
Splitting the sum at fixed fractions of $M$, and using exponential decay above
a fixed multiple of $M$, gives $M^{-2}\sum kp_k\to\int_0^1u\dd u$.

Near $k=M$, put $v=t(k-M)$. The log odds are
\[
 -v+\alpha\log\left(1+\frac{v}{\alpha s}\right)\longrightarrow-v.
\]
The mesh in $v$ is $t\to0$. The derivative estimate used in
Lemma~\ref{dpn:48:lem:width} supplies an exponentially integrable bound on
$k\ge M/2$. Thus a Riemann-sum limit gives
\[
 B\sim\frac{M^2}{t}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
                 \frac{e^v}{(1+e^v)^2}\dd v
       =\frac{M^2}{t}=\frac{M^3}{\alpha s}.
\]
The range below $M/2$ is negligible. To see this for any fixed moment order,
split it at $M^{1/4}$ as in Lemma~\ref{dpn:48:lem:width}, now multiplying by
$k^j$. The small range is $O(M^{(j+1)/4})$; the other range is
$O_\alpha(M^{j+1-c_\alpha})$. Above $M/2$, the exponential envelope gives
$\sum k^jp_k(1-p_k)=O_{\alpha,j}(M^{j+1}/s)$. This proves the cumulant bound,
and normalization by the variance proves \eqref{dpn:eq:normcumulants}.
\end{proof}

At the exact saddle, \eqref{dpn:eq:meanleading} gives $M\sim K$ and $s\sim r$.
For cumulants, write $b_1(p)=p$ and
$b_{j+1}(p)=p(1-p)b_j'(p)$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:cumulants}
 \kappa_j=\sum_{k\ge1}k^j b_j(p_k),\qquad
 |b_j(p)|\le C_jp(1-p)\quad(j\ge2).
\end{equation}
Consequently, with $\eps=s/M$,
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:normcumulants}
 \frac{|\kappa_j|}{B^{j/2}}
      =O_{\alpha,j}(\eps^{(j-2)/2}),\qquad j\ge3.
\end{equation}
These bounds are deliberately conservative; signed cancellations can make
individual corrections smaller. They suffice for all fixed Edgeworth orders.

\section{The local limit theorem, including noncentral arcs}\label{dpn:sec:llt}
\subsection{An elementary consecutive-block inequality}
\begin{lemma}[sources 40 and 48]\label{dpn:lem:block}
There is an absolute constant $c_0>0$ such that for every integer $h\ge2$,
every integer $a$, and every $|\theta|\le\pi$,
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:block}
 \sum_{k=a}^{a+h-1}\sin^2(k\theta/2)
       \ge c_0\min(h,h^3\theta^2).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The geometric-sum formula gives
\[
 \sum_{k=a}^{a+h-1}\sin^2(k\theta/2)
 \ge\frac12\left(h-
       \left|\frac{\sin(h\theta/2)}{\sin(\theta/2)}\right|\right).
\]
When $h|\theta|\le1$, Taylor bounds for sine show that the difference in
parentheses is at least $c h(h^2-1)\theta^2$.
For $h|\theta|\ge2\pi$, the bound
$|\sin(\theta/2)|\ge|\theta|/\pi$ makes the ratio at most $h/2$.
On the intermediate range $1\le h|\theta|\le2\pi$, its ratio to $h$ is
uniformly less than one. To see the uniformity, let $h\to\infty$ and use
uniform convergence to $|\sin(x/2)/(x/2)|<1$ on $1\le x\le2\pi$;
the finitely many remaining $h$ have the same strict inequality by the
strict triangle inequality for a nonconstant geometric progression.
Taking the minimum of the resulting positive constants proves the lemma.
\end{proof}

Source 48 states the lemma for every sufficiently large $h$ in the equivalent
form $\sum_{k=a}^{a+h-1}\sin^2(k\theta/2)\ge c_0h\min\{1,h^2\theta^2\}$.
\begin{proof}[Second proof \src{48}]
The sum is at least
\[
 \frac12\left(h-\left|\sum_{k=a}^{a+h-1}e^{\ii k\theta}\right|\right)
 =\frac12\left(h-\frac{|\sin(h\theta/2)|}{|\sin(\theta/2)|}\right).
\]
For $h|\theta|\le1$, Taylor's formula bounds the deficit below by a constant
times $h^3\theta^2$. For $1\le h|\theta|\le C$, divide by $h$ and use the
strict inequality $|\sin u|<|u|$ away from zero, uniformly on a compact
interval; the denominator converges uniformly to $|h\theta|/2$ in this range.
For $h|\theta|\ge C$, use $|\sin(\theta/2)|\ge|\theta|/\pi$, choosing a fixed
sufficiently large $C$. These three ranges prove the result.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Fourier decay and the central Gaussian}
Let $\phi_t(\theta)=\E_t e^{\ii\theta(S-\E_tS)}$. The modulus identity
for one Bernoulli factor implies
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:phibound}
 |\phi_t(\theta)|\le
 \exp\left(-2\sum_{k\ge1}p_k(1-p_k)\sin^2(k\theta/2)\right).
\end{equation}
In a consecutive block of length $h\asymp_\alpha M/s$ centered at $M$,
all $p_k(1-p_k)$ are bounded below by a positive constant. On
$|\theta|\le c_1/M$, this block gives
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:centralbound}
 |\phi_t(\theta)|\le e^{-c_2 B\theta^2}.
\end{equation}
Indeed $k\asymp M$ on the block, and $\sin(k\theta/2)$ is comparable with
$k\theta$ for sufficiently small $c_1$.
On its complement in $[-\pi,\pi]$, Lemma~\ref{dpn:lem:block} gives
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:noncentral}
 |\phi_t(\theta)|\le
 \exp(-c_3M/s^3),\qquad c_1/M\le|\theta|\le\pi.
\end{equation}
All constants here may depend on $\alpha$. Notice the power $s^3$, rather
than $s$: the random part sizes lie in a relatively narrow consecutive block,
so one must control possible near-resonances. An argument checking only a
neighborhood of zero would leave a genuine gap.  Source 48 proves the same two
bounds from the same block (its \eqref{dpn:eq:centralbound} and
\eqref{dpn:eq:noncentral} are written with $V$ for $B$ and $L$ for $s$): the
worst case of Lemma~\ref{dpn:lem:block} at $|\theta|=c_1/M$ is a constant
times $h^3/M^2\asymp_\alpha M/s^3$, so in particular no other root-of-unity
arc contributes at an algebraic order.

At the saddle, Fourier inversion gives
\[
 \Prob_t(S=n)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\phi_t(\theta)\dd\theta.
\]
For bounded $x=\theta\sqrt B$, Taylor expansion and
\eqref{dpn:eq:normcumulants} give
$\phi_t(x/\sqrt B)\to e^{-x^2/2}$.
The central bound dominates this integrand by $e^{-c x^2}$.
The noncentral integral, even after multiplication by $\sqrt B$, is
$O(\sqrt B e^{-c_3M/s^3})=o(1)$. Thus
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:llt}
 \Prob_t(S=n)\sim(2\pi B)^{-1/2}.
\end{equation}
Together with \eqref{dpn:eq:tiltidentity}, this proves the leading part of
Theorem~\ref{dpn:thm:saddle}. In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:logsaddle}
 \log a_\alpha(n)=F(t_n)+nt_n+O_\alpha(s),
\end{equation}
since $\log B=O(s)$.

\subsection{A uniform variant used for conditioning}\label{dpn:sec:uniform}
The same proof gives the following useful extension. Delete either a fixed
number of sites $k=O(M)$, or all sites above a cutoff $m$ for which
$m/M\to\gamma>1$ and the deleted mean and variance are $O(M)$ and $O(M^2)$.
The central consecutive block remains available, the retained variance is
$B(1+o(1))$, and the cumulant bounds remain valid. If an integer target differs
from the retained mean by $o(\sqrt B)$, its point probability is
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:uniformllt}
 (2\pi B)^{-1/2}(1+o(1)).
\end{equation}
This follows in the central integral by inserting
$e^{-\ii x(m-\E S)/\sqrt B}\to1$; all the dominating estimates are unchanged.
In the fixed-site deletion case, the deleted variance is automatically
$O(M^2)$. The assertion is uniform for boundedly many deletions and shifts
bounded by a fixed multiple of $M$.  Source 48 records the same extension
from its proof of Theorem~\ref{dpn:thm:saddle}, with a factor
$e^{-\ii h\theta}$ in the integral: uniformly for $h=o(\sqrt B)$,
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:48:eq:shiftedllt}
 \Prob_t(S=-F'(t)+h)=\frac{1+o(1)}{\sqrt{2\pi B}}
 \quad\text{whenever }-F'(t)+h\text{ is an integer},
\end{equation}
also after deleting finitely many summands, or after truncating well above
the active interval, provided the removed variance is $o(B)$ \src{48}.

\section{The all-orders coefficient correction}\label{dpn:sec:edgeworth}
The normalized Fourier integrand has the expansion
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:centralseries}
 \phi_t(x/\sqrt B)
 =e^{-x^2/2}\exp\left(
       \sum_{j\ge3}\frac{\kappa_j(\ii x)^j}{j!B^{j/2}}\right).
\end{equation}
Assign weight $j-2$ to the $j$th normalized cumulant. Every monomial of odd
weight has odd total power of $x$ and integrates to zero. A monomial of weight
$2\ell$ has order $O(\eps^\ell)$ by \eqref{dpn:eq:normcumulants}.
Gaussian integration therefore gives
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:edgepoly}
 \mathcal E_\ell=
 \sum_{\substack{m_3,\ldots,m_{2\ell+2}\ge0\\
                  \sum_{j=3}^{2\ell+2}(j-2)m_j=2\ell}}
 \frac{(-1)^{T/2}(T-1)!!}{B^{T/2}}
 \prod_{j=3}^{2\ell+2}
       \frac{\kappa_j^{m_j}}{(j!)^{m_j}m_j!},
 \qquad T=\sum_{j=3}^{2\ell+2}jm_j.
\end{equation}
Here $T$ is automatically even. Besides \eqref{dpn:eq:E1}, the next term is
\begin{align}\label{dpn:eq:E2}
 \mathcal E_2={}&-\frac{\kappa_6}{48B^3}
 +\frac{7\kappa_3\kappa_5}{48B^4}
 +\frac{35\kappa_4^2}{384B^4}\notag\\
 &-\frac{35\kappa_3^2\kappa_4}{64B^5}
 +\frac{385\kappa_3^4}{1152B^6}.
\end{align}

For completeness, the formal expansion above has the needed integrated
remainder. On $|\theta|\le c/M$, derivatives of order $j\ge2$ of
$\log\E_t e^{\ii\theta S}$ have absolute value at most
$C_{\alpha,j}M^{j+1}/s$. For $k\le2M$, take $c$ sufficiently small so that
the Bernoulli denominators have modulus comparable with $1+k^\alpha e^{-tk}$.
For $k>2M$, the odds tend uniformly to zero; the denominators are bounded
away from zero and the exponentially decreasing tails give the same bound.
These observations justify Taylor's formula with controlled remainders.

Fix $J$, choose a small $\delta>0$ depending on $J$, and first restrict the
normalized integral to $|x|\le\eps^{-\delta}$. Expand through weight $2J-1$,
using Taylor's formula for the exponential. The terms of weight $2J$ and
higher have an integrable bound of the form
$C_J\eps^J(1+|x|^{C_J})e^{-x^2/4}$, after choosing $\delta$ small enough.
The omitted odd-weight terms integrate to zero on the symmetric interval.
Between this interval and $c\sqrt B/M$, \eqref{dpn:eq:centralbound} makes the
integral smaller than every power of $\eps$. The remaining Fourier range is
bounded by \eqref{dpn:eq:noncentral}, with the same conclusion. This proves
\eqref{dpn:eq:edgeworth} and its error for every fixed $J$.

\begin{remark}[What an effective finite-size bound would contain]\label{dpn:rem:effective}
Before absorbing flat terms into the asymptotic $O$-term, the proof contains
an additional relative bound of the shape
$C_\alpha\sqrt B\exp(-c_\alpha M/s^3)$ for noncentral arcs.
This can be much larger than the central Edgeworth corrections at attainable
$n$, especially for larger $\alpha$. The theorem is not a claim that its
first few terms have small relative error uniformly in $\alpha$ or for all
moderate $n$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The cumulative tuple form and a second proof \src{48}}
Source 48 writes the corrections cumulatively.  For a finitely supported tuple
$\bm m=(m_3,m_4,\ldots)$ of nonnegative integers put
$w(\bm m)=\sum_{j\ge3}(j-2)m_j$ and $T(\bm m)=\sum_{j\ge3}jm_j$.  Then, for
every $J\ge1$,
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:48:eq:edgeworth}
 1+\sum_{\ell=1}^{J-1}\mathcal E_\ell=
 \sum_{\substack{w(\bm m)<2J\\T(\bm m)\text{ even}}}
 (-1)^{T(\bm m)/2}(T(\bm m)-1)!!
 \prod_{j\ge3}\frac{1}{m_j!}
       \left(\frac{\kappa_j}{j!B^{j/2}}\right)^{m_j},
\end{equation}
where the empty tuple contributes $1$ and $(-1)!!=1$.  This is
\eqref{dpn:eq:edgepoly} summed over $\ell<J$ (an even $T$ is the same as an
even weight), and \eqref{dpn:eq:edgeworth} reads
$a_\alpha(n)=\frac{\exp(F(t_n)+nt_n)}{\sqrt{2\pi B}}(\text{\eqref{dpn:48:eq:edgeworth}}
+O_{\alpha,J}((s/M)^J))$; the leading formula is a genuine multiplicative
equivalent.

\begin{proof}[Second proof of Theorem~\ref{dpn:thm:saddle} \src{48}]
Fourier inversion and \eqref{dpn:eq:tiltidentity} reduce the assertion to
the probability $\Prob_t(S=n)$ at the exact saddle.  Set $u=\theta\sqrt B$
and center the characteristic function; its expansion at zero is
\eqref{dpn:eq:centralseries}, interpreted to any fixed finite Taylor order
with a remainder.

The Taylor expansion is uniform for the following reasons.  For
$|\theta|\le c_0/M$, derivatives of order $j\ge2$ of the logarithm of the
characteristic function are $O_{\alpha,j}(M^{j+1}/s)$.  For $k$ in a fixed
multiple of $[1,M]$, either the Bernoulli parameter is away from $1/2$, or the
small phase keeps the factor uniformly away from zero.  For $k$ above that
multiple of $M$, $p_k$ is small and its factor is again uniformly away from
zero.  The derivatives in both ranges are bounded by $C_jk^jp_k(1-p_k)$;
summation uses Lemma~\ref{dpn:lem:scales}.  Taylor's integral remainder
therefore obeys the corresponding bound from \eqref{dpn:eq:normcumulants}.

Explicit finite-order bookkeeping for the central integral: fix $J$, put
$D=2J+2$, and write
\[
 Z(u)=\sum_{j=3}^{D}\frac{\kappa_j(\ii u)^j}{j!B^{j/2}}.
\]
Taylor's remainder in the exponent is bounded by
$C_{\alpha,J}\eps^{(D-1)/2}|u|^{D+1}$.  Choose $0<\sigma<1/6$ sufficiently
small that also $\sigma<(D-1)/(2(D+1))$.  On $|u|\le\eps^{-\sigma}$, both
$Z(u)$ and this remainder tend uniformly to zero.  Consequently replacing the
exact exponential by $e^{-u^2/2}e^{Z(u)}$ has integrated error
$O_{\alpha,J}(\eps^{J+1/2})$, by the bound $|e^{a+b}-e^a|\le|b|e^{|a|+|b|}$
and a fixed Gaussian moment.

Next expand $e^{Z}$ through degree $2J$.  Its remainder, integrated against
the Gaussian, is again $O_{\alpha,J}(\eps^{J+1/2})$: every coefficient in $Z$
is $O_{\alpha,J}(\eps^{1/2})$, so $|Z|^{2J+1}$ is bounded by that power of
$\eps$ times a fixed polynomial in $|u|$.  In the finite expansion of the
retained powers, any monomial of weight at least $2J$ has integrated absolute
size $O_{\alpha,J}(\eps^J)$.  Every monomial of smaller weight occurs in these
powers, because each factor has positive weight and the largest required
index is at most $2J+1$.  Odd total degree integrates to zero on the symmetric
central interval.  The surviving terms are precisely those in
\eqref{dpn:48:eq:edgeworth}.  This argument uses only finite Taylor expansions
and their remainders, not uniform convergence of an infinite series.

The remainder of $|\theta|\le c_0/M$ is smaller than every power of $\eps$ by
\eqref{dpn:eq:centralbound}; the rest of $[-\pi,\pi]$ is likewise negligible
by \eqref{dpn:eq:noncentral}.  Integrating each retained polynomial over the
whole real line introduces only another negligible Gaussian tail.  The
Gaussian moment is zero for odd $T$ and equals $(T-1)!!$ for even $T$; the
phase $\ii^T$ supplies the sign.  At weight two the only possibilities are
$m_4=1$ and $m_3=2$, giving exactly \eqref{dpn:eq:E1}.
\end{proof}

\section{Continuum smoothing to every logarithmic order}\label{dpn:sec:sommerfeld}
\subsection{The inverse at the occupation boundary}
Define
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:hs}
 h_s(u)=s(u-1)-\log u.
\end{equation}
Near $u=1$ it is strictly increasing. Let $u_s(v)$ be its inverse with
$u_s(0)=1$, and put
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:U}
 U_m(s)=u_s^{(m)}(0).
\end{equation}
The derivative operator along the inverse is
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:Urec}
 \frac\dd{\dd v}=\frac1{s-1/u}\frac\dd{\dd u}.
\end{equation}
Starting with $u$ and applying \eqref{dpn:eq:Urec} repeatedly, then setting $u=1$,
gives an effective rational-function recursion. Equivalently, Lagrange
inversion gives
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:Ulagrange}
 U_m(s)=(m-1)!\,[z^{m-1}]
       \left(\frac{z}{sz-\log(1+z)}\right)^m.
\end{equation}
The first required odd derivatives are
\begin{align}\label{dpn:eq:Uexplicit}
 U_1(s)&=\frac1{s-1},\notag\\
 U_3(s)&=\frac{2s+1}{(s-1)^5},\notag\\
 U_5(s)&=\frac{24s^3+58s^2+22s+1}{(s-1)^9},\\
 U_7(s)&=\frac{720s^5+3708s^4+4400s^3+1452s^2+114s+1}
                    {(s-1)^{13}}.\notag
\end{align}

Write $\eta(z)=(1-2^{1-z})\zeta(z)$. The elementary even kernel
$g(v)=\log(1+e^{-|v|})$ has moments
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:kernelmom}
 \int_\R v^{2j}g(v)\dd v=2(2j)!\eta(2j+2),\qquad j\ge0.
\end{equation}
Indeed expand the logarithm on $(0,\infty)$ and integrate each exponentially
decaying term. Odd moments vanish.

\begin{lemma}[Boundary smoothing expansion]\label{dpn:lem:smoothing}
For every fixed $J\ge1$,
\begin{align}\label{dpn:eq:smoothing}
 \int_0^\infty\log(1+x^\alpha e^{-tx})\dd x
 =M\left(\alpha(s/2-1)+
      \sum_{j=1}^J\frac{2\eta(2j)}{\alpha^{2j-1}}U_{2j-1}(s)
      +O_{\alpha,J}(s^{-2J-1})\right),
\end{align}
where $t=\alpha s e^{-s}$. The expansion may be differentiated with respect
to $s$ any fixed number of times, retaining a remainder smaller than any
prescribed inverse power of $s$ by taking sufficiently many terms first.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
After $x=Mu$, the integrand is $\log(1+e^{-\alpha h_s(u)})$.
The baseline is
$\alpha M\int_0^1[s(1-u)+\log u]\dd u=\alpha M(s/2-1)$.
The discrepancy between this signed baseline and the positive-part baseline
is confined to the small lower crossing and is exponentially small in $s$
before multiplication by $M$.

Choose a fixed small $\rho>0$ and restrict initially to
$|u-1|<\rho$. Replacing the original integrand by its positive-part baseline
leaves $g(\alpha h_s(u))$. On a symmetric $v$-interval $|v|<\gamma s$
inside the image of this neighborhood, changing variables $v=h_s(u)$ gives
\[
 \int g(\alpha v)u_s'(v)\dd v.
\]
The omitted portions have exponentially small tails. Taylor-expand
$u_s'(v)$ through degree $2J-1$. On the chosen interval, repeated use of
\eqref{dpn:eq:Urec} bounds the derivative of order $2J$ of $u_s'$ by
$O_J(s^{-2J-1})$. The Taylor remainder is thus bounded by
$C_Js^{-2J-1}|v|^{2J}$, whose product with $g(\alpha v)$ is integrable.
After extending the polynomial terms to the whole real line, odd terms
vanish, and \eqref{dpn:eq:kernelmom} supplies exactly
$2\eta(2j)U_{2j-1}(s)/\alpha^{2j-1}$.

Here is a more explicit treatment of the region near zero, which must not
be mistaken for part of the main boundary. On $0<u<e^{-s/2}$, both the
original integrand and the absolute baseline have integral at most
$C_\alpha s e^{-s/2}$. On
$e^{-s/2}\le u\le1-\rho$, the concavity of
$s(1-u)+\log u$ puts it above a positive multiple of $s$ for large $s$.
The smoothing error there is exponentially small. On $u\ge1+\rho$ the
log odds are negative and decrease at rate comparable with $s$.
All these contributions are $O_{\alpha,J}(s^{-2J-1})$ for every fixed $J$.

Differentiation with respect to $s$ inserts powers of $1-u$ and derivatives
of the same logistic kernel. On the boundary interval the inverse derivatives
still have polynomial bounds in $s^{-1}$; away from it, exponential tail bounds
remain after multiplication by any fixed polynomial. Taylor expansion to a
higher order therefore gives the differentiated version with any prescribed
inverse-power precision. No assumption about differentiating an arbitrary
$O$-term is being used.
\end{proof}

\subsection{From the continuum to the lattice}
As in Lemma~\ref{dpn:lem:rough}, the difference between $F(t)$ and its continuum
integral is $O_\alpha(s)$. We also need the mean. The function
\[
 x\longmapsto \frac{x^{\alpha+1}e^{-tx}}{1+x^\alpha e^{-tx}}=xp(x)
\]
is unimodal and has maximum $O_\alpha(M)$. To verify unimodality, its derivative
has the sign of $1+(\alpha-tx)(1-p(x))$. This is positive for
$x\le\alpha/t$. For $x>\alpha/t$, the function
$(tx-\alpha)(1-p(x))$ increases strictly from zero to infinity. Thus
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:meanlattice}
 \sum_{k\ge1}kp_k=\int_0^\infty xp(x)\dd x+O_\alpha(M).
\end{equation}
This exact lattice error is negligible at every fixed inverse-logarithmic
order relative to the mean $M^2$.

Introduce the formal Laurent series at $s=\infty$
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:dformal}
 d_\alpha(s)=\sum_{j\ge1}
       \frac{2\eta(2j)}{\alpha^{2j}}U_{2j-1}(s).
\end{equation}
The notation is asymptotic/formal: no convergence of the infinite sum in $j$
is asserted. Each coefficient of $s^{-1}$ uses only finitely many summands,
since $U_m(s)=O_m(s^{-m})$. The expansion begins
\begin{align}\label{dpn:eq:dseries}
 d_\alpha(s)={}&\frac c s+\frac c{s^2}+\frac c{s^3}
 +\frac{c+7c^2/5}{s^4}
 +\frac{c+77c^2/10}{s^5}\notag\\
 &+\frac{c+49c^2/2+372c^3/35}{s^6}+\cdots.
\end{align}
In the all-orders logarithmic sense,
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:Fformal}
 F(t)\sim\alpha M\bigl(s/2-1+d_\alpha(s)\bigr).
\end{equation}
The lattice error $O(s)$ and the non-boundary continuum pieces are smaller
than $M/s^R$ for every fixed $R$. They are \emph{not} thereby zero and must
not be discarded when a multiplicative coefficient equivalent is wanted.

\subsection{A second route: an explicit differential operator \src{48}}\label{dpn:48:sec:operator}
Source 48 proves the two lattice estimates by the bounded-variation inequality
$|\sum_{k\ge1}f(k)-\int_0^\infty f|\le\int_0^\infty|f'|=2\max f$ for a
nonnegative unimodal $f$ with $f(0)=0$ tending to zero at infinity, applied to
$\log(1+\xi^\alpha e^{-t\xi})$ (maximum $O_\alpha(s)$) and to $\xi p(\xi)$
(maximum $O_\alpha(M)$, unimodal by the argument before
\eqref{dpn:eq:meanlattice}).  It then replaces the inverse $u_s$ and the
Lagrange coefficients $U_m$ by one explicit operator.  Let
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:48:eq:operator}
 f(s)=\frac{s}{s-1},\qquad
 \Top=\frac{s}{s-1}\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm ds},
\end{equation}
with $\eta$ as in Section~\ref{dpn:sec:sommerfeld}, and define the asymptotic
series at $s=\infty$
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:48:eq:Cs}
 \boxed{\displaystyle
 \Cop(s)\sim2\sum_{j\ge0}
       \frac{\eta(2j+2)}{\alpha^{2j+2}}\Top^{2j}f(s),
 \qquad \Bop(s)=\Cop(s)+\Top\Cop(s).}
\end{equation}
The formula is locally finite after expansion in $s^{-1}$: for $j\ge1$,
$\Top^{2j}f(s)=O(s^{-2j-1})$, so only finitely many summands affect any
prescribed coefficient.

\begin{lemma}[upper-transition expansion; source 48]\label{dpn:48:lem:sommerfeld}
With $t=\alpha se^{-s}$, in the sense of asymptotic series to every fixed
finite order,
\begin{align}
 F(t)&\sim\frac{\alpha^2}{t}
       \left(\frac{s^2}{2}-s+\Cop(s)\right),\label{dpn:48:eq:Sseries}\\
 -F'(t)&\sim\frac{\alpha^2}{t^2}
       \left(\frac{s^2}{2}+\Bop(s)\right).\label{dpn:48:eq:mseries}
\end{align}
More precisely, replacing $\Cop$ or $\Bop$ by its polynomial through $s^{-R}$
leaves $O_{\alpha,R}(s^{-R-1})$ inside the corresponding parentheses, for every
fixed $R\ge0$.
\end{lemma}
Since $\alpha^2/t=\alpha M/s$, \eqref{dpn:48:eq:Sseries} is
\eqref{dpn:eq:Fformal} with $d_\alpha(s)=\Cop(s)/s$, and
\eqref{dpn:48:eq:mseries} is the mean relation \eqref{dpn:eq:Q} with
$Q_\alpha(s)=1+2\Bop(s)/s^2$.
\begin{proof}
In the continuum integral of $F$, with integration variable $\xi$, put
$y=t\xi/\alpha$.  Since $\log(\alpha/t)=s-\log s$, the exponent becomes
\[
 \alpha\log\xi-t\xi=\alpha\tilde h_s(y),\qquad
 \tilde h_s(y)=s-y+\log(y/s).
\]
The zero-temperature contribution is
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:48:eq:zerotempintegral}
 \int_0^s \alpha\tilde h_s(y)\dd y=\alpha(s^2/2-s).
\end{equation}
Subtracting this reference integral is legitimate even though $\tilde h_s$
becomes negative very near zero; that region is included in the error
estimate.

On $y\ge s/2$, use the increasing coordinate $v=y-s-\log(y/s)=-\tilde h_s(y)$.
If $y=y(v)$ is its inverse, then $y(0)=s$ and $y'(v)=y(v)/(y(v)-1)=f(y(v))$.
The difference from the reference integrand is $g(\alpha v)=\log(1+e^{-\alpha|v|})$,
an even exponentially decreasing function, with moments
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:48:eq:moments}
 \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}v^{2j}g(\alpha v)\dd v
    =\frac{2(2j)!}{\alpha^{2j+1}}\eta(2j+2),
\end{equation}
by \eqref{dpn:eq:kernelmom}; these are the elementary Fermi--Dirac moments
underlying Sommerfeld expansions \cite{DLMFFermi}.  Since $\mathrm d/\mathrm dv
=f(y)\,\mathrm d/\mathrm dy$, Taylor expansion of $y'(v)$ at zero gives
\[
 \left.\frac{\mathrm d^{2j}}{\mathrm dv^{2j}}y'(v)\right|_{v=0}
       =\Top^{2j}f(s).
\]
On $|v|\le s/4$, $y(v)\asymp s$, and for each fixed $a\ge1$,
$\Top^af(y)=O_a(s^{-a-1})$.  Expanding through the odd degree $2J+1$,
integrating against the even kernel, and bounding the next derivative gives an
error $O_{\alpha,J}(s^{-2J-3})$.  The portions with $|v|>s/4$ are exponentially
small, since $y'(v)$ stays bounded on the domain $y\ge s/2$.

The range $0<y<s/2$ also has exponentially small error \emph{inside the scaled
integral}.  Split it at $e^{-s/2}$.  On the small interval, integrate the bound
$C_\alpha(1+s+|\log y|)$, obtaining $O_\alpha(se^{-s/2})$.  On
$[e^{-s/2},s/2]$, concavity gives $\tilde h_s(y)\ge s/2-\log s-O(1)$, so the
discrepancy is $O_\alpha(s^{\alpha+1}e^{-\alpha s/2})$.  These errors need not
remain exponentially small in the original, unscaled variable, but they are
smaller than every inverse power of $s$ inside the present parentheses.
Multiplying the integral by $\alpha/t$, the moments \eqref{dpn:48:eq:moments}
give \eqref{dpn:48:eq:Sseries}; the sum--integral error is absorbed at every
fixed order.

The mean formula is proved directly, rather than by differentiating an
unspecified big-$O$ term.  The scaled mean integral is
$\frac{\alpha^2}{t^2}\int_0^\infty y\,(1+e^{\alpha v(y)})^{-1}\dd y$, with
reference integral $\int_0^sy\dd y=s^2/2$.  Under the same coordinate change
the difference kernel is the odd function
\[
 \mathcal K_\alpha(v)=\frac{1}{1+e^{\alpha v}}-\ind_{v<0},
\]
integrated against $y(v)y'(v)$.  Its odd moments are
\[
 \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}v^{2j+1}\mathcal K_\alpha(v)\dd v
       =\frac{2(2j+1)!}{\alpha^{2j+2}}\eta(2j+2).
\]
The elementary operator identity $\Top(sf(s))=f(s)+\Top f(s)$ shows that the
resulting correction is precisely $\Cop+\Top\Cop$.  The same cutoff and Taylor
estimates apply, and \eqref{dpn:eq:meanlattice} absorbs the discretization
error.  This proves \eqref{dpn:48:eq:mseries} with controlled remainders,
independently of any differentiation rule for errors.
\end{proof}

In the parameter $c$, the beginning of \eqref{dpn:48:eq:Cs} is
\begin{align}
 \Cop(s)={}&c+\frac cs+\frac c{s^2}
    +\frac{c+\tfrac75c^2}{s^3}
    +\frac{c+\tfrac{77}{10}c^2}{s^4}
    +\frac{c+\tfrac{49}{2}c^2+\tfrac{372}{35}c^3}{s^5}
    +O_\alpha(s^{-6}),\label{dpn:48:eq:Cfirst}\\
 \Bop(s)={}&c+\frac cs
   +\frac{-2c+\tfrac75c^2}{s^3}
   +\frac{-5c+\tfrac72c^2}{s^4}+O_\alpha(s^{-5}).\label{dpn:48:eq:Bfirst}
\end{align}
In particular, the coefficient of $s^{-2}$ in $\Bop$ is zero, and
$\Cop(s)/s$ reproduces \eqref{dpn:eq:dseries} term by term.

\section{Eliminating the saddle and generating all coefficients}\label{dpn:sec:eliminate}
Since $\dd t/\dd s=-\alpha(s-1)/M$, differentiating the continuum expansion
and using \eqref{dpn:eq:meanlattice} gives the formal mean relation
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:Q}
 n\sim\frac{M^2}{2}Q_\alpha(s),\qquad
 Q_\alpha(s)=1+\frac{2(d_\alpha(s)+d_\alpha'(s))}{s-1}.
\end{equation}
Here $d_\alpha'$ denotes formal differentiation. Thus
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:rfroms}
 r=s+\tfrac12\log Q_\alpha(s).
\end{equation}
Because $Q_\alpha(s)=1+O(s^{-2})$, this has a unique formal inverse
$s=r+O(r^{-2})$. It is computed recursively, for example by iterating
$s\leftarrow r-\frac12\log Q_\alpha(s)$ in the ring of formal series in
$r^{-1}$. Every iteration determines additional coefficients.

Substitution in $F(t)+nt$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:formalH}
 \asym(r)=
 \frac{s(Q_\alpha(s)+1)/2-1+d_\alpha(s)}{\sqrt{Q_\alpha(s)}},
 \qquad r=s+\tfrac12\log Q_\alpha(s).
\end{equation}
By construction,
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:Hseries}
 \asym(r)=r-1+\sum_{j\ge1}P_j(c)r^{-j}.
\end{equation}
Equations \eqref{dpn:eq:Urec}, \eqref{dpn:eq:dformal}, \eqref{dpn:eq:Q}, and
\eqref{dpn:eq:formalH} are an all-orders algorithm involving only rational
operations and expansions of logarithms and square roots. Since even zeta
values are rational multiples of powers of $\pi^2$, the coefficients are
polynomials over $\mathbb Q$ in $c$.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{dpn:thm:log}]
Fix the desired order $R$. Apply Lemma~\ref{dpn:lem:smoothing} and its mean
version with more than enough terms that both remainders, after all
operations, are $O_{\alpha,R}(r^{-R-1})$ in the normalized entropy.
The exact mean relation has an additional error $O(M)$, negligible compared
with $M^2r^{-A}$ for every fixed $A$. Inverting
\eqref{dpn:eq:rfroms} is stable because its derivative is $1+O(s^{-3})$.
Finite Taylor substitution is consequently legitimate with remainders, not
only as a formal manipulation.

We obtain
\[
 F(t_n)+nt_n=\alpha K\left(r-1+\sum_{j=1}^R P_j(c)r^{-j}
                         +O_{\alpha,R}(r^{-R-1})\right).
\]
Equation \eqref{dpn:eq:logsaddle} contributes only $O(s)$, which is
$o(Kr^{-R-1})$. This proves \eqref{dpn:eq:logall}. Direct expansion gives
\eqref{dpn:eq:P}; the supplied symbolic script reproduces those coefficients.
\end{proof}

 P_8(c)=c+\frac{259}2c^2+\frac{7271}{10}c^3
                  +\frac{157813}{840}c^4.
\end{equation}

\subsection{The same elimination in operator form \src{48}}\label{dpn:48:sec:formal}
At the exact saddle, Lemma~\ref{dpn:48:lem:sommerfeld} gives, to every finite
inverse-logarithmic order,
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:48:eq:xr}
 K\sim M\left(1+\frac{2\Bop(s)}{s^2}\right)^{1/2},
\end{equation}
meaning the full finite-order expansion, not merely a leading ratio.
Equivalently, its formal inverse is
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:48:eq:Linverse}
 r=s+\frac12\log\left(1+\frac{2\Bop(s)}{s^2}\right),
\end{equation}
which is \eqref{dpn:eq:rfroms}.  There is a unique solution with
$s=r+O(r^{-2})$ as a formal series in $r^{-1}$.  Indeed, put $z=r^{-1}$ and
$\nu=s-r$.  Then $s^{-1}=z/(1+z\nu)$.  The right-hand side of the fixed-point
equation
\[
 \nu=-\frac12\log\left(1+\frac{2\Bop(r+\nu)}{(r+\nu)^2}\right)
\]
has order $z^2$, and changing $\nu$ by $O(z^d)$ changes the right-hand side by
$O(z^{d+3})$.  Iteration starting with $\nu=0$ therefore determines every
coefficient after finitely many steps.  The first terms are
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:48:eq:sfirst}
 s=r-\frac c{r^2}-\frac c{r^3}+\frac{c^2}{r^4}
       +\frac{2c-\tfrac75c^2}{r^5}+O_\alpha(r^{-6}).
\end{equation}

At the saddle, addition of \eqref{dpn:48:eq:Sseries} and $nt$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:48:eq:Legendre}
 F(t)+nt\sim\alpha M\left(s-1+\frac{2\Cop(s)+\Top\Cop(s)}{s}\right).
\end{equation}
The logarithm of the Gaussian prefactor in Theorem~\ref{dpn:thm:saddle} is
$O_\alpha(s)$, and the logarithm of its relative correction is $o(1)$; both
are smaller than every scale $K/r^R$.  Thus the formal series determining
Theorem~\ref{dpn:thm:log} is
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:48:eq:Hrule}
 \boxed{\displaystyle
 \asym(r)=
 \frac{s-1+(2\Cop(s)+\Top\Cop(s))/s}
      {\sqrt{1+2\Bop(s)/s^2}},
 \qquad
 \asym(r)=r-1+\sum_{j\ge1}P_j(c)r^{-j},}
\end{equation}
with $s$ substituted from \eqref{dpn:48:eq:Linverse}.  This is
\eqref{dpn:eq:formalH}: with $d_\alpha=\Cop/s$ and $Q_\alpha=1+2\Bop/s^2$ the
numerators agree, since $s(Q_\alpha+1)/2-1+d_\alpha=s-1+(\Bop+\Cop)/s$.

\paragraph{Why the formal rule proves actual asymptotics.}
Fix a desired final order $R$.  Take sufficiently many terms, for example
through order $s^{-R-4}$, in the two estimates of
Lemma~\ref{dpn:48:lem:sommerfeld}.  Their remainders are genuine uniform
estimates on large real $s$.  The derivative of the finite approximation in
\eqref{dpn:48:eq:Linverse} is $1+O(s^{-3})$, bounded away from zero.  The mean
value theorem transports its error to an error of the same inverse-log order.
Substitution into \eqref{dpn:48:eq:Legendre}, whose normalized derivative is
bounded at the relevant order, then gives the remainder in
\eqref{dpn:eq:logall}.  This is a finite calculation with controlled
remainders, not an assumption that the infinite formal series converges.
Together with Theorem~\ref{dpn:thm:saddle}, this is source 48's proof of
Theorem~\ref{dpn:thm:log}; substitution of
\eqref{dpn:48:eq:Cfirst}--\eqref{dpn:48:eq:sfirst} into
\eqref{dpn:48:eq:Hrule} gives $P_1,\dots,P_6$.

No special symbolic integration routine is needed.  In the variable
$z=s^{-1}$, the operator is
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:48:eq:Tz}
 \Top=-\frac{z^2}{1-z}\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dz},\qquad f=\frac1{1-z}.
\end{equation}
The factors $2\eta(2j+2)/\alpha^{2j+2}$ are rational multiples of $c^{j+1}$,
since even zeta values are rational multiples of even powers of $\pi$.
Truncated polynomial arithmetic with \eqref{dpn:48:eq:Tz}, followed by the
fixed-point substitution, generates the coefficients at any prescribed finite
order.  The program \file{code/48-a022629-coefficients.py} implements this
construction and verifies the six displayed polynomials and six inverse
coefficients exactly.

\subsection{An entropy factor beyond the largest summand \src{48}}
Combining Theorem~\ref{dpn:thm:log} with
Proposition~\ref{dpn:48:prop:maxweight} gives an additional conclusion.
\begin{corollary}[source 48]\label{dpn:48:cor:entropy}
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:48:eq:entropy}
 \log\frac{a_\alpha(n)}{\Pi_\alpha(n)}\sim\frac{\pi^2}{6\alpha}\frac{K}{r}.
\end{equation}
Thus the total weighted enumeration exceeds its largest individual weight by a
factor that tends to infinity, and whose logarithm has a precise leading
constant.
\end{corollary}
For $\alpha=1$, this explains why using only the most heavily weighted
partition gets the first two large terms right but misses a multiplicatively
enormous factor.  The transition contains approximately $M/s$ available sizes,
and its accumulated smoothing contribution produces this factor.

\section{\texorpdfstring{Lambert-$W$}{Lambert-W} inversion with an error theorem}\label{dpn:sec:inverse}
The coefficients are strictly increasing for $n\ge1$. Indeed, increasing the
largest part of a distinct partition by one is an injection into distinct
partitions of $n+1$, and strictly increases each positive norm weight.
Thus the threshold index $N_\alpha(Y)$ in Theorem~\ref{dpn:thm:inverse} is
well-defined for large $Y$.

The dominant inversion problem is
\[
 Y=\alpha K(\log K-1).
\]
Writing $w=\log K-1$ gives $we^w=Y/(\alpha e)$, proving
\eqref{dpn:eq:Wlead}. In particular $\log K_0=R_0$.
Set $K=K_0E$. The all-orders formal inversion is the single equation
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:Einverse}
 E\,\asym(R_0+\log E)=R_0-1,
 \qquad E=1+O(R_0^{-2}).
\end{equation}
The derivative with respect to $E$ is $R_0+O(1)$ at $E=1$, so successive
coefficients are uniquely determined. In terms of $c$, they are
\begin{align}\label{dpn:eq:inverseE}
 E={}&1-\frac c{R_0^2}-\frac c{R_0^3}
       +\frac{3c^2/2-c}{R_0^4}
       -\frac{c+2c^2/5}{R_0^5}\notag\\
 &-\frac{c+46c^2/5+5c^3/2}{R_0^6}+\cdots,
\end{align}
and hence
\begin{align}\label{dpn:eq:inverseE2}
 E^2={}&1-\frac{2c}{R_0^2}-\frac{2c}{R_0^3}
       +\frac{4c^2-2c}{R_0^4}
       +\frac{6c^2/5-2c}{R_0^5}\notag\\
 &-\frac{2c+77c^2/5+8c^3}{R_0^6}+\cdots.
\end{align}
The asymptotic index is $K_0^2E^2/2$.

To justify inversion of the sequence rather than just of a formal expression,
fix an order, use Theorem~\ref{dpn:thm:log} two orders farther than needed, and
bracket the target by the corresponding smooth functions with the two signs
of its error bound. Their derivatives with respect to $K$ are
$\alpha r(1+o(1))>0$. An entropy error $O(Kr^{-R-1})$ therefore changes $K$
by $O(Kr^{-R-2})$, and changes $n=K^2/2$ by
$O(K^2r^{-R-2})$. These bounds transfer the formal inversion to the actual
threshold by monotonicity. Rounding the brackets to integers costs $O(1)$,
smaller than all of these fixed-order error scales. This proves
Theorem~\ref{dpn:thm:inverse} and its all-orders extension.

\begin{remark}[Two different Lambert branches]
The saddle phase uses $W_{-1}$ because $s\to\infty$ solves
$se^{-s}=t/\alpha\to0^+$. The threshold inverse uses $W_0$ at a positive
argument. Interchanging the branches would destroy the relevant scale.
If inversion in elementary logarithms is desired, the usual formal solution
of $w+\log w=\log(Y/(\alpha e))$ can be substituted into
\eqref{dpn:eq:inverseE2}; retaining $W_0$ is shorter and preserves more precision.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The same inversion in source 48's form \src{48}}
Source 48 proves Theorem~\ref{dpn:thm:inverse} independently (its
Theorem~6.1), with the threshold written in the logarithm,
$N_\alpha(Y)=\min\{n\ge1:\log a_\alpha(n)\ge Y\}$; for a threshold on the
sequence itself one uses $N_\alpha(\log\,\cdot\,)$.  The minimum is well
defined because the coefficients diverge (Proposition~\ref{dpn:prop:elementary})
and increase strictly for $n\ge1$ by the injection above; the initial equality
$a_\alpha(0)=a_\alpha(1)=1$ is immaterial.  Its statement displays two more
orders than \eqref{dpn:eq:inverselead}:
\begin{align}
 N_\alpha(Y)=\frac{K_0^2}{2}\bigg(&1-\frac{2c}{R_0^2}
                       -\frac{2c}{R_0^3}
                       +\frac{4c^2-2c}{R_0^4}
                       +\frac{\tfrac65c^2-2c}{R_0^5}\notag\\
                       &-\frac{8c^3+\tfrac{77}{5}c^2+2c}{R_0^6}
                       +O_\alpha(R_0^{-7})\bigg),\label{dpn:48:eq:inversefull}
\end{align}
the coefficients of \eqref{dpn:eq:inverseE2}; integer rounding changes the
result by at most an additional $O(1)$, absorbed in every fixed
inverse-logarithmic remainder.  With $E=1+\delta$ and
$C(r)=\sum_{j\ge1}P_j(c)r^{-j}$, its normalized equation for the corrected
inverse,
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:48:eq:inversefixed}
 \boxed{\displaystyle
 \delta=-\frac{(1+\delta)\log(1+\delta)-\delta
           +(1+\delta)C(R_0+\log(1+\delta))}{R_0},}
\end{equation}
is \eqref{dpn:eq:Einverse} solved for $\delta$.  Starting from $\delta=0$,
finite formal iteration gives the unique series with $\delta=O(R_0^{-2})$,
whose coefficients are those of $E-1$ in \eqref{dpn:eq:inverseE}.

\begin{proof}[Second proof of the threshold transfer \src{48}]
The core equation $K(\log K-1)=Y/\alpha$ is solved exactly by $K=K_0$, using
$we^w=Y/(\alpha e)$.  This is the principal, positive Lambert branch, distinct
from the lower branch used for the saddle.  Choose any finite truncation of
Theorem~\ref{dpn:thm:log} and define its continuous approximation on large
real $K$.  Its derivative is $\alpha(\log K+O_\alpha(1/\log K))$, positive and
comparable to $\alpha\log K$.  An error $O_{\alpha,R}(Kr^{-R-1})$ in the
logarithm therefore moves its inverse by $O_{\alpha,R}(Kr^{-R-2})$.  This
follows directly from the mean value theorem on a fixed relative neighborhood
of the core root; the preliminary leading asymptotic puts the true root in
that neighborhood.  For the discrete threshold, apply these upper and lower
approximations at $N_\alpha(Y)$ and $N_\alpha(Y)-1$.  Adjacent values of
$K=\sqrt{2n}$ differ by $O(1/K)$, so the final bracketing introduces only an
$O(1)$ uncertainty in $n$.  Taking the forward expansion to sufficiently high
fixed order proves any desired inverse order.
\end{proof}

The first inverse correction is negative: the actual enumeration is larger
than its core approximation, so it reaches a specified threshold sooner.  For
A022629 ($\alpha=1$, $2c=\pi^2/3$) this begins
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:48:eq:N1}
 N_1(Y)=\frac12\left(\frac{Y}{W_0(Y/e)}\right)^2
 \left(1-\frac{\pi^2}{3(1+W_0(Y/e))^2}
          +O\bigl((1+W_0(Y/e))^{-3}\bigr)\right).
\end{equation}
The distinction between inversion in $Y=\log a(n)$ and inversion in $a(n)$ is
essential; replacing $Y$ by the original threshold would give a wholly wrong
scale \src{48}.

\section{Conditioned geometry: a logistic boundary and a Gumbel maximum}
Conditioning the Bernoulli law \eqref{dpn:eq:pk} on $S=n$ gives exactly
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:conditioned}
 \Prob_n(\lambda)=\frac{\normp(\lambda)^\alpha}{a_\alpha(n)}.
\end{equation}
No approximation is involved in this identification.

\subsection{The boundary profile}
Fix distinct integer sites $k_{1,n},\ldots,k_{d,n}$ with
$(k_{j,n}-M)/H\to x_j\in\R$. Their unconditioned probabilities tend to
$p(x_j)=(1+e^{x_j})^{-1}$ by Lemma~\ref{dpn:lem:scales}.
For any prescribed pattern $\epsilon_j\in\{0,1\}$, independence gives
\begin{align}\label{dpn:eq:pattern}
 &\Prob_n(X_{k_{j,n}}=\epsilon_j\text{ for all }j)\notag\\
 &\quad=\prod_{j=1}^d p_{k_{j,n}}^{\epsilon_j}
                       (1-p_{k_{j,n}})^{1-\epsilon_j}
 \frac{\Prob_t\{S^{\rm rem}=n-\sum_jk_{j,n}\epsilon_j\}}
      {\Prob_t(S=n)}.
\end{align}
The shifted target differs from the remaining mean by $O(M)=o(\sqrt B)$.
The uniform local limit theorem in Section~\ref{dpn:sec:uniform} makes the ratio
of point probabilities tend to one. This proves both the logistic limit and
finite-site asymptotic independence. Similarly, a site at $\gamma M$ has
limiting occupation $1$ for $0<\gamma<1$, and $0$ for $\gamma>1$.

\subsection{The correct centering of the maximum}
The large solution of \eqref{dpn:eq:bdef} exists uniquely for sufficiently small
$t$. With $z=tb$, the equation is $z^\alpha e^{-z}=t^{\alpha+1}$, so
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:blambert}
 b(t)=-\frac\alpha t\,
 W_{-1}\left(-\frac{t^{1+1/\alpha}}\alpha\right).
\end{equation}
Solving its logarithm,
$z-\alpha\log z=-(\alpha+1)\log t$, with $t=\alpha s e^{-s}$ yields
\begin{align}\label{dpn:eq:bexpand}
 \frac bM={}&1+\frac1\alpha
 +\frac{-\log s+\alpha\log(\alpha+1)-(\alpha+1)\log\alpha}
                {\alpha s}
 +O_\alpha\left(\frac{\log s}{s^2}\right).
\end{align}
Keeping only $b\sim(1+1/\alpha)M$ is not adequate for the Gumbel
centering: the omitted shift has size $M\log s/s$, larger than $1/t$.
Keeping all the displayed terms in \eqref{dpn:eq:bexpand} does give an
$o(1/t)$ centering error. The exact definition \eqref{dpn:eq:bdef} or
\eqref{dpn:eq:blambert} avoids this truncation bookkeeping altogether.

\begin{proof}[Proof of the maximum assertion in Theorem~\ref{dpn:thm:geometry}]
Fix $x\in\R$ and put $m=\lfloor b+x/t\rfloor$. Then $tm\to\infty$ and
$m/M\to1+1/\alpha>1$. In the tail $k>m$, all odds tend uniformly to zero.
A sum--integral comparison followed by integration by parts gives
\begin{align}\label{dpn:eq:tailint}
 \sum_{k>m}p_k
 &\sim\sum_{k>m}k^\alpha e^{-tk}
 \sim\frac{m^\alpha e^{-tm}}t
 \longrightarrow e^{-x}.
\end{align}
For the second equivalence, substitute $v=tx$ in the integral and use
$\int_z^\infty v^\alpha e^{-v}\dd v
=z^\alpha e^{-z}(1+O_\alpha(z^{-1}))$.
The endpoint discretization has relative size $O(t)$.
The last limit follows from $b^\alpha e^{-tb}/t=1$ and
$m/b\to1$. Also, $\sup_{k>m}p_k=O(t)$, so $\sum_{k>m}p_k^2=o(1)$.
Therefore the unconditioned probability of no part above $m$ is
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:tailzero}
 \prod_{k>m}(1-p_k)\longrightarrow e^{-e^{-x}}.
\end{equation}

It remains to justify conditioning, rather than silently replacing one
ensemble by another. The removed mean and variance satisfy
\[
 \sum_{k>m}kp_k=O(M),\qquad
 \sum_{k>m}k^2p_k(1-p_k)=O(M^2).
\]
These bounds follow from the same tail-integral calculation with one or two
extra powers of $k$. The remaining sum $S_{\le m}$ thus has mean
$n-O(M)$ and variance $B(1+o(1))$. Its central block around $M$ is intact.
The uniform local limit estimate gives
\[
 \frac{\Prob_t(S_{\le m}=n)}{\Prob_t(S=n)}\longrightarrow1.
\]
Independence of the retained and removed sites now gives
\[
 \Prob_n(L_n\le m)=
       \prod_{k>m}(1-p_k)
       \frac{\Prob_t(S_{\le m}=n)}{\Prob_t(S=n)}
       \longrightarrow e^{-e^{-x}}.
\]
This proves the Gumbel limit. Its tightness, \eqref{dpn:eq:bexpand},
$M\sim K$, and $(tM)^{-1}=1/(\alpha s)\to0$ imply
$L_n/K\to1+1/\alpha$ in probability.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{The two locations are genuinely different.}
The occupation boundary lies near $M\sim\sqrt{2n}$, while the maximum lies
near $(1+1/\alpha)M$. For $\alpha=1$, a typical largest part is asymptotic
to $2\sqrt{2n}$, not to $\sqrt{2n}$. Individual occupancies far above the
boundary are small, but there are enough possible sites to move the extreme
by a macroscopic multiple of $M$. This is why a boundary profile alone does
not determine the largest part.

\subsection{Explicit centring in \texorpdfstring{$\log\sqrt{2n}$}{log sqrt(2n)} \src{48}}\label{dpn:48:sec:gumbel}
Source 48 proves the Gumbel law independently, with the centring written in
$K=\sqrt{2n}$ and $r=\log K$ instead of the exact root $b(t_n)$.  For positive
integer $\alpha$ the measure $\Prob_n$ is the size distribution obtained by
choosing uniformly among the coloured objects; for every $t>0$ it is exactly
the conditional distribution of $\{k:X_k=1\}$ given $S=n$ \src{48}.

\begin{corollary}[Gumbel law with explicit centring; sources 48 and 40]\label{dpn:48:thm:gumbel}
Define
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:48:eq:centering}
 \beta_\alpha(r)=(\alpha+1)r-\log r+\alpha\log(\alpha+1)-(\alpha+1)\log\alpha.
\end{equation}
For every fixed real $x$,
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:48:eq:gumbel}
 \Prob_n\left(\frac{\alpha r}{K}L_n-\beta_\alpha(r)\le x\right)
                 \longrightarrow\exp(-e^{-x}).
\end{equation}
For A022629 ($\alpha=1$), an equivalent form is
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:48:eq:gumbels1}
 \frac{L_n-\left(2K-\frac{K}{r}\log(r/2)\right)}{K/r}
                  \ \Longrightarrow\ G,
 \qquad \Prob(G\le x)=e^{-e^{-x}}.
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}
Given Theorem~\ref{dpn:thm:geometry}, the corollary amounts to
$t_nb(t_n)-\beta_\alpha(r)\to0$: by \eqref{dpn:eq:bexpand},
$t_nb=\alpha s\,b/M$ agrees with
$(\alpha+1)s-\log s+\alpha\log(\alpha+1)-(\alpha+1)\log\alpha$ up to
$O_\alpha(\log s/s)$, and $s=r+O(r^{-2})$, $t_n=(\alpha r/K)(1+O(r^{-2}))$ by
\eqref{dpn:48:eq:xr} and \eqref{dpn:48:eq:sfirst}.  (The intake compared the
two centrings numerically for $\alpha=1,2,5$; the difference decays like
$\log s/s$.)  Source 48 proves it directly.

\begin{proof}[Proof \src{48}]
Work first with the unconditioned Bernoulli variables at the exact saddle,
with $s,M,t$ as in \eqref{dpn:eq:phase}.  Set
\[
 d=(\alpha+1)s-\log s+\alpha\log(\alpha+1)-(\alpha+1)\log\alpha,
 \qquad m=\left\lfloor\frac{d+x}{t}\right\rfloor.
\]
Then $tm\to\infty$ and $m/M\to(\alpha+1)/\alpha>1$.  Summation versus
integration for a decreasing tail, followed by one integration by parts (or
its standard real-power analogue), gives
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:48:eq:tailasympt}
 \sum_{k>m}k^\alpha e^{-tk}
   \sim t^{-\alpha-1}(tm)^\alpha e^{-tm}.
\end{equation}
For real $\alpha>0$, the relative error from the integral asymptotic is
$O_\alpha((tm)^{-1})$; the relative discretization error is $O_\alpha(t)$.
Now $\log(1/t)=s-\log s-\log\alpha$ and
$\log(d+x)=\log s+\log(\alpha+1)+o(1)$.  Hence
\[
 (\alpha+1)\log(1/t)+\alpha\log(tm)-tm=-x+o(1),
\]
and the tail in \eqref{dpn:48:eq:tailasympt} tends to $e^{-x}$.  The maximum
individual odds on this tail are $O_\alpha(t)$ and tend to zero, so replacing
odds by $p_k$ changes the sum by $o(1)$, and $\sum_{k>m}p_k^2=o(1)$.
Independence gives
\[
 \Prob_t(X_k=0\text{ for every }k>m)=\prod_{k>m}(1-p_k)\longrightarrow e^{-e^{-x}}.
\]

It remains to justify conditioning on total size, which cannot be omitted.
Let $T=\sum_{k>m}kX_k$ and $S^{\le m}=S-T$.  The same tail calculation with
extra factors of $k$ gives $\E_tT=O_\alpha(M)$ and $\Var_t(T)=O_\alpha(M^2)$.
By Lemma~\ref{dpn:lem:scales}, $\E_tT/\sqrt B\to0$ and $\Var_t(T)/B\to0$.  The
entire active interval near $M$ lies below $m$ for large $n$.  Thus the
shifted local limit estimate \eqref{dpn:48:eq:shiftedllt} applies to the
truncated sum, and $\Prob_t(S^{\le m}=n)/\Prob_t(S=n)\to1$.  Independence of
the tail and the truncated sum now gives
\[
 \Prob_n(L_n\le m)
 =\Prob_t(T=0)\frac{\Prob_t(S^{\le m}=n)}{\Prob_t(S=n)}
 \longrightarrow e^{-e^{-x}}.
\]
This proves the result in the exact saddle coordinates.  Finally,
\eqref{dpn:48:eq:xr} and \eqref{dpn:48:eq:sfirst} imply $M/K=1+O_\alpha(r^{-2})$,
$s=r+O_\alpha(r^{-2})$, and $t=(\alpha r/K)(1+O_\alpha(r^{-2}))$.  The change in
the normalized extreme-value coordinate is $o(1)$, since $L_n/M$ is tight by
the limit just proved.  Replacing $s,M,t$ by $r,K,\alpha r/K$ therefore proves
\eqref{dpn:48:eq:gumbel}; tightness of the Gumbel coordinate also gives
$L_n/K\to1+1/\alpha$.  For $\alpha=1$, $\beta_1(r)=2r-\log(r/2)$, which is
\eqref{dpn:48:eq:gumbels1}.
\end{proof}

The upper transition is near $M\sim K$, but the largest part is near
$(1+1/\alpha)K$.  There is no contradiction: a long tail of individually rare
occupancies produces an extreme well beyond the transition \src{48}.

Source 48 also proves the logistic part of Theorem~\ref{dpn:thm:geometry} (its
Proposition~7.2): for fixed distinct reals $u_1,\dots,u_d$ and
$k_j=\lfloor M+u_j/t\rfloor$ at the exact saddle, the inclusion indicators
converge jointly to independent Bernoulli variables with probabilities
$(1+e^{u_j})^{-1}$.  Since $tH=s/(s-1)\to1$, its coordinate $u=t(k-M)$ and
source 40's $x=(k-M)/H$ have the same limit, and its proof is the argument
around \eqref{dpn:eq:pattern}: the log odds at $k_j$ are $-u_j+o(1)$, and
fixing finitely many indicators moves the remaining target by $O_\alpha(M)$
and removes variance $O_\alpha(M^2)$, both negligible.

\section{A proved exponential scale from Fourier resonances}\label{dpn:sec:resonance}
The bound \eqref{dpn:eq:noncentral} suggests an exponentially small scale not
visible in either the logarithmic block or the central Edgeworth series.
We can identify its constant at each fixed integer resonance.

\begin{theorem}[Fixed-resonance modulus]\label{dpn:thm:resonance}
For every fixed nonzero integer $\ell$, with $\phi_t$ as in
Section~\ref{dpn:sec:llt},
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:eq:resonance}
 -\log\left|\phi_t\left(\frac{2\pi\ell}{M}\right)\right|
 \sim\frac{2\pi^4\ell^2}{3}\frac{H^3}{M^2}
 \sim\frac{2\pi^4\ell^2}{3\alpha^3}\frac M{s^3}.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The exact modulus formula is
\[
 \log|\phi_t(\theta)|
  =\frac12\sum_{k\ge1}\log\left(1-4p_k(1-p_k)
                                    \sin^2(k\theta/2)\right).
\]
At $\theta=2\pi\ell/M$, write $k=M+xH$. For bounded $x$,
\[
 \sin^2(\pi\ell k/M)
 =\pi^2\ell^2 x^2H^2/M^2+O_\ell(x^4H^4/M^4).
\]
The logarithm is therefore $-4p_k(1-p_k)$ times this sine square to leading
order. Lemma~\ref{dpn:lem:scales}, applied with moments $2$ and $4$, gives
\[
 -\log|\phi_t(2\pi\ell/M)|
 \sim\frac{2\pi^2\ell^2H^3}{M^2}
     \int_\R x^2\frac{e^x}{(1+e^x)^2}\dd x.
\]
One may first restrict to $|x|\le s^\delta$ with small fixed $\delta>0$;
Taylor remainders are then uniformly controlled. Outside this interval,
the exponential boundary tails and the lower-endpoint bounds in
Lemma~\ref{dpn:lem:scales} are negligible at the displayed scale. The quadratic
logarithm error is $O(H^5/M^4)=o(H^3/M^2)$.
Finally,
\[
 \int_\R x^2\frac{e^x}{(1+e^x)^2}\dd x
 =4\sum_{j\ge1}\frac{(-1)^{j-1}}{j^2}=\frac{\pi^2}{3},
\]
by expansion on the positive half-line. This proves the theorem.
\end{proof}

For fixed $\alpha$, the scale $\exp[-C_\alpha M/s^3]$ is smaller than
every power of $s/M$, because $M/s^3$ dominates $\log M$. However, its
constant deteriorates as $\alpha^{-3}$. This makes the fixed-$\alpha$
asymptotic theorem compatible with large visible resonances at moderate $n$.

The theorem concerns the \emph{modulus at a specified point}. It does not
yet determine the shifted secondary saddles, their phases, or the contribution
of their full neighborhoods to the coefficient integral. Calling it a
complete multi-saddle transseries would be incorrect. It does identify a
rigorous exponential scale and a concrete next target for exponential
improvement.

\subsection{What the numerical implementation guarantees}
The integer arithmetic and recurrence comparisons are exact. For the saddle
calculations, the program uses arbitrary-precision arithmetic and chooses a
cutoff from a geometric majorant for
$\sum k^{\alpha+6}e^{-tk}$. This bounds the tails relevant to all cumulants
through order six, apart from harmless fixed constants. The test used
50 requested decimal digits with guard digits; the saved mean residuals are
far smaller than the displayed precision. Nevertheless, the majorant and
Newton iteration are evaluated in floating point, not outward-rounded
interval arithmetic. Accordingly, the CSV values are diagnostics rather than
certificates of rigorous numerical error bounds.

\subsection{Source 48's computations for A022629 \src{48}}\label{dpn:48:sec:computations}
\paragraph{Two exact coefficient constructions.}
For an integer exponent $\alpha\ge1$, multiply the product factors
successively.  Starting with $a_0=1$ and $a_n=0$ for $n>0$, process each $k$ by
updating in \emph{decreasing} order of $n$:
$a_n\leftarrow a_n+k^\alpha a_{n-k}$ $(n\ge k)$.  Descending order enforces at
most one use of size $k$; increasing order would compute a different
partition problem.  The independent check is the logarithmic-derivative
recurrence \eqref{dpn:eq:exactrec}.  That formal identity is valid regardless
of the analytic restriction in Proposition~\ref{dpn:48:prop:zeros}: only
finitely many terms contribute to a fixed degree.  The program
\file{code/48-a022629-verify.py} compares the two constructions through degree
100, checks the initial OEIS values and strict monotonicity, and computes the
whole exact table through $n=6400$ for $\alpha=1$
(\file{data/48-a022629-exact_coefficients.csv}).

also recomputed both from the product through $n=6400$.}

\paragraph{Saddle diagnostics.}
The exact saddle is found by Newton iteration,
$t_{\rm new}=t+(-F'(t)-n)/B(t)$, with a positivity safeguard.  The first four
cumulants use $b_2=p(1-p)$, $b_3=p(1-p)(1-2p)$ and
$b_4=p(1-p)\{1-6p(1-p)\}$.  For $\beta\ge0$ the explicit tail bound
\begin{equation}\label{dpn:48:eq:numerictail}
 \sum_{k>k_0}k^\beta e^{-tk}
 \le e^tt^{-\beta-1}\Gamma(\beta+1,t(k_0+1))
\end{equation}
follows by integrating $(u+1)^\beta e^{-tu}$ over $u\ge k_0$.  It controls
truncation of the log product, mean, variance, and fixed-order cumulants after
multiplication by a suitable polynomial constant.  This addresses the infinite
sums, but the implementation does not turn floating-point rounding into
certified interval arithmetic.

\section{Further research questions and proposed projects}\label{dpn:sec:future}
The proved results leave several substantial and concrete directions.

\paragraph{1. An effective multi-saddle expansion.}
Locate the secondary saddles near $2\pi\ell/M$, compute their phases and
Gaussian factors, and sum the relevant arcs with a uniform remainder. Theorem
\ref{dpn:thm:resonance} supplies the first exponential action but not the full
contribution. The moderate-$n$ failures for $\alpha=3,4,5$ make this a practical
as well as theoretical priority.

\paragraph{2. A complete two-scale coefficient transseries.}
Refine the continuum-to-lattice passage sufficiently to retain endpoint terms,
Euler--Maclaurin corrections, the central determinant, and the resonance
sectors in one consistent normalization. Determine which of these expansions
are Gevrey and whether optimal truncation produces a useful exponential
improvement. The present all-logarithmic series alone does not settle this.

\paragraph{3. The crossover as the tilt vanishes.}
The fixed-$\alpha$ expansion is singular as $\alpha\downarrow0$, while the
unweighted distinct-partition count has a different growth law. A natural
crossover parameter is $\alpha\log K$. Derive a uniform family of saddle
profiles when this parameter stays bounded, and match it to both the
unweighted regime and the fixed-positive-tilt regime. The shrinking-tilt
analysis in Gikunda's thesis provides an important comparison point
\cite{gikunda}, but matching the full regimes still requires work.

\paragraph{4. Growing moments and concentration near maximal norm.}
Allow $\alpha=\alpha(n)\to\infty$. The fluctuation width $H$ and resonance
action show that several thresholds can occur before the model collapses onto
maximal-norm partitions. Determine the sharp parameter ranges for a local
limit theorem, for single-saddle dominance, and for localization near an
extremal partition. The fixed-$\alpha$ proof does not justify substituting an
arbitrary growing parameter.

\paragraph{5. The joint law of the largest several parts.}
The independent tail calculation suggests a Poisson process with intensity
$e^{-x}\dd x$ for the rescaled upper parts. Prove its conditioned point-process
limit and obtain joint distributions of the largest $j$ parts, including
second-order corrections to their centering. The maximum theorem is the
one-dimensional starting point, not a substitute for tightness of the process.

\paragraph{6. Fluctuations of the number of parts and of the log norm.}
A multivariate conditioned local limit theorem should describe the number of
occupied sites and the logarithm of the norm under fixed positive tilt.
Conditioning removes a covariance direction and can cancel a leading
variance term; computing the surviving scale requires more than plugging the
unconditioned variance into a Gaussian formula.

\paragraph{7. More general weights.}
Replace $k^\alpha$ by $k^\alpha(\log k)^\beta$ away from the harmless small
indices, or by a smooth regularly varying weight. Identify hypotheses that
preserve a single large crossing, the logistic boundary, and a computable
inverse expansion. Arithmetic thinning of the allowed part sizes would also
require a replacement for the consecutive-block lemma and may introduce
persistent lattice obstructions.

\paragraph{8. Certified numerics and formal verification.}
Give explicit constants in the Fourier bounds, add interval-enclosed saddle
solving, and certify a chosen relative-error target at a stated $n$ and
$\alpha$. For Lean, a sensible modular route is: the formal Euler product and
recurrence; the Bernoulli coefficient identity; the consecutive-block
inequality; an abstract triangular-array local-limit theorem; and finally the
boundary smoothing lemma. The algebraic generation of $P_j$ and the inverse
coefficients can be separately certified by finite polynomial identities.
This would connect directly to the repository's transseries infrastructure
without pretending that formal algebra alone proves analytic asymptotics.

\subsection{Source 48's proposed projects \src{48}}
Source 48 lists eight next steps, stated for A022629 and the real family.
They are proposed research tasks, not claims that the corresponding problems
are all globally open.  Its numbers are kept; overlaps with the projects above
are marked.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{48.\arabic*}, leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Resolve all subdominant asymptotic blocks} (overlaps project 2).
The upper-transition calculation determines every $K/r^j$ term.  Determine the
smaller contributions from the lower endpoint and the sum--integral
discrepancy, including any powers of $K$, logarithms, and constants that
appear for different $\alpha$.  Prove a fully explicit multiplicative
equivalent with no implicit exact saddle sums.  Distinguish this task from
merely computing more terms in \eqref{dpn:eq:logall}.
\item \textbf{The transition as $\alpha\downarrow0$} (overlaps project 3).
At $\alpha=0$ the model is ordinary distinct-part enumeration; for every fixed
$\alpha>0$ the leading logarithm has the different scale $\alpha K\log K$.
Develop uniform asymptotics in a double limit, for example when $\alpha\log n$
stays bounded.  The present proof explicitly does not cover this regime, and
the change in the active interval makes it a useful test case for uniform
asymptotic methods.
\item \textbf{The number of parts after conditioning} (overlaps project 6).
The finite-dimensional occupation profile is simple, but conditioning on total
size creates long-range correlations.  Determine the mean, variance, and
limiting law of the total number of parts to several orders.  A naive
unconditioned variance calculation is not sufficient because number and size
are strongly correlated in the narrow transition region.
\item \textbf{Joint extremes and point processes} (overlaps project 5).
Upgrade Corollary~\ref{dpn:48:thm:gumbel} to joint laws of the largest several
parts and convergence of the rescaled tail point process.  Prove quantitative
error bounds, uniform tail estimates, and asymptotics of the expectation and
variance of the largest part.  Distributional convergence alone does not
justify taking expectations.
\item \textbf{Stable weight perturbations} (overlaps project 7).
Replace $k^\alpha$ by $k^\alpha(\log k)^\beta$ or by
$k^\alpha\exp(c_1/k+c_2/k^2+\cdots)$, with a positive convention for small
$k$.  Determine which coefficients and extreme-value centring terms are
universal and which remember the perturbation.  Establish explicit uniform
hypotheses rather than assuming regular variation is automatically enough.
\item \textbf{Optimal saddle remainder scales.}
The absolute cumulant bound gives the safe parameter $s/M$.  Signed cumulant
cancellations can make actual errors much smaller, as
Table~\ref{dpn:48:tab:numerics} suggests.  Determine the sharp first relative
correction, its complete expansion, and the optimal error after each
correction.  This would improve both numerical evaluation and rigorous
inverse brackets.
\item \textbf{Effective inversion and formalization} (overlaps project 8).
Turn the asymptotic $O$ constants into explicit bounds, combine them with
finite exact verification, and produce a certified algorithm for
$N_\alpha(Y)$.  A suitable ProveIt formalization can separate finite
coefficient algebra, the positive product measure, the block minor-arc
estimate, remainder transport, and integer-threshold bracketing.  None of
those formalization tasks should be replaced by unchecked numerical fits.
\item \textbf{OEIS and literature follow-through.}
Submit a concise, independently reviewed proof of \eqref{dpn:eq:conjecture}
and the first sharper correction to the OEIS editors.  Continue the
literature audit for products with coefficients \emph{inside} their factors,
rather than mistaking $\prod(1+k^\alpha q^k)$ for $\prod(1+q^k)^{k^\alpha}$.
Compare any earlier theorem at the level of hypotheses and error terms before
making a priority claim.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
The five checked OEIS conjectures share a single analytic mechanism. Filling
parts up to a moving boundary produces the dominant factorial-scale entropy;
smoothing that boundary produces the $\pi^2$ correction and its all-orders
inverse-logarithmic successors. A conditioned Bernoulli model supplies the
local probability that converts this entropy into an actual coefficient,
and also gives logistic occupation and a Gumbel extreme. Lambert-$W$
inversion turns the same expansion into a quantitative threshold law.

The resulting package contains explicit proofs rather than numerical
extrapolation. It also records an important limitation: central asymptotics
can converge extremely slowly for higher moments because noncentral
resonances are only suppressed on the scale $\exp[-C_\alpha K/(\log K)^3]$.
That proved scale and the visible finite-size discrepancies identify a
well-defined next research problem: a phase-sensitive, exponentially
improved multi-saddle expansion.

Source 48 reaches the same conclusions for A022629 by partly different routes:
an elementary proof with a stronger additive remainder, an explicit
differential operator that generates the entire dominant logarithmic block by
finite exact algebra, a Lambert-based inverse for the integer threshold, a
precise comparison with the largest individual partition weight, and a Gumbel
law centred explicitly in $\log\sqrt{2n}$.  The remaining
distinctions---formal versus convergent series, logarithmic versus relative
accuracy, and proved mathematics versus formal-machine verification---are
essential parts of the result rather than editorial qualifications
\src{48}.
