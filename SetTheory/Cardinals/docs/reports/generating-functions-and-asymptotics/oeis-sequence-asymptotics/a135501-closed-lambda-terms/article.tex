\begin{abstract}
Let $A_s(n)$ count the closed lambda terms of size $n$ up to alpha-equivalence, when abstractions and applications have size one and variable occurrences size $s\in\{0,1\}$; $A_1$ is OEIS A135501 and $A_0$ is OEIS A220894. Put $r=s+1$, $N=n+1$, $t=W(4eNe^{-r/2})$ and $M_r(N)=\frac Nr(t-2+t^{-1})+\frac N2$. This report prints two manuscripts as Parts~I and~II. Part~I proves
$M_r(N)-O(\log N)\le\log A_s(n)\le M_r(N)+O(N^{1-1/(3r)}(\log N)^{-2/3+1/(3r)})$,
hence every fixed inverse-logarithmic order of $\log A_s(n)$ (in particular the coefficient $\frac12-\frac{\log4}r$ of $n/\log n$), with polynomial recurrences for all orders and a controlled threshold inverse with an asymmetric bracket. Part~II proves the weaker four-scale form, with error $O(n/\log n)$, by a second route, and a joint large-deviation principle at speed $n$ for the scaled number of abstractions and the maximum unary height of a uniform random closed term, with rate $(t-1-\log x)/r$ on $0<x\le t$: both scaled parameters tend to $1$ in every $L^p$, and a longest unary path carries all but $o(n/\log n)$ abstractions in probability. A remark added in the merge shows that Part~I's estimates settle, to leading order, the ratio $R(b,u)=A(b,u)/(C_bu^{b+1})$ that Part~II leaves open: $\log R(b,u)/u\to\frac12$ at the moving saddle. No multiplicative equivalent, amplitude or transseries is proved, and the error exponent $1-1/(3r)$ is an upper bound only. The report is unrefereed, and nothing in it is formalized.
\end{abstract}

\section{Models and main conclusions}
A lambda term is built from variables, abstractions $\lambda x.M$, and ordered applications $(M\,N)$. Alpha-equivalence identifies consistent changes of bound-variable names. A term is closed if every variable occurrence is bound by an enclosing abstraction. The two sequences considered here count all closed terms up to alpha-equivalence, without typing, normalization, linearity, or index restrictions.

Let variables have size $s\in\{0,1\}$ and abstractions and applications size one. Write $A_s(n)$ for the number of closed alpha-classes of size $n$. Then
\[
 A_1(n)=\href{https://oeis.org/A135501}{\mathrm{A135501}(n)},\qquad
 A_0(n)=\href{https://oeis.org/A220894}{\mathrm{A220894}(n)}.
\]
The index starts at $n=0$. The term $\lambda x.x$ has size two for $s=1$ and size one for $s=0$. All logarithms are natural. If a skeleton has $b$ application vertices and $u$ abstraction vertices, it has $b+1$ variable leaves. Consequently, with $r=s+1$ and $N=n+1$,
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:size}
 n=u+rb+s,\qquad b=\frac{N-u}{r}-1.
\end{equation}
An admissible $u$ is a positive integer with $N-u$ a positive multiple of $r$. This congruence is retained throughout. Let $A(b,u)$ count the closed terms with these fixed parameters; the size convention does not otherwise affect it.

The positive Lambert function satisfies $W(z)e^{W(z)}=z$ for $z>0$. Define
\begin{align}
 t&=W(4eNe^{-r/2}),& u_*&=N/t,\label{lam:ao:eq:saddledef}\\
 M_r(N)&=\frac Nr\left(t-2+\frac1t\right)+\frac N2,&
 R_r(N)&=N^{1-1/(3r)}(\log N)^{-2/3+1/(3r)}.\label{lam:ao:eq:MR}
\end{align}
\begin{theorem}[Uniform logarithmic approximation]\label{lam:ao:thm:main}
For each $s\in\{0,1\}$ and $n\to\infty$,
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:main}
 M_r(N)-O(\log N)\le\log A_s(n)\le M_r(N)+O(R_r(N)).
\end{equation}
The constants depend at most on $r$. Since there are only two choices, common constants can be chosen.
\end{theorem}
For every fixed real $k$,
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:scale}
 \frac{R_r(N)}{N/(\log N)^k}
 =N^{-1/(3r)}(\log N)^{k-2/3+1/(3r)}\longrightarrow0.
\end{equation}
Thus the main expression determines every fixed inverse-logarithmic order of the log-count. This is stronger than merely expanding a candidate saddle expression formally: the error in the full count lies below every scale being identified.
\begin{writenote}[5 October 2026]
Part~II, Theorem~\ref{lam:bg:thm:count}, is a weaker form of \eqref{lam:ao:eq:main}: $\log A_s(n)=\frac Nr(w-2+\frac1w)+O(N/\log N)$ with $w=W(4eN)$, proved by a second route (the root-chain lower bound $C_bu^{b+1}$ of Lemma~\ref{lam:bg:lem:skeleton} and the coarse majorant of Lemma~\ref{lam:ao:lem:coarse}). Theorem~\ref{lam:ao:thm:main} implies it: by the expansion of Section~\ref{lam:ao:sec:expansion} with $J=1$ and Part~II's expansion of $w-2+1/w$ (Section~\ref{lam:bg:sec:saddle}), $M_r(N)-\frac Nr(w-2+\frac1w)=\frac N{2\log N}+O(N(\log\log N)^2/(\log N)^2)$, which lies inside Part~II's error. The upper error $R_r(N)$ is an upper bound only: its exponent $1-1/(3r)$ comes from maximizing a majorant over height deficits (Proposition~\ref{lam:ao:prop:upper}, \eqref{lam:ao:eq:Enarrow}), and nothing here shows that $\log A_s(n)-M_r(N)$ is ever of that size; this Part says so itself in Section~\ref{lam:ao:sec:scope} (question~1). The true residual lies between $-O(\log N)$ and $O(R_r(N))$.
\end{writenote}

For an explicit form, set
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:z}
 L=\log N,\quad \ell=\log L,\quad a=1+\log4-r/2,\quad z=\ell-a.
\end{equation}
Section~\ref{lam:ao:sec:expansion} proves that for every fixed integer $J\ge1$,
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:allorders}
 \log A_s(n)=\frac Nr\left[L-\ell+\log4-1+
        \sum_{j=1}^J\frac{P_j(z)}{L^j}\right]
 +O\!\left(\frac{N(1+\ell)^{J+1}}{L^{J+1}}\right).
\end{equation}
In particular, the coefficient at the newly separated $n/\log n$ scale is determined by
\[
 \frac nr\,\frac{\log\log n-\log4+r/2}{\log n}.
\]
The polynomial recurrences specify arbitrary fixed order; they make no assertion about truncation orders growing with $n$.

\subsection{The threshold inverse}
Define
\[
 \nu_s(y)=\min\{n\in\N:A_s(n)\ge y\},\qquad Y=\log y.
\]
Adding an unused root abstraction is an injection from size $n$ into size $n+1$ in both models, so $A_s(n)$ is nondecreasing. Theorem~\ref{lam:ao:thm:main} makes it unbounded.
\begin{theorem}[Controlled inverse]\label{lam:ao:thm:inverse}
Let $N_0$ be the unique sufficiently large solution of $M_r(N_0)=Y$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:threshold}
 \nu_s(y)=N_0-1+O\!\left(\frac{R_r(N_0)}{\log N_0}+1\right).
\end{equation}
More precisely,
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:asymmetric}
 N_0-O\!\left(\frac{R_r(N_0)}{\log N_0}\right)
 \le\nu_s(y)+1\le N_0+O(1).
\end{equation}
Put
\[
 K_r=\frac{e^{r/2}}{4er},\qquad \Pi_r(t)=t^2+(r/2-2)t+1.
\]
If $t_y$ is the unique large positive solution of
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:inverseparam}
 Y=K_re^{t_y}\Pi_r(t_y),
 \qquad\text{then}\qquad N_0=\frac{rYt_y}{\Pi_r(t_y)}.
\end{equation}
In the variables $v=\log Y$ and $k=\log v$,
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:inverseexplicit}
 \nu_s(y)=\frac{rY}{v-2k+\log(4r)-1+
            [4k+1+r/2-2\log(4r)]/v}
       +O\!\left(\frac{Y(1+k)^2}{v^4}\right).
\end{equation}
An arbitrary-order inverse recurrence with a proved remainder is given in Section~\ref{lam:ao:sec:inverse}.
\end{theorem}
The notation $\Pi_r$ distinguishes the exact quadratic from the forward coefficient polynomials $P_j$. The error terms in these statements are asymptotic, with no claimed explicit numerical onset.
\begin{writenote}[5 October 2026]
Part~II, Theorem~\ref{lam:bg:thm:inverse}, is the weaker form of \eqref{lam:ao:eq:inverseexplicit}. In this Part's letters ($Y=\log y$, $v=\log Y$, $k=\log v$; Part~II writes $L$, $\ell$, $\log\ell$) it reads $\nu_s(y)=rY/(v-2k+\log r+\log4-1+4k/v)+O(Y/v^3)$: the first four terms of the denominator coincide with those of \eqref{lam:ao:eq:inverseexplicit}, and this Part adds the term $[1+r/2-2\log(4r)]/v$, whose coefficient is $\approx-1.273$ for $r=1$ and $\approx-2.159$ for $r=2$. Because that coefficient is not zero, the two approximations differ by $r\,|1+r/2-2\log(4r)|\,Y/v^3\,(1+o(1))$, so Part~II's error $O(Y/v^3)$ is of exact order at its truncation, and its approximation lies below $\nu_s(y)$ for large $y$. The asymmetric bracket \eqref{lam:ao:eq:asymmetric} and the inverse recurrence of Section~\ref{lam:ao:sec:inverse} have no counterpart in Part~II.
\end{writenote}

\section{Exact recurrence and reduced trees}\label{lam:ao:sec:recurrence}
Let $T_m(x,z)$ count terms with $m$ available outer binders, where $x$ marks applications and $z$ marks abstractions. A variable occurrence chooses one of its available binders. This is the alpha-class encoding, so no labeling factorial is present. The root decomposition is
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:T}
 T_m=m+zT_{m+1}+xT_m^2.
\end{equation}
Write $B_{m,k}(x)=[z^k]T_m(x,z)$. Then
\begin{align}
 B_{m,0}(x)&=\frac{1-\sqrt{1-4mx}}{2x},\label{lam:ao:eq:B0}\\
 B_{m,k}(x)&=\frac{B_{m+1,k-1}(x)+x\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}B_{m,j}(x)B_{m,k-j}(x)}
                  {\sqrt{1-4mx}},\qquad k\ge1.\label{lam:ao:eq:B}
\end{align}
The value at $x=0$ in \eqref{lam:ao:eq:B0} is interpreted as $m$. To obtain \eqref{lam:ao:eq:B}, move the two terms $xB_{m,0}B_{m,k}$ to the left and use $1-2xB_{m,0}=\sqrt{1-4mx}$. These are identities of nonnegative formal power series, and
\[
 A(b,u)=[x^b]B_{0,u}(x).
\]
The recursion in \eqref{lam:ao:eq:B} terminates: a unary branch decreases $k$ by one, and both children of a binary branch have smaller positive unary counts.
\begin{writenote}[5 October 2026]
Part~II derives the same series in its Section~3 (Report~109's (3.2)--(3.5)) with $t$ instead of $z$ marking abstractions; its identities are \eqref{lam:ao:eq:T}--\eqref{lam:ao:eq:B} and the display above, printed once here. Part~II, Section~\ref{lam:bg:sec:skeleton}, adds the binder-position reading of a fixed skeleton (a term is a skeleton with a choice of ancestor abstraction at each leaf), the size recurrence \eqref{lam:bg:eq:size-rec} and the skeleton sandwich, which this Part does not use.
\end{writenote}

Expanding this finite recursion gives plane reduced trees with exactly $u$ unary vertices. A unary vertex increments $m$; a binary vertex has two children each containing at least one unary vertex. A terminal leaf carries $B_{m,0}$. Every terminal leaf therefore has a unary parent. At a vertex, $m$ is the number of strict unary ancestors. Every internal vertex contributes a radical $(1-4mx)^{-1/2}$ and every binary vertex also contributes $x$.

Let $q$ be the number of binary vertices and let $h$ be the largest unary depth of a terminal leaf. Write $d=u-h$. A deepest path contains $h$ unary vertices. There are $q+1$ terminal leaves in all. The other $q$ leaves have distinct unary parents off this path, because a unary vertex on the selected path has its unique child on that path. Hence
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:qdeficit}
 1\le h\le u,\qquad q\le d=u-h.
\end{equation}
Every terminal depth is at most $h$. Every internal depth is at most $h-1$, since its descendant subtree still contains a unary vertex.

\begin{lemma}[Exact reduced shape count]\label{lam:ao:lem:shapes}
For $u\ge1$ and $0\le q\le u-1$, the number of reduced shapes with $u$ unary and $q$ binary vertices is
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:shapes}
 C_q\binom{u+q-1}{2q},\qquad C_q=\frac1{q+1}\binom{2q}{q}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Suppress all unary vertices. The remaining full plane binary tree has $q$ binary vertices and is counted by $C_q$. Its $2q+1$ incoming vertex slots, including the root slot, receive unary chains. Every one of the $q+1$ leaf slots must contain at least one unary. After assigning these mandatory vertices, distribute $u-q-1$ remaining unaries among $2q+1$ slots. Stars and bars gives the binomial in \eqref{lam:ao:eq:shapes}. Conversely every such assignment is a permitted reduced tree. For $q=0$ this gives the unique unary chain.
\end{proof}
\begin{writenote}[5 October 2026]
The same formula, with the same contraction of unary chains and the same stars-and-bars count, is stated and proved in Part~II's checks section (Section~\ref{lam:bg:sec:checks}, item~7 and the paragraph after the list), where its weighted sum $d_u=\sum_q2^{-q}C_q\binom{u+q-1}{2q}$ is compared through $u=100$ with the quadratic shape recurrence.
\end{writenote}

\section{A coarse bound for global localization}\label{lam:ao:sec:coarse}
We include the uniform majorant needed to exclude the tails of the abstraction count. This makes the later moving-saddle argument independent of an external asymptotic result.
\begin{lemma}[Coarse uniform majorant]\label{lam:ao:lem:coarse}
For every integer $u\ge1$ and $b\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:coarse}
 A(b,u)\le2uK^u(4u)^b,\qquad K=e(2+\sqrt3).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{writenote}[5 October 2026]

The proof is printed once, here, in Report~109's fuller wording; it occupies Sections~3.1--3.3 below, and Lemmas~\ref{lam:ao:lem:forest} and~\ref{lam:ao:lem:product} and the remark at the end of Section~3.3 keep Report~109's numbers. Report~110's condensed proof takes the same steps in the same order (finiteness at $\rho=1/(4u)$ by induction on $k$, the normalization by $2u$, the bound $2(u-m)-1$ for the internal vertices of unary depth at least $m$, padding the deficit list to $(u!)^2$, and the shape series with $D(\tau)=\sqrt3-1$); it is not reprinted and remains in the arrival commit. \subsection{The radical recurrence and its endpoint}
Fix $u\ge1$ and set $\rho=1/(4u)$. Every member of the finite triangular family $B_{m,k}(\rho)$ with $m+k\le u$ is finite. Indeed, the Catalan values $B_{m,0}(\rho)$ are finite even at $m=u$, where the square root vanishes but its inverse is not used. If $k\ge1$, then $m<u$, so the denominator in \eqref{lam:ao:eq:B} is strictly positive. Induction on $k$ proves finiteness of the numerator and the endpoint value. Nonnegative coefficients identify the limit as $x\uparrow\rho$ with the sum of the series at $\rho$. Thus this is a valid positive-series endpoint evaluation, not a singularity-transfer approximation.

Normalize by
\[
 H_{m,k}=\frac{B_{m,k}(\rho)}{2u},\qquad d_m=\sqrt{1-m/u}.
\]
Then
\begin{align}
 H_{m,0}&=1-d_m\in[0,1],\label{lam:ao:eq:H0}\\
 H_{m,k}&=d_m^{-1}\left(H_{m+1,k-1}
            +\frac12\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}H_{m,j}H_{m,k-j}\right).
 \label{lam:ao:eq:Hk}
\end{align}
The coefficient $1/2$ is exactly $\rho(2u)$.

\subsection{Reduced trees and a depth forest}
Expand \eqref{lam:ao:eq:Hk} from $(m,k)=(0,u)$. A unary expansion consumes one abstraction and increments $m$; a binary expansion splits a positive remaining unary count into two positive parts. A terminal is reached only by a unary expansion. These expansion histories are finite plane unary--binary trees with exactly $u$ unary vertices, and every terminal leaf has a unary parent. They are \emph{reduced} trees: their binary count $q$ need not be the original application count $b$.

The subtree unary counts uniquely recover every convolution index, so no additional multiplicity factor is missing. If $m(v)$ counts the strict unary ancestors of an internal vertex $v$, a reduced shape has weight
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:reduced-weight}
 2^{-q}\prod_{v\text{ internal}}(1-m(v)/u)^{-1/2}
      \prod_{v\text{ terminal}}H_{m(v),0}.
\end{equation}
Each terminal factor is at most one. There are $q+1$ terminal leaves, all with distinct unary parents, so $q\le u-1$ and there are at most $2u-1$ internal vertices.

\begin{lemma}[Depth-forest inequality]\label{lam:ao:lem:forest}
If a reduced tree with $u$ unary vertices has any internal vertex of unary depth at least $m$, the number of such internal vertices is at most
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:forest}
 2(u-m)-1.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Take all internal vertices at unary depth at least $m$, together with their descendant terminal leaves. They form a descendant-closed forest with $c\ge1$ components. At least $m$ unary vertices lie outside this forest: follow a path to any component root and take its first $m$ unary ancestors. Thus the forest has $U\le u-m$ unary vertices. If it has $Q$ binary vertices and $V$ leaves, the forest identity is $V=Q+c$. Every included terminal still has its unary parent in the forest, hence $V\le U$. It follows that
\[
 U+Q\le2U-c\le2(u-m)-1.
\]
A terminal whose parent has depth $m-1$ is not an isolated component of this forest: it is not a descendant of any included internal vertex. This convention is needed for $V\le U$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Uniform radical product]\label{lam:ao:lem:product}
For every reduced shape with $u$ unary vertices,
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:product}
 \prod_{v\text{ internal}}(1-m(v)/u)^{-1/2}
       \le\frac{u^{u-1/2}}{u!}\le e^u.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Let $\delta(v)=u-m(v)\in\{1,\ldots,u\}$. Applying Lemma~\ref{lam:ao:lem:forest} at depth $u-j$, at most $2j-1$ deficits are at most $j$. Pad the deficit list with copies of $u$ until its length is $2u-1$. The sorted padded list is coordinatewise at least
\[
 1,2,2,3,3,\ldots,u,u,
\]
so its product is at least $(u!)^2$. Padding preserves the squared radical ratio, since each extra entry contributes $u/u=1$. The ratio is therefore at most $u^{2u-1}/(u!)^2$. Finally, $u!\ge(u/e)^u$ gives the claimed bound, including $u=1$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The weighted shape generating function}
Let $d_u$ be the sum of $2^{-q}$ over reduced shapes with $u$ unary vertices. A root is either unary with a terminal or nonempty child, or binary with two nonempty children. Thus
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:shape-gf}
 D(z)=\sum_{u\ge1}d_uz^u=z(1+D(z))+\tfrac12D(z)^2,
 \quad D(z)=1-z-\sqrt{1-4z+z^2}.
\end{equation}
At $\tau=2-\sqrt3$, monotone convergence of the nonnegative series gives
\[
 D(\tau)=\sqrt3-1<1,\qquad d_u\le\tau^{-u}=(2+\sqrt3)^u.
\]
Combining this with \eqref{lam:ao:eq:reduced-weight} and Lemma~\ref{lam:ao:lem:product} proves
\[
 H_{0,u}\le[e(2+\sqrt3)]^u=K^u,
 \qquad B_{0,u}(\rho)\le2uK^u.
\]
Positivity and endpoint finiteness now give
\[
 [x^b]B_{0,u}(x)\le\rho^{-b}B_{0,u}(\rho)\le2uK^u(4u)^b.
\]
The lower bound in Theorem~\ref{lam:bg:thm:uniform} is Lemma~\ref{lam:bg:lem:skeleton}, completing the proof.
\begin{writenote}[5 October 2026]
The last display is \eqref{lam:ao:eq:coarse}, which proves Lemma~\ref{lam:ao:lem:coarse}. The final sentence concerns Report~109's two-sided statement, Theorem~\ref{lam:bg:thm:uniform}.
\end{writenote}

\begin{remark}[Boundary sanity checks]
When $b=0$, the exact count is $u$; when $u=1$, it is $C_b$. In the endpoint evaluation, $m=u$ occurs only at a terminal with $k=0$, so there is no division by zero. Closed terms with $u=0$ do not occur and are never assigned a logarithm.
\end{remark}

\section{A height sensitive uniform upper bound}\label{lam:ao:sec:height}
The key improvement comes from retaining the height before coefficient extraction. Let $A_h(b,u)$ count the terms belonging to reduced shapes with maximum unary depth $h$.
\begin{lemma}[Height bound]\label{lam:ao:lem:height}
For $b\ge1$, $1\le h<u$, and $d=u-h$,
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:heightbound}
 A_h(b,u)\le2h e^{h/2}(4h)^b(d+1)
                  \left[\frac{2e^3u^2(b+d)}{d^3}\right]^d.
\end{equation}
For $h=u$,
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:heightzero}
 A_u(b,u)\le2u e^{u/2}(4u)^b.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Fix the height subclass first. For $0<\delta<1$ evaluate only its finite reduced-tree products at
\[
 x=\frac{1-\delta}{4h}.
\]
Every such product converges there: terminal parameters satisfy $m\le h$ and internal parameters satisfy $m<h$. When $h<u$, this radius may exceed the unrestricted radius $1/(4u)$; we never evaluate the unrestricted $B_{0,u}$ there.

Normalize every factor $B$ in \eqref{lam:ao:eq:B} by $2h$. A binary vertex has normalized factor $2hx=(1-\delta)/2\le1/2$. For $0\le m\le h$, a terminal factor satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:terminal}
 \frac{B_{m,0}(x)}{2h}
 =\frac{1-\sqrt{1-(1-\delta)m/h}}{1-\delta}
 \le\frac{1-\sqrt\delta}{1-\delta}
 =\frac1{1+\sqrt\delta}\le1.
\end{equation}
There is no extra factor $(1-\delta)^{-1}$ to retain. The $q+1$ terminal normalizations and $q$ binary normalizations leave one exterior factor $2h$.

On a selected deepest path, the $h$ unary vertices have depths $0,1,\ldots,h-1$. Their radical product is at most
\[
 \prod_{m=0}^{h-1}(1-m/h)^{-1/2}=\sqrt{h^h/h!}\le e^{h/2}.
\]
There are $u+q-h=d+q\le2d$ other internal vertices, each with radical at most $\delta^{-1/2}$. A normalized shape contribution is therefore at most
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:shapebound}
 e^{h/2}\delta^{-d}2^{-q}.
\end{equation}
For $h=1$, the distinguished unary radical is simply one, so the argument still applies.

For $1\le q\le d\le u-1$, the elementary estimates $C_q\le4^q$ and $\binom nk\le(en/k)^k$ give
\[
 2^{-q}C_q\binom{u+q-1}{2q}\le[2e^2(u/q)^2]^q.
\]
The function $v\log(2e^2(u/v)^2)$ increases on $0<v\le u$, since its derivative is $\log2+2\log(u/v)>0$. Including the $q=0$ summand gives
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:shapesum}
 \sum_{q=0}^{d}2^{-q}C_q\binom{u+q-1}{2q}
       \le(d+1)[2e^2(u/d)^2]^d.
\end{equation}
This overcounts heights other than $h$, which is harmless; every desired shape has $q\le d$. Positivity and \eqref{lam:ao:eq:shapebound}--\eqref{lam:ao:eq:shapesum} imply
\[
 A_h(b,u)\le2h e^{h/2}(4h)^b(d+1)
    (1-\delta)^{-b}\delta^{-d}[2e^2(u/d)^2]^d.
\]
Choose $\delta=d/(b+d)$. Then $(1-\delta)^{-b}=(1+d/b)^b\le e^d$, proving \eqref{lam:ao:eq:heightbound}.

If $d=0$, \eqref{lam:ao:eq:qdeficit} forces $q=0$: there is one reduced unary chain. Evaluate it at $x=1/(4u)$. Its terminal factor is $2u$ and its radical product is at most $e^{u/2}$. All internal depths are below $u$, so this endpoint is legitimate. This proves \eqref{lam:ao:eq:heightzero}, also for $u=1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Uniform fixed parameter estimate]\label{lam:ao:prop:upper}
For $u\ge1$, $b\ge0$, and $\alpha=b/u$, put
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:E}
 E(b,u)=3\,2^{1/3}u(1+\alpha)^{1/3}e^{-\alpha/3}.
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{align}
 A(b,u)&\le2u^2(u+1)(4u)^b\exp\{u/2+E(b,u)\},\label{lam:ao:eq:uniform}\\
 \log A(b,u)&\le b\log(4u)+u/2+E(b,u)+O(\log(u+1)),\label{lam:ao:eq:loguniform}
\end{align}
with an absolute uniform constant.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Relative to $(4u)^be^{u/2}$, the exponential correction in \eqref{lam:ao:eq:heightbound} is at most
\[
 d\left[-\alpha+\log(2e^3(1+\alpha))+3\log(u/d)\right].
\]
Here we used $(h/u)^b\le e^{-bd/u}$ and $b+d\le u(1+\alpha)$, and discarded the favorable term $-d/2$. For $A=-\alpha+\log(2e^3(1+\alpha))$, the maximum of $v(A-3\log v)$ over all $v>0$ is $3e^{A/3-1}$. Apply this with $v=d/u$ to get exactly $E(b,u)$. Summing at most $u$ heights and using $2h\le2u$, $d+1\le u+1$ proves \eqref{lam:ao:eq:uniform} when $b\ge1$. The $d=0$ term obeys the same bound. When $b=0$, the only skeleton is a unary chain, with $u$ choices for its final variable; thus $A(0,u)=u$ and the inequality is immediate. Finally $\log(2u^2(u+1))\le4\log(u+1)$.
\end{proof}
\begin{writenote}[5 October 2026]
Proposition~\ref{lam:ao:prop:upper} and the single-spine inequality \eqref{lam:ao:eq:convolution} of the next section are the two halves of Remark~\ref{lam:bg:rem:ratio} in Part~II, which determines Part~II's ratio $R(b,u)=A(b,u)/(C_bu^{b+1})$ to leading order at the moving saddle.
\end{writenote}

\section{A single spine lower bound}\label{lam:ao:sec:lower}
A single-spine term is one in which every abstraction lies on one ancestor path. Applications and unary-free Catalan branches are allowed along that path; the abstractions need not form a consecutive initial chain. Iterating only the unary term of \eqref{lam:ao:eq:B} gives its exact application generating function
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:spine}
 L_u(x)=B_{u,0}(x)Q_u(x),\qquad
 Q_u(x)=\prod_{m=0}^{u-1}(1-4mx)^{-1/2}.
\end{equation}
Equivalently, between consecutive abstractions, a path application has two possible continuing children and a unary-free side branch. The context sequence has generating function
$1/(1-2xB_{m,0}(x))=(1-4mx)^{-1/2}$. This proves the interpretation without any singular asymptotic approximation.

At $\rho=1/(4u)$ all factors of $Q_u$ are finite, and
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:Q}
 Q_u(\rho)=\sqrt{u^u/u!},\qquad
 \log Q_u(\rho)=u/2+O(\log(u+1)).
\end{equation}
The logarithmic factorial estimate follows from upper and lower integral bounds on $\sum_{j=1}^u\log j$.

Normalize these nonnegative coefficients into a probability law:
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:J}
 \Prob(J=j)=\frac{[x^j]Q_u(x)\rho^j}{Q_u(\rho)},\qquad
 \E[z^J]=\prod_{m=0}^{u-1}
       \left(\frac{1-m/u}{1-(m/u)z}\right)^{1/2}.
\end{equation}
Each factor is a negative-binomial probability generating function of shape $1/2$; this displayed formula specifies the parameter convention. The factor with $m=0$ is identically one. Thus $J$ is a sum of independent such variables. Differentiating the factors gives
\begin{align}
 \mu=\E J&=\frac12\sum_{m=0}^{u-1}\frac m{u-m}
       =\frac u2(H_u-1)\le\frac u2\log u,\label{lam:ao:eq:mean}\\
 \sigma^2=\Var J&=\frac12\sum_{k=1}^{u-1}\frac{u(u-k)}{k^2}\le u^2.\label{lam:ao:eq:var}
\end{align}
Here $H_u=\sum_{j=1}^u1/j\le1+\log u$, and $\sum_{j\ge1}j^{-2}<2$ follows by an integral bound. Empty sums for $u=1$ cause no problem.

The Catalan ratio $C_{k+1}/(4C_k)=(2k+1)/(2k+4)<1$ implies $C_{b-j}\ge4^{-j}C_b$ for $0\le j\le b$. Coefficient convolution in \eqref{lam:ao:eq:spine} therefore yields the exact lower inequality
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:convolution}
 A(b,u)\ge[x^b]L_u(x)
       \ge C_bu^{b+1}Q_u(\rho)\Prob(J\le b).
\end{equation}
Indeed the convolution sum is $\sum_{j=0}^b[x^j]Q_u\,C_{b-j}u^{b-j+1}$, and the stated Catalan inequality produces the factor $\rho^j$.

Define the two smooth functions
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:FH}
 F_N(u)=\frac{N-u}{r}\log(4u),\qquad H_N(u)=F_N(u)+u/2.
\end{equation}
Direct differentiation gives
\[
 H_N'(u)=\frac{N/u-1-\log(4u)}r+\frac12,\qquad
 H_N''(u)=-\frac1r\left(\frac N{u^2}+\frac1u\right)<0.
\]
The derivative changes from positive to negative, so the maximum is unique. Its critical point is $u_*=N/t$, where
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:saddle}
 t+\log t=\log(4eN)-r/2,\qquad
 \log(4u_*)=t-1+r/2,\qquad H_N(u_*)=M_r(N).
\end{equation}
In particular $t\sim\log N$ and $u_*\sim N/\log N$.

Choose an admissible integer $u_0$ within $r/2$ of $u_*$. For large $N$ it lies in the admissible interior, since $u_*\to\infty$ and $N-r-u_*\to\infty$. Put $b_0=(N-u_0)/r-1$. The bounded rounding shift changes $N/u$ by $O((\log N)^2/N)$, and
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:roundalpha}
 \frac{b_0}{u_0}=\frac{\log(4u_0)}r-\frac12+o(1).
\end{equation}
Combining \eqref{lam:ao:eq:mean} and \eqref{lam:ao:eq:roundalpha},
\[
 \frac{b_0-\mu}{u_0}\ge
 \left(\frac1r-\frac12\right)\log u_0+\frac{\log4}r-\frac12+o(1).
\]
For $r=2$, the right side tends to $\log2-1/2>0$; for $r=1$, it tends to infinity. Hence the common constant $a_0=(\log2-1/2)/2>0$ satisfies $b_0-\mu\ge a_0u_0$ eventually. Cantelli's inequality and \eqref{lam:ao:eq:var} give
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:Cantelli}
 \Prob(J\le b_0)\ge\frac{a_0^2}{1+a_0^2}>0.
\end{equation}
For completeness, if $X$ has mean zero and variance $\sigma^2$, Markov's inequality applied to $(X+h)^2$ on $X\ge c>0$ gives
$\Prob(X\ge c)\le(\sigma^2+h^2)/(c+h)^2$. Taking $h=\sigma^2/c$ proves Cantelli; zero variance is immediate. Here $\{J>b_0\}\subseteq\{J-\mu\ge a_0u_0\}$, so the integer endpoint is harmless.

The elementary bound $C_b\ge4^b/((2b+1)(b+1))$, \eqref{lam:ao:eq:Q}, \eqref{lam:ao:eq:convolution}, and \eqref{lam:ao:eq:Cantelli} now imply
\[
 \log A_s(n)\ge H_N(u_0)-O(\log N).
\]
Taylor's theorem at $u_*$ loses only $O((\log N)^2/N)=o(1)$, because $|u_0-u_*|\le r/2$ and $|H_N''|=O((\log N)^2/N)$ on the intervening segment. This proves the lower half of Theorem~\ref{lam:ao:thm:main}, including both residue classes in the $r=2$ model.
\begin{writenote}[5 October 2026]
Part~II's lower bound is the root-chain subclass alone (all abstractions in the root slot, Lemma~\ref{lam:bg:lem:skeleton}), which gives $C_bu^{b+1}$ and the envelope $F_N$. The single-spine class gains at least the factor $Q_u(\rho)\Prob(J\le b)=e^{u/2+O(\log u)}$ near the saddle, by \eqref{lam:ao:eq:convolution}, \eqref{lam:ao:eq:Q} and \eqref{lam:ao:eq:Cantelli}. This factor is the term $u/2$ of the envelope $H_N(u)=F_N(u)+u/2$ of \eqref{lam:ao:eq:FH}; at the saddle it is worth about $\frac N{2\log N}$, the difference between $M_r(N)$ and Part~II's Lambert expression.
\end{writenote}

\section{Two stages of saddle localization}\label{lam:ao:sec:localization}
We prove the upper half without assuming a uniform fixed-$u$ singular expansion. Put $L=\log N$ and $c=\log K$, where $K$ is from Lemma~\ref{lam:ao:lem:coarse}. Since $b+1=(N-u)/r$, the coarse bound gives exactly
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:G}
 \log A(b,u)\le G_N(u):=F_N(u)+cu-\log2.
\end{equation}
There is no omitted $u$-dependent prefactor. This envelope is strictly concave, with maximizer $v_N=N/W(4eNe^{-rc})\sim N/L$. For large $N$, this point lies in
\[
 I_N=[N/(2L),2N/L].
\]
Concavity implies that the largest envelope value outside $I_N$ is bounded by one of its endpoint values. For fixed $x\in\{1/2,2\}$,
\begin{align*}
 G_N(xN/L)&=\frac Nr[L-\log L+\log4+\log x-x]+o(N),\\
 M_r(N)&=\frac Nr[L-\log L+\log4-1]+o(N).
\end{align*}
Both numbers $\log x-x+1$ are strictly negative. There is therefore $\eta>0$ such that every admissible $u\notin I_N$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:outer}
 \log A(b,u)\le M_r(N)-\eta N.
\end{equation}
This includes the possible endpoint $b=0$.
\begin{writenote}[5 October 2026]
The envelope $G_N$ of \eqref{lam:ao:eq:G} is not Part~II's placement envelope $G_N$ of \eqref{lam:bg:eq:G}.
\end{writenote}

Within $I_N$, $\alpha=(N/u-1)/r-1/u$ satisfies $L/(2r)-O(1)\le\alpha\le2L/r$. Use these separately in the factors of \eqref{lam:ao:eq:E} to obtain the uniform bound
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:Ewide}
 E(b,u)=O(N^{1-1/(6r)}L^{-2/3})=o(N/L^2).
\end{equation}
By \eqref{lam:ao:eq:loguniform} and $b\log(4u)=F_N(u)-\log(4u)$,
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:Hupper}
 \log A(b,u)\le H_N(u)+E(b,u)+O(\log N).
\end{equation}
Throughout $I_N$, $H_N''(u)\le-L^2/(4rN)$. Also $u_*\in I_N$ and $u_*\ge N/(2L)$ eventually. If $u\in I_N$ but $|u/u_*-1|\ge1/L$, Taylor's theorem on the segment from $u_*$ to $u$ gives
\[
 M_r(N)-H_N(u)\ge\frac12\frac{L^2}{4rN}\left(\frac{N}{2L^2}\right)^2
                  =\frac{N}{32rL^2}.
\]
Combining with \eqref{lam:ao:eq:Ewide} and \eqref{lam:ao:eq:Hupper}, eventually
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:middle}
 \log A(b,u)\le M_r(N)-\frac{N}{64rL^2}.
\end{equation}

It remains to consider $|u/u_*-1|<1/L$. Write $u=u_*(1+\varepsilon)$, so $|\varepsilon|<1/L$. Since $t\asymp L$,
\[
 \alpha-\alpha_*=O(1),\qquad
 \alpha_*:=\frac{N/u_*-1}r-\frac1{u_*}
           =\frac{\log(4u_*)}r-\frac12-\frac1{u_*}.
\]
Consequently $1+\alpha_*\asymp L$ and $e^{-\alpha_*/3}\asymp u_*^{-1/(3r)}$. The bounded change in $\alpha$ changes its exponential factor only by a bounded multiplier. Thus
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:Enarrow}
 E(b,u)=O(u_*^{1-1/(3r)}L^{1/3})
       =O(N^{1-1/(3r)}L^{-2/3+1/(3r)})=O(R_r(N)).
\end{equation}
Now \eqref{lam:ao:eq:Hupper} and $H_N(u)\le M_r(N)$ prove the desired bound in this narrow interval. Sum it with \eqref{lam:ao:eq:outer} and \eqref{lam:ao:eq:middle} over at most $N$ admissible values of $u$. Taking the logarithm costs at most $\log N$, which is absorbed in $R_r(N)$. This finishes Theorem~\ref{lam:ao:thm:main}.

\section{Every fixed logarithmic order}\label{lam:ao:sec:expansion}
We now convert the controlled main expression to polynomial expansions, including analytic error estimates. Retain $L,\ell,a,z$ from \eqref{lam:ao:eq:z}. The equation defining $t$ is
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:timplicit}
 t+\log t=L+a.
\end{equation}
Define polynomials $p_j(z)$ successively, using a formal variable $x$, by
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:p}
 p_j(z)=-[x^j]\log\left(1-zx+\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}p_k(z)x^{k+1}\right),\qquad j\ge1.
\end{equation}
This is genuinely triangular: the coefficient of $x^j$ involves only the earlier polynomials. Induction gives $\deg p_j\le j$. Set
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:P}
 P_j(z)=p_j(z)+[x^{j-1}]
       \left(1-zx+\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}p_k(z)x^{k+1}\right)^{-1}.
\end{equation}
The first six coefficient polynomials are
\begin{align}
 P_1(z)&=z+1,& P_2(z)&=z^2/2,\nonumber\\
 P_3(z)&=z^3/3-z^2/2,& P_4(z)&=z^4/4-5z^3/6+z^2/2,\label{lam:ao:eq:Pfirst}\\
 P_5(z)&=z^5/5-13z^4/12+3z^3/2-z^2/2,\nonumber\\
 P_6(z)&=z^6/6-77z^5/60+71z^4/24-7z^3/3+z^2/2.\nonumber
\end{align}
The coefficient identities are reproducible by two independent exact-rational series algorithms in the package.

\begin{lemma}[Analytic remainder for the reversion]\label{lam:ao:lem:reversion}
For each fixed $J\ge1$,
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:texpand}
 t=L-z+\sum_{j=1}^Jp_j(z)L^{-j}
       +O((1+|z|)^{J+1}L^{-J-1}).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Let $T_J$ be the displayed finite expression without its error and set $U_J=T_J/L$. Then
\[
 T_J+\log T_J-L-a=\sum_{j=1}^Jp_j(z)L^{-j}+\log U_J.
\]
The recurrence \eqref{lam:ao:eq:p} cancels all powers $L^{-1}$ through $L^{-J}$. Put $D=1+|z|$. For fixed $J$, $D/L\to0$, $T_J>0$ eventually, and $U_J-1=O(D/L)$. The analytic Taylor remainder of the logarithm through degree $J$ is $O((D/L)^{J+1})$. Every uncancelled monomial in its finite Taylor part has power of $L^{-1}$ at least $J+1$, and degree in $z$ at most that power, so its value has the same bound. Thus the full residual is $O(D^{J+1}L^{-J-1})$. The derivative of $t+\log t$ is $1+1/t\ge1$ for $t>0$. The mean value theorem transfers the residual bound to $T_J-t$.
\end{proof}

Since $1/T_J=L^{-1}U_J^{-1}$, the reciprocal contribution at $L^{-j}$ is the coefficient in \eqref{lam:ao:eq:P}. Substitution in $M_r$ preserves the error in Lemma~\ref{lam:ao:lem:reversion}; the derivative of $t-2+1/t$ is bounded near the large solution. The leading constant becomes $a-2+r/2=\log4-1$. By \eqref{lam:ao:eq:scale}, the counting uncertainty is smaller than the fixed-order remainder. This proves \eqref{lam:ao:eq:allorders}.

For example,
\begin{align*}
 t={}&L-z+\frac zL+\frac{z^2/2-z}{L^2}
       +\frac{z^3/3-3z^2/2+z}{L^3}
       +O\!\left(\frac{(1+|z|)^4}{L^4}\right),\\
 \log A_s(n)={}&\frac Nr\left[L-\ell+\log4-1
        +\frac{z+1}L+\frac{z^2}{2L^2}
        +\frac{z^3/3-z^2/2}{L^3}\right]
       +O\!\left(\frac{N(1+\ell)^4}{L^4}\right).
\end{align*}
Here $z+1=\log\log N-\log4+r/2$. Replacing $N=n+1$ by $n$ in any fixed smooth truncation changes its value by $O(\log n)$, because that truncation has derivative $O(\log n)$. This change is below the displayed remainders. We differentiate the explicitly given truncation, not an unknown counting error.

\section{The controlled inverse to every fixed order}\label{lam:ao:sec:inverse}
Differentiate the exact envelope $M_r(N)=H_N(u_*(N))$. The term involving $du_*/dN$ vanishes because $H_N'(u_*)=0$, so
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:Mprime}
 M_r'(N)=\frac{\log(4u_*)}r=\frac{t-1+r/2}r\sim\frac{\log N}r.
\end{equation}
This is positive eventually, and $M_r$ tends to infinity. Hence $M_r(N_0)=Y$ has a unique sufficiently large solution.

Choose a sufficiently large fixed $C$ and put $\Delta=C R_r(N_0)/\log N_0=o(N_0)$. On shifts of this size, $M_r'$ is comparable to $\log N_0$ and $R_r(N)$ is comparable to $R_r(N_0)$. The upper bound in Theorem~\ref{lam:ao:thm:main} is strictly below $Y$ at $N_0-\Delta$ for large enough $C$, so monotonicity and integer rounding give
\[
 \nu_s(y)+1\ge N_0-O(R_r(N_0)/\log N_0).
\]
For the opposite side, the lower counting error is only $O(\log N)$. A sufficiently large fixed positive shift of $N_0$ raises $M_r$ by more than this loss, by \eqref{lam:ao:eq:Mprime}. Taking the next integer adds at most one. Thus
\[
 \nu_s(y)+1\le N_0+O(1).
\]
This proves both \eqref{lam:ao:eq:asymmetric} and \eqref{lam:ao:eq:threshold}. It uses explicit envelope derivatives and monotone bracketing, never derivatives of an unknown remainder.

The saddle equation gives the exact relation $N=e^{r/2}te^t/(4e)$. Substitute it in $M_r$ to get
\[
 M_r(N)=K_re^t\Pi_r(t),\qquad
 K_r=\frac{e^{r/2}}{4er},\quad \Pi_r(t)=t^2+(r/2-2)t+1.
\]
This proves \eqref{lam:ao:eq:inverseparam}. The quadratics for $r=1,2$ are positive on the real line, and the derivative of $t+\log\Pi_r(t)$ is positive for large $t$, verifying the intended branch directly. Since $N_0\sim rY/\log Y$, the counting uncertainty in \eqref{lam:ao:eq:threshold} is equivalently
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:inverseerrorY}
 O\!\left(Y^{1-1/(3r)}(\log Y)^{-8/3+2/(3r)}+1\right).
\end{equation}

\subsection{The first explicit inverse correction}
Put $v=\log Y$, $k=\log v$, $A=r/2-2$, and $B=-\log K_r=1+\log(4r)-r/2$. The scalar equation becomes
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:invimplicit}
 t+2\log t+\log(1+A/t+1/t^2)=v+B.
\end{equation}
With $s_0=B-2k$, expansion gives
\[
 t=v+s_0+\frac{-2s_0-A}{v}+O((1+k)^2/v^2).
\]
The exact denominator in $N_0=rY/(t+A+1/t)$ is consequently
\[
 t+A+1/t=v-2k+(A+B)+\frac{4k-2B-A+1}{v}
          +O((1+k)^2/v^2).
\]
The identities
\[
 A+B=\log(4r)-1,\qquad -2B-A+1=1+r/2-2\log(4r)
\]
produce the denominator in \eqref{lam:ao:eq:inverseexplicit}. Its omitted error is $O((1+k)^2/v^2)$, and both denominators are asymptotic to $v$, so the induced error in $N_0$ is $O(Y(1+k)^2/v^4)$. The much smaller counting error \eqref{lam:ao:eq:inverseerrorY} and the shift $-1$ are absorbed at this explicit truncation scale. The shift remains present in the more precise controlled formula \eqref{lam:ao:eq:threshold}.

\subsection{A recurrence for all inverse orders}
Set $z=2\log v-B$ and use a formal variable $x$. When computing $q_j(z)$, put
\[
 U=1-zx+\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}q_k(z)x^{k+1}
\]
and define
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:q}
 q_j(z)=-[x^j]\left\{2\log U+\log(1+Ax/U+x^2/U^2)\right\}.
\end{equation}
This is triangular and $\deg q_j\le j$. The first two are
\[
 q_1=2z-A,\qquad
 q_2=z^2-(A+4)z+A^2/2+2A-1.
\]
For every fixed $J$, the finite approximation
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:Tinv}
 T_J=v-z+\sum_{j=1}^Jq_j(z)v^{-j}
\end{equation}
has residual $O((1+\log v)^{J+1}/v^{J+1})$ in \eqref{lam:ao:eq:invimplicit}. To see this, divide $T_J$ by $v$, use the recurrence to cancel the first $J$ powers of $v^{-1}$, and expand the logarithms and reciprocals analytically near $(v^{-1},T_J/v)=(0,1)$. Here $z=O(\log v)$, and the same polynomial-degree argument as in Lemma~\ref{lam:ao:lem:reversion} bounds the finite uncancelled terms. The derivative of the left side of \eqref{lam:ao:eq:invimplicit} is
\[
 1+\frac{2t+A}{t^2+At+1}\ge1
\]
for all sufficiently large positive $t$. The mean value theorem therefore proves
\[
 t_y=T_J+O((1+\log v)^{J+1}/v^{J+1}).
\]
Substitution into the denominator $t+A+1/t$ gives the useful quantitative form
\begin{equation}\label{lam:ao:eq:invarbitrary}
 \nu_s(y)=\frac{rY}{T_J+A+1/T_J}-1
 +O\!\left(\frac{Y(1+\log v)^{J+1}}{v^{J+3}}
       +Y^{1-1/(3r)}v^{-8/3+2/(3r)}+1\right).
\end{equation}
Further expansion of the denominator or its reciprocal gives every prescribed fixed inverse-logarithmic order. The counting uncertainty lies below each such order. This completes Theorem~\ref{lam:ao:thm:inverse}.

\section{Historical comparison and scope}\label{lam:ao:sec:scope}
The two size conventions are essential. Natural-size de Bruijn terms charge increasing size for deeper indices and have different asymptotics. Fixed index or unary-height bounds, linear or affine terms, typed terms, and beta-normal subclasses likewise do not settle these unrestricted sequences.

Bodini, Gardy, Gittenberger, and Jacquot~\cite{bggj2013} give explicit unrestricted bounds for the variable-size-one model, with exponential bases corresponding to $4n/(e\log n)$ and $9(1+\varepsilon)n/(e\log n)$ raised to $n/2$, up to lower-order factors. These locate the first two logarithmic scales and leave a linear-size logarithmic gap. David, Grygiel, Kozik, Raffalli, Theyssier, and Zaionc~\cite{david2013} study the variable-size-zero convention and prove broad enumeration and structural estimates. Bodini, Gardy, Gittenberger, and Go\l\k{e}biewski~\cite{bggg2018} analyze fixed abstraction counts and bounded unary-height structures. Their checked expanded 2015 preprint provides fixed-parameter singularity results, but no proved error uniform at the moving regime $u\asymp n/\log n$ used here.

\section{The sequences and the main conclusions}

\subsection{Why this family is a useful asymptotic target}
A lambda term is built from variables, abstraction $\lambda x.M$, and ordered application $(M\,N)$. Alpha-equivalence identifies consistent changes of bound-variable names. A term is closed when every variable occurrence is bound by an enclosing abstraction. These are fundamental objects in the combinatorics of binding syntax and random program generation. The two size conventions below give concrete, positive, eventually nondecreasing integer sequences with a clear exact recurrence; neither is an artificially oscillating or sporadic example.

Let a variable occurrence have size $s\in\{0,1\}$, and let each abstraction and application have size one. Write $A_s(n)$ for the number of alpha-equivalence classes of closed terms of size $n$. Then

\subsection{Count and inverse}
The positive real Lambert function $W$ is defined by $W(z)e^{W(z)}=z$ for $z>0$.
\begin{theorem}[Four logarithmic scales]\label{lam:bg:thm:count}
For either $s=0$ or $s=1$, put $w=W(4eN)$. As $n\to\infty$,
\begin{equation}\label{lam:bg:eq:lambert}
 \log A_s(n)=\frac Nr\left(w-2+\frac1w\right)
                  +O\!\left(\frac N{\log N}\right).
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\begin{equation}\label{lam:bg:eq:four}
 \log A_s(n)=\frac nr\left(\log n-\log\log n+\log4-1
          +\frac{\log\log n}{\log n}\right)
                +O\!\left(\frac n{\log n}\right),
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}\label{lam:bg:eq:rootlimit}
 \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log n}{n}\,A_s(n)^{r/n}=\frac4e.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
Thus the coefficient of $n\log\log n/\log n$ is $1/r$. The coefficient of the next scale $n/\log n$ is not determined by this theorem. Expanding the Lambert expression to arbitrarily many orders would expand a lower-envelope model; it would not supply an all-orders expansion of the full count.

\subsection{The geometry of a random term}
Choose uniformly from the $A_s(n)$ closed alpha-classes. Let $U_n$ be its number of abstractions, and let $H_n$ be the maximum number of abstraction vertices on a root-to-leaf path. Binary vertices on the path do not count toward $H_n$. Put
\begin{equation}\label{lam:bg:eq:scaled}
 T_n=\frac{U_n\log N}{N},\qquad X_n=\frac{H_n\log N}{N}.
\end{equation}
\begin{theorem}[Joint large deviations]\label{lam:bg:thm:joint}
The pair $(T_n,X_n)$ satisfies a large-deviation principle on $\R^2$, with speed $n$ and good rate function
\begin{equation}\label{lam:bg:eq:joint-rate}
 J_r(t,x)=
 \begin{cases}
 (t-1-\log x)/r,&0<x\le t,\\
 +\infty,&\text{otherwise}.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Both marginals have rate
\begin{equation}\label{lam:bg:eq:marginal-rate}
 I_r(x)=
 \begin{cases}(x-1-\log x)/r,&x>0,\\+\infty,&x\le0.\end{cases}
\end{equation}
Moreover, for every fixed $0<p<\infty$,
\[
 T_n\longrightarrow1\quad\hbox{and}\quad X_n\longrightarrow1
 \qquad\hbox{in }L^p.
\]
In particular, $\E U_n\sim\E H_n\sim n/\log n$.
\end{theorem}
For clarity, a speed-$n$ large-deviation principle with rate $J$ means that every open set $O$ and closed set $F$ satisfy
\begin{align*}
 \liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac1n\log\Prob((T_n,X_n)\in O)&\ge-\inf_O J,\\
 \limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac1n\log\Prob((T_n,X_n)\in F)&\le-\inf_F J.
\end{align*}
``Good'' means that all finite sublevel sets of $J$ are compact.

\begin{corollary}[Abstractions off a longest unary path]\label{lam:bg:cor:deficit}
The variable $D_n=(U_n-H_n)\log N/N$ has rate
\[
 K_r(d)=d/r\quad(d\ge0),\qquad K_r(d)=+\infty\quad(d<0).
\]
For every $\delta>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{lam:bg:eq:deficit-tail}
 \Prob(D_n\ge\delta)=\exp\{-n\delta/r+o(n)\}.
\end{equation}
Hence a longest unary path contains all but $o(n/\log n)$ of the abstractions, in probability.
\end{corollary}
This concerns the entire root-to-leaf path. It does not say that the abstractions form a consecutive chain above the root.

\section{Exact combinatorics and the first sandwich}\label{lam:bg:sec:skeleton}

\subsection{Binder positions rather than names}
For a fixed unary--binary syntax tree, a leaf at unary depth $d$ has exactly $d$ available binders. Selecting one ancestor abstraction at each leaf determines exactly one closed alpha-class. Equivalently, this selects a valid de Bruijn index at each leaf. Distinct ancestor choices remain distinct under alpha-equivalence. Consequently the number of classes supported by a skeleton is the product of its leaf depths; a leaf of depth zero contributes zero.

If a term has $u$ unary vertices, $b$ binary vertices, and $v$ leaves, then $v=b+1$. Its size is
\begin{equation}\label{lam:bg:eq:size}
 n=u+b+s(b+1),\qquad v=\frac{N-u}{r},\qquad
 b=\frac{N-u}{r}-1.
\end{equation}
Call $u$ admissible at size $n$ when $u\ge1$ and $N-u$ is a positive multiple of $r$. For $r=2$ this is a parity condition; it cannot be discarded in lower-bound constructions. Let $A_s(n,u)$ count classes with exactly $u$ abstractions. The Catalan number
\[
 C_b=\frac1{b+1}\binom{2b}{b}
\]
counts plane full binary trees with $b$ internal vertices.

\begin{lemma}[Skeleton sandwich]\label{lam:bg:lem:skeleton}
For every admissible pair $(n,u)$,
\begin{equation}\label{lam:bg:eq:sandwich}
 C_bu^{b+1}\le A_s(n,u)
       \le C_b\binom{u+2b}{u}u^{b+1}.
\end{equation}
More exactly, if $\mathcal B_b$ denotes the plane full binary trees with $b$ internal vertices, then
\begin{equation}\label{lam:bg:eq:exact-weight}
 A_s(n,u)=\sum_{B\in\mathcal B_b}
 \ \sum_{\substack{\alpha_1+\cdots+\alpha_{2b+1}=u\\\alpha_i\ge0}}
 \ \prod_{v\text{ leaf of }B}\left(\sum_{i\text{ on the path to }v}\alpha_i\right).
\end{equation}
Each path includes the root slot and the slot directly above its leaf.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Suppress unary vertices. Each remaining binary skeleton has $2b+1$ vertices and hence $2b+1$ slots directly above those vertices, including a root slot. Reinserting unary chains amounts to a weak composition of $u$ into these slots, giving $\binom{u+2b}{u}$ placements. A leaf has at most $u$ ancestors, proving the upper bound and the exact product formula. For the lower bound, place all $u$ abstractions in the root slot. Every leaf then has $u$ choices independently. There is no factorial for variable names.
\end{proof}
We shall use the elementary estimates
\begin{equation}\label{lam:bg:eq:catalan-bounds}
 \frac{4^b}{(2b+1)(b+1)}\le C_b\le4^b.
\end{equation}
The lower one follows because the middle binomial coefficient is the largest of the $2b+1$ coefficients whose sum is $4^b$.

\subsection{An exact recurrence}
Let $L_m(z)$ count terms in a context with $m$ distinguishable available binder positions. Variables, abstraction, and ordered application give the formal identity
\begin{equation}\label{lam:bg:eq:size-gf}
 L_m(z)=mz^s+zL_{m+1}(z)+zL_m(z)^2.
\end{equation}
For $s=0$ its constant term is $m$; this causes no infinite coefficient because every application or abstraction has positive size. With $a_s(n,m)=[z^n]L_m(z)$, the recurrence is
\begin{equation}\label{lam:bg:eq:size-rec}
 a_s(n,m)=m\mathbf1_{n=s}
    +\mathbf1_{n\ge1}\left(a_s(n-1,m+1)
      +\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}a_s(i,m)a_s(n-1-i,m)\right).
\end{equation}
In particular, $A_s(n)=a_s(n,0)$. To compute through size $M$, only the triangular array $n+m\le M$ is needed. All dependencies have smaller size. The code in the accompanying package also differentiates a unary marker to obtain exact first moments and independently checks them with the bivariate recurrence below.

The lower half of the sandwich, the root-chain subclass, is the lower bound of this Part's counting theorem; Part~I replaces it by the single-spine class.

\section{A uniform critical value majorant}\label{lam:bg:sec:majorant}
The crude placement factor in \eqref{lam:bg:eq:sandwich} is too large to resolve the fourth logarithmic scale. The key is to sum the zero-abstraction subtrees first and then bound the much smaller reduced-tree family.

\begin{theorem}[Uniform coefficient bound]\label{lam:bg:thm:uniform}
Let $K=e(2+\sqrt3)$. For all admissible $(n,u)$,
\begin{equation}\label{lam:bg:eq:uniform}
 C_bu^{b+1}\le A_s(n,u)\le 2uK^u(4u)^b.
\end{equation}
The upper bound is uniform in both $b$ and $u$.
\end{theorem}

\section{The moving saddle and four logarithmic scales}\label{lam:bg:sec:saddle}
Define the real envelope
\begin{equation}\label{lam:bg:eq:F}
 F_N(u)=\frac{N-u}{r}\log(4u),\qquad0<u<N.
\end{equation}
It is strictly concave, since
\begin{equation}\label{lam:bg:eq:F-derivs}
 F_N'(u)=\frac{N/u-1-\log(4u)}r,\qquad
 F_N''(u)=-\frac{N/u^2+1/u}{r}<0.
\end{equation}
Its maximizer is $u_*=N/w$, with $w=W(4eN)$, and direct substitution gives
\begin{equation}\label{lam:bg:eq:Fmax}
 F_N(u_*)=\frac Nr\left(w-2+\frac1w\right).
\end{equation}

For sufficiently large $n$, a nearest admissible integer lies within $r/2$ of $u_*$. The saddle is far from either endpoint, so this choice has $b\ge0$. Taylor's theorem and \eqref{lam:bg:eq:F-derivs} give a lattice loss $O((\log N)^2/N)=o(1)$. The lower Catalan estimate in \eqref{lam:bg:eq:catalan-bounds} then yields
\begin{equation}\label{lam:bg:eq:lower-saddle}
 \log A_s(n)\ge F_N(u_*)-O(\log N).
\end{equation}

Let $c=\log K$. The uniform upper bound simplifies exactly to
\begin{equation}\label{lam:bg:eq:upper-individual}
 \log A_s(n,u)\le F_N(u)+cu-\log2.
\end{equation}
There is no additional $\log u$ term: it has already been absorbed into $F_N$ using $b+1=(N-u)/r$. The maximizer $u_c$ of $F_N(u)+cu$ is, for large $N$, interior and satisfies
\[
 u_c=N/x_c,\qquad
 x_c+\log x_c=\log(4eN)-rc,
 \qquad x_c=W(4eNe^{-rc})\sim\log N.
\]
Thus $u_c=O(N/\log N)$, and
\[
 F_N(u_c)+cu_c\le F_N(u_*)+O(N/\log N).
\]
Summing at most $N$ admissible values costs only $O(\log N)$. Together with \eqref{lam:bg:eq:lower-saddle}, this proves \eqref{lam:bg:eq:lambert}.
\begin{writenote}[5 October 2026]
This proof is the second, weaker route to the count. Its lower bound uses the root-chain subclass at Part~II's saddle $u_*=N/w$ (\eqref{lam:bg:eq:lower-saddle}), and its upper bound gives away $cu=O(N/\log N)$; Part~I gains the factor $e^{u/2}$ in the lower bound by the single-spine class and controls the upper bound to $O(R_r(N))$ by the height-sensitive majorant and two localization stages.
\end{writenote}

Write $q=\log N$ and $h=\log q$. The identity $w+\log w=q+1+\log4$ gives, by substitution,
\begin{align*}
 w&=q-h+1+\log4+\frac{h-1-\log4}{q}
                              +O(h^2/q^2),\\
 w-2+w^{-1}&=q-h+\log4-1+\frac{h-\log4}{q}
                              +O(h^2/q^2).
\end{align*}
Since $h^2/q\to0$, the last remainder is $o(1/q)$. The constant $-\log4/q$ lies within the unresolved $O(1/q)$ error for the full count, but $h/q$ does not. Replacing $N$ by $n$ costs $O(\log n)$ and proves \eqref{lam:bg:eq:four}. Dividing that equation by $n/r$ and exponentiating proves \eqref{lam:bg:eq:rootlimit}.
\begin{writenote}[5 October 2026]
For the full count the constant at this scale is determined by Part~I: inside the bracket the coefficient of $1/q$ is $h-\log4+r/2$, not $h-\log4$ (\eqref{lam:ao:eq:allorders} with $J=1$; Part~I's $z+1=\ell-\log4+r/2$). The extra $r/2$ is the enhancement $u/2$ at $u\approx N/\log N$ (Remark~\ref{lam:bg:rem:ratio}).
\end{writenote}

\begin{remark}[What exponential precision is available]

 A_s(n)=\exp\!\left\{\frac nr\left(\log n-\log\log n+\log4-1

\section{The threshold inverse}\label{lam:bg:sec:inverse}
Set
\[
 f(x)=\frac xr\left(\log x-\log\log x+c_0
              +\frac{\log\log x}{\log x}\right).
\]
Theorem~\ref{lam:bg:thm:count} says that, at integer arguments,
\begin{equation}\label{lam:bg:eq:f-error}
 \log A_s(n)=f(n)+O(n/\log n).
\end{equation}
For $L=\log y$, $\ell=\log L$, let $k=\log\ell$, $a=\log r+c_0$, and
\[
 D=\ell-2k+a+4k/\ell,\qquad n_0=rL/D.
\]
As $y\to\infty$, $D\sim\ell$ and $n_0\to\infty$. Expanding only the explicit smooth function, write $v=\log n_0$ and obtain
\begin{align*}
 v&=\ell-k+\log r+(2k-a)/\ell+O(k^2/\ell^2),\\
 \log v&=k+(-k+\log r)/\ell+O(k^2/\ell^2),\\
 \frac{\log v}{v}&=k/\ell+O(k^2/\ell^2).
\end{align*}
Therefore
\[
 v-\log v+c_0+\frac{\log v}{v}
   =D-\frac{a+\log r}{\ell}+O(k^2/\ell^2)
   =D+O(1/\ell),
\]
where $k^2/\ell\to0$. It follows that
\begin{equation}\label{lam:bg:eq:inverse-residual}
 f(n_0)-L=O(L/\ell^2).
\end{equation}
The count error in \eqref{lam:bg:eq:f-error} is also $O(L/\ell^2)$ locally, because $n_0\asymp L/\ell$ and $\log n_0\sim\ell$.

For completeness, make the bracketing step explicit. Choose a fixed sufficiently large $M>0$ and integers
\[
 n_- =\left\lfloor n_0-M L/\ell^3\right\rfloor,
 \qquad
 n_+ =\left\lceil n_0+M L/\ell^3\right\rceil.
\]
The derivative of the explicit $f$ satisfies $f'(x)\sim\ell/r$ uniformly between these points. The mean value theorem therefore makes $f(n_\pm)-f(n_0)$ have opposite signs and magnitudes asymptotic to $ML/(r\ell^2)$. Choose $M$ to dominate the fixed constants in \eqref{lam:bg:eq:f-error} and \eqref{lam:bg:eq:inverse-residual}. Then for all sufficiently large $y$,
\[
 \log A_s(n_-)<L\le\log A_s(n_+).
\]
Monotonicity gives $n_-<\nu_s(y)\le n_+$. Rounding contributes $O(1)$, negligible relative to $L/\ell^3$. This proves Theorem~\ref{lam:bg:thm:inverse} without any regularity assumption on the unknown error.

The denominator form cleanly separates the known scales. In particular, the simpler expansion
\[
 \nu_s(y)=\frac{rL}{\ell}
 \left[1+\frac{2\log\ell-\log r-c_0}{\ell}
       +O\!\left(\frac{(\log\ell)^2}{\ell^2}\right)\right]
\]
is valid but hides the improvement supplied by the determined $4\log\ell/\ell$ denominator term.
\begin{writenote}[5 October 2026]
Superseded by Part~I, Theorem~\ref{lam:ao:thm:inverse} (see the note after Theorem~\ref{lam:bg:thm:inverse}); the bracketing method, monotonicity with an explicit smooth function and no derivative of an unknown error, is the same as Part~I's. Letters here: $L=\log y$, $\ell=\log L$, $k=\log\ell$, $D$ the denominator, $n_0=rL/D$, $v=\log n_0$; Part~I's are $Y$, $v$, $k=\log v$ and $N_0$.
\end{writenote}

\section{Total abstraction deviations}\label{lam:bg:sec:unary}
We first establish the marginal estimates needed to control noncompact regions in the joint problem. These use only the weaker $o(n)$ part of Theorem~\ref{lam:bg:thm:count}.

From the skeleton sandwich and $u+2b\le2N$, the inequality $\binom Mu\le(eM/u)^u$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{lam:bg:eq:G}
 \log A_s(n,u)\le G_N(u)-\log4,
 \qquad G_N(u)=F_N(u)+u\log(2eN/u).
\end{equation}
For $u\log N/N$ in a fixed compact subset of $(0,\infty)$, the last term is $O(N\log\log N/\log N)=o(N)$. The lower bound differs from $F_N(u)$ by only $O(\log N)$. Hence, uniformly in such a compact interval,
\begin{equation}\label{lam:bg:eq:unary-local}
 \frac1n\log\Prob(U_n=u)=-I_r\!\left(\frac{u\log N}{N}\right)+o(1).
\end{equation}
Indeed, putting $t=u\log N/N$,
\[
 F_N(u)=\frac Nr\left(\log N-\log\log N+\log4+\log t-t\right)+o(N).
\]
Subtract the total-count logarithm to get \eqref{lam:bg:eq:unary-local}.

The envelope $G_N$ is strictly concave. If its maximizer is $N/x$, its stationary equation is
\begin{equation}\label{lam:bg:eq:G-saddle}
 x+(r+1)\log x=\log N+1+(2-r)\log2.
\end{equation}
This has a unique positive solution, with $x\sim\log N$, so the scaled maximizing unary count tends to one. For every fixed $0<a<1<b$, the envelope is eventually increasing up to $aN/\log N$ and decreasing beyond $bN/\log N$. Bound each tail by at most $N$ copies of the appropriate endpoint envelope. A single admissible integer within distance $r$ on the correct side supplies the matching lower bound. Thus
\begin{align}
 \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac1n\log\Prob(T_n\le a)&=-I_r(a),\label{lam:bg:eq:unary-lower}\\
 \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac1n\log\Prob(T_n\ge b)&=-I_r(b).\label{lam:bg:eq:unary-upper}
\end{align}
In particular, $I_r(a)\to\infty$ as $a\downarrow0$ and $I_r(b)\to\infty$ as $b\to\infty$. These provide the lower-boundary exclusion and exponential tightness. The local estimate gives the open lower bound and compact upper bound, while the two tails extend the latter to every closed set. This proves the marginal LDP for $T_n$.
\begin{writenote}[5 October 2026]
The envelope $G_N$ of \eqref{lam:bg:eq:G} is not Part~I's coarse envelope $G_N$ of \eqref{lam:ao:eq:G}, although both are $F_N$ plus a term of order $N/\log N$ at the saddle.
\end{writenote}

\section{The joint abstraction and height principle}\label{lam:bg:sec:joint}

\subsection{A local construction that reaches the boundary}
Let $A_s(n,u,h)$ count terms with exactly $u$ abstractions and maximum unary height $h$. Every leaf now has at most $h$ binders, so
\begin{equation}\label{lam:bg:eq:joint-upper}
 A_s(n,u,h)\le C_b\binom{u+2b}{u}h^{b+1}.
\end{equation}
For a lower bound, suppose
\begin{equation}\label{lam:bg:eq:witness-conditions}
 h\ge2,\qquad1\le u-h+1\le b+1.
\end{equation}
Start with any plane full binary tree with $b$ internal vertices. Put $h-1$ unary vertices in its root slot, then put one unary vertex directly above each of the first $u-h+1$ leaves in left-to-right order. This gives exactly $u$ unary vertices and height exactly $h$. Every leaf has at least $h-1$ binders, hence
\begin{equation}\label{lam:bg:eq:joint-lower}
 A_s(n,u,h)\ge C_b(h-1)^{b+1}.
\end{equation}
Distinct binary trees and binder selections remain distinct terms.

Consider any compact subset of $0<x\le t<\infty$ bounded away from $x=0$, with
\[
 t=u\log N/N,\qquad x=h\log N/N.
\]
Conditions \eqref{lam:bg:eq:witness-conditions} hold uniformly for all sufficiently large $n$: $u,h=O(N/\log N)$, $h\to\infty$, and $b+1\sim N/r$. The construction also works on $x=t$ by taking $h=u$, so it loses neither the cone boundary nor the $r=2$ parity subsequence.

The loss from replacing $h$ with $h-1$ is
\[
 (b+1)\log\frac h{h-1}=O(\log N).
\]
The Catalan correction is $O(\log N)$ and the placement entropy in \eqref{lam:bg:eq:joint-upper} is $o(N)$. Consequently,
\begin{align}
 \log A_s(n,u,h)
 &=\frac Nr\left(\log N-\log\log N+\log4+\log x-t\right)+o(N),
 \label{lam:bg:eq:joint-log}\\
 \frac1n\log\Prob(U_n=u,H_n=h)&=-J_r(t,x)+o(1),\label{lam:bg:eq:joint-local}
\end{align}
uniformly on the indicated compacts.

\subsection{The singular small-height boundary}
Local estimates alone do not prove the full two-dimensional LDP because $x$ can approach zero. Fix $0<a<1$ and $h=\lfloor aN/\log N\rfloor$. For the event $H_n\le h$, each leaf has at most $\min(u,h)$ choices. For $u\le h$, the logarithmic upper envelope is $G_N(u)-\log4$ from \eqref{lam:bg:eq:G}, which is maximized at $h$ for large $N$.

For $u\ge h$, use instead
\[
 Q_h(u)=\frac{N-u}{r}\log(4h)+u\log(2eN/u).
\]
Its derivative is
\[
 Q_h'(u)=-\frac1r\log(4h)+\log(2N/u).
\]
At $u=h$, the first term is $-\log N/r+O(\log\log N)$ whereas the second is only $O(\log\log N)$. The derivative is therefore negative for large $N$ and decreases further with $u$. The two envelopes agree at $h$, so summing at most $N$ values of $u$ gives
\[
 \limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac1n\log\Prob(X_n\le a)\le-I_r(a).
\]
For the matching lower bound, take the largest admissible $u\le h$. Then $h-u<r$. Count the entire root-chain subclass, with $C_bu^{b+1}$ terms, all of height $u\le h$. The scaled $u$ tends to $a$, so the same logarithmic expansion gives
\begin{equation}\label{lam:bg:eq:height-boundary}
 \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac1n\log\Prob(X_n\le a)=-I_r(a).
\end{equation}
As $a\downarrow0$, these rates tend to infinity.

\subsection{Globalization and contractions}
Because $0<X_n\le T_n$, the upper tail \eqref{lam:bg:eq:unary-upper} supplies exponential tightness of the pair. Every point of $0<x\le t$ admits admissible lattice approximations; on $x=t$ choose $h=u$. Equation~\eqref{lam:bg:eq:joint-local} gives the open-set lower bound. On a compact set separated from $x=0$, the same uniform estimate and at most $N^2$ lattice pairs give the compact-set upper bound.

For a general closed set, split the event into a compact middle region, $\{X_n\le a\}$, and $\{T_n\ge b\}$. Apply the compact bound, \eqref{lam:bg:eq:height-boundary}, and \eqref{lam:bg:eq:unary-upper}, then let $a\downarrow0$ and $b\to\infty$. This proves the closed-set bound. The rate is lower semicontinuous, including at the cone and at $x=0$. Moreover,
\[
 J_r(t,x)=I_r(x)+(t-x)/r\ge I_r(x).
\]
A finite sublevel set keeps $x$ away from zero and infinity; the identity $t=rJ_r(t,x)+1+\log x$ then bounds $t$. Hence the rate is good, proving the joint LDP.

For fixed $x$, minimizing $J_r(t,x)$ over $t\ge x$ gives $t=x$ and rate $I_r(x)$. For fixed $t$, minimizing over $0<x\le t$ likewise gives $x=t$. For the difference $d=t-x\ge0$,
\[
 J_r(x+d,x)=\frac{d+x-1-\log x}{r},
\]
whose minimum over $x>0$ occurs at $x=1$ and equals $d/r$. The contraction principle proves both marginal rates and the deficit rate. Since $K_r$ is continuous for $d>0$, the closed tail $[\delta,\infty)$ and open tail $(\delta,\infty)$ have equal infimum $\delta/r$, proving \eqref{lam:bg:eq:deficit-tail}.

Both marginal rates have unique zero at one. For any fixed $\varepsilon>0$, each fixed relative deviation has exponentially small probability. Since $T_n,X_n\le\log N$, its contribution to the $p$th absolute centered moment is at most $(\log N+1)^p$ times that exponentially small probability. Inside the $\varepsilon$ window it is at most $\varepsilon^p$. Let $n\to\infty$ and then $\varepsilon\downarrow0$ to prove the stated $L^p$ convergence.

Finally, changing the normalization from $\log(n+1)/(n+1)$ to $\log n/n$ changes either normalized variable by at most $O(\log n/n)$ deterministically, since $U_n,H_n\le n$. This is exponential equivalence at every speed, so every stated LDP and limit also holds with the simpler normalization.

\section{Historical comparison and the unresolved frontier}\label{lam:bg:sec:history}

\subsection{What the checked sources establish}
Bodini, Gardy, Gittenberger, and Jacquot~\cite{bggj2013} treat the unrestricted size-one class in Section~5. Their Theorem~22 bounds its growth between expressions with principal bases $4n/(e\log n)$ and $9(1+\varepsilon)n/(e\log n)$, raised to the power $n/2$, with further lower-order factors. These identify the first two logarithmic scales but leave a linear-in-$n$ logarithmic gap. Theorem~\ref{lam:bg:thm:count} fixes that linear term and one additional logarithmic scale.

For variable size zero, David, Grygiel, Kozik, Raffalli, Theyssier, and Zaionc~\cite{david2013} establish broad enumeration bounds and structural results. In particular, their Theorem~5.4 places the abstraction count, with high probability, between $n/(3\log n)$ and $3n/\log n$, and gives a unary-height lower bound. Theorem~\ref{lam:bg:thm:joint} supplies the limiting constant one for both parameters and the complete rate functions in the present unrestricted model. This comparison does not purport to subsume their normalization or head-abstraction theorems.

The expanded preprint of Bodini, Gardy, Gittenberger, and Go\l\k{e}biewski~\cite{bggg2018} proves fixed-abstraction singularity asymptotics and studies bounded unary height and bounded indices. It also describes empirical unary-height growth of order $n/\log n$. A fixed-$u$ asymptotic cannot be inserted into a saddle with $u\sim n/\log n$ without a uniform error estimate. The proof in Section~\ref{lam:bg:sec:majorant} provides its own finite, uniform majorant instead.

The introduction of Gittenberger and Larcher~\cite{parameters} describes the unrestricted logarithm through its second-order term. Grygiel and Larcher~\cite{unaryprofile} explicitly describe the unrestricted size-zero and size-one enumeration problems as open; their own results impose a fixed index bound. These directly relevant sources were checked, together with targeted searches for later refinements through the report date. This is a bounded comparison, not a certificate that no earlier, unpublished, or poorly indexed matching result exists.

The natural-size model studied by Bendkowski, Bodini, and Dovgal~\cite{natural} charges for the size of a de Bruijn index. It has different enumeration and probability laws. Likewise, linear, affine, beta-normal, simply typed, bounded-index, and bounded-height classes are not substitutes for A135501 or A220894.

\subsection{Why the result is not a complete transseries}
The two decisive sources of information are an exact lower subclass and a uniform upper estimate whose logarithmic ratio is $O(u+\log(b+1))$. At the saddle, $u\asymp n/\log n$, so this leaves precisely the error scale in Theorem~\ref{lam:bg:thm:count}. Nothing here determines the actual weighted-placement enhancement at that scale.
\begin{writenote}[5 October 2026]
Determined at the moving saddle by Part~I. Part~I's Proposition~\ref{lam:ao:prop:upper} replaces the logarithmic ratio $O(u+\log(b+1))$ by $u/2+E(b,u)+O(\log(b+u+1))$ with $E(b,u)=o(u)$ at the saddle, and its single-spine bound \eqref{lam:ao:eq:convolution} gives $u/2-O(\log u)$ from below; the weighted-placement enhancement over the root-chain subclass is therefore $e^{u/2+o(u)}$ there (Remark~\ref{lam:bg:rem:ratio}). It accounts for the missing coefficient of $n/\log n$ (note after Theorem~\ref{lam:bg:thm:count}).
\end{writenote}

A concrete next object is
\begin{equation}\label{lam:bg:eq:ratio-frontier}
 R(b,u)=\frac{A_s(n,u)}{C_bu^{b+1}},\qquad n=u+rb+s.
\end{equation}
The ratio is independent of the size convention once $(b,u)$ is fixed. The present proof supplies an upper bound exponential in $u$, up to a polynomial factor in $b$, but no asymptotic formula for $\log R(b,u)/u$ in the moving regime. Establishing its limit, with adequate uniformity, would be a natural route to the missing $n/\log n$ coefficient. A full equivalent would require far sharper control, including effective prefactors and uniform summation errors.

\begin{remark}[\wtag\ The ratio frontier to leading order; 5 October 2026]\label{lam:bg:rem:ratio}
Neither manuscript states this; it combines two estimates of Part~I. Let $R(b,u)$ be as in \eqref{lam:bg:eq:ratio-frontier}, write $A(b,u)=A_s(n,u)$, $\alpha=b/u$, $H_u=\sum_{j=1}^u1/j$, and let $E(b,u)=3\,2^{1/3}u(1+\alpha)^{1/3}e^{-\alpha/3}$ be as in \eqref{lam:ao:eq:E}.
\begin{enumerate}
\item For all integers $u\ge1$ and $b\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{lam:bg:eq:ratio-upper}
 0\le\log R(b,u)\le\frac u2+E(b,u)+\log\bigl(2u(u+1)(2b+1)(b+1)\bigr).
\end{equation}
\item For every $a>0$ and all integers $u\ge1$, $b\ge0$ with $b\ge\frac u2(H_u-1)+au$,
\begin{equation}\label{lam:bg:eq:ratio-lower}
 \log R(b,u)\ge\frac u2-\frac14\log u-\frac12-\log\bigl(1+a^{-2}\bigr).
\end{equation}
\item Consequently $\log R(b,u)/u\to\frac12$ along every sequence of pairs with $u\to\infty$, $b\ge\frac u2(H_u-1)+au$ for one fixed $a>0$, and $\log(b+1)=o(u)$.
\item \emph{At the moving saddle.} Let $t$ and $u_*=N/t$ be Part~I's \eqref{lam:ao:eq:saddledef}, and let $u=u_N$ be admissible with $(u-u_*)\log N/u_*\to0$ (for example the admissible $u_0$ within $r/2$ of $u_*$ chosen in Section~\ref{lam:ao:sec:lower}), with $b=(N-u)/r-1$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{lam:bg:eq:ratio-saddle}
 -O(\log u)\le\log R(b,u)-\frac u2\le O\bigl(u^{1-1/(3r)}(\log u)^{1/3}\bigr),
\end{equation}
so that $\log R(b,u)/u\to\frac12$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{remark}
\begin{proof}
(1) The lower bound $R\ge1$ is the left inequality of \eqref{lam:bg:eq:sandwich}. For the upper bound, Proposition~\ref{lam:ao:prop:upper}, \eqref{lam:ao:eq:uniform}, gives $A(b,u)\le2u^2(u+1)(4u)^b\exp\{u/2+E(b,u)\}$ for all $u\ge1$, $b\ge0$, and \eqref{lam:bg:eq:catalan-bounds} gives $C_b\ge4^b/((2b+1)(b+1))$. Dividing, $R(b,u)\le2u(u+1)(2b+1)(b+1)\exp\{u/2+E(b,u)\}$.

(2) Part~I's inequality \eqref{lam:ao:eq:convolution} gives $R(b,u)\ge Q_u(\rho)\Prob(J\le b)$, with $Q_u(\rho)=\sqrt{u^u/u!}$ by \eqref{lam:ao:eq:Q}. Since $\log$ is concave, $\int_{j-1}^j\log x\,dx\ge\frac12(\log(j-1)+\log j)$ for $j\ge2$; summing over $2\le j\le u$ gives $u\log u-u+1\ge\log u!-\frac12\log u$, also for $u=1$. Hence $\log Q_u(\rho)=\frac12(u\log u-\log u!)\ge\frac u2-\frac12-\frac14\log u$. By \eqref{lam:ao:eq:mean} and \eqref{lam:ao:eq:var}, $J$ has mean $\mu=\frac u2(H_u-1)$ and variance $\sigma^2\le u^2$. If $b\ge\mu+au$, then $J>b$ implies $J-\mu>au$, so Cantelli's inequality, proved in Section~\ref{lam:ao:sec:lower} in the form $\Prob(X\ge c)\le\sigma^2/(\sigma^2+c^2)$ for $c>0$, gives
\[
 \Prob(J>b)\le\frac{\sigma^2}{\sigma^2+a^2u^2}\le\frac{u^2}{u^2+a^2u^2}=\frac1{1+a^2},
\]
because $\sigma^2\mapsto\sigma^2/(\sigma^2+c^2)$ is increasing. Thus $\Prob(J\le b)\ge a^2/(1+a^2)=1/(1+a^{-2})$, and \eqref{lam:bg:eq:ratio-lower} follows.

(3) Since $H_u\ge\log(u+1)\to\infty$, the hypothesis gives $\alpha\ge\frac12(H_u-1)+a\to\infty$, hence $E(b,u)/u=3\,2^{1/3}(1+\alpha)^{1/3}e^{-\alpha/3}\to0$. The logarithmic terms in \eqref{lam:bg:eq:ratio-upper} and \eqref{lam:bg:eq:ratio-lower} are $O(\log(u+1)+\log(b+1))=o(u)$. Dividing both inequalities by $u$ gives the limit $\frac12$.

(4) Put $L=\log N$. By \eqref{lam:ao:eq:saddle}, $N/u_*=t=\log(4u_*)+1-r/2$ and $t\sim L$, so $u_*\sim N/L$, and $u\sim u_*$ by hypothesis. Write $\alpha_*=(N/u_*-1)/r-1/u_*=\log(4u_*)/r-\frac12-1/u_*$, as in Section~\ref{lam:ao:sec:localization}. Then $\alpha=(N/u-1)/r-1/u$ and
\[
 \Bigl|\frac Nu-\frac N{u_*}\Bigr|=t\,\frac{|u-u_*|}{u}=O\Bigl(L\cdot\frac{|u-u_*|}{u_*}\Bigr)\longrightarrow0,
\]
so $\alpha=\alpha_*+o(1)$. With $H_u\le1+\log u$ (Section~\ref{lam:ao:sec:lower}) and $\log u=\log u_*+o(1)$,
\[
 \alpha-\tfrac12(H_u-1)\ge\Bigl(\frac1r-\frac12\Bigr)\log u_*+\frac{\log4}r-\frac12+o(1).
\]
For $r=2$ the right side tends to $\log2-\frac12>0$, and for $r=1$ to $+\infty$; so for large $N$ the hypothesis of~(2) holds with $a=a_0=(\log2-\frac12)/2$, the constant of Section~\ref{lam:ao:sec:lower}, and \eqref{lam:bg:eq:ratio-lower} gives the left inequality of \eqref{lam:bg:eq:ratio-saddle}. For the right one, $\alpha=\log(4u_*)/r-\frac12+o(1)$ gives $e^{-\alpha/3}=O(u^{-1/(3r)})$ and $(1+\alpha)^{1/3}=O((\log u)^{1/3})$, so $E(b,u)=O(u^{1-1/(3r)}(\log u)^{1/3})$; and $b<N$ with $\log N=O(\log u)$ makes the last term of \eqref{lam:bg:eq:ratio-upper} $O(\log u)$. Finally $|u_0-u_*|\le r/2$ gives $(u_0-u_*)L/u_*=O(L^2/N)\to0$, so $u_0$ is an example.
\end{proof}
\begin{writenote}[5 October 2026]
What the remark settles and what it does not. The paragraph above asks for the limit of $\log R(b,u)/u$ in the moving regime and calls it a natural route to the missing coefficient of $n/\log n$. Part~(4) gives that limit, $\frac12$, with the error $O(u^{1-1/(3r)}(\log u)^{1/3})$, which at the saddle is $O(R_r(N))$, the error of Part~I's Theorem~\ref{lam:ao:thm:main}. Part~I's proof of that theorem is the completed route: $\log(C_bu^{b+1})+u/2=H_N(u)-O(\log N)$ is its envelope \eqref{lam:ao:eq:FH}. The remark does not by itself reprove Theorem~\ref{lam:ao:thm:main}: the global upper bound needs Part~I's localization (Section~\ref{lam:ao:sec:localization}) for $u$ away from the saddle, where the hypothesis of~(3) can fail. Not settled: the second-order behaviour of $\log R(b,u)-\frac u2$ at the saddle (between $-O(\log u)$ and $O(u^{1-1/(3r)}(\log u)^{1/3})$), and the limit for bounded $\alpha$, or for $\alpha\to\infty$ with $\alpha-\frac12(H_u-1)$ not bounded below by a positive constant (for $r=2$ this includes part of Part~I's window $|u/u_*-1|<1/\log N$, where $|\alpha-\alpha_*|$ can approach $\frac12$); see Section~\ref{lam:bg:sec:further}, item~5. Evidence only, from the exact recurrence \eqref{lam:ao:eq:B} computed at the write for $r=2$ at the admissible $u$ nearest $u_*$: $\log R(b,u)/u=0.737$, $0.738$, $0.732$ at $(b,u)=(13,10)$, $(25,16)$, $(38,22)$ ($N=38,68,100$), and the single-spine part $[x^b]L_u/(C_bu^{b+1})$ alone gives $0.576$, $0.568$, $0.559$; both lie above $\frac12$ and decrease slowly, as the error term allows. These values neither confirm nor refute the rate.
\end{writenote}


Other substantive questions remain:
\begin{enumerate}
 \item What are the centered fluctuation scales and limiting laws of $U_n$ and $H_n$? A large-deviation principle for fixed relative deviations does not by itself prove a central limit theorem.
 \item How large is $U_n-H_n$ on its typical, sub-$n/\log n$ scale? The fixed-deficit rate is informative but does not answer this local question.
 \item What fraction of a longest unary path is a consecutive initial root chain? The path concentration theorem does not identify that statistic.
 \item Can the weighted-placement sum \eqref{lam:bg:eq:exact-weight} be controlled uniformly to successive orders, leading to a genuine expansion and a correspondingly sharper inverse?
\end{enumerate}
These are mathematically meaningful continuations; no answer is inferred from finite numerical agreement or from expanding the Lambert lower envelope alone.

\section{Reproducibility and finite validation}\label{lam:bg:sec:checks}
The source package contains this \TeX{} file, its PDF, an exact checker, integer data tables, integrity and corruption tests, and deterministic build instructions. It contains no downloaded source-paper PDFs. The bibliography links the primary sources instead.

The executable mathematical checker uses only the Python standard library, integers, and \texttt{fractions.Fraction}. Every test uses an explicit conditional exception; no validation depends on \texttt{assert}, so Python's \texttt{-O} mode does not disable checks. The suite performs the following logically independent finite comparisons:
\begin{enumerate}
 \item Reconstruct both total-count sequences and first unary moments through $n=200$ by the size recurrence. Check monotonicity, moment ranges, and the total skeleton sandwich.
 \item Compare the first 16 terms of each model with the published prefixes. The complete remote OEIS b-files were not successfully retrieved, so the longer computed tables are not claimed to have been checked against them.
 \item Compare totals and first moments through $n=20$ with a bivariate decomposition indexed by unary and binary counts.
 \item Compare that direct decomposition with the coefficient form of the radical recurrence. Check an exact integer upper corollary using $K<12$, for $1\le u\le9$ and $0\le b\le30$.
 \item Enumerate every binary skeleton and unary-slot composition for $0\le b\le4$, $1\le u\le6$, accumulating exact products of leaf depths by height. Verify the total and all applicable joint upper and constructive lower bounds.
 \item Compute the maximum squared radical product dynamically through $u=75$ and compare it with $u^{2u-1}/(u!)^2$ using rational arithmetic.
 \item Compare two exact reduced-shape counts through $u=100$: the quadratic recurrence and
 \[
 d_u=\sum_{q=0}^{u-1}2^{-q}C_q\binom{u+q-1}{2q}.
 \]
 Also check the weaker rational-radius bound $d_u\le4^u/2$.
\end{enumerate}
The last formula follows by contracting the unary chains in a reduced shape with $q$ binary vertices. Its $q+1$ leaf slots require at least one unary vertex each, while its other slots may be empty. A weak composition of the remaining $u-q-1$ vertices into $2q+1$ slots gives $\binom{u+q-1}{2q}$ possibilities.

These tests check finite instances and implementation consistency. They do not prove the asymptotic theorems, certify an error constant, establish an eventual numerical threshold, or prove global novelty. The proofs above are independent of the data.

\section{Further questions and research}\label{lam:bg:sec:further}
\wtag\ (5 October 2026.) Under Vladimir's standing rule of 4~October 2026, this section collects every claim of this Part that is stated without proof, with its source, the argument sketched for it and what is missing, and records how Part~I and Remark~\ref{lam:bg:rem:ratio} re-scope it. Nothing in this Part was found to be wrong; its stale statements carry dated notes in place. The questions on the count itself (equivalent, amplitude, residual scale, the weighted-placement sum, constants, literature) are in Section~\ref{lam:ao:sub:further}.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Fluctuations of $U_n$ and $H_n$} (Section~\ref{lam:bg:sec:history}, question~1): the centred fluctuation scales and limit laws. Theorem~\ref{lam:bg:thm:joint} concerns fixed relative deviations at speed $n$ and does not give a central limit theorem. Missing: a local analysis below the scale $n/\log n$. The rate of the $L^p$ convergence is not stated either; evidence only, from the shipped first moments (\file{data/109-binding-A135501_unary_moment.txt} and the A220894 file), computed at the write: $\E U_n\log N/N$ is $1.217$, $1.245$, $1.252$ for $s=1$ and $1.015$, $1.018$, $1.018$ for $s=0$ at $n=50,100,200$, still well above~$1$ for $s=1$.
\item \emph{The typical size of $U_n-H_n$} (merged: this Part's question~2 and Part~I's question~2). Report~109 asks how large $U_n-H_n$ is on its typical, sub-$n/\log n$ scale; Report~110 asks for the typical off-spine deficit $u-h$ near the moving saddle, where $h$, the largest unary depth of a terminal leaf of the reduced tree, is $H_n$ for an actual term. Known: Corollary~\ref{lam:bg:cor:deficit} gives the deficit rate $d/r$ at speed $n$ and $U_n-H_n=o(n/\log n)$ in probability. Report~110 adds that the deficit $d$ optimizing its majorant (Proposition~\ref{lam:ao:prop:upper}) is not a distributional law. Missing: any estimate of the typical order.
\item \emph{The fraction of a longest unary path that is a consecutive initial root chain} (question~3). Corollary~\ref{lam:bg:cor:deficit} is about a root-to-leaf path and does not identify this statistic.
\item \emph{Uniform control of the weighted-placement sum} (question~4): merged with Part~I's question~3, Section~\ref{lam:ao:sub:further}, item~3, where its partial answer is recorded.
\item \emph{The ratio frontier beyond Remark~\ref{lam:bg:rem:ratio}} (added by the write). Open: the second-order term of $\log R(b,u)-\frac u2$ at the moving saddle (known to lie between $-O(\log u)$ and $O(u^{1-1/(3r)}(\log u)^{1/3})$; for single $(b,u)$ this is the analogue of Section~\ref{lam:ao:sub:further}, item~2), and $\lim\log R(b,u)/u$ for bounded $\alpha=b/u$, or for $\alpha\to\infty$ with $\alpha-\frac12(H_u-1)$ not bounded below by a positive constant. Known there: $0\le\log R(b,u)\le\frac u2+E(b,u)+O(\log(u+b+1))$, so $\limsup\log R/u\le\frac12+3\,2^{1/3}(1+\alpha)^{1/3}e^{-\alpha/3}$ at fixed $\alpha$. Sketch: the single-spine lower bound needs $\Prob(J\le b)$ in a lower-tail regime of the negative-binomial sum $J$, which Cantelli's inequality does not reach.
\end{enumerate}
This Part's other limitations are printed in place and are not questions: the comparison with David et al.\ does not subsume their normalization or head-abstraction theorems; natural-size, linear, affine, beta-normal, typed, bounded-index and bounded-height classes are not substitutes; only 16 OEIS prefix terms per model were compared, the remote b-files were not retrieved, and the longer tables are internally computed; \texttt{--fixtures-only} is a prefix check only; finite checks prove no asymptotic statement.
