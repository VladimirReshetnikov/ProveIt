\begin{abstract}
An extensional acyclic digraph has pairwise distinct out-neighborhoods.
We give a self-contained proof of the formula
$a(n)=n-\lceil\log_2n\rceil$ for its maximum possible number of sources,
the formula still marked conjectural in OEIS A182220 when consulted.
The pigeonhole upper bound is already present in Tomescu's 2011 thesis;
we credit that fact and do not present it as a new discovery.

The main development concerns the boundary of the source-count triangle
A182162. If $u_m=\mathrm{A001192}(m)$, $q=\lceil\log_2 n\rceil$, and
$b_n^{(d)}$ counts isomorphism classes with exactly $n-q-d$ sources,
we obtain an exact $(d+1)$-term formula and, in particular,
$b_n^{(0)}=u_q\binom{2^q-q}{n-q}$.
A uniform covering estimate then proves a dyadic entropy law for every
fixed $d$. The complete set of subsequential exponential growth rates
is an explicit interval, equal to $[1,4]$ when $d=0$.
The exponential jumps across powers of two imply that none of these
boundary sequences is P-recursive. Consequently their ordinary generating
functions are not D-finite.

We also give an all-orders expansion of the Boolean-subset factor,
retaining the small-core count exactly, and a total-variation binomial
law for the number of missing arcs near a complete Boolean layer.
The proofs are accompanied by independently implemented counting
recurrences and exhaustive small-graph checks. These are conventional
mathematical proofs with computational tests, not Lean-verified results.
Historical priority for the boundary refinements is not established.
\end{abstract}

\part{The source boundary: finite-defect enumeration and dyadic non-holonomicity}\label{sbd:part:one}

\section{Problem, contribution, and prior-art boundary}

\subsection{What is being answered}
Let $a(n)$ be the largest possible number of sources in an extensional
acyclic digraph on $n\ge1$ vertices. OEIS A182220 identifies this quantity
and records, among other equivalent conjectural expressions,
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:eq:oeis-conjecture}
 a(n)=n-1-\lfloor\log_2(n-1)\rfloor\qquad(n\ge3).
\end{equation}
The entry attributes this formula to Antti Karttunen, August 17, 2013
\cite{oeis220}. For $n\ge2$, the right side equals
$n-\lceil\log_2n\rceil$; the latter also gives $a(1)=1$.
The problem is not an issue of numerical fitting. We prove both the
universal obstruction and an explicit construction for every admissible
source count.

The more substantial question is: \emph{how many graphs lie on, or a
fixed distance below, this extremal boundary, and how do those counts
behave?} Write $u(n,k)$ for the number of isomorphism classes with $n$
vertices and $k$ sources. The labeled count is $n!u(n,k)$; the factor
$n!$ will be justified, not assumed. Define
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:eq:boundary-def}
 q=q(n)=\lceil\log_2n\rceil,
 \qquad b_n^{(d)}=u(n,n-q-d),\qquad d\in\N_0,
\end{equation}
with value zero if the requested number of sources is not positive.
For each fixed $d$, this convention affects only finitely many terms.

\subsection{What was already known}
Peddicord studied the numbers $u_m$ of finite transitive, or full, sets
\cite{peddicord,oeis1192}. Policriti and Tomescu developed source-sensitive
enumeration of extensional acyclic digraphs \cite{policriti}.
Tomescu's thesis explicitly gives the source bound
$k\le n-\lceil\log_2n\rceil$ on printed page 29, along with source-counting
recurrences and rigidity results \cite{tomescu}. The source sieve used
below is also encoded in Johnston's 2012 Maple program in A182162
\cite{oeis162}. Wagner studied asymptotic enumeration and distributions
in the unrestricted graph class \cite{wagner}.

Accordingly, neither the upper bound, the correspondence with full sets,
rigidity, nor the general source sieve is claimed as new. An OEIS
conjectural label does not establish that a statement is an unsolved
research problem in the literature. Our proof supplies a direct resolution
of the displayed formula; its upper-bound half has explicit prior art.

The exact boundary classification, the finite-defect specialization,
the uniform entropy profile, and the non-P-recursiveness theorem form
the development of this article. Some exact identities are short
consequences of the classical source sieve. We make no assertion of
historical priority for those consequences or for the probabilistic
refinements. The meaningful claim is that complete proofs are supplied
here, not that an exhaustive literature search has certified novelty.

\section{Definitions and finite-set foundations}

\subsection{Orientation conventions}
A digraph in this article is finite, loopless, and has no parallel arcs.
An arc $v\to w$ means that $w$ is an out-neighbor of $v$.
A source has indegree zero; a sink has outdegree zero.
A digraph is \emph{extensional} when the map
\[
 v\longmapsto N^+(v)=\{w:v\to w\}
\]
is injective. It is acyclic when it contains no directed cycle.
We abbreviate ``extensional acyclic digraph'' to EAD.
Every nonempty EAD has exactly one sink: a DAG has a sink, and two sinks
would have the same empty out-neighborhood. No transitive closure or
transitive reduction of the arc relation is implicit in this definition.

Let $u_m$ be the number of EAD isomorphism classes with $m$ vertices,
including $u_0=1$. We shall identify it with A001192. We use
$\binom{M}{r}=0$ if $r<0$ or $r>M$ for nonnegative integer $M$.
All logarithms without a subscript are natural.

\subsection{Collapse, transitivity, and rigidity}
\begin{lemma}[Finite collapse]\label{sbd:lem:collapse}
For every EAD $D$, the recursion
\[
 \phi(v)=\{\phi(w):w\in N^+(v)\}
\]
defines an injective map into the hereditarily finite sets. Its image
$F=\{\phi(v):v\in V(D)\}$ is a finite transitive set, and $D$ is
isomorphic to the membership digraph on $F$, with arcs $x\to y$ iff
$y\in x$. Conversely, every finite transitive set yields an EAD this way.
This gives a bijection between isomorphism classes of EADs and finite
transitive sets.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Order the vertices so that every out-neighbor precedes its parent.
The recursion is then finite and well defined. To prove injectivity,
induct on the maximum height of two vertices, where height is the
maximum length of a directed path from the vertex. Equal collapsed sets
at height at most $h$ have the same collapsed members. By the induction
hypothesis the corresponding vertices of smaller height are identical.
Thus their out-neighborhoods coincide, and extensionality identifies
the two vertices. The base case is the unique sink.

Every member of $\phi(v)$ is another element of the image, so $F$ is
transitive: $\bigcup F\subseteq F$. Injectivity shows that membership
reproduces exactly the arcs. Conversely, on a finite transitive set,
Foundation precludes membership cycles, and Extensionality says that
distinct elements have distinct member sets. These two constructions
are inverse, including at the empty set.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Rigidity]\label{sbd:cor:rigid}
Every EAD has trivial automorphism group. Consequently the number of
labeled EADs with $n$ vertices and $k$ sources is $n!u(n,k)$, and the total
number is $n!u_n$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
An automorphism preserves the recursive collapsed value of every vertex.
The collapse is injective, so the automorphism fixes every vertex.
There are therefore exactly $n!$ different labelings of each isomorphism
class, and source count is preserved by relabeling.
\end{proof}
This explains the normalization relating A182162 to $u(n,k)$ and
A182161 to A001192 \cite{oeis162,oeis161,oeis1192}.

\begin{lemma}[A sufficient bound on the small-core count]\label{sbd:lem:ubound}
For every $m\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:eq:smallcore-bound}
 1\le u_m\le 2^{\binom m2}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The finite ordinal $m=\{0,1,\ldots,m-1\}$ is a transitive set of size $m$,
so at least one class exists. Every DAG has a vertex ordering in which
all arcs point to earlier vertices. For a fixed such ordering there are
$2^{\binom m2}$ possible arc sets. Their isomorphism classes cover all
DAG isomorphism classes, and hence all EAD classes. Injectivity of this
covering is not needed for the upper bound.
\end{proof}

\section{The exact source maximum and all feasible source counts}

\begin{theorem}[Complete source support]\label{sbd:thm:support}
For $n\ge1$ and $k\ge1$, an EAD on $n$ vertices with exactly $k$ sources
exists if and only if
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:eq:feasible}
 k\le n,\qquad n\le 2^{n-k}.
\end{equation}
In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:eq:max}
 \boxed{\ a(n)=n-\lceil\log_2n\rceil\ }.
\end{equation}
Every integer from $1$ through $a(n)$ occurs, so A182162 has no holes
in the positive support of any row.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Suppose $D$ has $k$ sources and let $C$ be its set of nonsources,
$m=|C|=n-k$. No arc enters a source. Thus every out-neighborhood is a
subset of $C$. The injective map $v\mapsto N^+(v)$ takes $n$ vertices
into a set of size $2^m$, proving $n\le2^m$.

For sufficiency, first take $m=0$. The conditions force $n=k=1$,
and the one-vertex edgeless digraph works. Suppose $m\ge1$.
Take a directed path on $m$ core vertices, with arcs from each vertex
to the preceding one. Its $m$ out-neighborhoods are distinct. The full
set $C$ is not among them, since it would contain the vertex whose
neighborhood it was, creating a loop. There are $2^m-m$ unused subsets
of $C$, at least $k$ by the assumed inequality. Choose $k$ distinct
unused subsets, one of them equal to $C$, and add a new vertex for each
chosen subset, with exactly that subset as its out-neighborhood.

There are no arcs into the new vertices, so they are sources. All core
vertices receive an arc from the new vertex whose neighborhood is $C$,
so none is a source. Acyclicity and extensionality are immediate from
the construction. Hence the graph has exactly $k$ sources.

Finally, $n\le2^{n-k}$ is equivalent to $k\le n-q(n)$.
For $n\ge1$, this upper endpoint is at least one. This proves both the
maximum and the entire support assertion.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Elementary consequences for A182220}
For $n\ge2$, $\lceil\log_2n\rceil=1+\lfloor\log_2(n-1)\rfloor$,
which proves \eqref{sbd:eq:oeis-conjecture}. Also,
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:eq:step}
 a(n+1)-a(n)=1-\boldsymbol 1_{\{n\text{ is a power of }2\}},
 \qquad a(1)=1.
\end{equation}
This accounts for the repeated values. In particular the initial value
$1$ occurs three times, because both $1$ and $2$ are powers of two.
For an interpretation by counting nonpowers of two, zero must be included:
\[
 a(n)=\#\{0\le j\le n-1:j\text{ is not a positive power of }2\},
\]
where ``a power of $2$'' includes $2^0=1$. Omitting zero would create an
off-by-one error. We do not assert every ancillary OEIS cross-reference
without checking its conventions; the explicit formulas above remove
that ambiguity.

\subsection{A lacunary generating function for the maximum}
The next consequence is included to separate the simple maximum sequence
from the more intricate sequence counting its extremizers.

\begin{proposition}\label{sbd:prop:maxgf}
For $|z|<1$,
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:eq:maxgf}
 \sum_{n\ge1}a(n)z^n
 =\frac{z}{(1-z)^2}-\frac{z}{1-z}\sum_{j\ge0}z^{2^j}.
\end{equation}
The unit circle is a natural boundary for this generating function.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Multiply the left side by $1-z$ and apply \eqref{sbd:eq:step} to obtain the
identity. Let $L(z)=\sum_{j\ge0}z^{2^j}$ and let $\zeta$ be a root of unity
of order $2^s$. For all $j\ge s$, $\zeta^{2^j}=1$; hence
\[
 L(r\zeta)=O(1)+\sum_{j\ge s}r^{2^j},\qquad r\uparrow1,
\]
and its real part tends to infinity. At every such $\zeta\ne1$, the
rational factor $z/(1-z)$ tends to a nonzero finite value, so the right
side of \eqref{sbd:eq:maxgf} cannot extend holomorphically through $\zeta$.
These roots are dense on the circle. Analytic continuation across any
point would give a neighborhood containing one of them, a contradiction.
\end{proof}
The proof uses only a dense set of explicit singularities; no general
lacunary-series theorem is needed.

\section{Boolean-core classification and exact extremizer counts}

\subsection{The core is intrinsic}
Let $F$ be the collapsed transitive set of a graph. Its nonsources are
exactly the elements which occur as members of other elements of $F$.
Thus the nonsource core is
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:eq:union-core}
 C=\bigcup F.
\end{equation}
It is itself transitive. Indeed, $x\in y\in C$ means that $y\in z$ for
some $z\in F$; then $y\in F$, and therefore $x\in\bigcup F=C$.
If $L=F\setminus C$ is the set of sources, then
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:eq:coreform}
 F=C\cup L,\qquad L\subseteq\PP(C)\setminus C.
\end{equation}
The last exclusion uses transitivity of $C$, so that $C\subseteq\PP(C)$.
Conversely, \eqref{sbd:eq:coreform} always defines a transitive set, but its
nonsource core equals $C$ only if $\bigcup(C\cup L)=C$.
That covering condition is the sole extra constraint.

\begin{lemma}[Automatic covering above half density]\label{sbd:lem:auto}
Let $C$ be any transitive set of size $m\ge1$. If
\[
 L\subseteq\PP(C)\setminus C,\quad |L|=k,\quad m+k>2^{m-1},
\]
then $\bigcup(C\cup L)=C$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
If some $c\in C$ were outside the union, every member of the family
$C\cup L$ would avoid $c$. All $m+k$ distinct subsets would then lie in
$\PP(C\setminus\{c\})$, which contains only $2^{m-1}$ subsets.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Complete classification of extremizers]\label{sbd:thm:classify}
Let $n\ge1$, $q=\lceil\log_2n\rceil$, and $k=n-q$.
The EAD isomorphism classes attaining $a(n)$ sources are in bijection
with pairs
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:eq:extremal-pairs}
 C\in\U_q,\qquad L\in\binom{\PP(C)\setminus C}{k},
\end{equation}
where $\U_q$ is the set of transitive sets of size $q$.
Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:eq:extremal-count}
 \boxed{\ b_n^{(0)}=u_q\binom{2^q-q}{n-q},\qquad
 E(n,a(n))=n!u_q\binom{2^q-q}{n-q}\ }.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Every extremizer has $q$ nonsources by Theorem~\ref{sbd:thm:support}, and
\eqref{sbd:eq:union-core}--\eqref{sbd:eq:coreform} recover its unique pair $(C,L)$.
Conversely, if $q\ge1$, then $n>2^{q-1}$, so Lemma~\ref{sbd:lem:auto}
shows that every pair in \eqref{sbd:eq:extremal-pairs} has precisely $C$ as
its core and precisely $L$ as its source set. No two different pairs give
the same transitive set, since the core is its union. Each core offers
exactly $2^q-q$ available subsets. Rigidity supplies the labeled factor.
For $n=1$, $q=0$, and the single pair is
$C=\varnothing$, $L=\{\varnothing\}$, giving the same formula.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Complete layers and sparse deletions]\label{sbd:cor:power}
For $m\ge1$ and $0\le r<2^{m-1}$,
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:eq:deletions}
 b_{2^m-r}^{(0)}=u_m\binom{2^m-m}{r}.
\end{equation}
In particular $b_{2^m}^{(0)}=u_m$, and every extremizer on $2^m$ vertices
is the membership graph of $\PP(C)$ for a unique transitive $m$-element
set $C$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Complement the chosen source family in $\PP(C)\setminus C$.
The omitted family has size $r$, and at $r=0$ no subsets are omitted.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}
For $n=8$ we have $q=3$, $u_3=2$, and $b_8^{(0)}=2$.
For $n=9$ the core size becomes four, and
\[
 b_9^{(0)}=u_4\binom{12}{5}=9\cdot792=7128.
\]
The jump is not a numerical accident: the minimum allowed core size has
increased, making a much larger Boolean layer available. Later sections
quantify this phenomenon at every power of two.
\end{example}

\section{The marked-source sieve and finite-defect diagonals}

\subsection{A classical identity, proved in the present normalization}
\begin{proposition}[Marked sources]\label{sbd:prop:marked}
For $0\le t\le n$,
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:eq:marked}
 \sum_{s=t}^n\binom st u(n,s)
 =u_{n-t}\binom{2^{n-t}-(n-t)}{t}.
\end{equation}
For $n>0$ we set $u(n,0)=0$; for $n=0$ we set $u(0,0)=1$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The left side counts an EAD isomorphism class together with a chosen
$t$-element subset of its sources. Rigidity ensures that different
chosen subsets cannot be identified by a nontrivial automorphism.
Delete the marked sources. Out-neighborhoods of the remaining vertices
are unchanged, since no arc entered a source, so the remaining graph is
an EAD on $n-t$ vertices.

Conversely, choose any such graph and choose $t$ distinct subsets of
its vertex set that are not already its out-neighborhoods. There are
$2^{n-t}-(n-t)$ available subsets. Adjoin one marked source for each
chosen subset. Existing sources are allowed to remain sources, which is
why no covering constraint occurs in this construction. Deleting the
marked sources reverses it. This proves the identity.
\end{proof}

The identity is the marked-source version of the classical enumeration
underlying the OEIS source sieve \cite{oeis162}. We use it as a proved
starting point rather than a new counting principle.

\begin{corollary}[Binomial inversion]\label{sbd:cor:sieve}
For $1\le k\le n$,
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:eq:sieve}
 u(n,k)=\sum_{t=k}^n(-1)^{t-k}\binom tk
 u_{n-t}\binom{2^{n-t}-(n-t)}{t}.
\end{equation}
Also
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:eq:total-rec}
 u_0=1,\qquad
 u_n=\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{n-m-1}
 \binom{2^m-m}{n-m}u_m\quad(n\ge1).
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
If $P_n(z)=\sum_su(n,s)z^s$, then \eqref{sbd:eq:marked} is equivalent to
\[
 P_n(z)=\sum_{t=0}^n u_{n-t}
 \binom{2^{n-t}-(n-t)}{t}(z-1)^t.
\]
Extract $z^k$ to get \eqref{sbd:eq:sieve}. For $n>0$, evaluate at $z=0$,
use $P_n(0)=0$, and isolate the term $t=0$ to get \eqref{sbd:eq:total-rec}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Only finitely many correction terms survive}
\begin{theorem}[Exact finite-defect formula]\label{sbd:thm:defect}
Fix $n\ge1$ and $d\ge0$ with $k=n-q-d\ge1$, and put $m=q+d$.
Then
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:eq:defect}
 \boxed{\ b_n^{(d)}=
 \sum_{j=0}^{d}(-1)^j\binom{k+j}{j}
 u_{m-j}\binom{2^{m-j}-(m-j)}{k+j}\ }.
\end{equation}
Thus an entry at distance $d$ below the source boundary requires exactly
$d+1$ displayed terms, using total counts only at the logarithmic indices
$q,q+1,\ldots,q+d$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Substitute $t=k+j$ in \eqref{sbd:eq:sieve}. A potentially nonzero term must
satisfy
\[
 k+j\le2^{m-j}-(m-j),\quad\text{equivalently}\quad n\le2^{m-j}.
\]
Since $q=\lceil\log_2n\rceil$, this requires $m-j\ge q$, or $j\le d$.
The surviving terms are exactly those displayed. All have a positive
binomial coefficient in the stated range, though signs alternate.
\end{proof}

For $d=0$, \eqref{sbd:eq:defect} recovers the exact extremizer count, so
that count is also an immediate boundary specialization of the known
sieve. At distance one it becomes
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:eq:d1}
 b_n^{(1)}=u_{q+1}\binom{2^{q+1}-q-1}{n-q-1}
 -(n-q)b_n^{(0)}.
\end{equation}
At distance two, writing $k=n-q-2$,
\begin{align}\label{sbd:eq:d2}
 b_n^{(2)}={}&u_{q+2}\binom{2^{q+2}-q-2}{k}
 -(k+1)u_{q+1}\binom{2^{q+1}-q-1}{k+1}\notag\\
 &+\binom{k+2}{2}u_q\binom{2^q-q}{k+2}.
\end{align}
These identities should not be mistaken for asymptotic expansions:
they are exact integer formulas, even when early terms nearly cancel.

\section{Uniform covering estimates and random cores}

\subsection{An estimate valid away from the extremal boundary as well}
\begin{theorem}[Uniform core-covering bound]\label{sbd:thm:cover}
Let $m\ge0$, $k\ge1$, $n=m+k$, and $k\le M=2^m-m$.
Set $A=u_m\binom Mk$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:eq:cover}
 \boxed{\quad 0\le A-u(n,k)\le m\,2^{-k}A.\quad}
\end{equation}
For $m\ge1$, a sharper upper bound on the relative deficit is
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:eq:cover-sharp}
 \frac{A-u(n,k)}{A}
 \le\frac{\binom{2^{m-1}-m}{k}}{\binom{2^m-m}{k}}
 \frac{\sum_s s\,u(m,s)}{u_m}.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For a fixed transitive core $C$ of size $m$, choose a uniform $k$-subset
$L$ of $\PP(C)\setminus C$. The resulting graph is counted by $u(n,k)$
exactly when $\bigcup(C\cup L)=C$.
Every element already in $\bigcup C$ is covered. If
$c\in C\setminus\bigcup C$, then none of the $m$ members of $C$ contains
$c$. Of the $2^{m-1}$ subsets avoiding $c$, precisely $m$ are therefore
forbidden core subsets. The probability that no chosen subset contains
$c$ is
\[
 \frac{\binom{2^{m-1}-m}{k}}{\binom{2^m-m}{k}}\le2^{-k}.
\]
If the numerator vanishes the assertion is immediate. Otherwise compare
successive factors of the two falling factorials; each ratio is at most
$1/2$. Apply the union bound over the sources of $C$, whose number is
at most $m$, and sum over all $u_m$ cores. The sharper average-source
version follows before replacing the number of sources by $m$.
For $m=0$, the only admissible choice is $k=1$, and equality holds.
\end{proof}

The average core-source count in \eqref{sbd:eq:cover-sharp} is itself exact:
Proposition~\ref{sbd:prop:marked}, with $t=1$, gives
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:eq:mean-sources}
 \frac{\sum_s s\,u(m,s)}{u_m}
 =\frac{(2^{m-1}-m+1)u_{m-1}}{u_m}\qquad(m\ge1).
\end{equation}
In applications where $k-\log_2m\to+\infty$, the simpler bound already
proves
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:eq:general-equivalent}
 u(m+k,k)=u_m\binom{2^m-m}{k}
 \left(1+O(m2^{-k})\right).
\end{equation}
The implicit error here is one-sided: the true value never exceeds the
main expression.

\subsection{Uniform sampling and the core law}
There is an exact rejection sampler for a uniformly random isomorphism
class with $n=m+k$ vertices and $k$ sources. First draw a uniform core
from $\U_m$, then a uniform $k$-subset of its $M$ available neighborhoods.
Keep the pair if it covers the core; otherwise repeat. Before rejection,
every pair has exactly the same probability, and every accepted graph
corresponds to one pair. Thus the accepted graph is exactly uniform.
If $m2^{-k}<1$, the expected number of trials is at most
$(1-m2^{-k})^{-1}$. This is conditional on having a procedure for uniformly
sampling the much smaller core; it is not a claim of a complete
polynomial-time sampler for arbitrary $m$.

At the extremal boundary, rejection is never necessary, and the core
of a uniform extremizer is \emph{exactly} uniform on $\U_q$.
More generally, write $\delta$ for the actual rejection probability.
Conditioning a finite probability distribution on an event of probability
$1-\delta$ changes it in total variation by exactly $\delta$.
Taking the core marginal cannot increase total variation. Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:eq:core-tv}
 \TV\bigl(\text{core law of a uniform }(n,k)\text{ EAD},
               \operatorname{Unif}(\U_m)\bigr)
 \le m2^{-k}.
\end{equation}
This bound is uniform over all core structures. It explains why small-core
statistics can be transferred to the high-source part of the triangle.

\section{The dyadic entropy law}\label{sbd:sec:entropy}

\subsection{Uniform logarithmic asymptotics}
Define the binary entropy, in natural units, by
\[
 H(p)=-p\log p-(1-p)\log(1-p),\qquad H(0)=H(1)=0.
\]
For a fixed defect $d$, set
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:eq:phidef}
 \rho_n=\frac{n}{2^{q(n)}}\in(1/2,1],\qquad
 \Phi_d(\rho)=\frac{2^d}{\rho}H\!\left(\frac{\rho}{2^d}\right).
\end{equation}
The strict lower endpoint for $\rho_n$ does not preclude subsequences
converging to $1/2$.

\begin{theorem}[Entropy profile]\label{sbd:thm:entropy}
For every fixed $d\ge0$, as $n\to\infty$,
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:eq:entropy}
 \boxed{\quad \log b_n^{(d)}
       =n\Phi_d(\rho_n)+O_d((\log n)^2).\quad}
\end{equation}
The bound is uniform over the entire dyadic block, including its
endpoints. No asymptotic formula for $u_m$ is needed.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $m=q+d$, $T=2^m$, and $k=n-m$. These parameters satisfy
$m=O_d(\log n)$, $n\le T<2^{d+1}n$, and $k=n-O_d(\log n)$.
Theorem~\ref{sbd:thm:cover} gives
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:eq:loglead}
 \log b_n^{(d)}=\log u_m+\log\binom{T-m}{n-m}
                  +O_d(m2^{-k}).
\end{equation}
The small-core logarithm is $O_d((\log n)^2)$ by
Lemma~\ref{sbd:lem:ubound}. The exact identity
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:eq:binratio}
 \binom{T-m}{n-m}=\binom Tn\frac{\fall nm}{\fall Tm}
\end{equation}
shows that the two binomial logarithms differ by $O_d(m)$:
every factor $(n-i)/(T-i)$ is bounded below by a positive constant
depending only on $d$, for all sufficiently large $n$.

For completeness, the elementary entropy bounds are
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:eq:entropy-binom}
 \frac{e^{T H(n/T)}}{T+1}\le\binom Tn\le e^{T H(n/T)}.
\end{equation}
For $0<n<T$, consider a $\Bin(T,n/T)$ random variable. Its probability
at $n$ is a maximum probability, hence is at least $1/(T+1)$ and at most
one. Rearranging gives \eqref{sbd:eq:entropy-binom}; the endpoint cases are
immediate. It follows that $\log\binom Tn=T H(n/T)+O(\log T)$.
Finally $T H(n/T)=n\Phi_d(\rho_n)$. Combining these estimates proves
\eqref{sbd:eq:entropy}, with no loss of uniformity as $n/T\to1$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Every subsequential exponential rate}
Set
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:eq:lambdadef}
 \lambda_d=2^d H(2^{-d}),\qquad d\ge0.
\end{equation}
In particular $\lambda_0=0$, $\lambda_1=\log4$, and
$e^{\lambda_2}=256/27$.

\begin{theorem}[Complete growth-rate interval]\label{sbd:thm:rates}
For each fixed $d\ge0$, the set of subsequential limits of
$(b_n^{(d)})^{1/n}$ is exactly
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:eq:rates}
 [e^{\lambda_d},e^{\lambda_{d+1}}].
\end{equation}
Thus
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:eq:extreme-rates}
 \liminf_{n\to\infty}(b_n^{(0)})^{1/n}=1,
 \qquad
 \limsup_{n\to\infty}(b_n^{(0)})^{1/n}=4.
\end{equation}
No fixed-defect boundary sequence has a single exponential growth
constant.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Write $p=\rho/2^d$. Then $\Phi_d(\rho)=H(p)/p$.
For $0<p<1$,
\[
 \frac{d}{dp}\frac{H(p)}p
 =\frac{pH'(p)-H(p)}{p^2}
 =\frac{\log(1-p)}{p^2}<0.
\]
Hence $\Phi_d$ is strictly decreasing, with values $\lambda_{d+1}$ at
$\rho=1/2$ and $\lambda_d$ at $\rho=1$.
Every $\rho\in(1/2,1)$ is the limit of phases obtained by rounding
$\rho2^q$ to integers. Powers $n=2^q$ realize phase one, while
$n=2^{q-1}+1$ tends to phase $1/2$. The entropy theorem gives the entire
claimed interval. Conversely, compactness of $[1/2,1]$ and continuity of
$\Phi_d$ exclude any other subsequential limits.
\end{proof}

In particular the first three intervals, for $d=0,1,2$, are
\[
 [1,4],\qquad[4,256/27],\qquad
 [256/27,\,2^{24}/7^7].
\]
The upper endpoint for one defect equals the lower endpoint for the next.
This expresses the fact that crossing a dyadic boundary adds one core
vertex, just as increasing the allowed source deficit does.

\section{Non-P-recursiveness of every fixed-defect boundary}

A sequence $(c_n)$ is P-recursive if, for all sufficiently large $n$,
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:eq:prec}
 \sum_{j=0}^{h}p_j(n)c_{n+j}=0
\end{equation}
for polynomials $p_j$, not all zero. Allowing a finite exceptional initial
segment makes the conclusion below stronger, not weaker.
A formal power series is D-finite if it satisfies a nonzero homogeneous
linear differential equation with polynomial coefficients.

\begin{lemma}[An exponential-jump obstruction]\label{sbd:lem:jump}
Suppose a nonnegative sequence $(c_n)$ has integers $N_r\to\infty$ and
constants $\alpha<\beta$ such that, for every fixed $j\ge0$,
\[
 c_{N_r-j}=\exp(\alpha N_r+o(N_r)),\qquad
 c_{N_r+1}=\exp(\beta N_r+o(N_r)).
\]
Then $(c_n)$ is not P-recursive.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Suppose \eqref{sbd:eq:prec} holds, and choose $h$ as the largest index with
$p_h\ne0$. If $h=0$, positivity along $(N_r)$ contradicts the recurrence.
Otherwise apply it at $n=N_r+1-h$. For sufficiently large $r$, $p_h(n)$
is nonzero, and ratios of fixed polynomials give a bound
\[
 c_{N_r+1}\le C N_r^A\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}c_{N_r+1-h+j}
             =\exp(\alpha N_r+o(N_r))
\]
for constants $C,A$. This contradicts $\beta>\alpha$.
Absolute values of the coefficients were used, so cancellation cannot
invalidate the estimate. The argument also applies to complex
polynomial coefficients.
\end{proof}

\begin{writenote}[Added 3 October 2026, batch~85]
The collection report
\nolinkurl{generating-functions-and-asymptotics/oeis-sequence-asymptotics/a003407-dyadic-scaling-rigidity}
proves, in its Part~II, an obstruction that runs the other way. Its
Corollary \nolinkurl{dsr:nh:cor:jumps} (a consequence of its local-growth
rigidity theorem \nolinkurl{dsr:nh:thm:rigidity}, which rests on a G-function
input) says that an eventually positive, exponentially bounded, integer
P-recursive sequence without an $n$th-root limit must have
$u_{n+1}/u_n>e^{\varepsilon n}$ for infinitely many $n$: upward
exponential jumps are \emph{necessary} for such oscillation, and their
absence rules P-recursiveness out. Lemma~\ref{sbd:lem:jump} is a
\emph{sufficient} condition in the opposite situation: a jump preceded by a
plateau of every fixed length at the lower rate rules P-recursiveness out.
It needs neither integrality nor an exponential bound, and its proof is
elementary. The two criteria are complementary, not competing. The
boundary sequences $b^{(d)}$ do have the upward jumps (from
$e^{\lambda_dN_r}$ to $e^{\lambda_{d+1}N_r}$ at $N_r=2^r$), so the a003407
criterion cannot exclude their P-recursiveness, and
Lemma~\ref{sbd:lem:jump} is the one that applies. Conversely, the
3AP-free permutation counts of a003407 have only subexponential forward
increases, so Lemma~\ref{sbd:lem:jump} does not apply to them. The
plateau hypothesis ``for every fixed $j\ge0$'' is essential: a003407's
example $3^n+(-3)^n+2^n$, which satisfies a constant-coefficient
recurrence, jumps from rate $\log2$ at odd indices to $\log3$ at even
ones, but its two-term window never stays at the lower rate.
\end{writenote}

\begin{theorem}[Non-holonomicity]\label{sbd:thm:nonholonomic}
For every fixed $d\ge0$, neither $(b_n^{(d)})$ nor $(n!b_n^{(d)})$ is
P-recursive. The ordinary generating function
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:eq:Bgf}
 B_d(z)=\sum_{n\ge1}b_n^{(d)}z^n
\end{equation}
is not D-finite. Its radius of convergence is
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:eq:radius}
 R_d=e^{-\lambda_{d+1}};
 \qquad R_0=\frac14.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Take $N_r=2^r$. For every fixed $j\ge0$, the index $N_r-j$ remains in
the dyadic block with $q=r$ when $r$ is large, and its phase tends to
one. By Theorem~\ref{sbd:thm:entropy},
\[
 \log b_{N_r-j}^{(d)}=\lambda_d N_r+o(N_r).
\]
At $N_r+1$, $q=r+1$ and the phase tends to $1/2$, giving
\[
 \log b_{N_r+1}^{(d)}=\lambda_{d+1}N_r+o(N_r).
\]
The strict decrease proved in Theorem~\ref{sbd:thm:rates} yields
$\lambda_{d+1}>\lambda_d$. Lemma~\ref{sbd:lem:jump} applies.

If $n!b_n^{(d)}$ satisfied a polynomial recurrence, division by $n!$
would give one for $b_n^{(d)}$: each factor $(n+j)!/n!$ is a polynomial
in $n$. Thus the labeled sequence is also non-P-recursive.

For completeness, extracting coefficients from a nonzero polynomial
linear differential equation for $B_d$ yields a nonzero finite-shift
polynomial recurrence for its coefficients. Indeed, coefficients of
$z^i(d/dz)^jB_d$ are shifted coefficients multiplied by falling
factorials in the index; a common shift removes any negative indices.
This contradicts what was just proved. Finally, the Cauchy--Hadamard
formula and Theorem~\ref{sbd:thm:rates} give \eqref{sbd:eq:radius}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[What the theorem does not say]
Non-D-finiteness does not by itself imply that the circle $|z|=R_d$ is a
natural boundary, nor does it rule out useful nonlinear functional
equations. We prove a natural boundary for the maximum sequence in
Proposition~\ref{sbd:prop:maxgf}, but do not make that stronger claim for
$B_d$. The labeled ordinary generating series has radius zero; the
series $B_d$ is also the exponential generating series of the labeled
boundary counts.
\end{remark}

\begin{writenote}[Added 5 October 2026, batch~98]
The stronger claim is now proved, in Part~\ref{sbd:part:two}: for every
fixed $d\ge0$ the circle $|z|=R_d$ is a natural boundary of $B_d$
(Theorem~\ref{sbd:xtm:thm:main-boundary}, proved independently by both
Part~II manuscripts), and $B_d$ is unbounded, faster than every power of
$1/t$, on the radius to every point $R_d\zeta$ with $\zeta$ a root of unity
of power-of-two order. The natural boundary gives a second route to the
non-D-finiteness of Theorem~\ref{sbd:thm:nonholonomic} and adds
non-algebraicity (Corollary~\ref{sbd:xtm:cor:nonholonomic}). The remark's
other caveat stands: nonlinear functional or differential equations are not
excluded, and Part~\ref{sbd:part:two} lists this as an open question
(Section~\ref{sbd:xtm:sec:future}).
\end{writenote}

\section{An all-orders expansion inside a dyadic block}\label{sbd:sec:expansion}

The entropy law is uniform even at complete layers. More detailed
prefactors require staying away from the degeneracy $p=1$.
This section retains $u_m$ exactly; it does not purport to replace the
classical, separate asymptotic analysis of the total EAD count.

\begin{theorem}[Explicit expansion with exact core amplitude]\label{sbd:thm:expansion}
Fix $d\ge0$ and $\varepsilon>0$. Let
\[
 m=q+d,\quad T=2^m,\quad p=n/T,\quad k=n-m,
 \qquad \varepsilon\le p\le1-\varepsilon.
\]
Define $S_\ell(m)=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}i^\ell$ and let $B_{2j}$ denote the
Bernoulli numbers with $B_2=1/6$. For fixed integers $L\ge0$, $J\ge1$,
\begin{align}\label{sbd:eq:allorders}
 \log b_n^{(d)}={}&\log u_m+T H(p)
 -\tfrac12\log\bigl(2\pi T p(1-p)\bigr)+m\log p\notag\\
 &-\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}
       \frac{p^{-\ell}-1}{\ell T^\ell}S_\ell(m)\notag\\
 &+\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{B_{2j}}{2j(2j-1)T^{2j-1}}
       \bigl[1-p^{1-2j}-(1-p)^{1-2j}\bigr]\notag\\
 &+O_{d,\varepsilon,L,J}\left(
       \frac{m^{L+2}}{T^{L+1}}+T^{-(2J+1)}+m2^{-k}\right).
\end{align}
The first sum is empty when $L=0$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Start with \eqref{sbd:eq:loglead} and \eqref{sbd:eq:binratio}.
The logarithm of the falling-factorial ratio is
\[
 m\log p+\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}
 \left[\log\left(1-\frac{i}{pT}\right)
             -\log\left(1-\frac{i}{T}\right)\right].
\]
Since $m=O_d(\log T)$ and $p\ge\varepsilon$, expand both logarithms
through degree $L$. The coefficient of $T^{-\ell}$ is exactly the
first sum in \eqref{sbd:eq:allorders}. Summing the elementary Taylor
remainders gives $O(m^{L+2}/T^{L+1})$.

Apply the Stirling expansion for $\log(T!)$, $\log((pT)!)$, and
$\log(((1-p)T)!)$. Subtraction produces $T H(p)$, the displayed
Gaussian prefactor, and the Bernoulli sum. Its remainder after $J$
terms is $O_{\varepsilon,J}(T^{-(2J+1)})$. This standard expansion follows,
for example, by Euler--Maclaurin summation of $\log x$; its remainder
has the asserted bound because $pT$ and $(1-p)T$ are both at least
$\varepsilon T$. Finally include the covering error from
\eqref{sbd:eq:loglead}.
\end{proof}

In particular,
\begin{align}\label{sbd:eq:first-prefactor}
 b_n^{(d)}={}&
 \frac{u_m p^m e^{T H(p)}}{\sqrt{2\pi T p(1-p)}}
 \left[1+\frac{C_1(m,p)}T
             +O_{d,\varepsilon}\left(\frac{m^4}{T^2}\right)\right],\\
 C_1(m,p)={}&-\frac{1-p}{2p}m(m-1)
       +\frac{1-p^{-1}-(1-p)^{-1}}{12}.\notag
\end{align}
Taking larger $L$ and $J$ supplies arbitrarily many logarithmic
corrections. Exponentiating gives a multiplicative expansion whose
coefficients are explicit polynomials in $m$, with rational functions
of $p$ as coefficients.

For $d\ge1$, the actual phases have
$p\in(2^{-d-1},2^{-d}]$, so a suitable fixed $\varepsilon$ covers the
entire dyadic block. For $d=0$, the complete-layer edge $p\to1$ must
instead be treated by the exact deletion formula
\eqref{sbd:eq:deletions}. This is a genuine boundary change of asymptotic
regime, not an error to be removed by formally substituting $p=1$ in
the Gaussian prefactor.

\subsection{The fixed-deletion regime}
For fixed $r\ge0$ and $N=2^m$, Corollary~\ref{sbd:cor:power} gives
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:eq:fixedr}
 b_{N-r}^{(0)}
 =u_m\frac{N^r}{r!}
 \left[1-\frac{rm+r(r-1)/2}{N}
          +O_r\left(\frac{m^2}{N^2}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
This follows directly by expanding
$\binom{N-m}{r}=N^r/r!\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}(1-(m+i)/N)$.
More generally, for $r=o(N)$ with $m+r<N$, an exact logarithmic series is
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:eq:deletion-series}
 \log\frac{r!\,b_{N-r}^{(0)}}{u_mN^r}
 =-\sum_{\ell\ge1}\frac{1}{\ell N^\ell}
       \sum_{i=0}^{r-1}(m+i)^\ell.
\end{equation}
It converges because $(m+r-1)/N<1$, and truncation is controlled by the
geometric tail of the logarithm series. This supplies a separate exact
route toward a matched sparse-to-bulk expansion.

\section{Arc counts and a binomial law near complete layers}

\subsection{A core-dependent polynomial with a simple complement form}
Let $e(F)$ be the number of arcs in the membership graph of $F$.
Since arcs are membership incidences,
\[
 e(F)=\sum_{S\in F}|S|.
\]
For $n=2^m-r$ with $0\le r<2^{m-1}$, an extremizer is
$F=\PP(C)\setminus R$, where $C\in\U_m$ and
$R\subseteq\PP(C)\setminus C$ has size $r$. Hence
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:eq:arc-deficit}
 e(F)=m2^{m-1}-Z,\qquad Z=\sum_{S\in R}|S|.
\end{equation}
In particular, \emph{all} extremizers at $n=2^m$ have exactly
$m2^{m-1}$ arcs, regardless of the core graph.

For a fixed core $C$, the full deficit-counting polynomial is
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:eq:arc-poly}
 [z^r]\prod_{S\in\PP(C)\setminus C}(1+z w^{|S|}).
\end{equation}
Summing this over $C\in\U_m$ gives the exact unlabeled joint count by
size and arc deficit. Equivalently, the product is the formal quotient
\[
 \frac{\displaystyle\prod_{j=0}^{m}(1+z w^j)^{\binom mj}}
      {\displaystyle\prod_{x\in C}(1+z w^{|x|})}.
\]
The denominator divides the numerator as a polynomial product; it is
not an analytic singularity assertion.

\begin{theorem}[Total-variation binomial approximation]\label{sbd:thm:binomial}
Let $F$ be uniform among extremal EAD isomorphism classes on $n=2^m-r$
vertices, where $m\ge1$ and $0\le r<2^{m-1}$. Define
$Z=m2^{m-1}-e(F)$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:eq:tvbin}
 \boxed{\quad
 \TV\bigl(\mathcal L(Z),\Bin(rm,1/2)\bigr)
 \le \min\left\{1,\frac{rm+\binom r2}{2^m}\right\}.
 \quad}
\end{equation}
The same statement holds for a uniformly labeled extremizer.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
By Theorem~\ref{sbd:thm:classify}, the core is uniform on $\U_m$, and,
conditional on it, $R$ is a uniform $r$-subset of the available family.
Fix a core and draw $r$ independent uniform subsets
$S_1,\ldots,S_r$ of the entire Boolean cube $\PP(C)$.
Each $S_i$ has a $\Bin(m,1/2)$ size, independently; therefore
$\sum_i|S_i|$ is exactly $\Bin(rm,1/2)$.

Condition this ordered sample on avoiding the $m$ forbidden core
subsets and on having no repetition. The conditional distribution is
exactly uniform sampling without replacement from
$\PP(C)\setminus C$, with an irrelevant ordering. By the union bound,
the conditioning event fails with probability at most
\[
 r\frac{m}{2^m}+\binom r2\frac1{2^m}.
\]
Conditioning changes total variation by the failure probability, and
mapping to the sum of the sizes cannot increase total variation.
The estimate is independent of the core, so it survives mixing over
cores. Rigidity implies that uniform labeling gives the same induced
law on isomorphism classes, proving the last assertion.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Boundary central limit law]\label{sbd:cor:clt}
If $m\to\infty$, $r=r(m)\ge1$, and $r(m+r)=o(2^m)$, then
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:eq:clt}
 \frac{m2^{m-1}-e(F)-rm/2}{\sqrt{rm}/2}
 \ \Longrightarrow\ N(0,1).
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The total-variation error in \eqref{sbd:eq:tvbin} tends to zero.
For $B\sim\Bin(rm,1/2)$, the characteristic function of
$(B-rm/2)/(\sqrt{rm}/2)$ is
$\cos(t/\sqrt{rm})^{rm}$, which tends to $e^{-t^2/2}$.
The claimed convergence follows, since $rm\to\infty$.
\end{proof}
For fixed $r\ge1$, the error is $O_r(m2^{-m})$ and the Gaussian limit
already applies. For $r=0$, the distribution is instead deterministic.
The theorem concerns graphs conditioned to maximize their source count;
it is not Wagner's normal limit for unrestricted EADs.

\subsection{Exact conditional moments}
Let $N=2^m$, $M=N-m$, and define two core statistics
\[
 e_C=\sum_{x\in C}|x|,\qquad h_C=\sum_{x\in C}|x|^2.
\]
The sum and squared sum of subset sizes in the available population are
\[
 A_1=\frac{mN}{2}-e_C,\qquad
 A_2=\frac{(m^2+m)N}{4}-h_C.
\]
Writing $\mu_C=A_1/M$ and $v_C=A_2/M-\mu_C^2$, uniform
sampling without replacement gives
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:eq:moments}
 \E(Z\mid C)=r\mu_C,\qquad
 \Var(Z\mid C)=\frac{r(M-r)}{M-1}v_C\quad(M>1).
\end{equation}
The variance identity follows by expanding the variance of the sum of
$r$ sampled population entries: the covariance of two distinct sampled
entries is $-v_C/(M-1)$. This derivation also specifies the variance
normalization. When $M=1$, the sample is deterministic.
These exact moments remain available outside the small-error range of
Theorem~\ref{sbd:thm:binomial}.

\begin{align}\label{sbd:eq:deletionrec}

\subsection{Computational complexity and numerical stability}
For fixed $d$, \eqref{sbd:eq:defect} uses only $d+1$ terms after the total
counts through $q+d$ have been tabulated. This is an arithmetic-operation
statement, not a bit-complexity claim: the binomial coefficients and the
answers themselves have $\Theta_d(n)$ bits in the bulk. Near a complete
layer, the exact complement form \eqref{sbd:eq:deletions} can be much smaller.

Use integer binomial coefficients for exact evaluation. For large
floating-point evaluations, use logarithms and either the covering
approximation or the all-orders expansion; do not subtract enormous,
nearly equal floating-point source-sieve terms near small parameters.
The program caps brute-force enumeration at seven vertices to make the
exponential nature of that check explicit rather than silently attempting
an infeasible extension.

\section{Formalization route and further research}

\subsection{A finite, auditable formalization route}\label{sbd:sec:formal}
A formal development need not begin with the full set-theoretic collapse.
Represent a finite digraph by a Boolean relation on a finite vertex type.
Define the finite set of out-neighbors, sourcehood, acyclicity, and
extensionality. The maximum-source proof then reduces to an injection
from vertices into the finite powerset of the nonsources, followed by
the explicit construction in Theorem~\ref{sbd:thm:support}.

A useful next layer would prove that deletion of sources preserves
out-neighborhoods and extensionality. This supports a marked-source
bijection and finite binomial inversion. To treat unlabeled counts,
one may either prove rigidity and use labeled counts throughout, or
introduce finite collapse and quotient by graph isomorphism. The former
avoids set-theoretic infrastructure for the first main counting results.

The entropy and non-holonomicity proofs can be separated from graph
semantics. A generic exponential-jump lemma is particularly suitable for
reuse: its hypotheses are scalar asymptotic statements, and its proof
only needs eventual polynomial-ratio bounds and the triangle inequality.
The deliverable contains no Lean source and makes no claim of kernel
verification. This paragraph is a development plan, not an assertion that
particular library declarations already implement the argument.

\subsection{Specific research questions}\label{sbd:sec:questions}
The following questions are proposed directions, not claims that each
has been certified open in the published literature.

\paragraph{1. Natural boundaries of the extremizer generating functions.}
Does $B_d(z)$ have a natural boundary on $|z|=e^{-\lambda_{d+1}}$?
The proof here establishes non-D-finiteness but not a dense set of
singularities. Unlike the lacunary maximum sequence, $B_d$ has positive
coefficients throughout its eventual support. A useful first target is
an analysis of contributions from short intervals immediately after
powers of two.

\begin{writenote}[Added 5 October 2026, batch~98: answered]
Yes, for every fixed $d\ge0$: Part~\ref{sbd:part:two} proves that
$|z|=R_d$ is a natural boundary of $B_d$
(Theorem~\ref{sbd:xtm:thm:main-boundary}). Two independent manuscripts of
batch~98 prove it, both by the mechanism suggested here: on each dyadic
block the coefficients just after the power of two, normalized, approach a
geometric profile uniformly, and at roots of unity of power-of-two order
the late blocks start in phase, so $B_d$ blows up along a dense set of
radii. Part~\ref{sbd:part:two} adds the radial phase with an $O(t)$ rate,
the two-term radial growth law, an $L^p$ comparison on every arc, natural
boundaries for the arithmetic sections of the coefficient sequence (of
power-of-two step in one manuscript, of every step in the writing step's
Theorem~\ref{sbd:wr:thm:sections}), and the absence of a bidisk of
convergence for the size-and-defect series. The proofs are unrefereed and
not formalized.
\end{writenote}

\paragraph{2. A sharper small-core amplitude.}
Combine an explicit, verified asymptotic expansion for $u_m$ with
\eqref{sbd:eq:allorders}. Since $m\asymp\log n$, the resulting corrections
live on scales different from the Boolean-layer saddle. Distinguish
statements uniform in the dyadic phase from fixed-phase expansions.

\paragraph{3. Defects growing with the graph size.}
How far can the entropy law and exponential-jump obstruction be extended
when $d=d(n)$ increases? The covering theorem itself remains valid, but
$\log u_{q+d}$ ceases to be a negligible amplitude if the core becomes
too large. Identify the transition at which the large-source boundary
analysis must connect with unrestricted EAD enumeration.

\paragraph{4. A matched bulk-to-complete-layer expansion.}
The bulk formula and the fixed-deletion formula overlap when the number
$r=2^m-n$ tends to infinity slowly. Derive explicit uniform remainder
bounds covering $r$ from fixed values to a positive fraction of $2^m$,
without using a Gaussian prefactor at a degenerate endpoint.

\paragraph{5. Extend the arc law beyond the collision estimate.}
The total-variation coupling loses strength when $r$ is comparable to
$2^{m/2}$. This is a limitation of comparing whole subset samples, not
necessarily of comparing their sizes. Can a finite-population local
limit theorem recover the arc distribution for all densities, with an
explicit correction for the $m$ forbidden core subsets?

\paragraph{6. Joint degree statistics.}
Study the empirical outdegree profile of source vertices and the joint
indegree distribution of core vertices. The source-neighborhood family
is a sample without replacement from a Boolean cube with a small deleted
family. This suggests multivariate hypergeometric limits, but the exact
core-induced correlations should be retained rather than discarded.

\paragraph{7. Source layers under repeated deletion.}
Successively delete all sources. Conditional on the first layer being
extremal, the remaining graph is exactly a uniform small core. Can this
be iterated to obtain precise distributions for the number of rounds,
the full layer-size vector, or the maximum rank under extremal source
conditioning?

\paragraph{8. Restricted degree and weak extensionality.}
If outdegrees are at most $\Delta$, the Boolean capacity becomes
$\sum_{j\le\Delta}\binom mj$ rather than $2^m$. Determine when its
pigeonhole bound is sharp, how covering changes, and whether analogous
boundary jumps imply non-P-recursiveness. A related problem permits
bounded multiplicity of out-neighborhoods or distinguished atoms.

\paragraph{9. Exact ranking and sampling of boundary classes.}
Implement ranking and unranking for the core-plus-subset representation.
A ranked core together with a combinatorial-number-system rank for its
chosen neighborhoods gives a natural interface. Specify bit complexity,
including the cost of representing deeply nested finite sets.

\paragraph{10. Formal proofs and OEIS editorial updates.}
Formalize the source support theorem and the boundary bijection first.
After independent mathematical review, the proof of A182220 and the
boundary formula can be proposed to OEIS with the historical upper-bound
citation. No OEIS submission or repository commit was made during this
work.

\section{Conclusion}
The source maximum has a short Boolean-capacity proof, and its known
upper bound should not be confused with a new breakthrough. The
interesting structure appears when one counts the graphs attaining,
or nearly attaining, that maximum. Their core has logarithmic size,
while their sources select a large family of subsets of that core.
At maximum density, coverage is automatic and the count is exactly
$u_q\binom{2^q-q}{n-q}$. At fixed defect, only finitely many terms of the
classical sieve survive.

This decomposition makes it possible to prove a uniform entropy law
without solving the separate asymptotic problem for the core count.
Its phase resets at powers of two. The resulting exponential jumps
rule out every polynomial-coefficient linear recurrence for each fixed
boundary diagonal. Exact arc formulas and the binomial coupling provide
further information about the extremizers themselves.

The principal deliverable is therefore a chain of proved results:
sharp source support, exact boundary enumeration, finite-defect
formulas, uniform high-source estimates, complete growth-rate intervals,
and non-holonomicity, with reproducible finite checks. Questions about
historical priority, natural boundaries of the extremizer series,
growing defects, and full formal verification remain explicitly separate.

Part~\ref{sbd:part:two} proves that $|z|=R_d$ is a natural boundary of
$B_d$ for every fixed $d$. Priority, growing defects and formal
verification remain separate, for Part~\ref{sbd:part:two} as well.
\end{writenote}

\paragraph{Summary.}
Let $u(n,s)$ count isomorphism classes of extensional acyclic digraphs with
$n$ vertices and $s$ sources, and for a fixed defect $d\ge0$ let
$b_n^{(d)}=u(n,n-\lceil\log_2n\rceil-d)$, with inadmissible terms zero, and
$B_d(z)=\sum_{n\ge1}b_n^{(d)}z^n$, as in Part~\ref{sbd:part:one}. We prove
that the circle of convergence
\[
 |z|=R_d,\qquad
 R_d=\frac{(c_d-1)^{c_d-1}}{c_d^{c_d}},\qquad c_d=2^{d+1},
\]
is a natural boundary of $B_d$ for every fixed $d$. This answers
Question~1 of Section~\ref{sbd:sec:questions}. The proof gives more than
noncontinuation. At every root of unity $\zeta$ of power-of-two order, the
quotient $B_d(R_d\e^{-t}\zeta)/B_d(R_d\e^{-t})$ has an explicit nonzero
limit, with error $O_{d,\zeta}(t)$. Writing $L=\log(1/t)$, the logarithm of
the radial magnitude is $L^2/(2\log2)+L\log L/\log2+O_d(L)$. On every
nonempty open arc, every fixed positive power of the modulus has integral
comparable to the positive radial value raised to that power, so the
boundary is strong in a quantitative local $L^p$ sense. Every arithmetic
subsequence of power-of-two step also has a natural boundary, and the
joint size-and-defect series has no nontrivial bidisk of convergence.

The mechanism is a uniform geometric limit for the normalized coefficients
of each dyadic block, together with the common phase of the late block
starts at dyadic roots of unity. All graph-counting estimates and analytic
arguments are proved here; the counting identities inherited from
Part~\ref{sbd:part:one} and from the classical literature are credited and
re-proved, not claimed. Exact computations and numerical diagnostics
accompany the proofs; the analytic conclusions do not rely on finite
experiments. These are conventional proofs in unrefereed manuscripts, not
formally verified, and not certified as historically first.

\section{The question and the results}
\label{sbd:xtm:sec:introduction}

\subsection{From a source maximum to the number of near-extremizers}
An extensional acyclic digraph is a finite directed graph with no directed
cycle in which different vertices have different out-neighborhoods; these
graphs encode finite transitive sets, an arc from $x$ to $y$ corresponding
to $y\in x$. OEIS A001192 counts their isomorphism classes, and A182162
refines their labeled counts by the number of sources
\cite{oeis1192,oeis162}. The relevant OEIS entries distinguish the
following quantities \cite{oeis220,oeis162,oeis1192,oeis161}.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.15\textwidth}p{0.73\textwidth}@{}}
\toprule
Entry & Quantity \\
\midrule
A001192 & $u_m$, the number of finite transitive sets of size $m$,
equivalently EAD isomorphism classes.\\
A182161 & $m!u_m$, the number of EADs on a fixed labeled vertex set.\\
A182162 & $n!u(n,k)$, the labeled source-count triangle.\\
A182220 & $n-\lceil\log_2n\rceil$, the maximum possible number of sources.\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The source maximum is
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:maximum}
 s_{\max}(n)=n-q(n),\qquad q(n)=\lceil\log_2 n\rceil\quad(n\ge1),
\end{equation}
the quantity in A182220 \cite{oeis220}. Its elementary upper bound is in
Tomescu's thesis, printed page~29 \cite{tomescu}; Part~\ref{sbd:part:one}
supplies the matching construction and develops the enumeration near this
boundary (Theorem~\ref{sbd:thm:support}).

For a fixed integer $d\ge0$, as in \eqref{sbd:eq:boundary-def} and
\eqref{sbd:eq:Bgf},
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:defect-definition}
 b_n^{(d)}=
 \begin{cases}
 u(n,n-q(n)-d),&n-q(n)-d\ge1,\\
 0,&n-q(n)-d<1,
 \end{cases}
 \qquad
 B_d(z)=\sum_{n\ge1}b_n^{(d)}z^n.
\end{equation}
Thus $d=0$ counts the source-maximizing graphs, and $d=1$ counts graphs
with one fewer source. A fixed $d$ leaves a core of $q(n)+d$ nonsources,
which is only logarithmic in $n$. When subsequences are extracted we also
set $b_0^{(d)}=0$, and $\binom Mk=0$ for $k<0$ or $k>M$.

Part~\ref{sbd:part:one} proves that each $B_d$ is not D-finite and has
radius $R_d$ (Theorem~\ref{sbd:thm:nonholonomic}), and asks, in
Section~\ref{sbd:sec:questions}, Question~1, whether its entire circle of
convergence is a natural boundary; it distinguishes that question
expressly from non-D-finiteness (Remark~8.3). Non-D-finiteness by itself
would not answer it. The argument below identifies a dense set of radial
singularities and quantifies their size.

\subsection{Conventions and main theorems}
All logarithms without a subscript are natural. A statement with an $O_d$
remainder permits its constant to depend on $d$; it does not assert
uniformity for a growing defect. For fixed $d$, set
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:constants}
 c=c_d=2^{d+1},\qquad a=a_d=c-1,\qquad
 R=R_d=\frac{a^a}{c^c},\qquad h=\log2.
\end{equation}
Then $R_d=\e^{-\lambda_{d+1}}$ is the radius \eqref{sbd:eq:radius} of
Part~\ref{sbd:part:one}, since $cH(1/c)=c\log c-a\log a$, and
$0<aR=(1-1/c)^c<1$. A \emph{dyadic root of unity} is a complex number $\zeta$
with $\zeta^{2^j}=1$ for some integer $j\ge0$; these form a dense subset of
the unit circle containing~$1$.

\begin{definition}[source~01]\label{sbd:edge:def:boundary}
The circle $|z|=R$ is a \emph{natural boundary} for a holomorphic function
on $|z|<R$ if the function has no holomorphic continuation through any point
of that circle. Continuation through a point means a holomorphic function
on a neighborhood of the point that agrees with the original function on
the portion inside its disk.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Natural boundary and quantitative radial phase; sources~05 and~01]
\label{sbd:xtm:thm:main-boundary}
For every fixed integer $d\ge0$, $B_d$ has radius of convergence $R_d$.
The circle $|z|=R_d$ is a natural boundary. Moreover, for every fixed
dyadic root of unity $\zeta$,
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:phase-main}
 \frac{B_d(R_d\e^{-t}\zeta)}{B_d(R_d\e^{-t})}
 =\zeta\frac{1-a_dR_d}{1-a_dR_d\zeta}
   +O_{d,\zeta}(t)\qquad(t\downarrow0).
\end{equation}
The displayed limit is nonzero, and $B_d(R_d\e^{-t})\to+\infty$.
\end{theorem}
In manuscript~01's normalization ($\rho_d=a_dR_d$, $r=\e^{-t}$) the
limit reads
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:edge:eq:radial-ratio}
 \lim_{r\uparrow1}\frac{B_d(R_dr\zeta)}{B_d(R_dr)}
 =\frac{\zeta(1-a_dR_d)}{1-a_dR_d\zeta}.
\end{equation}
Manuscript~05 proves the theorem with the $O(t)$ rate
(Section~\ref{sbd:xtm:sec:boundary}); manuscript~01 proves the limit
\eqref{sbd:edge:eq:radial-ratio} and the natural boundary through the
abstract transfer Lemma~\ref{sbd:edge:lem:transfer}
(Section~\ref{sbd:edge:sec:transfer}).

\begin{theorem}[Radial size; sources~05 and~01]
\label{sbd:xtm:thm:main-growth}
For each fixed $d\ge0$, with $L=\log(1/t)$ and
$\mathcal T(L)=L^2/(2h)+L\log L/h$,
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:growth-main}
 \log B_d(R_d\e^{-t})
 =\frac{L^2}{2h}+\frac{L\log L}{h}+O_d(L).
\end{equation}
At every fixed dyadic root $\zeta$, the same formula holds for
$\log|B_d(R_d\e^{-t}\zeta)|$, with the constant in the error allowed to
depend on $\zeta$. In particular, for every fixed $M>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:superpole}
 t^M\,|B_d(R_d\e^{-t}\zeta)|\longrightarrow\infty.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Local $L^p$ comparison and growth; source~05, the log-level law also source~01]
\label{sbd:xtm:thm:main-lp}
Let $d\ge0$ and $p>0$ be fixed, and let $I$ be a nonempty bounded open
interval of angles. For all sufficiently small $t>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:lp-comparison}
 C_{d,p,I}^{-1}B_d(R_d\e^{-t})^p
 \ \le\int_I |B_d(R_d\e^{-t+\ii\theta})|^p\dd\theta
 \ \le C_{d,p,I}B_d(R_d\e^{-t})^p
\end{equation}
for a positive constant $C_{d,p,I}$. Consequently,
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:lp-growth}
 \log\int_I |B_d(R_d\e^{-t+\ii\theta})|^p\dd\theta
 =\frac{pL^2}{2h}+\frac{pL\log L}{h}+O_{d,p,I}(L).
\end{equation}
In particular these radial integral means tend to infinity on every
nonempty open arc, for every $p>0$.
\end{theorem}
The logarithmic law \eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:lp-growth} is manuscript~01's
Theorem~1.4, proved there by a different, single-point argument
(Section~\ref{sbd:xtm:sec:lp}); the two-sided comparison
\eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:lp-comparison} is manuscript~05's.

Section~\ref{sbd:edge:sec:subsequences} proves a further stability result
(manuscript~01): for every $s\ge0$ and $0\le r_0<2^s$ the series
$\sum_{\ell\ge0}b_{2^s\ell+r_0}^{(d)}z^\ell$ has natural boundary
$|z|=R_d^{2^s}$; the convention at $n=0$ does not affect this. The writing
step extends it to every step $r\ge1$
(Theorem~\ref{sbd:wr:thm:sections}). Section~\ref{sbd:xtm:sec:consequences}
establishes a zero-radius result for positive defect fugacity
(manuscript~05).

In particular the covering estimate is used below in the form
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:covering-bound}
 0\le u_m\binom{2^m-m}k-u(m+k,k)
 \le m2^{-k}u_m\binom{2^m-m}k\qquad(k\ge1,\ k\le2^m-m),
\end{equation}
which is \eqref{sbd:eq:cover}, and the finite-defect formula in the form
\eqref{sbd:eq:defect}. Uniqueness of the intrinsic core prevents double
counting, and the bound is uniform over the shape of the core. The two
manuscripts add the following remarks to these background statements.
Collapse and rigidity are classical facts of the graph representation of
finite sets \cite{policriti,tomescu}; they explain why counting
source-neighborhood families requires no division by automorphism groups
(05). The upper source bound is explicitly on printed page~29 of Tomescu's
thesis \cite{tomescu}, and the construction and the OEIS formula are
background to the question solved here (01). The covering condition
$\bigcup(C\cup L)=C$ matters because it has a small failure probability
when $L$ is large (01). The source-sensitive enumeration is classical, the
OEIS entries contain Johnston's implementation of the sieve and core
recurrence \cite{oeis162,oeis1192}, and the identities are re-proved so
that all normalizations and the exact computations can be checked within
the manuscripts, where they also provide exact independent checks of the
asymptotic reasoning (both). The covering estimate is useful even though
$m2^{-k}$ can exceed one at small parameters: the upper error inequality
is still true, while its asymptotic use has $k$ exponentially larger than
$m$ (05). The analytic work below uses the positive approximation
\eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:covering-bound} instead of the cancellation in
\eqref{sbd:eq:defect}, whose alternating terms are an exact identity, not
an asymptotic series (05, 01).

\begin{remark}[Routes that differ from Part~I's]\label{sbd:xtm:rem:routes}
(i) Manuscript~01's collapse proof assigns $x_v=\{x_w:v\to w\}$ and argues
by set-theoretic rank: assignments at lower ranks are distinct by
induction, extensionality separates those at the next rank, and different
ranks give different sets. (ii) Its support construction takes \emph{any}
transitive core $C$ of size $m$ and any $k$-family of available subsets
containing $C$ itself (available because $C\notin C$); every core element
is then covered. Part~\ref{sbd:part:one} and manuscript~05 use a directed
path as the core. (iii) Manuscript~05 proves the inversion
\eqref{sbd:eq:sieve} directly: if $f_t=\sum_{s\ge t}\binom st u(n,s)$, the
coefficient of $u(n,j)$ in $\sum_{t\ge s}(-1)^{t-s}\binom ts f_t$ is
$\binom js\sum_{t=s}^j(-1)^{t-s}\binom{j-s}{t-s}$, which vanishes unless
$j=s$; and it obtains the total recurrence \eqref{sbd:eq:total-rec} from
$\sum_{t=1}^s(-1)^{t-1}\binom st=1$ for $s\ge1$, since every nonempty
acyclic graph has a source. Part~\ref{sbd:part:one} and manuscript~01 use
the polynomial $P_n(z)=\sum_su(n,s)z^s$. (iv) Manuscript~05 bounds the
covering failure by
$\binom{2^{m-1}-m}k/\binom{2^m-m}k\le\bigl((2^{m-1}-m)/(2^m-m)\bigr)^k\le2^{-k}$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The chain lower bound for the core count}
The core count affects the singular strength even though it does not affect
the exponential radius of the boundary sequence (01). The following
two-sided bound is the only information about $u_m$ that the analytic
arguments use. Its lower half is new to this report.

\begin{lemma}[Elementary core-count bounds; sources~05 and~01]\label{sbd:xtm:lem:core-bounds}
For every $m\ge1$,
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:core-bounds}
 2^{\binom{m-1}{2}}\le u_m\le2^{\binom m2}.
\end{equation}
Consequently $\log u_m=\tfrac h2m^2+O(m)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The upper bound is Lemma~\ref{sbd:lem:ubound}. For the lower bound, use
vertices $0,\ldots,m-1$. Require the arcs $i\to i-1$ for $1\le i<m$ and
choose each arc $i\to j$ with $j<i-1$ independently. The largest member of
$N^+(i)$ is $i-1$, so the neighborhoods are distinct. The required path
makes the reachability order total; hence the topological order is unique.
An isomorphism must preserve that order and therefore every vertex.
Different arc choices are nonisomorphic, giving $2^{0+1+\cdots+(m-2)}$
classes. There is no factorial loss in this construction. Taking
logarithms proves the final assertion.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Second route for the rigidity step (source~01)]
In the same graphs, the longest directed path starting at vertex $i$ has
length exactly $i$. These lengths are graph invariants and are all
different, so an isomorphism between two such graphs preserves every
vertex index, and different choices of optional arcs give different
classes. There are $\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}(i-1)=(m-1)(m-2)/2$ optional arcs.
Vertex~$0$ alone has the empty neighborhood.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[source~01]\label{sbd:edge:rem:m0}
The range $m\ge1$ in \eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:core-bounds} matters: $u_0=1$,
whereas substituting $m=0$ into the displayed lower power would give $2$.
All asymptotic block arguments use $m\to\infty$.
\end{remark}

Wagner's work gives much sharper unrestricted enumeration and
distributional results \cite{wagner,wagner13}. The elementary squeeze above
already gives the exact quadratic coefficient needed for the radial laws,
with no unspecified enumeration constant; none of the analytic theorems
uses a sharp asymptotic formula for $u_m$.

\subsection{A direct proof of the deletion recurrence}
Part~\ref{sbd:part:one} quotes the source-deletion recurrence
\eqref{sbd:eq:deletionrec} from Tomescu's Corollary~2.1.7 \cite{tomescu}
and uses it only in its verification program. Both manuscripts prove it;
manuscript~01's appendix gives the argument in full, and manuscript~05's
Section~9.1 gives the same construction in brief.

\begin{proposition}[Source deletion; sources~01 and~05]\label{sbd:edge:prop:deletion}
For $n\ge2$ and $1\le s\le n$, with $u(1,1)=1$ and zero outside the valid
source range,
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:edge:eq:deletion-rec}
 s\,u(n,s)
 =\bigl(2^{n-s}-(n-1)\bigr)u(n-1,s-1)
 +\sum_{j=0}^{n-s-1}
 \binom{s+j}{j+1}2^{n-1-s-j}u(n-1,s+j),
\end{equation}
which is \eqref{sbd:eq:deletionrec} multiplied by $s$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Mark one source in a graph with $s$ sources and delete it. If no new
source is exposed, the remaining graph has $s-1$ sources. The deleted
vertex must avoid these sources, and its out-neighborhood must differ from
all $n-1$ existing neighborhoods. There are $2^{n-s}-(n-1)$ choices. When
such a remaining graph exists, the source-support bound guarantees this
quantity is nonnegative.

Otherwise let $j+1\ge1$ be the number of newly exposed sources. The
remaining graph has $s+j$ sources. Choose exactly $j+1$ of them as
neighbors of the vertex being restored; choose any subset of the other
$n-1-s-j$ vertices as further neighbors. These neighborhoods are
automatically distinct from every old neighborhood, because they contain
an old source. This gives the summand in \eqref{sbd:edge:eq:deletion-rec}.
Rigidity makes the number of choices of a marked source on the left
exactly $s$. Thus the recurrence is independent of binomial inversion,
while counting the same objects.
\end{proof}

\section{Dyadic blocks and their analytic profile}
\label{sbd:xtm:sec:blocks}

Fix $d$ for the remainder of the analytic argument and suppress its
subscript in $c=c_d$, $a=c-1$ and $R=R_d$. On a block
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:block-parameters}
 K=2^{q-1}<n\le2K,\qquad m=q+d,
\end{equation}
one has $q(n)=q$ and $2^m=cK$. For sufficiently large $q$, define
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:amplitude-definition}
 k_0=K+1-m,\quad
 C_q=\binom{cK-m}{k_0},\quad
 A_q=u_mC_qR^{K+1}.
\end{equation}
All these quantities are positive. Choose a fixed lower block index
$q_0=q_0(d)$ large enough that $k_0\ge1$ and $m\le K/2$ thereafter.
The omitted initial coefficients make a polynomial $E_d(z)$. The
normalization $u_mC_q$ is the positive leading count of the block, not
necessarily the exact count $b_{K+1}^{(d)}$; for $d=0$ the two coincide.
By the covering estimate \eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:covering-bound},
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:edge:eq:eta}
 b_{K+1+j}^{(d)}=u_m\binom{cK-m}{k_0+j}(1-\eta_{q,j}),\qquad
 0\le\eta_{q,j}\le m2^{-(k_0+j)}.
\end{equation}
Set
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:block-polynomial}
 P_q(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{K-1}
       \frac{b_{K+1+j}^{(d)}}{u_mC_q}z^j.
\end{equation}
The exact block decomposition is
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:block-decomposition}
 B_d(z)=E_d(z)+\sum_{q\ge q_0}A_q(z/R)^{K+1}P_q(z),
\end{equation}
initially as a formal series and later as a convergent analytic identity.
(Manuscript~01 writes the same decomposition in the variable $w=z/R$, with
its $P_q(w)$ equal to $P_q(Rw)$ here and its amplitude $c_q$ equal to $A_q$.)

\subsection{The uniform geometric profile}
The relevant geometric parameter is safely inside the unit disk:
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:margin}
 aR=\left(1-\frac1c\right)^c<\e^{-1}<1.
\end{equation}
Thus one may choose a real $T$ with $R<T<1/a$.

\begin{lemma}[Quantitative block profile; source~05, source~01's Theorem~5.1]\label{sbd:xtm:lem:block-profile}
For any fixed $0<T<1/a$, uniformly on $|z|\le T$,
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:block-profile}
 P_q(z)=g(z)+O_{d,T}(m/K),\qquad g(z)=\frac1{1-az}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
First replace the count in \eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:block-polynomial} by its binomial
proxy. Write
\[
 v_{q,j}=\frac{\binom{cK-m}{k_0+j}}{\binom{cK-m}{k_0}},
 \qquad v_{q,0}=1.
\]
For $1\le j<K$ the product representation is
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:consecutive-ratios}
 v_{q,j}=\prod_{s=1}^jr_s,\qquad
 r_s=\frac{aK-s}{K+1-m+s}.
\end{equation}
The ratios are nonnegative and decrease with $s$. Since $m\le K/2$,
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:ratio-bounds}
 r_s\le a+O_d(m/K),\qquad
 |r_s-a|\le C_d\frac{m+s}{K}.
\end{equation}
Choose $\epsilon>0$ such that $(a+\epsilon)T<1$. For sufficiently
large $q$, every $r_s$ is at most $a+\epsilon$. Telescoping the two
products gives, for $j\ge1$,
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:product-difference}
 |v_{q,j}-a^j|
 \le\frac{C_d(mj+j^2)}{K}(a+\epsilon)^{j-1}.
\end{equation}
After multiplication by $T^j$ this is summable uniformly in $q$.
The resulting error is $O_{d,T}(m/K)$. The missing geometric tail
from $j=K$ onward is at most $(aT)^K/(1-aT)$ and satisfies the same bound.

By the covering estimate, the error from replacing the actual
coefficient by its proxy is at most $m2^{-k_0-j}v_{q,j}$. Its sum on
$|z|\le T$ is bounded by
\[
 m2^{-k_0}\sum_{j=0}^{K-1}
   \bigl((a+\epsilon)T/2\bigr)^j
 =O_{d,T}(m2^{-k_0}).
\]
This is smaller than $m/K$ for large $q$. Combining the three
bounds proves \eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:block-profile}.
\end{proof}

Manuscript~01 states the case $T=R$ in the variable $w=z/R$: with
$aR=\rho_d$ in its notation,
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:edge:eq:profile-limit}
 \sup_{|w|\le1}\Bigl|P_q(Rw)-\frac1{1-aRw}\Bigr|
 =O_d\Bigl(\frac{m+1}K\Bigr)=O_d(q2^{-q}),
\end{equation}
so the convergence includes the whole circle of convergence and is
stronger than convergence of any fixed number of coefficients. Its proof
is the same telescoping argument, in the normalized coefficients
$v_{q,j}R^j$, whose consecutive ratios it writes exactly: for
$0\le\ell\le K-2$,
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:edge:eq:ratio-difference}
 R\,r_{\ell+1}=R\frac{aK-1-\ell}{K+2+\ell-m},\qquad
 R\,r_{\ell+1}-aR=R\frac{am-(2a+1)-c\ell}{K+2+\ell-m},
\end{equation}
so that $|Rr_{\ell+1}-aR|\le C_d(m+\ell+1)/K$ once $K\ge2m$, with a
constant $C_d$; these ratios decrease with $\ell$, start near $aR<1$, and
are eventually bounded by a fixed $\rho_0\in(aR,1)$. The covering error
contributes at most $\delta_q/(1-\rho_0)$ with $\delta_q=m2^{-k_0}$, and
the missing tail at most $(aR)^K/(1-aR)$.

The coefficients of $B_d$ are eventually all positive. The lacunary
structure is in the \emph{locations of dominant blocks}.
Lemma~\ref{sbd:xtm:lem:block-profile} makes that concentration usable: it
replaces each long block by a common nonvanishing analytic profile with a
uniform error.

\subsection{The size of a block amplitude}
\begin{lemma}[Quadratic logarithmic amplitudes; source~05]\label{sbd:xtm:lem:amplitudes}
There is a constant $C_d>0$ such that, for every sufficiently large $q$,
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:amplitude-envelope}
 \exp\bigl(hq^2/2-C_dq\bigr)
 \le A_q\le
 \exp\bigl(hq^2/2+C_dq\bigr).
\end{equation}
In particular, $A_q\to\infty$ and $\log A_q=o(K)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Falling factorial cancellation gives the exact identity
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:binomial-ratio}
 \frac{C_q}{\binom{cK}{K}}
 =\frac{aK\,\fall K{m-1}}{\fall{cK}m}
 =ac^{-m}
 \frac{\prod_{i=0}^{m-2}(1-i/K)}
      {\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}(1-i/(cK))}.
\end{equation}
The logarithm of the product quotient is $O_d(m^2/K)$, so this
quotient lies between $1/2$ and $2$ for large $q$.

The binomial probability distribution with parameters $cK$ and $1/c$
has a mode at $K$. Its mass there is
$\binom{cK}{K}R^K$. The largest of its $cK+1$ masses is between
$1/(cK+1)$ and $1$, hence
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:binomial-mode}
 \frac1{cK+1}\le\binom{cK}{K}R^K\le1.
\end{equation}
Combining \eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:binomial-ratio}--\eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:binomial-mode} gives
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:amplitude-elementary}
 \frac{Ra}{2c^m(cK+1)}u_m
 \le A_q\le\frac{2Ra}{c^m}u_m.
\end{equation}
Now apply Lemma~\ref{sbd:xtm:lem:core-bounds}, $m=q+d$, and $\log K=(q-1)h$.
Every contribution other than $hq^2/2$ is $O_d(q)$.
\end{proof}

The argument does not require Stirling's formula. When a more precise
normalization is wanted, Stirling's expansion gives the following, which
both manuscripts state (manuscript~01 as its Proposition~5.2, with the proof
below; manuscript~05 as a display).

\begin{proposition}[Stirling form of the amplitude; sources~05 and~01]\label{sbd:edge:prop:amplitude}
For fixed $d$, as $q\to\infty$,
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:amplitude-stirling}
 A_q=u_mc^{-m}R\sqrt{\frac{ca}{2\pi K}}
             \left(1+O_d\left(\frac{m^2}{K}\right)\right).
\end{equation}
Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:edge:eq:amplitude-log}
 \log A_q=\frac{h}{2}q^2+O_d(q),
 \qquad A_q\longrightarrow\infty.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Proof (source~01)]
In \eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:binomial-ratio}, since $m=O_d(\log K)$, the product
estimates $\fall K{m-1}=K^{m-1}(1+O(m^2/K))$ and
$\fall{cK}m=(cK)^m(1+O_d(m^2/K))$ follow by summing $\log(1-j/K)$ and its
counterpart. Stirling's formula, with its usual real positive remainder
\cite{dlmf}, gives
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:edge:eq:stirling-binomial}
 \binom{cK}{K}
 =R^{-K}\sqrt{\frac{c}{2\pi aK}}
 \left(1+O_d(K^{-1})\right).
\end{equation}
Multiplying \eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:binomial-ratio} and
\eqref{sbd:edge:eq:stirling-binomial} by $u_mR^{K+1}$ yields
\eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:amplitude-stirling}. Taking logarithms and using
Lemma~\ref{sbd:xtm:lem:core-bounds} gives
$\log A_q=\tfrac h2m^2-m\log c-\tfrac12\log K+O_d(m)=\tfrac h2q^2+O_d(q)$;
the positive quadratic term proves divergence.
\end{proof}
Section~\ref{sbd:xtm:app:expansions} gives a first correction to
\eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:amplitude-stirling}.

\section{Positive dyadic sums and concentration of block mass}
\label{sbd:xtm:sec:dyadic-sums}

The following purely analytic estimates will be applied to the amplitudes
$A_q$. They also explain why a small uniform block error can be summed near
the boundary with a quantitative remainder.

\begin{lemma}[A discrete Laplace estimate; source~05, source~01's Lemma~7.1]\label{sbd:xtm:lem:laplace}
Suppose $A_q>0$ for $q\ge q_0\ge1$ and
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:generic-envelope}
 \e^{hq^2/2-Cq}\le A_q\le\e^{hq^2/2+Cq},\qquad h=\log2,
\end{equation}
after enlarging $C$ to include finitely many initial indices. Put
\[
 K_q=2^{q-1},\quad
 S(t)=\sum_{q\ge q_0}A_q\e^{-t(K_q+1)},\quad
 L=\log(1/t).
\]
Then $S(t)$ is finite for $t>0$, and
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:laplace-law}
 \log S(t)=\frac{L^2}{2h}+\frac{L\log L}{h}+O_C(L)
 \qquad(t\downarrow0).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Finiteness follows because $t2^{q-1}$ eventually dominates any
quadratic in $q$. The harmless common factor $\e^{-t}$ may be
removed in the estimates. Let $X=L+\log L$ and
$q_* =\lfloor X/h\rfloor+1$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:qstar}
 \frac L2<tK_{q_*}\le L,\qquad q_* =X/h+O(1).
\end{equation}
The single $q_*$ term yields
\[
 \log S(t)\ge\tfrac h2q_*^2-Cq_*-L-t
             =\frac{X^2}{2h}-O_C(L).
\]

For the upper bound set $Q=\lceil X/h\rceil+4$, so
$tK_Q\ge8L$. For all sufficiently large $L$, $hQ+C\le2L$ and
$h\le L$. Put $M=\exp(hQ^2/2+CQ)$. The terms with $q\le Q$
have sum at most $QM$. For $q=Q+j$, $j\ge0$, their upper envelope
divided by $M$ has logarithm at most
\[
 2Lj+\tfrac h2j^2-8L2^j\le-5L2^j.
\]
Here $j\le2^j$ and $j^2\le2^{j+1}$ were used. This tail is
summable and at most $M$ after increasing $L$ if needed. Therefore
$S(t)\le(Q+1)M$, and
$\log S(t)\le X^2/(2h)+O_C(L)$. Finally
$(\log L)^2=O(L)$, so expanding $X^2$ proves the statement.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Second proof of the upper bound (source~01)]
Put $x=(L+\log L)/h$, $Q=\lceil x\rceil+3$ and
$f(q)=\tfrac h2q^2+Cq-t2^{q-1}$. The head $q\le Q$ has $O(Q)$ terms, each
at most $\exp(\tfrac h2Q^2+CQ)$, whose logarithm is
$\mathcal T(L)+O(L)$, the term $(\log L)^2$ being absorbed. For the tail,
$f(q+1)-f(q)=hq+\tfrac h2+C-t2^{q-1}$. At $q=Q$ the negative exponential
term has magnitude at least $4L$, whereas $hQ=L+\log L+O(1)$; thus the
difference is at most $-L$ for all sufficiently large $L$. Its subsequent
differences are $h-t2^{q-1}<0$, so the same bound holds for every $q\ge Q$,
and the tail is at most $\e^{f(Q)}/(1-\e^{-L})$, which has the same
logarithmic upper bound as the head. For the lower bound manuscript~01 takes
the single term $q=\lfloor x\rfloor$, for which $\tfrac h2q^2=\mathcal
T(L)+O(L)$, $t2^{q-1}\le L/2$ and $q=O(L)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Negligible early blocks and a weighted error bound; source~05]
\label{sbd:xtm:lem:concentration}
Under the hypotheses of Lemma~\ref{sbd:xtm:lem:laplace}, let $q_*$ be as in
\eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:qstar}. For any prescribed $A>0$, one can choose a fixed
integer $D=D(C,A)$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:early-blocks}
 \frac{\sum_{q_0\le q\le q_*-D}A_q\e^{-t(K_q+1)}}{S(t)}
 =O_{C,A}(t^A).
\end{equation}
For every fixed $p>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:weighted-error}
 \frac1{S(t)}\sum_{q\ge q_0}
       \left(\frac q{K_q}\right)^p A_q\e^{-t(K_q+1)}
 =O_{C,p}(t^p).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Use the $q_*$ term as a lower bound for the denominator. Monotonicity
of the upper envelope bounds the numerator of \eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:early-blocks}
by $q_*\exp(h(q_*-D)^2/2+C(q_*-D))$. Thus the logarithm of its
ratio to that lower bound is at most
\begin{align*}
 &\log q_*-hDq_*+\tfrac h2D^2+2Cq_*-CD+L+t\\
 &\hspace{8mm}=\left(-D+\frac{2C}h+1\right)L+O_{C,D}(\log L).
\end{align*}
Taking $D>A+2C/h+2$ proves \eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:early-blocks}.

For \eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:weighted-error}, choose such a $D$ with $A>p$.
On $q\ge q_*-D$, the function $q/2^{q-1}$ is eventually decreasing,
and \eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:qstar} gives
\[
 \frac q{K_q}\le\frac{q_*-D}{K_{q_*-D}}=O_D(t).
\]
This portion contributes $O(t^p)$. On earlier indices $q/K_q$ is
bounded by an absolute constant, while their relative mass is
$O(t^A)$. This proves the weighted estimate.
\end{proof}

The dominant block indices are therefore near $(L+\log L)/\log2$.
Manuscript~05 uses only the one-sided concentration proved above; no
unproved smooth approximation to the discrete maximizing index is needed.
(Section~\ref{sbd:wr:sec:dominant} sharpens this: along most radii a
single block carries almost all of the mass.)

\section{Natural boundaries and radial asymptotics}
\label{sbd:xtm:sec:boundary}

\subsection{Convergence inside the stated circle}
Choose $R<T<1/a$. Lemma~\ref{sbd:xtm:lem:block-profile} makes $P_q$
uniformly bounded on $|z|\le T$, and Lemma~\ref{sbd:xtm:lem:amplitudes}
bounds $A_q$ by a quadratic exponential in $q$. For $|z|\le rR$ with $r<1$,
the series in \eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:block-decomposition} is dominated by
\[
 C\sum_{q\ge q_0}\exp(hq^2/2+C_dq)r^{2^{q-1}+1},
\]
which converges. Thus it defines an analytic function throughout $|z|<R$
and agrees there with the original generating series.

Let $S_d(t)$ be the sum in Lemma~\ref{sbd:xtm:lem:laplace} formed from these
$A_q$. Lemma~\ref{sbd:xtm:lem:concentration}, with $p=1$ and $m=q+d=O_d(q)$,
gives
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:sum-error}
 \sum_{q\ge q_0}\frac mK A_q\e^{-t(K+1)}
   =O_d(tS_d(t)).
\end{equation}
The polynomial $E_d$ is bounded near $|z|=R$, whereas
\eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:laplace-law} shows $S_d(t)$ grows faster than every power
of $1/t$. In particular $E_d/S_d=O(t)$.

Substituting the uniform profile in \eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:block-decomposition}
on the positive radius yields
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:positive-transfer}
 B_d(R\e^{-t})=g(R\e^{-t})S_d(t)+O_d(tS_d(t)).
\end{equation}
Since $g(R)>0$, this tends to infinity. The radius of convergence is at
most $R$, and hence is exactly $R$. Taking logarithms and using
Lemma~\ref{sbd:xtm:lem:laplace} proves \eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:growth-main}.

\begin{proof}[Second route to the radius (source~01)]
At the block starts, $b_{K+1}^{(d)}R^{K+1}=A_q(1-\eta_{q,0})$ by
\eqref{sbd:edge:eq:eta}, and $\log(A_q(1-\eta_{q,0}))/(K+1)\to0$ by
\eqref{sbd:edge:eq:amplitude-log}. Therefore
$(b_{K+1}^{(d)})^{1/(K+1)}\to R^{-1}$, so the radius is exactly $R$; this
recovers \eqref{sbd:eq:radius} without Part~\ref{sbd:part:one}'s full
entropy theorem. Convergence for $|z|<R$ follows as above from
$\sum_qA_qr^{2^{q-1}+1}\le\sum_q\exp(C_dq^2-|\log r|2^{q-1})<\infty$ for
$0<r<1$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Alignment at dyadic roots}
Fix $\zeta$ with $\zeta^{2^j}=1$. For every $q\ge j+1$, $K=2^{q-1}$ is
divisible by its order, so
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:phase-align}
 (\e^{-t}\zeta)^{K+1}=\zeta\e^{-t(K+1)}.
\end{equation}
Only finitely many blocks fail this relation; their contribution is a
polynomial bounded as $t\downarrow0$. The same uniform block error and
\eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:sum-error} therefore give
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:complex-transfer}
 B_d(R\e^{-t}\zeta)
 =\zeta g(R\e^{-t}\zeta)S_d(t)+O_{d,\zeta}(tS_d(t)).
\end{equation}
Divide by \eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:positive-transfer}. The denominators stay bounded
away from zero, and replacing $\e^{-t}$ by $1$ changes $g$ by $O_d(t)$.
This proves \eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:phase-main}. It also proves
\eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:growth-main} for the complex radial magnitude and then
\eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:superpole}, because $L^2/(2h)$ dominates $ML$.

\subsection{Why dense radial divergence gives a natural boundary}
The nonzero limit in \eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:phase-main} and the positive radial
divergence show that $B_d$ is unbounded on the inward radius ending at
$R\zeta$, for every dyadic root $\zeta$. It cannot be holomorphically
continued through such a point: a holomorphic extension would be bounded
on a smaller closed neighborhood of that point.

Dyadic roots of unity are dense on the unit circle. If the original
analytic germ extended through any point of $|z|=R$, it would extend
through a small arc containing a dyadic point, contradicting the preceding
paragraph. This proves Theorem~\ref{sbd:xtm:thm:main-boundary}.

The stronger growth \eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:superpole} also excludes meromorphic
continuation at each dyadic point. A meromorphic extension has either a
removable singularity or a pole of finite order locally; neither can have
this radial growth. Density then rules out meromorphic continuation through
any arc. (Manuscript~01 argues the last step differently: a smaller arc
would contain infinitely many dyadic blow-up points, incompatible with the
locally discrete poles of a meromorphic function.) These boundary points
are not isolated singularities, so the terminology ``isolated essential
singularity'' does not apply.

\subsection{A general transfer principle (source~01)}
\label{sbd:edge:sec:transfer}
Manuscript~01 isolates the analytic step from the graph enumeration. The
lemma gives the limit \eqref{sbd:edge:eq:radial-ratio} without a rate, and
applies to any series built from coherent dyadic blocks.

\begin{lemma}[Coherent dyadic blocks; source~01]
\label{sbd:edge:lem:transfer}
Suppose, for $|w|<1$ and an integer $q_0\ge1$,
\[
 F(w)=E(w)+\sum_{q\ge q_0}\alpha_qw^{2^{q-1}+1}\Pi_q(w),
\]
where $E$ and every $\Pi_q$ are polynomials, $\alpha_q>0$, and the series
converges locally uniformly. Suppose $\Pi_q\to\Pi$ uniformly on $|w|\le1$,
$\Pi(1)>0$, and
\[
 S(r)=\sum_{q\ge q_0}\alpha_qr^{2^{q-1}+1}\longrightarrow\infty
 \quad(r\uparrow1).
\]
Then for every dyadic root of unity $\zeta$,
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:edge:eq:transfer}
 \frac{F(r\zeta)}{S(r)}\longrightarrow\zeta\Pi(\zeta),
 \qquad
 \frac{F(r)}{S(r)}\longrightarrow\Pi(1).
\end{equation}
If $\Pi(\zeta)\ne0$ for every dyadic root of unity $\zeta$, then the unit
circle is a natural boundary for $F$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Fix a dyadic root $\zeta$. For all sufficiently large $q$,
$\zeta^{2^{q-1}}=1$. After moving finitely many blocks into the bounded
head, the sum at $r\zeta$ is
\[
 \zeta\sum_q\alpha_qr^{2^{q-1}+1}\Pi_q(r\zeta).
\]
Given $\delta>0$, choose $Q$ such that
$\sup_{|w|\le1}|\Pi_q(w)-\Pi(w)|<\delta$ for $q\ge Q$. After division by
$S(r)$, the contribution of the finitely many indices below $Q$ tends to
zero. The error from the remaining indices is at most $\delta$, because
their normalized positive weights sum to at most one. Finally
$\Pi(r\zeta)\to\Pi(\zeta)$. This proves the first limit; the case $\zeta=1$
proves the second.

If $\zeta\Pi(\zeta)\ne0$, the first limit implies $|F(r\zeta)|\to\infty$. A
holomorphic continuation through $\zeta$ would be bounded on a smaller
neighborhood and is therefore impossible. Every open arc contains such a
dyadic point. A continuation through any point of the unit circle would
contain an arc and would continue through one of those points, a
contradiction.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Second proof of Theorem~\ref{sbd:xtm:thm:main-boundary}, without the rate (source~01)]
By \eqref{sbd:edge:eq:amplitude-log}, $A_q\to\infty$; in particular
$\sum_qA_q=\infty$, and monotonicity of the nonnegative terms gives
$S(r)\to\infty$. Apply Lemma~\ref{sbd:edge:lem:transfer} to
$F(w)=B_d(Rw)$ with $\alpha_q=A_q$ and $\Pi_q(w)=P_q(Rw)$. By
\eqref{sbd:edge:eq:profile-limit} the limiting profile is
$\Pi(w)=1/(1-aRw)$, which is nonzero on the closed unit disk. Hence
\[
 \frac{B_d(Rr\zeta)}{S(r)}\longrightarrow\frac{\zeta}{1-aR\zeta},
 \qquad
 \frac{B_d(Rr)}{S(r)}\longrightarrow\frac1{1-aR},
\]
whose quotient is \eqref{sbd:edge:eq:radial-ratio}, and the circle
$|z|=R$ is a natural boundary. With Lemma~\ref{sbd:xtm:lem:laplace} and
\eqref{sbd:edge:eq:amplitude-log}, $B_d(R\e^{-t})\sim S_d(t)/(1-aR)$ gives
$\log B_d(R\e^{-t})=\mathcal T(L)+O_d(L)$; at a fixed dyadic $\zeta$ the
quotient in \eqref{sbd:edge:eq:radial-ratio} tends to a nonzero constant, so
its logarithmic magnitude is bounded and convergent, which gives
Theorem~\ref{sbd:xtm:thm:main-growth}. Finally
$\log(t^M|B_d(R\e^{-t}\zeta)|)=\mathcal T(L)-ML+O_{d,\zeta}(L)\to+\infty$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[The first two defects; sources~05 and~01]\label{sbd:xtm:ex:d01}
For $d=0$ one has $c=2$, $a=1$, and $R=1/4$. Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:d0-phase}
 \frac{B_0(\tfrac14\e^{-t}\zeta)}{B_0(\tfrac14\e^{-t})}
 =\frac{3\zeta}{4-\zeta}+O_\zeta(t).
\end{equation}
For $\zeta=-1$ the limit is $-3/5$; for $\zeta=\ii$ it is $(-3+12\ii)/17$.
The negative real direction exhibits cancellation between individual
coefficients, but that cancellation has an explicit nonzero limiting
effect; the common phase of late dyadic blocks is what makes the limit
determinable (manuscript~01). For $d=1$, $c=4$, $a=3$, and $R=27/256$. The
radius becomes smaller, while the first two terms of the logarithmic radial
growth remain exactly the same.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[What the boundary theorem says; source~01]\label{sbd:edge:rem:says}
The conclusion prohibits analytic continuation through every point of the
circle. The explicit radial blow-up has been proved at the dense set of
points $R\zeta$, $\zeta$ a dyadic root of unity. It does not assert
pointwise radial blow-up at every irrational angle. Also, these are
nonisolated boundary singularities; the classification of isolated
singularities into removable points, poles and essential singularities is
not directly applicable.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Why the error stops at $O(L)$; sources~01 and~05]\label{sbd:edge:rem:orderL}
The dominant block indices lie around
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:edge:eq:dominant-index}
 q\approx\frac{L+\log L}{\log2}.
\end{equation}
Changing $q$ by one changes $2^{q-1}$ by a factor of two, and the core
bounds \eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:core-bounds} allow a linear uncertainty in
$\log u_m$, which contributes at order $L$. Thus
\eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:growth-main} does not imply an expansion with a fixed next
coefficient times $L$. Both manuscripts add that the competition between
adjacent blocks ``remains visible at order $L$'', so that a smooth saddle
alone cannot justify a constant coefficient at that order; they argue this
heuristically. It is stated as a question in
Section~\ref{sbd:xtm:sec:future}, where
Proposition~\ref{sbd:wr:prop:orderL} [write] proves it under a hypothesis on
$u_m$ and gives unconditional bounds for the coefficient.
\end{remark}

\section{Large boundary values on every arc}
\label{sbd:xtm:sec:lp}

The radial theorem already implies singularities at a dense set. We now
prove the stronger comparison in Theorem~\ref{sbd:xtm:thm:main-lp}. The
issue is quantitative: one very large value on a tiny interval would not by
itself give a fixed proportion of the radial $L^p$ size. Manuscript~05 uses
a growing grid of dyadic points and a derivative bound.

\subsection{A derivative bound from positivity}
Write $F_t=B_d(R\e^{-t})>0$ and
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:angular-derivative}
 D_t=\sum_{n\ge1}n b_n^{(d)}R^n\e^{-tn}.
\end{equation}
Absolute convergence inside the disk implies
\[
 \left|\frac\partial{\partial\theta}
 B_d(R\e^{-t+\ii\theta})\right|\le D_t.
\]
Let $f(t)=\log F_t$. Differentiation of the positive series gives
$-f'(t)=D_t/F_t$ and $f''(t)\ge0$: the latter is the variance of the index
under the probabilities proportional to $b_n^{(d)}R^n\e^{-tn}$. Convexity
implies
\[
 f(t/2)-f(t)\ge\frac t2\frac{D_t}{F_t}.
\]
By \eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:growth-main}, with $L$ replaced by $L+h$ at $t/2$,
$f(t/2)-f(t)=O_d(L)$. Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:derivative-bound}
 D_t\le C_d\frac Lt F_t
\end{equation}
for sufficiently small $t$.

\subsection{A moving dyadic grid}
Let $q_* =\lfloor(L+\log L)/h\rfloor+1$ as before. Choose a fixed large $D$
so that the relative weight of all blocks $q<q_b=q_*-D$ is $O_d(t)$, using
Lemma~\ref{sbd:xtm:lem:concentration}. Set $K_b=2^{q_b-1}$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:grid-size}
 2^{-D-1}\frac Lt<K_b\le2^{-D}\frac Lt.
\end{equation}
Consider all $K_b$ roots of unity $\zeta$ with $\zeta^{K_b}=1$, and their
periodic angular lifts $\theta_j=2\pi j/K_b$ for $j\in\mathbb Z$. For every
block $q\ge q_b$, its $K_q$ is a multiple of $K_b$, and the alignment
\eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:phase-align} holds simultaneously for all these $\zeta$.

The early blocks have modulus at most a fixed constant times their positive
amplitude sum, because the $P_q$ are uniformly bounded. Their contribution
divided by $S_d(t)$ is $O_d(t)$; the finite polynomial is smaller still.
Also $\sup_{q\ge q_b}(m/K_q)=O_d(t)$ by \eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:grid-size}. The
profile estimate is uniform in $\zeta$, so
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:uniform-grid}
 \sup_{\zeta^{K_b}=1}
 \left|\frac{B_d(R\e^{-t}\zeta)}{S_d(t)}
               -\zeta g(R\e^{-t}\zeta)\right|=O_d(t).
\end{equation}
The rational profile has modulus at least $1/(1+aR)$ on the closed disk
$|z|\le R$. Combining this with \eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:positive-transfer}, there
is $\kappa_d>0$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:grid-lower}
 |B_d(R\e^{-t}\zeta)|\ge\kappa_d F_t
 \quad\hbox{for every }\zeta^{K_b}=1
\end{equation}
when $t$ is sufficiently small.

This estimate is uniform over a grid whose order grows with $t$. It is
proved directly by discarding negligible early blocks; it does not assume
that the constant in the fixed-root Theorem~\ref{sbd:xtm:thm:main-boundary}
was uniform in the order of the root.

\subsection{Proof of the local comparison}
By \eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:derivative-bound}, the modulus stays at least
$\kappa_dF_t/2$ within angular distance
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:arc-width}
 \delta_t=\eta_d\frac tL
\end{equation}
of each grid point, for a fixed sufficiently small $\eta_d>0$. Choose
$\eta_d$ also small enough that these intervals are disjoint; this is
possible by \eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:grid-size}, since the grid spacing is
$2\pi/K_b$.

Every fixed open interval $I$ contains a closed interval $J$ of positive
length in its interior. The number of grid points in $J$ is at least
$|J|K_b/(4\pi)$ for large $K_b$. Their intervals of radius $\delta_t$ lie in
$I$ for small $t$. Their total length is bounded below by a positive
constant depending only on $d$ and $I$, because $K_b\delta_t$ is bounded
below in \eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:grid-size}--\eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:arc-width}. Hence
\[
 \int_I|B_d(R\e^{-t+\ii\theta})|^p\dd\theta
 \ge c_{d,I}(\kappa_dF_t/2)^p.
\]
For the upper bound, coefficient positivity gives
$|B_d(R\e^{-t+\ii\theta})|\le F_t$, so the integral is at most $|I|F_t^p$.
This proves \eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:lp-comparison} for every $p>0$; no norm
inequality restricted to $p\ge1$ has been used. Taking logarithms and using
\eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:growth-main} proves \eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:lp-growth}.

\begin{proof}[Second proof of the logarithmic law \eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:lp-growth} (source~01)]
Set $\widetilde B(w)=B_d(Rw)=\sum_{n\ge1}\beta_nw^n$, where
$\beta_n=b_n^{(d)}R^n\ge0$, and let $r=\e^{-t}$, $s=\e^{-t/2}$. Choose an
angle $\theta_0$ in the interior of $I$ with $\zeta=\e^{\ii\theta_0}$ a
dyadic root of unity. Equation \eqref{sbd:edge:eq:radial-ratio} supplies a
constant $\kappa_\zeta>0$ such that, for all sufficiently small $t$,
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:edge:eq:arc-point}
 |\widetilde B(r\zeta)|\ge\kappa_\zeta \widetilde B(r).
\end{equation}
Nonnegativity and absolute convergence allow differentiation inside the
disk. Since $x\e^{-tx/2}\le2/(\e t)$ for $x\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:edge:eq:angular-derivative}
 \sup_\theta
 \left|\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\widetilde B(r\e^{\ii\theta})\right|
 \le\sum_{n\ge1}n\beta_nr^n
 \le\frac{2}{\e t}\sum_{n\ge1}\beta_ns^n
 =\frac{2\widetilde B(s)}{\e t}.
\end{equation}
Take $\delta=\kappa_\zeta\e t\widetilde B(r)/(8\widetilde B(s))$. Because
$\widetilde B(r)\le\widetilde B(s)$, this width tends to zero and eventually
$(\theta_0-\delta,\theta_0+\delta)\subset I$. On that interval,
\eqref{sbd:edge:eq:angular-derivative} changes $\widetilde B$ by at most
$\kappa_\zeta\widetilde B(r)/4$; in particular
$|\widetilde B(r\e^{\ii\theta})|\ge\kappa_\zeta\widetilde B(r)/2$. For every
fixed $p>0$, integration over this interval gives
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:edge:eq:arc-lower}
 \int_I|\widetilde B(r\e^{\ii\theta})|^p\dd\theta
 \ge
 C_{\zeta,p}\,t\,
 \frac{\widetilde B(r)^{p+1}}{\widetilde B(s)},
\end{equation}
while the upper bound is simply
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:edge:eq:arc-upper}
 \int_I|\widetilde B(r\e^{\ii\theta})|^p\dd\theta
 \le |I|\widetilde B(r)^p.
\end{equation}
The radial growth theorem gives $\log\widetilde B(r)=\mathcal T(L)+O_d(L)$
and $\log\widetilde B(s)=\mathcal T(L+\log2)+O_d(L)$, and direct expansion
shows $\mathcal T(L+\log2)-\mathcal T(L)=O(L)$. Taking logarithms in
\eqref{sbd:edge:eq:arc-lower} and \eqref{sbd:edge:eq:arc-upper} places the
integral between $\exp(p\mathcal T(L)-O_{d,I,p}(L))$ and
$\exp(p\mathcal T(L)+O_{d,I,p}(L))$, as required. This route uses a single
dyadic point; it gives the logarithmic law but not the two-sided
comparison \eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:lp-comparison}, whose lower bound loses the
factor $t\widetilde B(r)/\widetilde B(s)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Strong natural boundary; source~05]\label{sbd:xtm:rem:strong}
Breuer and Simon call the unit circle a strong natural boundary when the
radial local $L^1$ integrals are not uniformly bounded on any nonempty open
arc \cite{breuersimon}. Apply this terminology to $w\mapsto B_d(R_dw)$.
Theorem~\ref{sbd:xtm:thm:main-lp} proves that condition, and more: there is
an actual limit $+\infty$, a comparison with the positive radial value, and
a quantitative logarithmic law for every positive exponent $p$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[source~01]\label{sbd:edge:rem:hardy}
The theorem excludes a bounded radial $L^p$ integral on every fixed nonempty
boundary arc, and in particular excludes membership in every Hardy class
$H^p$, $p>0$, after normalization to the unit disk. For $0<p<1$, only the
integral, not a norm inequality, is used. The conclusion makes no assertion
about the existence or values of radial limits at almost every individual
angle.
\end{remark}

\section{Consequences for generating functions and defect marking}
\label{sbd:xtm:sec:consequences}

\subsection{Algebraicity, differential equations, and labeling}
\begin{corollary}[sources~05 and~01]\label{sbd:xtm:cor:nonholonomic}
For each fixed $d$, $B_d$ is neither algebraic nor D-finite over $\C(z)$.
The sequences $b_n^{(d)}$ and $n!b_n^{(d)}$ are not P-recursive. The
ordinary generating function of $n!b_n^{(d)}$ has radius zero.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
An algebraic function has only finitely many possible finite branch points
and poles: away from the zeros of the leading coefficient and discriminant
of a square-free defining polynomial, the implicit function theorem
supplies continuation. A solution of a linear differential equation with
polynomial coefficients continues locally at every point where the leading
coefficient is nonzero, by the existence theorem for analytic linear
differential equations. Neither can have the natural boundary just proved.

A polynomial-coefficient recurrence for the coefficients translates, by
multiplying by powers of $z$ and summing, to a linear differential equation
with polynomial coefficients for the generating function; initial terms
contribute only a polynomial. Thus $b_n^{(d)}$ is not P-recursive. If
$n!b_n^{(d)}$ had such a recurrence, write it with forward shifts. Division
by $n!$ would replace each factorial quotient by a polynomial in $n$ and
give one for $b_n^{(d)}$. Finally the finite positive radius $R_d$ implies
a subsequence with $(b_n^{(d)})^{1/n}\to R_d^{-1}>0$, while
$(n!)^{1/n}\to\infty$. The labeled ordinary series therefore has radius
zero.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Second route for non-algebraicity (source~01)]
An algebraic function and all its derivatives belong to one
finite-dimensional algebraic extension of $\C(z)$. Linear dependence of
sufficiently many derivatives gives a differential equation over $\C(z)$,
and clearing denominators gives a polynomial-coefficient equation; so an
algebraic series is D-finite, and the D-finite case applies. Manuscript~01
also notes that the equivalence between D-finite series and P-recursive
coefficient sequences follows by coefficient extraction in the
differential equation and the converse construction.
\end{proof}

The non-P-recursiveness conclusion was already proved in
Part~\ref{sbd:part:one} (Theorem~\ref{sbd:thm:nonholonomic}, through the
exponential-jump Lemma~\ref{sbd:lem:jump}); the natural boundary strengthens
its complex-analytic explanation. It does not exclude nonlinear algebraic
differential equations. That is a separate question
(Section~\ref{sbd:xtm:sec:future}).

For labeled counts $\ell_n^{(d)}=n!b_n^{(d)}$, the exponential generating
function is exactly
\[
 \sum_{n\ge1}\ell_n^{(d)}\frac{z^n}{n!}=B_d(z).
\]
Thus the same boundary theorem describes labeled enumeration in its natural
exponential normalization; the ordinary generating function of
$\ell_n^{(d)}$, of radius zero, must not be confused with $B_d$
(manuscript~01).

\subsection{Finite combinations and elementary transformations}
\begin{corollary}[sources~05 and~01]\label{sbd:edge:cor:mixtures}
The radii are strictly decreasing in $d$: $R_0>R_1>\cdots$. If
$\gamma_D\ne0$, the finite linear combination $\sum_{d=0}^D\gamma_dB_d(z)$,
with complex coefficients, has natural boundary $|z|=R_D$. Adding to $B_d$
any function holomorphic on a larger disk also preserves its natural
boundary.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
As a function of real $c>1$,
\[
 \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dc}
 \log\frac{(c-1)^{c-1}}{c^c}=\log(1-1/c)<0,
\]
which gives the monotonicity. All terms with $d<D$ are analytic on a larger
disk than $|z|<R_D$, so continuation of the combination would give
continuation of the nonzero multiple $\gamma_DB_D$, which is impossible.
This argument permits complex coefficients because only one summand has the
smallest radius. The perturbation statement is the same subtraction
argument.
\end{proof}

Changing finitely many coefficients does not alter a natural boundary.
Neither does taking a derivative or a primitive: local continuation of a
derivative can be integrated on a small disk and matched to the original
function by an additive constant, while continuation of a primitive implies
that of its derivative. These facts transfer the result to shifted or
polynomially weighted enumerations without repeating the block proof
(manuscript~05).

\subsection{There is no analytic size-and-defect germ}
For each $n$, only finitely many defects are admissible. Thus
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:bivariate}
 \mathcal B(z,v)=\sum_{n\ge1}\sum_{d\ge0}b_n^{(d)}z^nv^d
\end{equation}
is a well-defined formal series, with a polynomial in $v$ as each
coefficient of $z^n$.

\begin{theorem}[Positive-fugacity obstruction; source~05]\label{sbd:xtm:thm:bivariate}
For every fixed real $v>0$, the series
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:positive-fugacity}
 \sum_{n\ge1}\left(\sum_{d\ge0}b_n^{(d)}v^d\right)z^n
\end{equation}
has radius zero. Consequently \eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:bivariate} is not a
holomorphic germ at $(0,0)$ and has no nontrivial bidisk of convergence.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Fix $D\ge0$. Nonnegativity gives $\sum_db_n^{(d)}v^d\ge v^Db_n^{(D)}$.
Therefore
\[
 \limsup_{n\to\infty}
 \left(\sum_db_n^{(d)}v^d\right)^{1/n}\ge R_D^{-1}.
\]
But
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:radius-asymptotic}
 R_D=\frac1{c_D}\left(1-\frac1{c_D}\right)^{c_D-1}
 \sim\frac1{\e c_D}\longrightarrow0.
\end{equation}
Since $D$ is arbitrary, the limsup is infinite. A holomorphic germ in two
variables would have a convergent power series on some positive-radius
bidisk, contradicting any sufficiently small positive choice of $v$.
\end{proof}

Keeping a defect fixed and summing over defects have different analytic
consequences. More generally, let $w_d\ge0$ be weights independent of $n$,
with $w_d>0$ for infinitely many $d$. Arbitrarily strong decay of $w_d$
cannot restore a positive size radius for $\sum_{n,d}w_db_n^{(d)}z^n$:
comparison with each fixed $D$ for which $w_D>0$ proves this extension. The
argument does not assert zero radius for every nonzero \emph{complex} $v$,
where signed or complex cancellation requires separate analysis.

\section{Arithmetic sections}
\label{sbd:edge:sec:subsequences}

\subsection{Filtering one residue class of power-of-two step (source~01)}
For a step $r\ge1$ and a residue $0\le r_0<r$ define
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:edge:eq:filtered-series}
 B_d^{(r,r_0)}(z)
 =\sum_{\substack{n\ge1\\ n\equiv r_0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ r)}}
       b_n^{(d)}z^n,\qquad
 G_d^{(r,r_0)}(z)
 =\sum_{\ell\ge0}b_{r\ell+r_0}^{(d)}z^\ell.
\end{equation}
(Here $r$ is a step, not Part~\ref{sbd:part:one}'s deletion number.) The
convention $b_0^{(d)}=0$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:edge:eq:subsequence-composition}
 B_d^{(r,r_0)}(z)=z^{r_0}G_d^{(r,r_0)}(z^r).
\end{equation}
In this subsection $r=2^s$ with $s\ge0$. Let $j_0\in\{0,\ldots,2^s-1\}$ be
the unique residue with $j_0\equiv r_0-1\pmod{2^s}$.

\begin{theorem}[Dyadic subsequences retain a natural boundary; source~01]
\label{sbd:edge:thm:subsequences}
Let $r=2^s$. The radii of $B_d^{(r,r_0)}$ and $G_d^{(r,r_0)}$ are
respectively $R_d$ and $R_d^r$, and their circles of convergence are
natural boundaries. For every dyadic root of unity $\zeta$,
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:edge:eq:filtered-ratio}
 \lim_{t\downarrow0}
 \frac{B_d^{(r,r_0)}(R_d\e^{-t}\zeta)}{B_d^{(r,r_0)}(R_d\e^{-t})}
 =
 \zeta^{1+j_0}
 \frac{1-(a_dR_d)^r}{1-(a_dR_d)^r\zeta^r}.
\end{equation}
The logarithm of the positive radial value $B_d^{(r,r_0)}(R_d\e^{-t})$ has
the same two-term expansion $\mathcal T(L)+O_{d,s}(L)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For all sufficiently large $q$, $K=2^{q-1}$ is divisible by $r$. The
allowed offsets $j=n-K-1$ in the block are then exactly $j\equiv j_0\pmod
r$. The proof of Lemma~\ref{sbd:xtm:lem:block-profile} (or of
\eqref{sbd:edge:eq:profile-limit}), restricted to these offsets, gives the
uniform limit
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:edge:eq:filtered-profile}
 P_{q}^{(r,r_0)}(Rw)\longrightarrow
 \sum_{\ell\ge0}(aRw)^{j_0+r\ell}
 =
 \frac{(aRw)^{j_0}}{1-(aRw)^r}
 \qquad(|w|\le1),
\end{equation}
where $P_q^{(r,r_0)}$ keeps the terms of $P_q$ with $j\equiv j_0$. Indeed
the sum of the absolute coefficient errors over a subset of offsets is no
larger than over all offsets. The limiting profile at $w=1$ is the positive
number $(aR)^{j_0}/(1-(aR)^r)$.

The same block amplitudes $A_q$ and weights $\e^{-t(2^{q-1}+1)}$ occur.
Lemma~\ref{sbd:edge:lem:transfer} therefore gives
\eqref{sbd:edge:eq:filtered-ratio}, positive radial divergence, and a
natural boundary for $B_d^{(r,r_0)}$. It also gives
\[
 B_d^{(r,r_0)}(R_d\e^{-t})
 \sim\frac{(aR)^{j_0}}{1-(aR)^r}S_d(t),
\]
which proves the radial expansion.

Absolute convergence and \eqref{sbd:edge:eq:subsequence-composition} show
that $G_d^{(r,r_0)}$ is analytic for $|z|<R_d^r$. If it extended
holomorphically through a point $z_0$ with $|z_0|=R_d^r$, choose any $w_0$
with $w_0^r=z_0$. Composing the extension with $w\mapsto w^r$ and
multiplying by $w^{r_0}$ would extend $B_d^{(r,r_0)}$ through $w_0$,
contradicting its natural boundary. This proves the remaining claims.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[source~01]\label{sbd:edge:ex:parity}
For the even and odd subsequences of the extremal count, both series
\[
 \sum_{\ell\ge0}b_{2\ell}^{(0)}z^\ell,\qquad
 \sum_{\ell\ge0}b_{2\ell+1}^{(0)}z^\ell
\]
have natural boundary $|z|=1/16$. For the corresponding filtered series in
the original variable, the limiting profiles are $aRw/(1-(aRw)^2)$ and
$1/(1-(aRw)^2)$, respectively, with $aR=1/4$.
\end{example}

\begin{corollary}[source~01]\label{sbd:edge:cor:dfinite}
None of $B_d^{(2^s,r_0)}$ or $G_d^{(2^s,r_0)}$ is D-finite. In particular
they are not algebraic over $\C(z)$, and every arithmetic subsequence
$(b_{2^s\ell+r_0}^{(d)})_{\ell\ge0}$ is not P-recursive.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
As for Corollary~\ref{sbd:xtm:cor:nonholonomic}: a D-finite function
continues analytically away from the finitely many zeros of its leading
coefficient, so a full circle of noncontinuability is impossible, and an
algebraic series is D-finite.
\end{proof}
The subsequence statement supplies additional non-P-recursive sequences;
the case of $B_d$ itself is Corollary~\ref{sbd:xtm:cor:nonholonomic}.

\subsection{A single dominant block, and sections of every step [write]}
\label{sbd:wr:sec:dominant}
Theorem~\ref{sbd:edge:thm:subsequences} uses that every late block starts at
the same residue modulo $2^s$. For a step with an odd factor the starting
residue $K_q+1\bmod r$ cycles with $q$, and both manuscripts leave such
sections open (manuscript~05's Question~5: ``root-of-unity filters can
introduce cancellation''; manuscript~01's Question~4). The writing step
closes the natural-boundary part of that question. The input is a
comparison of neighbouring blocks that is sharper than the envelope
\eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:amplitude-envelope}; it holds because the ratio
$u_{m+1}/u_m$ is known to within a factor $m+1$.

For $t>0$ put $L=\log(1/t)$, $X=(L+\log L)/h$, and
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:wr:eq:phase}
 \omega_q(t)=A_q\e^{-t(K_q+1)},\qquad
 y_0(L)=\lceil X\rceil-X\in[0,1),\qquad
 q_t=1+\lceil X\rceil,
\end{equation}
so that $S_d(t)=\sum_{q\ge q_0}\omega_q(t)$ and $tK_{q_t}=L\,2^{y_0(L)}$.

\begin{lemma}[A single dominant block; write]\label{sbd:wr:lem:dominant}
Fix $d\ge0$ and $\delta\in(0,1/2]$, and let $c_\delta=(1-2^{-\delta})/4$.
For all sufficiently small $t$ with $\delta\le y_0(L)\le1-\delta$,
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:wr:eq:dominant}
 \sum_{q\ne q_t}\omega_q(t)\le\e^{-c_\delta L}\,\omega_{q_t}(t).
\end{equation}
In particular $S_d(t)=\omega_{q_t}(t)\bigl(1+O(\e^{-c_\delta L})\bigr)$ along
such $t$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Neighbouring amplitudes.} Proposition~\ref{sbd:prop:marked} with
$n=m+1$ and $t=1$ gives $\sum_ss\,u(m+1,s)=u_m(2^m-m)$; every class counted
by $u_{m+1}$ has between $1$ and $m+1$ sources, so
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:wr:eq:core-ratio}
 \frac{2^m-m}{m+1}\le\frac{u_{m+1}}{u_m}\le2^m-m,\qquad
 \log\frac{u_{m+1}}{u_m}=hm+O(\log m).
\end{equation}
By \eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:amplitude-stirling},
$\log A_q=\log u_{q+d}-(q+d)\log c-\tfrac12(q-1)h+O_d(1)$, with
$\log c=(d+1)h$. Hence $\log A_{q+1}-\log A_q=hq+O_d(\log q)$, and since
$K_{q+1}-K_q=K_q$,
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:wr:eq:increment}
 \log\omega_{q+1}(t)-\log\omega_q(t)=hq-t2^{q-1}+\epsilon_q,\qquad
 |\epsilon_q|\le C_d\log q\quad(q\ge q_0).
\end{equation}

\emph{Blocks above $q_t$.} Write $q=q_t+k$ with $k\ge0$. Then
$hq=L+\log L+h(1+y_0+k)$ and $t2^{q-1}=L2^{y_0+k}$, so the increment
\eqref{sbd:wr:eq:increment} equals
$L(1-2^{y_0+k})+\log L+h(1+y_0+k)+\epsilon_q$. Here
$2^{y_0+k}-1\ge2^k(2^\delta-1)$, while
$\log L+h(2+k)+C_d\log(2X+2+k)\le2^{k-1}(2^\delta-1)L$ for all $k\ge0$ once
$L$ is large. So every increment with $q\ge q_t$ is at most
$-(2^\delta-1)L/2$, and
$\sum_{q>q_t}\omega_q\le2\e^{-(2^\delta-1)L/2}\omega_{q_t}$ for large $L$.

\emph{Blocks below $q_t$.} For $q\le q_t-1$ one has
$t2^{q-1}\le L2^{y_0-1}\le L2^{-\delta}$. If $hq\ge
\tfrac12(1+2^{-\delta})L+C_d\log q$, the increment is at least
$\tfrac12(1-2^{-\delta})L>0$. Otherwise $q\le(1+2^{-\delta})L/(2h)+O(\log
L)$, so $q_t-q\ge(1-2^{-\delta})L/(2h)-O(\log L)$ and
$t2^{q-1}=L2^{y_0+q-q_t}\le1$ for large $L$; then the increment is at least
$hq-1-C_d\log q\ge1$ once $q\ge q_1$, a constant depending on $d$. Hence
$\omega_q$ increases on $q_1\le q\le q_t$. At $q=q_t-1$,
$hq=L+\log L+hy_0\ge L$, so the last increment is at least
$L(1-2^{-\delta})-C_d\log q\ge\tfrac12(1-2^{-\delta})L$. Therefore
$\sum_{q_1\le q<q_t}\omega_q\le q_t\e^{-(1-2^{-\delta})L/2}\omega_{q_t}$,
with $q_t=O(L)$. The finitely many $q_0\le q<q_1$ have
$\omega_q\le A_q=O_d(1)$, whereas $\log\omega_{q_t}\ge\tfrac h2q_t^2-C_dq_t-
L2^{y_0}-t\ge L^2/(2h)-O_d(L)$ by \eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:amplitude-envelope}.
Since $2^\delta-1\ge1-2^{-\delta}$, the three bounds give
\eqref{sbd:wr:eq:dominant} for small $t$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Every arithmetic section has a natural boundary; write]
\label{sbd:wr:thm:sections}
Fix $d\ge0$, a step $r\ge1$ and a residue $0\le r_0<r$. Then
$B_d^{(r,r_0)}$ has radius $R_d$ and $G_d^{(r,r_0)}$ has radius $R_d^r$, and
both circles of convergence are natural boundaries. More precisely, let
$j_q\in\{0,\ldots,r-1\}$ be the residue of $r_0-1-K_q$ modulo $r$. For
every dyadic root of unity $\zeta$ and every $\delta\in(0,1/2]$, as
$t\downarrow0$ with $\delta\le y_0(L)\le1-\delta$,
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:wr:eq:sections}
 B_d^{(r,r_0)}(R_d\e^{-t}\zeta)
 =\zeta\,\omega_{q_t}(t)\Bigl[\frac{(a_dR_d\zeta)^{j_{q_t}}}{1-(a_dR_d\zeta)^r}
 +o(1)\Bigr],
\end{equation}
and $|B_d^{(r,r_0)}(R_d\e^{-t}\zeta)|\to\infty$ along such $t$, with
logarithm $\mathcal T(L)+O_{d,r}(L)$. Consequently every arithmetic
subsequence $(b_{r\ell+r_0}^{(d)})_{\ell\ge0}$ and
$((r\ell+r_0)!\,b_{r\ell+r_0}^{(d)})_{\ell\ge0}$ is not P-recursive, and
$B_d^{(r,r_0)}$, $G_d^{(r,r_0)}$ are neither D-finite nor algebraic.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
In block $q$ the coefficients with $n=K+1+j\equiv r_0\pmod r$ are those with
$j\equiv j_q\pmod r$. Let $P_q^{(r,r_0)}$ keep exactly these terms of $P_q$.
Since $(az)^{j_q}/(1-(az)^r)=\sum_{j\equiv j_q}(az)^j$, the three error terms
in the proof of Lemma~\ref{sbd:xtm:lem:block-profile} (proxy against
geometric coefficients, geometric tail beyond $K$, covering error) only lose
nonnegative terms when restricted to $j\equiv j_q$, so for a fixed
$T\in(R,1/a)$
\[
 \sup_{|z|\le T}\Bigl|P_q^{(r,r_0)}(z)-\frac{(az)^{j_q}}{1-(az)^r}\Bigr|
 =O_{d,T}(m/K),
\]
uniformly in $q$, and the $P_q^{(r,r_0)}$ are uniformly bounded there, by
$M_d$ say. The block decomposition \eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:block-decomposition}
restricted to $n\equiv r_0$ reads
$B_d^{(r,r_0)}(z)=E^{(r,r_0)}(z)+\sum_qA_q(z/R)^{K+1}P_q^{(r,r_0)}(z)$ with a
polynomial head. Let $\zeta^{2^j}=1$. By \eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:phase-align},
for $q>j$ the block $q$ contributes $\zeta\,\omega_q(t)P_q^{(r,r_0)}(R\e^{-t}\zeta)$
at $z=R\e^{-t}\zeta$; the finitely many other blocks and the head are
$O(1)$. Hence
\[
 \Bigl|B_d^{(r,r_0)}(R\e^{-t}\zeta)-\zeta\,\omega_{q_t}(t)
 P_{q_t}^{(r,r_0)}(R\e^{-t}\zeta)\Bigr|
 \le M_d\sum_{q\ne q_t}\omega_q(t)+O(1).
\]
By Lemma~\ref{sbd:wr:lem:dominant} the right side is
$O(\e^{-c_\delta L}\omega_{q_t}(t))+O(1)=o(\omega_{q_t}(t))$. Moreover
$P_{q_t}^{(r,r_0)}(R\e^{-t}\zeta)=(aR\e^{-t}\zeta)^{j_{q_t}}/(1-(aR\e^{-t}\zeta)^r)
+O(m/K)$, and replacing $\e^{-t}$ by $1$ changes this rational value by
$O_r(t)$ uniformly in $j_{q_t}<r$. This proves \eqref{sbd:wr:eq:sections}.
The bracket has modulus at least $(aR)^{r-1}/(1+(aR)^r)-o(1)>0$, and
$\omega_{q_t}(t)=S_d(t)(1+o(1))\to\infty$ with
$\log S_d(t)=\mathcal T(L)+O_d(L)$ by Lemma~\ref{sbd:xtm:lem:laplace}.
Since $y_0(L)$ runs through $[0,1)$ infinitely often as $L\to\infty$ (it is
continuous between its jumps, at which it returns from $0$ to $1$), such
$t\downarrow0$ exist, and $B_d^{(r,r_0)}$ is unbounded on the radius to
$R\zeta$. As in Section~\ref{sbd:xtm:sec:boundary}, it has no holomorphic
continuation through $R\zeta$, and density of the dyadic roots gives the
natural boundary. Its coefficients are nonnegative and dominated by those of
$B_d$, so it is analytic in $|z|<R$ and its radius is exactly $R$. The
statements for $G_d^{(r,r_0)}$ follow from
\eqref{sbd:edge:eq:subsequence-composition} exactly as in the proof of
Theorem~\ref{sbd:edge:thm:subsequences}. A D-finite or algebraic function
cannot have a natural boundary (Corollary~\ref{sbd:xtm:cor:nonholonomic}),
so $(b_{r\ell+r_0}^{(d)})$ is not P-recursive; a polynomial recurrence for
$((r\ell+r_0)!\,b_{r\ell+r_0}^{(d)})$, written with forward shifts and
divided by $(r\ell+r_0)!$, would give one for $(b_{r\ell+r_0}^{(d)})$,
because $(r(\ell+i)+r_0)!/(r\ell+r_0)!$ is a polynomial in $\ell$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[What Theorem~\ref{sbd:wr:thm:sections} does not say; write]\label{sbd:wr:rem:sections}
For $r=2^s$, $j_q=j_0$ for all large $q$ and \eqref{sbd:wr:eq:sections}
recovers Theorem~\ref{sbd:edge:thm:subsequences} along the restricted radii.
When $r$ has an odd factor $r'>1$, $j_q$ depends on $2^{q-1}\bmod r'$, which
is periodic in $q$ with period the order of $2$ modulo $r'$; the bracket in
\eqref{sbd:wr:eq:sections} then changes with $q_t$. Whether the radial ratio
at a dyadic root then has a limit is decided by
Corollary~\ref{sbd:wr:cor:ratio} below; nothing is proved about the radii
with $y_0(L)$ near $0$, where two blocks compete, beyond the sign change
noted after that corollary, or about non-dyadic angles. The theorem is the
writing step's; it has not been externally reviewed. Its only
inputs are Lemma~\ref{sbd:xtm:lem:block-profile}, the Stirling form
\eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:amplitude-stirling}, Part~\ref{sbd:part:one}'s marked-source
identity and the envelope \eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:amplitude-envelope}. A numerical
illustration for $d=0$ and $r=3$ is in Section~\ref{sbd:xtm:sec:computation}.

\begin{corollary}[Radial ratios of a section at a dyadic root; intake]
\label{sbd:wr:cor:ratio}
Fix $d\ge0$, a step $r\ge1$, a residue $0\le r_0<r$ and a dyadic root of
unity $\zeta$ of order $2^e$. Let $j_q$ be as in
Theorem~\ref{sbd:wr:thm:sections}, let $J$ be the set of residues that
$j_q$ takes for infinitely many $q$, and put
\[
 \kappa_j(\zeta)=\zeta^{1+j}\,\frac{1-(a_dR_d)^r}{1-(a_dR_d\zeta)^r}
 \qquad(0\le j<r),
\]
so that $\kappa_j(\zeta)/\kappa_{j'}(\zeta)=\zeta^{j-j'}$. Write
$\varrho(t)=B_d^{(r,r_0)}(R_d\e^{-t}\zeta)/B_d^{(r,r_0)}(R_d\e^{-t})$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item For every $j\in J$ and $\delta\in(0,1/2]$, $\varrho(t)\to\kappa_j(\zeta)$
as $t\downarrow0$ with $\delta\le y_0(L)\le1-\delta$ and $j_{q_t}=j$, and
such $t$ exist arbitrarily close to~$0$.
\item $\varrho(t)$ has a limit as $t\downarrow0$ if and only if
$\zeta^{j_q}$ is eventually constant, that is, $2^e$ divides $j-j'$ for all
$j,j'\in J$. The limit is then the common value of the $\kappa_j(\zeta)$,
$j\in J$, and it is attained along all $t\downarrow0$, with no restriction
on $y_0(L)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Write $a=a_d$, $R=R_d$, $B=B_d^{(r,r_0)}$ and $g_j(w)=w^j/(1-w^r)$. Then
$g_j(aR)\ge(aR)^{r-1}>0$ and $\zeta g_j(aR\zeta)=\kappa_j(\zeta)g_j(aR)$.
Since $K_q=2^{q-1}$ is eventually periodic modulo $r$, so is $j_q$; hence
$J$ is not empty and $j_q\in J$ for all large $q$.

(i) Along $\delta\le y_0(L)\le1-\delta$, \eqref{sbd:wr:eq:sections} at
$\zeta$ and at $1$ gives
$B(R\e^{-t}\zeta)=\omega_{q_t}(t)\bigl[\zeta g_{j_{q_t}}(aR\zeta)+o(1)\bigr]$
and $B(R\e^{-t})=\omega_{q_t}(t)\bigl[g_{j_{q_t}}(aR)+o(1)\bigr]$, and the
second bracket is at least $(aR)^{r-1}/2$ for small $t$; so
$\varrho(t)=\kappa_{j_{q_t}}(\zeta)+o(1)$. Since $X=(L+\log L)/h$ increases
continuously to $\infty$ with $L$, every large $q$ is $q_t$ for some $t$
with $y_0(L)=1/2$ (take $X=q-3/2$); taking the $q$ with $j_q=j$ gives the
required $t$.

(ii) If $\zeta^j\ne\zeta^{j'}$ for some $j,j'\in J$, part~(i) gives the two
subsequential limits $\kappa_j(\zeta)\ne\kappa_{j'}(\zeta)$, so there is no
limit. Conversely, let $\kappa$ be the common value of $\kappa_j(\zeta)$,
$j\in J$, and fix $\epsilon>0$. As in the proof of
Theorem~\ref{sbd:wr:thm:sections}, for $q>e$ block $q$ contributes
$\zeta\,\omega_q(t)P_q^{(r,r_0)}(R\e^{-t}\zeta)$ at $R\e^{-t}\zeta$ and
$\omega_q(t)P_q^{(r,r_0)}(R\e^{-t})$ at $R\e^{-t}$, and
$P_q^{(r,r_0)}(R\e^{-t}w)=g_{j_q}(aRw)+O_d(m/K)+O_r(t)$ uniformly in
$|w|\le1$ and $q$. Choose $Q>e$ with $j_q\in J$ for $q\ge Q$ and the
$O_d(m/K)$ term below $\epsilon$ there. Because
$\zeta g_{j_q}(aR\zeta)=\kappa g_{j_q}(aR)$ for $q\ge Q$, and the head and
the blocks $q<Q$ contribute $O_Q(1)$,
\[
 \bigl|B(R\e^{-t}\zeta)-\kappa B(R\e^{-t})\bigr|
 \le(1+|\kappa|)\bigl(\epsilon+O_r(t)\bigr)S_d(t)+O_Q(1).
\]
All coefficients of $B$ and of every $P_q^{(r,r_0)}$ are nonnegative, so
if $\epsilon\le(aR)^{r-1}/4$, then for small $t$
$B(R\e^{-t})\ge\sum_{q\ge Q}\omega_q(t)P_q^{(r,r_0)}(R\e^{-t})
\ge\tfrac12(aR)^{r-1}\bigl(S_d(t)-O_Q(1)\bigr)$. As $S_d(t)\to\infty$,
$\limsup_{t\downarrow0}|\varrho(t)-\kappa|\le2(1+|\kappa|)\epsilon/(aR)^{r-1}$
for every small $\epsilon$, so $\varrho(t)\to\kappa$.
\end{proof}
The converse in~(ii) is the argument of Lemma~\ref{sbd:edge:lem:transfer},
with the positive weights $\omega_q(t)g_{j_q}(aR)$; it uses neither
Lemma~\ref{sbd:wr:lem:dominant} nor a restriction on $y_0(L)$. For
$r=2^s$, $J=\{j_0\}$ and~(ii) is \eqref{sbd:edge:eq:filtered-ratio}.

its check of the write (item~13 of Section~\ref{sbd:xtm:sec:future}), which
observed that Theorem~\ref{sbd:wr:thm:sections} already answers the

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]

\subsection{What the computations test}
Each manuscript ships its own program, written independently of the other
and of Part~\ref{sbd:part:one}'s (\nolinkurl{code/03-source-extremum-verify.py}
and \nolinkurl{code/02-source-edge-verify.py}; the report's README gives the
delivery names and a safe rerun). Both use exact integers for finite
enumeration and identities, and both are error-detection companions to the
proofs: no numerical result is used to prove a natural boundary, an
asymptotic remainder, or the local $L^p$ theorem. In their packages,
manuscript~05's checks are reproduced by \code{python3 verify.py} from the
package directory (its PDF by \code{pdflatex} twice or its \code{build.sh}),
and manuscript~01's by \code{python code/verify.py} and
\code{python code/make_article_tables.py} (or its \code{Makefile}); neither
program accesses the network. In this report these files carry the prefixes
of the provenance note, and running them in place would write into the
report's directories; the README gives a rerun on a scratch copy.

Manuscript~05's program compares the marked-source sieve with the
independent source-deletion recurrence \eqref{sbd:edge:eq:deletion-rec},
and also performs a small exhaustive graph enumeration; these are three
different routes to the same finite objects. The exhaustive method examines
every graph whose arcs point from a larger to a smaller index. It rejects
repeated neighborhoods, collapses the survivors to finite sets, and
deduplicates the resulting transitive sets. Every acyclic isomorphism class
has such an ordering, so this exhausts the small cases independently of
either recurrence.

Manuscript~01's program independently generates finite transitive sets: it
starts with the empty transitive set and, from a transitive set $F$,
adjoins each subset $x\subseteq F$ with $x\notin F$, deduplicating the
resulting transitive sets $F\cup\{x\}$. Every nonempty finite transitive set
can be reduced by deleting a source, so this generation is exhaustive.
Nested immutable sets represent equality directly; no numerical coding with
a truncated rank is used. Its exact arithmetic uses \eqref{sbd:eq:total-rec},
\eqref{sbd:eq:sieve} and \eqref{sbd:eq:defect}, together with the deletion
recurrence, and checks the initial core counts, the source maximum, the
covering inequalities, the finite-defect formula and the elementary bounds
\eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:core-bounds}.

The small rows $u(n,s)$, $n\le7$, are those of Part~\ref{sbd:part:one}'s table
in Section~11.1 (manuscript~05 prints them again, through $n=7$, with $q(n)$
and $s_{\max}(n)$; that table is not repeated). In particular the
source-maximizer counts begin $1,1,2,1,20,20,10,2,7128,8316,7128,4455,1980,
594,108,9$ (Part~\ref{sbd:part:one}'s table in Section~11.2). The jump from
$b_8^{(0)}=2$ to $b_9^{(0)}=7128$ reflects a change from a three-vertex to a
four-vertex core. At the radius $1/4$, the first few entries in each
sufficiently late block provide most of its analytic mass, as quantified by
the profile $1/(1-z)$.

\section{First corrections inside a dyadic block}
\label{sbd:xtm:app:expansions}

This section is manuscript~05's Appendix~A. The natural-boundary proof
needs only an $O(m/K)$ error. The following refinements make the mechanism
more explicit and provide additional finite algebra for verification.

\begin{proposition}[First block correction; source~05]\label{sbd:xtm:prop:block-correction}
For every fixed $0<T<1/a$,
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:block-correction}
\begin{split}
 P_q(z)={}&\frac1{1-az}
 +\frac1K\left\{
   \frac{(m-1)az}{(1-az)^2}
       -\frac{cz}{(1-az)^3}\right\}\\
 &\hspace{20mm}+O_{d,T}(m^2/K^2),\qquad |z|\le T.
\end{split}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
From \eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:consecutive-ratios}, expansion of the logarithm gives
\[
 v_{q,j}=a^j\left[1+\frac1K
    \left\{j(m-1)-\frac c{2a}j(j+1)\right\}
    +\text{remainder}\right].
\]
To justify summation uniformly, fix $\rho<1$ with $(a+\epsilon)T<\rho$,
where $\epsilon$ is as in the proof of
Lemma~\ref{sbd:xtm:lem:block-profile}. Split the sum at
$J=\lfloor K^{1/4}\rfloor$. On $j\le J$, Taylor's formula for each logarithm
and then for the exponential bounds the weighted remainder by
\[
 \frac{C_{d,T}}{K^2}
   (m^2j^2+j^4+m^2j+j^3)\rho^j.
\]
Here $m=O_d(\log K)$ ensures the expansions are uniform. The sum of these
errors is $O_{d,T}(m^2/K^2)$. The tails $j>J$ of the exact product, the
leading geometric series, and the polynomial correction are exponentially
small in $J$, using the same ratio bound. The covering error is smaller
still. Finally
\[
 \sum_{j\ge0}jw^j=\frac w{(1-w)^2},\qquad
 \sum_{j\ge0}j(j+1)w^j=\frac{2w}{(1-w)^3},
\]
with $w=az$, give \eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:block-correction}.
\end{proof}
(In this proof $\rho$ is a local constant and $w$ a local variable.)

\begin{proposition}[First amplitude correction; source~05]\label{sbd:xtm:prop:amplitude-correction}
With the normalization \eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:amplitude-definition},
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:amplitude-correction}
 A_q=u_mc^{-m}R\sqrt{\frac{ca}{2\pi K}}
 \left[1+\frac{E_{c,m}}K+O_d(m^4/K^2)\right],
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:xtm:eq:amplitude-coefficient}
 E_{c,m}=(m-1)-\frac a{2c}m(m-1)
        +\frac{1/c-1-1/a}{12}.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The product quotient in \eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:binomial-ratio} has logarithm
\[
 \frac1K\left((m-1)-\frac a{2c}m(m-1)\right)
       +O_d(m^3/K^2).
\]
Exponentiation changes the relative remainder to $O_d(m^4/K^2)$. Stirling's
expansion gives
\[
 \binom{cK}{K}R^K
 =\sqrt{\frac c{2\pi aK}}
 \left[1+\frac{1/c-1-1/a}{12K}+O_d(K^{-2})\right].
\]
Multiply these formulas and the prefactor $u_mRac^{-m}$.
\end{proof}

Neither proposition alone provides a further universal term in
\eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:phase-main}. Summing a first correction introduces
weighted averages of $m/K$ and $1/K$ over competing blocks.
Lemma~\ref{sbd:xtm:lem:concentration} controls their order, but a limiting
coefficient would require additional information about those averages.

\section{Further questions and research}
\label{sbd:xtm:sec:future}

The natural-boundary question of Part~\ref{sbd:part:one} is settled by the
preceding proofs. The following questions merge the two manuscripts' lists
(manuscript~05's Section~10, ten items, and manuscript~01's Section~11,
nine items) with each other, with the related questions of
Part~\ref{sbd:part:one}'s Section~\ref{sbd:sec:questions}, and with the
claims of either manuscript that are argued but not proved. Each is
credited; the sources' status is relative to their inspected repository and
sources, not a claim that every formulation is historically open.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\arabic*.},leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{The term of order $L$ and the discrete saddle} (05, Q1; 01,
Q1 and Section~7.3). Determine the next term after $\mathcal T(L)$ in
\eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:growth-main}, including its dependence on the position of
the continuous maximizer relative to the integers. A sharper asymptotic for
$u_m$ can refine the block amplitudes (manuscript~01: combine a fully
verified precise asymptotic for $u_m$ with
\eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:amplitude-stirling}), but the maximizing block index is
an integer. Both manuscripts assert that rounding by a bounded amount
changes the logarithm by order $L$, so that a smooth saddle alone cannot
give a constant coefficient there, and ask for the correct phase variable
and any periodic or switching behavior, and for an expression uniform
through the crossover of two neighbouring dominant blocks. They argue this
only heuristically (Remark~\ref{sbd:edge:rem:orderL}).
Proposition~\ref{sbd:wr:prop:orderL} below [write] proves it under a
hypothesis on $u_m$ and bounds the coefficient unconditionally. Missing: a
proof of the hypothesis \eqref{sbd:wr:eq:alpha} (or of the oscillation
without it), and the $o(L)$ terms.

\item \textbf{Pointwise behavior at nondyadic angles} (05, Q2; 01, Q2). The
theorems prove a natural boundary everywhere and radial blow-up at dyadic
angles. They do not say that every irrational angle has the same radial
limit or magnitude. At a root of unity of odd order the block phases cycle;
at an irrational angle they may behave much more irregularly. Characterize
limsup and liminf radial growth there and at typical angles, retaining
possible cancellation between blocks; determine whether radial magnitudes
are unbounded and whether normalized limits exist on suitable subsequences.

Q3). Manuscript~05's proof uses a grid with spacing of order $t/L$ and shows

proves a profile on that scale. Study the function on windows

individual block frequencies: is there an explicit limiting transition
profile, or a family of subsequential profiles, including where two

identity makes $p=2$ a useful first target, but the competition between
neighboring dyadic blocks must still be controlled.

\begin{proposition}[The order-$L$ term; write]\label{sbd:wr:prop:orderL}
Let $\varphi(y)=1+y-2^y$ and let $y_0(L)$ be as in \eqref{sbd:wr:eq:phase}.
Fix $d\ge0$. As $t\downarrow0$, with $L=\log(1/t)$:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item unconditionally,
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:wr:eq:orderL-bounds}
 \bigl(-3+\varphi(y_0(L))\bigr)L-o(L)\le
 \log B_d(R_d\e^{-t})-\mathcal T(L)\le
 \bigl(-2+\varphi(y_0(L))\bigr)L+o(L);
\end{equation}
\item if, for a constant $\alpha$,
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:wr:eq:alpha}
 \log u_m=\tfrac h2m^2+\alpha m+o(m)\qquad(m\to\infty),
\end{equation}
then
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:wr:eq:orderL}
 \log B_d(R_d\e^{-t})=\mathcal T(L)
 +\Bigl(\frac\alpha h-\frac32+\varphi(y_0(L))\Bigr)L+o(L).
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
On $[0,1]$, $\varphi\ge0$ vanishes only at the endpoints and attains its
maximum $\varphi^*=1-(1+\log\log2)/\log2=0.08607\ldots$ at
$y^*=-\log_2\log2$, and $y_0(L)$ takes every value in $[0,1)$ along sequences
$L\to\infty$. Hence, under \eqref{sbd:wr:eq:alpha}, $(\log
B_d(R_d\e^{-t})-\mathcal T(L))/L$ has $\liminf$ equal to $\alpha/h-3/2$ and
$\limsup$ equal to $\alpha/h-3/2+\varphi^*$: the remainder of
\eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:growth-main} has no fixed coefficient of $L$, but
oscillates with the fractional part of $(L+\log L)/\log2$. The same holds,
with the same constants and independently of $d$, for every fixed $d\ge0$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By \eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:positive-transfer},
$\log B_d(R\e^{-t})=\log S_d(t)+\log g(R)+o(1)$, so it suffices to treat
$S_d(t)=\sum_q\omega_q(t)$. By \eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:amplitude-stirling},
$\log A_q=\log u_{q+d}-(q+d)(d+1)h-\tfrac12(q-1)h+O_d(1)$. Inserting the
bounds \eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:core-bounds}, $\tfrac h2(m-1)(m-2)\le\log u_m\le
\tfrac h2m(m-1)$ with $m=q+d$, gives
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:wr:eq:A-envelope}
 \tfrac h2q^2-3hq-C_d\le\log A_q\le\tfrac h2q^2-2hq+C_d,
\end{equation}
and \eqref{sbd:wr:eq:alpha} gives
$\log A_q=\tfrac h2q^2+(\alpha-\tfrac32h)q+o(q)$. Since $S_d$ is increasing
in each $A_q$, both parts follow from the claim: \emph{if
$\log A_q=\tfrac h2q^2+\beta q+e_q$ with $e_q=o(q)$ and $|e_q|\le C'q$, then
$\log S(t)=\mathcal T(L)+(\beta/h)L+L\varphi(y_0(L))+o(L)$.}

To prove the claim write $X=(L+\log L)/h$ and $q=1+X+y$, so that $y$ runs
through $y_0(L)+\mathbb Z$, and $t(K_q+1)=L2^y+t$. Expanding $\tfrac h2q^2$
and $\beta q$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{sbd:wr:eq:term}
 \log\omega_q(t)=\mathcal T(L)+\frac\beta hL+L\varphi(y)
 +\tfrac h2(1+y)^2+(1+y)\log L+\beta(1+y)+\frac{(\log L)^2}{2h}
 +\frac\beta h\log L-t+e_q.
\end{equation}
For the lower bound take the single term $y=y_0(L)\in[0,1)$; all terms
after $L\varphi(y)$ are then $O((\log L)^2)+o(L)$. For the upper bound,
$\varphi$ is concave with $\varphi(0)=\varphi(1)=0$, so $\varphi\le0$ off
$[0,1]$, and $y_0(L)$ is the only point of $y_0(L)+\mathbb Z$ in $[0,1)$;
hence $\varphi(y)\le\varphi(y_0(L))$ for every admissible $y$. For $|y|\le Y$,
a fixed bound, there are at most $2Y+2$ terms, each at most
$\mathcal T(L)+(\beta/h)L+L\varphi(y_0(L))+o(L)$. For $y>Y\ge3$,
$\varphi(y)\le-2^{y-1}$, while the other terms of \eqref{sbd:wr:eq:term}
are $O\bigl((1+y)^2+(1+y)\log L+(\log L)^2\bigr)+C'(X+1+y)$; for $Y$ with
$2^{Y-2}>C'/h+2$ and large $L$ these terms sum to at most
$\exp(\mathcal T(L)+(\beta/h)L-2L)$. For $y<-Y$, $1+y<0$ and $q\ge1$ gives
$|1+y|\le X$; then
$L\varphi(y)+(1+y)\log L+\tfrac h2(1+y)^2
\le(1+y)\bigl(L+\log L+\tfrac h2(1+y)\bigr)\le-\tfrac12|1+y|L$, so the term is
at most $\mathcal T(L)+(\beta/h)L-|1+y|(L/2-|\beta|-C')+C'X+O((\log L)^2)$;
there are at most $X+1=O(L)$ such terms, and for $Y$ with
$(Y-1)/3>C'/h+2$ their sum is again at most
$\exp(\mathcal T(L)+(\beta/h)L-2L)$ for large $L$. This proves the claim.
Part~(i) follows with $\beta=-3h$ and $\beta=-2h$ ($e_q$ bounded), part~(ii)
with $\beta=\alpha-\tfrac32h$. Finally $\varphi'(y)=1-h2^y$ vanishes at
$y^*=-\log_2h\in(0,1)$, where $\varphi(y^*)=1-\log_2h-1/h=\varphi^*$, and
$y_0(L)=\lceil X\rceil-X$ decreases continuously from near $1$ to $0$ on
each interval between consecutive integer values of $X$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Numerical evidence for \eqref{sbd:wr:eq:alpha}; write]\label{sbd:wr:rem:alpha}
The bounds \eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:core-bounds} only give $\alpha\in[-3h/2,-h/2]$
if $\alpha$ exists. From the exact values of $u_m$ shipped with this report
($m\le64$), $\log(u_{m+1}/u_m)-hm$ equals $-0.3974857\ldots$ to eight digits
for $30\le m\le63$, which suggests \eqref{sbd:wr:eq:alpha} with
$\alpha=-0.3974857\ldots-h/2\approx-0.7441$, hence a coefficient of $L$
oscillating between about $-2.574$ and $-2.487$ for every $d$. This is a
numerical observation, not a proof; Wagner's asymptotic enumeration
\cite{wagner,wagner13}, which the intake did not read, may settle it. A
synthetic check with $A_q=\exp(\tfrac h2q^2+\beta q)$ at $L$ up to $2\cdot10^5$
reproduces \eqref{sbd:wr:eq:orderL}, with the difference between the two
sides of order $(\log L)^2/L$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The mechanism (source~01)}
The mechanism behind the answer has three parts. The source-covering
estimate reduces exact graph counts to a binomial expression with a
uniformly tiny relative error. The normalized binomial expression has a
geometric profile across each late dyadic block. At dyadic roots of unity,
the block starting exponents eventually have a common phase. The block
amplitudes diverge, so the common profile forces nonzero radial growth at a
dense set of boundary points. The elementary chain construction determines
the quadratic logarithmic size of those amplitudes, and a discrete
exponential-sum estimate then gives the first two radial growth terms. The
arc-mean and subsequence results follow from the additional arguments
stated above.

For a reader interested only in the natural boundary, manuscript~01 names
the necessary chain: the finite collapse (Lemma~\ref{sbd:lem:collapse}) and
the intrinsic core identity \eqref{sbd:eq:union-core}; the covering estimate
\eqref{sbd:xtm:eq:covering-bound}, giving the exact-to-binomial comparison;
Lemma~\ref{sbd:xtm:lem:core-bounds} and
Proposition~\ref{sbd:edge:prop:amplitude}, giving $A_q\to\infty$; the
uniform profile \eqref{sbd:edge:eq:profile-limit} on the closed disk; and
Lemma~\ref{sbd:edge:lem:transfer}, giving dense radial singularities. The
exact sieve is used for reproducibility and contextual counting. The full
entropy theorem of Part~\ref{sbd:part:one}, a gap theorem, and a precise
unrestricted asymptotic formula for $u_m$ are not premises of this proof.

\section{Suggested concise OEIS-facing statements}\label{sbd:app:oeis}

\paragraph{A182220.}
For every $n\ge1$, $a(n)=n-\lceil\log_2n\rceil$.
Proof: if $k$ vertices are sources, all $n$ distinct out-neighborhoods
are subsets of the $n-k$ nonsources, so $n\le2^{n-k}$. Conversely,
take an extensional acyclic core on $m=\lceil\log_2n\rceil$ vertices
and choose $n-m$ unused subsets as new source neighborhoods. Since
$n>2^{m-1}$ for $m\ge1$, any resulting family covers the core, so it has
exactly $n-m$ sources. The case $n=1$ is immediate. The upper bound
already appears in Tomescu's 2011 thesis, printed page 29.

\paragraph{A182162.}
Let $T(n,k)$ denote the labeled count. For $q=\lceil\log_2n\rceil$,
\[
 T(n,n-q)=n!\,\mathrm{A001192}(q)\binom{2^q-q}{n-q}.
\]
More generally, for $d\ge0$ and $k=n-q-d\ge1$, formula
\eqref{sbd:eq:defect}, multiplied by $n!$, gives an exact $(d+1)$-term
expression. These are specializations of the marked-source sieve,
with the extremal case also admitting the direct Boolean-core bijection.

\paragraph{Boundary sequence for a possible separate entry.}
The unlabeled extremizer sequence begins
\[
 1,1,2,1,20,20,10,2,7128,8316,7128,4455,1980,594,108,9,\ldots.
\]
It satisfies $b_{2^m}^{(0)}=\mathrm{A001192}(m)$,
$\liminf(b_n^{(0)})^{1/n}=1$, and $\limsup(b_n^{(0)})^{1/n}=4$,
and it is not P-recursive. No assertion is made that this sequence
currently lacks an OEIS entry; a sequence-value and literature search
should precede any new-entry proposal.
