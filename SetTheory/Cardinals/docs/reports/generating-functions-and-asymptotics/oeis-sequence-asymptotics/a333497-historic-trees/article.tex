\begin{abstract}
For the all-one family $H_r^{(r)}=H_r^2$, we prove the $m=2$ and $m=3$ instances of Burghart and Wagner's 2026 historic-tree coefficient conjecture, where $r=m+1$, and disprove every instance with $m\ge29$ for the specified counting sequences. For reduced $5$- and $7$-historic trees, the respective equivalents are $30(n+2)!\rho_3^{-n-3}$ and $140(n+3)!\rho_4^{-n-4}$. Both have rigorously nonzero corrections oscillating in $\log n$, convergent local complex-power expansions, all fixed coefficient grades with exact Gamma ratios, and qualified eventual inverse brackets. Astashova's earlier real-axis blowup laws are credited. For every ODE order $r\ge30$, an elementary spectral estimate and a closed sign-variation cone exclude convergence of the actual all-one orbit to the normalized pole equilibrium. Exact rational certificates cover the finite bridge at orders $30$ through $37$. The spectrum itself has antecedents in B-urns and higher-order Emden--Fowler theory; the obstruction uses the specific initial data. Exact checks and replayable certificates are separated from non-certified numerical illustrations. No replacement high-order asymptotic or periodic limiting profile is asserted.
\end{abstract}

\section{The counting problem and the results}\label{his:sec:results}
\subsection{The published target}
Burghart and Wagner's model of reduced $(2m+1)$-historic trees has exponential generating function
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:family}
 H^{(m+1)}=H^2,\qquad H^{(j)}(0)=1\quad(0\le j\le m).
\end{equation}
A reduced tree of size $n$ corresponds to a B-tree history of length $n+m$. Their Conjecture~1 predicts
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:conjecture}
 h_n\sim\frac{(2m+1)!}{(m!)^2}(n+m)!\rho_m^{-n-m-1}.
\end{equation}
The journal statement appears in Section~4 of \cite{bw2026}; its predecessor is Conjecture~10 of \cite{bw2024}. The detailed argument in Sections~2--7 addresses $m=2$, for which
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:ivp}
 H'''(z)=H(z)^2,\qquad H(0)=H'(0)=H''(0)=1.
\end{equation}
Equating exponential coefficients gives
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:recurrence}
 h_0=h_1=h_2=1,\qquad
 h_{n+3}=\sum_{j=0}^n\binom njh_jh_{n-j},\qquad
 H(z)=\sum_{n\ge0}h_n\frac{z^n}{n!}.
\end{equation}
This recurrence identifies the sequence with OEIS A333497 \cite{oeis}. The identification is by recurrence; it is not an assertion that the journal paper names this OEIS entry. The beginning is
\[
1,1,1,1,2,4,8,18,48,144,456,1560,5808,23184,98160,440832.
\]

Two low-order instances hold, but the conjecture as stated for all orders is false. ODE order $r$ equals $m+1$; the historic-tree order is $2m+1$.

\subsection{The third order theorem}
Throughout, powers of $t=\rho-z$ use a logarithm that is real on $t>0$. Put
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:exponents}
 \la=\frac{13+i\sqrt{71}}2,\qquad
 \lab=\frac{13-i\sqrt{71}}2,\qquad
 \alpha=\frac{13}{2},\quad \omega=\frac{\sqrt{71}}2,\quad
 \nu_{kl}=k\la+l\lab.
\end{equation}
A \emph{grade} means the total degree $k+l$ in the two local complex powers. It is held fixed when $n\to\infty$.

\begin{theorem}[Counting asymptotics and convergent local expansion]\label{his:thm:main}
The solution of \eqref{his:eq:ivp} has a finite positive blowup point $\rho$, satisfying
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:rho-bounds}
 180^{1/4}\le\rho\le60^{1/3}.
\end{equation}
Its Taylor series at zero has radius $\rho$, with no other singularity on $|z|=\rho$. There is a uniquely normalized $C\in\C\setminus\{0\}$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:local}
 H(z)=60t^{-3}\mathcal F(Ct^\la,\overline C t^\lab),\qquad
 \mathcal F(X,Y)=\sum_{k,l\ge0}a_{kl}X^kY^l,
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal F$ is holomorphic in a bidisk at $(0,0)$,
\[
 a_{00}=a_{10}=a_{01}=1,\qquad a_{lk}=\overline{a_{kl}}.
\]
For each fixed finite angular interval on a chosen logarithmic sheet, \eqref{his:eq:local} converges for all sufficiently small $|t|$, uniformly on closed subsectors.

For every fixed integer $d\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:count-theorem}
 \frac{h_n}{30(n+2)!\rho^{-n-3}}
 =Q_d(n)+O_d\bigl(n^{-\alpha(d+1)}\bigr),
\end{equation}
where, for all sufficiently large real $x$,
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:Q}
 Q_d(x)=1+
 \sum_{\substack{1\le k+l\le d\\k\ne l}}
 \frac{2a_{kl}C^k\overline C^{\,l}\rho^{\nu_{kl}}}{\Gamma(3-\nu_{kl})}
 \frac{\Gamma(x+3-\nu_{kl})}{\Gamma(x+3)}.
\end{equation}
In particular, $h_n\sim30(n+2)!\rho^{-n-3}$.
\end{theorem}
The factor $2$ in each summand of \eqref{his:eq:Q} is present before conjugate pairing. Both $(k,l)$ and $(l,k)$ are included. All diagonal terms $k=l\ge1$ are excluded because they are analytic monomials at the singularity. The exact Gamma ratios preserve every integer inverse-$n$ correction belonging to a retained grade.

\begin{corollary}[A nonzero logarithmic oscillation]\label{his:cor:oscillation}
With
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:K}
 K=\frac{2C\rho^\la}{\Gamma(3-\la)}\ne0,\qquad
 R_n=\frac{h_n}{30(n+2)!\rho^{-n-3}},
\end{equation}
one has
\begin{align}
 R_n&=1+2\Rea\left\{K\frac{\Gamma(n+3-\la)}{\Gamma(n+3)}\right\}
       +O(n^{-13}),\label{his:eq:first-exact}\\
 &=1+2|K|n^{-13/2}\cos(\arg K-\omega\log n)
       +O(n^{-15/2}).\label{his:eq:first-simple}
\end{align}
Consequently,
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:limsup}
 \limsup_{n\to\infty}n^{13/2}(R_n-1)=2|K|,
 \qquad
 \liminf_{n\to\infty}n^{13/2}(R_n-1)=-2|K|.
\end{equation}
The error $R_n-1$ changes sign infinitely often and is not $O(n^{-7})$.
\end{corollary}

\subsection{Attribution and the logical route}
Astashova's Theorem~3 \cite{astashova2013} already gives the real-axis law
$H(x)\sim60(\rho-x)^{-3}$ for positive blowup solutions of this third-order equation. Her theorem covers orders $3$ and $4$ under the stated Emden--Fowler hypotheses; it is not a theorem for all orders. The self-contained real argument below also proves finite blowup for our selected initial data. It is an alternate derivation, not a claim of priority for the real-axis law.

Real-axis blowup alone does not justify individual coefficient asymptotics. The remaining work is to obtain a convergent holomorphic local representation, prove that the first complex amplitude cannot vanish, exclude competing singularities on the convergence circle, and establish a domain in which singularity analysis applies. Holomorphic Poincar\'e linearization \cite{iy2008}, Pringsheim's theorem, and the standard transfer theorems \cite{fs2009} are used in their usual forms. The fourth-order extension and the high-order obstruction follow in Sections~\ref{his:sec:r4} and \ref{his:sec:obstruction}. The arguments are conventional analytic proofs accompanied by exact checks, not proof-assistant formalizations.

\section{A global real solution and finite blowup}\label{his:sec:real}
\subsection{Desingularization}
For a positive solution set
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:coordinates}
 p=H'H^{-4/3},\qquad q=H''H^{-5/3},\qquad
 \frac{\dd\tau}{\dd x}=H^{1/3}.
\end{equation}
Direct differentiation gives the autonomous planar system
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:planar}
 \dot p=q-\frac43p^2,\qquad
 \dot q=1-\frac53pq,\qquad (p(0),q(0))=(1,1),
\end{equation}
where dots mean $\tau$ derivatives. Its positive quadrant is forward invariant: on $p=0$ the first component equals $q>0$, and on $q=0$ the second equals $1>0$. The unique positive equilibrium is
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:equilibrium}
 p_*=(9/20)^{1/3},\qquad q_*=\frac43p_*^2,
 \qquad p_*q_*=\frac35.
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}[Strict Lyapunov dissipation]\label{his:lem:lyapunov}
On $p\ge0$, $q>0$, let
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:V}
 V(p,q)=\frac56(p-p_*)^2+q-q_*-q_*\log(q/q_*).
\end{equation}
Then $V\ge0$, vanishing only at the equilibrium, and
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:Vdot}
 \dot V=-\frac{20}{9}(p-p_*)^2(p+p_*)
        -\frac53p_*\frac{(q-q_*)^2}{q}.
\end{equation}
The orbit in \eqref{his:eq:planar} exists for every $\tau\ge0$ and converges to $(p_*,q_*)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Write $A=4/3$ and $B=5/3$. At the equilibrium,
\[
 \dot p=(q-q_*)-A(p^2-p_*^2),\qquad
 \dot q=-B\{q(p-p_*)+p_*(q-q_*)\}.
\]
Multiply these equations by $B(p-p_*)$ and $1-q_*/q$, respectively. Their cross terms cancel, giving \eqref{his:eq:Vdot}. The logarithmic term in $V$ tends to infinity both as $q\downarrow0$ and as $q\to\infty$; the quadratic term bounds $p$ above. Thus the orbit stays in a compact sublevel in $p\ge0$, $q>0$. Such a set may meet $p=0$, but the polynomial vector field is regular there and points inward. This causes no loss of continuation.

On this sublevel, \eqref{his:eq:Vdot} bounds the integrals of $(p-p_*)^2$ and $(q-q_*)^2$. Their derivatives are bounded, hence the squared deviations are uniformly continuous. A nonnegative uniformly continuous integrable function tends to zero: otherwise separated intervals of fixed positive area contradict integrability. Applying this observation twice proves convergence. Equivalently, LaSalle's invariance principle applies because the zero set of \eqref{his:eq:Vdot} is a single point.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Reconstruction of the original time}
The construction can be reversed without any blowup assumption. Define
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:reconstruction}
 H(\tau)=\exp\!\left(\int_0^\tau p(s)\dd s\right),\qquad
 x(\tau)=\int_0^\tau H(s)^{-1/3}\dd s.
\end{equation}
For $x=x(\tau)$, direct differentiation using \eqref{his:eq:planar} verifies \eqref{his:eq:ivp} and its initial data. Since $p(\tau)\to p_*>0$, $H$ grows exponentially in $\tau$, and
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:rho}
 0<\rho:=\int_0^\infty H(s)^{-1/3}\dd s<\infty.
\end{equation}
Thus $x(\tau)\uparrow\rho$ and $H(x)\to\infty$. No earlier real obstruction occurs.

Put $v=H^{-1/3}$. In the original variable,
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:vprime}
 v'(x)=-\frac{p(x)}3,\qquad
 v(x)=\frac13\int_x^\rho p(s)\dd s
 \sim\frac{p_*}3(\rho-x).
\end{equation}
Here $p(x)$ means $p(\tau(x))$. It follows that
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:real-leading}
 H(x)\sim\left(\frac3{p_*}\right)^3(\rho-x)^{-3}
       =60(\rho-x)^{-3}.
\end{equation}
All three components required in Astashova's real-axis hypotheses diverge, since $p\to p_*>0$ and $q\to q_*>0$.

\subsection{Analytic bounds for the radius}
The rectangle
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:rectangle}
 \frac35\le p\le1,\qquad \frac{12}{25}\le q\le1
\end{equation}
is forward invariant and contains $(1,1)$. On its four faces,
\[
 \dot p\big|_{p=3/5}=q-12/25\ge0,\quad
 \dot p\big|_{p=1}\le-1/3,\quad
 \dot q\big|_{q=12/25}=1-4p/5\ge1/5,\quad
 \dot q\big|_{q=1}\le0.
\]
Integrating \eqref{his:eq:vprime} gives the useful tail bounds
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:tail-bounds}
 x+3H(x)^{-1/3}\le\rho\le x+5H(x)^{-1/3}
 \qquad(0\le x<\rho).
\end{equation}
At zero these imply $3<\rho<5$, with strictness because $p$ is not identically an endpoint.

A tighter initial bound comes from the exact pole solutions
\[
 P_s(x)=60(s-x)^{-3},\qquad
 (P_s(0),P_s'(0),P_s''(0))=(60s^{-3},180s^{-4},720s^{-5}).
\]
For $s=180^{1/4}$ this initial vector dominates $(1,1,1)$ componentwise; for $s=60^{1/3}$ it is dominated by $(1,1,1)$. The system
\[
 (H,H',H'')'=(H',H'',H^2)
\]
is cooperative in the nonnegative octant. Its comparison principle, up to the first blowup of either solution, therefore yields \eqref{his:eq:rho-bounds}. Numerically, only for orientation,
\[
 3.66284150148\ldots\le\rho\le3.91486764116\ldots.
\]
These bounds are analytic and independent of every fitted value below.

\section{A convergent expansion at the singularity}\label{his:sec:local}
\subsection{Holomorphic linearization}
The Jacobian of \eqref{his:eq:planar} at the equilibrium is
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:jacobian}
 A=\begin{pmatrix}-8p_*/3&1\\-5q_*/3&-5p_*/3\end{pmatrix},\qquad
 \det(\mu I-A)=\mu^2+\frac{13p_*}{3}\mu+\frac{20p_*^2}{3}.
\end{equation}
Its eigenvalues are $p_*(-13\pm i\sqrt{71})/6$. Put
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:a}
 a=\frac{p_*}3=60^{-1/3}.
\end{equation}
Label the eigenvalues $-a\la$ and $-a\lab$. Their convex hull excludes zero. A resonance of degree $k+l\ge2$ would require
$k(-a\la)+l(-a\lab)$ to equal one eigenvalue; equality of real parts would force $k+l=1$. Thus no resonance exists.

The holomorphic Poincar\'e linearization theorem \cite{iy2008} gives holomorphic coordinates $u_+,u_-$ near the equilibrium with
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:linearization}
 \dot u_+=-a\la u_+,\qquad \dot u_-=-a\lab u_-,\qquad
 p=p_*+P(u),\quad P(u)=u_++u_-+O(\|u\|^2).
\end{equation}
Every eigenvector of $A$ has a nonzero first component, so this normalization is possible. Choose conjugate eigenvectors; uniqueness of the normalized nonresonant conjugacy then preserves the real structure. Along our real orbit, $u_- =\overline{u_+}$.

\subsection{Removing the multiplicative correction}
The variable $v=H^{-1/3}$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:vdot}
 \dot v=-\bigl(a+P(u)/3\bigr)v.
\end{equation}
Let $\mathcal L=\la u_+\partial_{u_+}+\lab u_-\partial_{u_-}$. The homological equation
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:S}
 \mathcal L S=-P/p_*,\qquad S(0)=0
\end{equation}
has the unique holomorphic solution
\[
 S_{kl}=-\frac{P_{kl}}{p_*(k\la+l\lab)}\quad(k+l\ge1).
\]
Indeed, the real part of each denominator apart from $p_*$ is $\alpha(k+l)$, so division preserves convergence of the power series. Now
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:w}
 w=v\exp S(u)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \dot w=-aw.
\end{equation}
For a sufficiently late point on the real orbit, $w>0$ and
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:constants}
 u_+=K_+w^\la,\qquad u_-=K_-w^\lab,\qquad K_- =\overline{K_+}.
\end{equation}
If either constant vanished, both would vanish, making the local orbit the equilibrium. Uniqueness would then make the whole planar orbit the equilibrium, contradicting $(p(0),q(0))=(1,1)$. Hence both constants are nonzero.

Let $E(u)=\exp[-S(u)]$ and define
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:Btime}
 B(u)=\sum_{k,l\ge0}\frac{E_{kl}}{1+k\la+l\lab}u_+^ku_-^l.
\end{equation}
Again the denominators preserve convergence; $B(0)=1$ and $(1+\mathcal L)B=E$. The remaining original time is exactly
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:time-exact}
 t=\rho-x=\int_\tau^\infty v(s)\dd s=\frac{w}{a}B(u).
\end{equation}
For example, differentiating the right-hand side gives $-wE=-v$, and its limit at infinity is zero. This proves the identity without a merely formal integration.

\subsection{Analytic inversion and a nonzero linear term}
Set $\eta_+=K_+(at)^\la$, $\eta_-=K_-(at)^\lab$ and seek $w=at\mathcal R(\eta)$. Equation \eqref{his:eq:time-exact} becomes
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:implicit}
 \mathcal R\,B\bigl(\eta_+\mathcal R^\la,\eta_-\mathcal R^\lab\bigr)=1.
\end{equation}
Powers of $\mathcal R$ use the holomorphic logarithm near $1$. At $(\mathcal R,\eta)=(1,0)$ the derivative with respect to $\mathcal R$ equals $1$. The analytic implicit-function theorem gives a unique holomorphic $\mathcal R$ with $\mathcal R(0)=1$. Therefore
\begin{align}
 H&=60t^{-3}F(\eta),\label{his:eq:Fraw}\\
 F(\eta)&=\mathcal R(\eta)^{-3}
 \exp\!\left[3S\bigl(\eta_+\mathcal R(\eta)^\la,
                         \eta_-\mathcal R(\eta)^\lab\bigr)\right].\nonumber
\end{align}
This is a holomorphic function of two independent variables near zero.

The linear coefficients, with $e_j$ denoting a coordinate multi-index, are
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:linearcoeff}
 S_{e_j}=-\frac1{p_*\la_j},\qquad
 B_{e_j}=\frac1{p_*\la_j(1+\la_j)},\qquad
 F_{e_j}=-\frac3{p_*(1+\la_j)},
\end{equation}
where $\la_+=\la$, $\la_-=\lab$. Thus
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:Cnonzero}
 C=-\frac{3K_+a^\la}{p_*(1+\la)}\ne0.
\end{equation}
Absorbing these two nonzero linear coefficients into the arguments of $F$ produces exactly the normalization $\mathcal F$ in \eqref{his:eq:local}.

On every bounded interval of $\arg t$ on a logarithmic sheet,
\[
 |t^{\la_j}|=|t|^{13/2}\exp(-\Ima(\la_j)\arg t)\longrightarrow0.
\]
Hence the two-variable Taylor series converges throughout a sufficiently small corresponding sector. It agrees with the original solution along the positive real approach and therefore, by analytic uniqueness, on their common complex domain. The radius may shrink with the chosen angular interval; no radius uniform over arbitrarily many logarithmic sheets is claimed. In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:first-local}
 H(z)=60t^{-3}\left(1+Ct^\la+\overline C t^\lab+O(|t|^{13})\right).
\end{equation}
The normalization and distinct exponents determine $C$ uniquely on the specified branch.

\section{Universal coefficients and analytic diagonal terms}\label{his:sec:coeffs}
Substitution of \eqref{his:eq:local} into $H'''=H^2$ is justified by local convergence. For a monomial $t^{\nu-3}$, three $z$ derivatives multiply it by
$(3-\nu)(4-\nu)(5-\nu)$ and reduce its exponent by $3$. Isolating the two products with the constant term gives, for $k+l\ge2$,
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:akl}
 D(\nu_{kl})a_{kl}
 =60\!\sum_{\substack{0\le i\le k,\ 0\le j\le l\\
                   (i,j)\ne(0,0),(k,l)}}a_{ij}a_{k-i,l-j},
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:D}
 D(\nu)=(3-\nu)(4-\nu)(5-\nu)-120
       =-(\nu+1)(\nu-\la)(\nu-\lab).
\end{equation}
This recurrence is universal: initial data select $\rho$ and $C$, whereas the normalized $a_{kl}$ depend only on the equation. All denominators at degree at least two are nonzero, because $\Rea\nu_{kl}\ge13$ whereas the roots of $D$ have real parts $-1$ and $13/2$. There are no logarithmic resonance terms in the local expansion.

For example,
\begin{align}
 a_{11}&=-\frac17,&
 a_{20}&=\frac{359+57i\sqrt{71}}{17978},\label{his:eq:first-coeffs}\\
 a_{30}&=\frac{-17559+4223i\sqrt{71}}{78725662},&
 a_{21}&=\frac{2571}{1312394}
           -\frac{42477i\sqrt{71}}{119427854}.\nonumber
\end{align}
The reflected coefficients are their conjugates. The code calculates and checks exact values through degree four and numerical values through degree six.

The diagonal is exceptional for coefficient transfer. If $k=l\ge1$, then
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:diagonal}
 \nu_{kk}=13k,\qquad
 60a_{kk}|C|^{2k}t^{13k-3}
\end{equation}
is an analytic polynomial in $z$ of degree $13k-3$. Its $n$th Taylor coefficient is zero once $n>13k-3$. The convergent sum of the diagonal terms is an analytic \emph{local} background at $t=0$. Its local analyticity does not, by itself, give a global exponentially small error. For the fixed-grade theorem, only finitely many such polynomials need be removed, and their eventual contribution is exactly zero.

The indexing $(k,l)\mapsto\nu_{kl}$ is injective: its real and imaginary parts determine $k+l$ and $k-l$. Later, after integer inverse-$n$ corrections are introduced, different labels can have equal final exponents because $\la+\lab=13$. Such coefficients must be combined; the coincidence introduces no logarithms.

\section{The dominant circle and a transfer domain}\label{his:sec:circle}
\subsection{Identification of the radius}
Write $H(z)=\sum_{n\ge0}b_nz^n$ to avoid confusing ordinary coefficients with $a_{kl}$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:ordinary}
 b_0=b_1=1,\quad b_2=\frac12,\qquad
 b_{n+3}=\frac{\sum_{j=0}^nb_jb_{n-j}}{(n+1)(n+2)(n+3)}.
\end{equation}
Every $b_n$ is strictly positive. The radius $R$ of this Taylor series equals $\rho$. Indeed, real blowup implies $R\le\rho$. If $R<\rho$, Pringsheim's theorem would force a singularity at the positive point $R$, whereas analytic ODE continuation along the finite real solution makes $H$ regular there. This contradiction proves equality. The real solution and $\rho$ were established before this argument, so there is no circular definition.

\subsection{Strict majorization away from the positive point}
Fix $0<r<\rho$ and $\theta\notin2\pi\mathbb Z$. Each of $H,H',H''$ has strictly positive coefficients at all powers, including consecutive powers. Equality in the triangle inequality is therefore impossible, and
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:strict}
 |H^{(j)}(re^{i\theta})|<H^{(j)}(r)\qquad(j=0,1,2).
\end{equation}
By continuity, choose one $0<\eps<r$ so that all three left sides are at most $H^{(j)}(r-\eps)$.

Let $z_0=re^{i\theta}$. The Taylor recurrence at $z_0$ has the same polynomial convolution form as \eqref{his:eq:ordinary}. Its first three coefficients, after taking absolute values, are bounded by those of $H(r-\eps+w)$. Induction in the recurrence gives coefficientwise domination at every degree. The real-centered comparison series has nonnegative coefficients and radius $\rho-r+\eps$: this follows from the same real continuation, blowup, and Pringsheim argument used above. The series at $z_0$ consequently converges in
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:disk}
 |z-z_0|<\rho-r+\eps.
\end{equation}
Since $|\rho e^{i\theta}-z_0|=\rho-r$, this disk contains $\rho e^{i\theta}$ in its interior. Every point on the dominant circle other than $\rho$ is regular.

\subsection{Gluing into a single dented domain}
The continued germs from \eqref{his:eq:disk} agree with the original solution on their intersections with $|z|<\rho$. By uniqueness they agree on sufficiently small overlapping neighborhoods along the circle. Any compact arc avoiding $\rho$ has a finite covering by such neighborhoods and hence an analytic collar. Near $\rho$, the convergent representation of Section~3 supplies the needed punctured sector. Shrinking the collar and the sector and using compactness gives some $\eta>0$ and $0<\vartheta<\pi/2$ such that $H$ is single-valued and holomorphic in the standard Delta domain
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:delta}
 \{z:|z|<\rho+\eta,\ z\ne\rho,\ |\arg(z-\rho)|>\vartheta\}.
\end{equation}
The excluded wedge points outwards along the positive real axis. This local-to-global collar argument is sufficient for every fixed-order transfer. It makes no classification of more distant singularities.

\section{All fixed coefficient grades and the oscillation}\label{his:sec:grades}
\subsection{Exact transfer of each retained monomial}
For a noninteger complex $\nu$, the binomial identity gives
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:gamma-transfer}
 [z^n](\rho-z)^{\nu-3}
 =\rho^{\nu-3-n}\frac{\Gamma(n+3-\nu)}{
                         \Gamma(3-\nu)\Gamma(n+1)}.
\end{equation}
All off-diagonal $\nu_{kl}$ are nonreal, so this identity applies directly. The leading pole has coefficient
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:pole-transfer}
 [z^n]60(\rho-z)^{-3}
 =30(n+1)(n+2)\rho^{-n-3}.
\end{equation}
Multiplying \eqref{his:eq:gamma-transfer} by $60a_{kl}C^k\overline C^{\,l}$ and dividing by \eqref{his:eq:pole-transfer} gives each summand of \eqref{his:eq:Q}, including its factor $2$. Multiplication by $n!$ then gives the exact factorial normalization in \eqref{his:eq:count-theorem}.

\subsection{A remainder valid for every fixed grade}
Fix $d\ge0$. Expand the convergent $\mathcal F$ through degree $d+1$, one grade beyond the desired truncation. Every off-diagonal term of degree $d+1$ has coefficient
\[
 O_d\bigl(\rho^{-n}n^{2-\alpha(d+1)}\bigr),
\]
by \eqref{his:eq:gamma-transfer} and the standard Gamma-ratio estimate. Every diagonal term is eventually zero. The remaining analytic-sector tail is
\[
 O_d\bigl(|t|^{\alpha(d+2)-3}\bigr).
\]
Choose a real noninteger $\beta$ strictly between $\alpha(d+1)-3$ and $\alpha(d+2)-3$. The tail is also $O_d(|t|^\beta)$. The Delta-domain $O$-transfer theorem \cite[Chapter~VI]{fs2009} yields
\[
 O_d(\rho^{-n}n^{-\beta-1})
 =o_d\bigl(\rho^{-n}n^{2-\alpha(d+1)}\bigr).
\]
After division by the exact pole coefficient, this proves \eqref{his:eq:count-theorem}. Keeping one extra grade and using a noninteger tail avoids any borderline integer-exponent transfer convention.

The quantifier is important. The local two-variable series converges near the singularity, but the infinite sum obtained by transferring all grades is \emph{not asserted to converge at fixed $n$}. The theorem is an all-fixed-grade asymptotic result.

\subsection{Nonvanishing and phase winding}
Take $d=1$ in \eqref{his:eq:count-theorem}. Conjugate pairing gives \eqref{his:eq:first-exact}. Since $C\ne0$, $\rho>0$, and the Gamma function has no zeros or pole at $3-\la$, one has $K\ne0$. The fixed-parameter expansion
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:gamma-first}
 \frac{\Gamma(n+3-\la)}{\Gamma(n+3)}
 =n^{-\la}\left(1+\frac{\la(\la-5)}{2n}+O(n^{-2})\right)
\end{equation}
proves \eqref{his:eq:first-simple}. The phase $\arg K-\omega\log n$ winds without bound, while its increment tends to zero. Integers nearest the exponential targets for phase $0$ or $\pi$ approach those phases along subsequences. The cosine therefore has limsup $1$ and liminf $-1$, proving \eqref{his:eq:limsup} and the sign changes.

No pure integer inverse-$n$ corrections precede this oscillation when the exact factor $(n+2)!$ is retained. Expanding that factorial by Stirling would introduce the usual separate Stirling corrections. In contrast, expanding every exact Gamma ratio to any fixed order supplies all requested relative algebraic orders of the form
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:flattened}
 \nu_{kl}+j,\qquad k+l\ge1,\quad k\ne l,\quad j\ge0.
\end{equation}
Only finitely many grades and integer corrections are needed below any fixed real-order cutoff. Coincident exponents in \eqref{his:eq:flattened} are combined as noted in Section~4.

\section{Inverse thresholds with controlled rounding}\label{his:sec:inverse}
\subsection{An eventual inverse bracket}
The coefficient equivalent implies
\[
 \frac{h_{n+1}}{h_n}\sim\frac{n+3}{\rho}>1,
\]
so choose $n_0$ after which $h_n$ is strictly increasing. For sufficiently large $X$, define
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:inverse}
 N(X)=\min\{n\ge n_0:h_n\ge X\}.
\end{equation}
Put
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:Fd}
 \mathcal B(x)=30\Gamma(x+3)\rho^{-x-3},\qquad
 F_d(x)=\log\mathcal B(x)+\log Q_d(x).
\end{equation}
For fixed $d$, $Q_d(x)=1+O(x^{-13/2})>0$ eventually and
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:Fderivative}
 F_d'(x)=\psi(x+3)-\log\rho+O_d(x^{-15/2})
 \sim\log(x/\rho)>0.
\end{equation}
Thus there is a unique sufficiently large root $x_d=x_d(X)$ of $F_d(x_d)=\log X$.

\begin{theorem}[Eventual shrinking ceiling window]\label{his:thm:inverse}
For each fixed $d\ge0$, there are constants $M_d>0$ and $X_d$ such that, for $X\ge X_d$, setting
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:epsilon-inverse}
 \eps_d(X)=M_d\frac{x_d(X)^{-\alpha(d+1)}}{\log x_d(X)}
\end{equation}
gives
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:ceil}
 \left\lceil x_d(X)-\eps_d(X)\right\rceil
 \le N(X)\le
 \left\lceil x_d(X)+\eps_d(X)\right\rceil.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Taking logarithms in \eqref{his:eq:count-theorem}, with $Q_d$ bounded away from zero, gives
$\log h_n=F_d(n)+O_d(n^{-\alpha(d+1)})$.
For integers near $x_d$, the mean value theorem and \eqref{his:eq:Fderivative} show that displacement of size a sufficiently large constant times
$x_d^{-\alpha(d+1)}/\log x_d$ dominates this error. Thus integers strictly below the lower endpoint have $h_n<X$, and integers at or above the upper endpoint have $h_n\ge X$, after increasing the constant if needed. Eventual monotonicity handles all more distant integers, proving the stated ceiling window.
\end{proof}
The constants $M_d$ and $X_d$ are existence constants here. This is not a certified finite-$X$ numerical enclosure. If $x_d$ lies exceptionally near an integer, its ceiling alone need not equal $N(X)$; both sides of \eqref{his:eq:ceil} must be retained. The theorem is deliberately formulated to avoid an unjustified rounding rule.

\subsection{A Lambert W starting point}
For a simple initial estimate, write $L=\log X$ and use the positive branch of Lambert $W$:
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:y0}
 y_0=\frac{L}{W(L/(e\rho))},\qquad
 c=\log(30\sqrt{2\pi})-3\log\rho.
\end{equation}
The shifted Stirling expansion is
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:stirling}
 \log\mathcal B(x)=x\bigl(\log(x/\rho)-1\bigr)
 +\frac52\log x+c+\frac{37}{12x}+O(x^{-2}).
\end{equation}
The coefficient is $37/12$, not the unshifted $1/12$; equivalently it is $B_2(3)/2$. Since $y_0(\log(y_0/\rho)-1)=L$, one correction gives
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:y1}
 y_1=y_0-\frac{(5/2)\log y_0+c}{\log(y_0/\rho)},\qquad
 x_0=y_1+O\!\left(\frac1{y_0\log y_0}\right).
\end{equation}
Indeed $y_1-y_0=O(1)$, and the residual after this step in \eqref{his:eq:stirling} is $O(1/y_0)$, while the derivative is asymptotic to $\log y_0$.

\subsection{The first oscillatory displacement}
Let $x_0$ solve the exact main equation $\log\mathcal B(x_0)=L$ and let $x_1$ solve $F_1(x_1)=L$. Expansion of $\log Q_1$ and the mean value theorem yield
\begin{equation}\label{his:eq:inverse-oscillation}
 x_1-x_0=
 -\frac{2\Rea\{K\Gamma(x_0+3-\la)/\Gamma(x_0+3)\}}
        {\psi(x_0+3)-\log\rho}
 +O\!\left(\frac{x_0^{-13}}{\log x_0}\right).
\end{equation}
The squared first correction is $O(x_0^{-13})$ in the logarithm. Evaluation shifts and derivative corrections are smaller at this scale. Higher grades are handled by the exact $F_d$ equations, or by fixed-order Newton expansions. The inverse accuracy improves with every fixed grade, while integer recovery continues to obey \eqref{his:eq:ceil}.

\section{The exact model and the result}\label{his:o5:sec:model}
\subsection{Sequence and counting convention}
Let $h_0=h_1=h_2=1$ and define
\begin{equation}\label{his:o5:rec}
h_{n+3}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom nk h_kh_{n-k}\qquad(n\ge0).
\end{equation}
Then
\[
(h_n)_{n\ge0}=1,1,1,1,2,4,8,18,48,144,456,1560,5808,\ldots,
\]
which is the defining recurrence and offset-zero sequence A333497
in the OEIS~\cite{oeis}. Its exponential generating function is
\begin{equation}\label{his:o5:ode}
H(z)=\sum_{n\ge0}h_n\frac{z^n}{n!},\qquad
H'''(z)=H(z)^2,\qquad H(0)=H'(0)=H''(0)=1.
\end{equation}
Local analytic existence for a polynomial differential system supplies a
positive Taylor radius, and coefficient comparison proves \eqref{his:o5:rec}.
The OEIS description is the equivalent statement that squaring the EGF
shifts its sequence three places left.

Burghart and Wagner~\cite[Section 4, equation (4)]{bw2026} associate
$H^{(m+1)}=H^2$ with reduced $(2m+1)$-historic trees. The reduction removes
the first $m$ vertices. A reduced tree with $n$ internal vertices therefore
corresponds to a B-tree history of length $n+m$. Here $m=2$: $h_n$ counts
reduced 5-historic trees with $n$ internal vertices, or histories of length
$n+2$. It is not the number of insertion permutations; distinct permutations
can produce the same history.

The paper's previously settled $m=1$ case concerns A007558 and the
second-order equation $H''=H^2$, whose solution is expressed by an elliptic
function. It is a different sequence. The third-order problem treated here is separate from that solved case.

\subsection{Main theorem}
Set

\alpha=\frac{13+i\sqrt{71}}2,\qquad
\beta_{k\ell}=k\alpha+\ell\overline\alpha,\qquad
G_n(b)=\frac{\Gamma(n+3-b)}{\Gamma(3-b)\Gamma(n+3)}.
\end{equation}
For nonnegative integral $b-3$, the last expression is interpreted through
its entire continuation in $b$, equivalently as a binomial coefficient;
for all sufficiently large $n$ it is zero.

\begin{theorem}\label{his:o5:main}
There are a positive real number $\rho$ and a nonzero complex number $C$
with the following properties.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item $\rho$ is the positive-real blow-up time, the Taylor radius of $H$,
and its unique singularity on $|z|=\rho$. Moreover,
\begin{equation}\label{his:o5:bounds}
180^{1/4}\le\rho\le60^{1/3}.
\end{equation}
\item In a slit neighborhood of $\rho$, on the branch real for $z<\rho$,
\begin{equation}\label{his:o5:local-main}
H(z)=60(\rho-z)^{-3}
\left[1+U\left(C(1-z/\rho)^\alpha,
\overline C(1-z/\rho)^{\overline\alpha}\right)\right],
\end{equation}
where $U(q_1,q_2)=\sum_{k+\ell\ge1}a_{k\ell}q_1^kq_2^\ell$
is a convergent bivariate power series with $a_{10}=a_{01}=1$.
\item For every fixed integer $J\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{his:o5:allsectors}
\frac{h_n}{30\Gamma(n+3)\rho^{-n-3}}
=1+2\!\sum_{1\le k+\ell\le J}
 a_{k\ell}C^k\overline C^{\ell}G_n(\beta_{k\ell})
 +O\!\left(n^{-13(J+1)/2}\right).
\end{equation}
The constant in the remainder can depend on $J$. In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{his:o5:first}
h_n=30(n+2)!\rho^{-n-3}
\left[1+4\Rea\{C G_n(\alpha)\}+O(n^{-13})\right].
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

Conjecture 1 of~\cite[p.~10]{bw2026}, since
$(2m+1)!/(m!)^2=5!/(2!)^2=30$.
The number $60$ is the local pole coefficient; the coefficient asymptotic
contains $30$ because $[z^n](1-z)^{-3}=(n+1)(n+2)/2$.

\section{Positive real blow up}\label{his:o5:real}
Let $y(x)=H(x)$ be the real solution while it exists. Initially $y,y',y''$
are positive; because $y'''=y^2$, all remain positive and increasing.
Introduce
\[
u=\frac{y'}{y^{4/3}},\qquad v=\frac{y''}{y^{5/3}},
\qquad \frac{\dd s}{\dd x}=y^{1/3}.
\]
Direct differentiation gives
\begin{equation}\label{his:o5:uv}
\frac{\dd u}{\dd s}=v-\frac43u^2,\qquad
\frac{\dd v}{\dd s}=1-\frac53uv.
\end{equation}
The positive equilibrium is
\[
u_*=(9/20)^{1/3},\qquad v_*=\frac43u_*^2.
\]
With $X=u/u_*$, $Y=v/v_*$ and $\tau=u_*s$,
\begin{equation}\label{his:o5:XY}
\dot X=\frac43(Y-X^2),\qquad
\dot Y=\frac53(1-XY).
\end{equation}
Here a dot means differentiation in $\tau$.

\begin{lemma}\label{his:o5:lyap}
Every positive orbit of \eqref{his:o5:XY} is contained in a compact subset of the
positive quadrant and tends to $(1,1)$ as $\tau\to\infty$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Use the explicit Lyapunov function
\[
V(X,Y)=\frac56(X-1)^2+\frac43(Y-1-\log Y).
\]
Its derivative is the exact identity
\begin{equation}\label{his:o5:Vdot}
\dot V=-\frac{20}{9}\left[
\frac{(Y-1)^2}{Y}+(X-1)^2(X+1)\right].
\end{equation}
A sublevel set bounds $X$ above and bounds $Y$ above and away from zero.
Write $Y\ge a>0$. At $X=\sqrt a$ the first component of the vector field
is nonnegative, so
$X\ge\min\{X(0),\sqrt a\}>0$.
This proves compact containment. Ordinary continuation gives existence
for every $\tau\ge0$.

Integrating \eqref{his:o5:Vdot} shows that the two nonnegative summands on its
right are integrable. Their derivatives are bounded on the compact
trapping set, hence they are uniformly continuous. A uniformly continuous,
nonnegative, integrable function tends to zero: otherwise intervals of a
fixed positive width around a sequence of positive peaks would contradict
integrability. Consequently $X\to1$ and $Y\to1$.
\end{proof}

\section{The dominant singularity is unique}\label{his:o5:dominant}
All $h_n$ in \eqref{his:o5:rec} are strictly positive. Pringsheim's theorem says
that a power series of nonnegative coefficients and finite radius has a
singularity at its positive radius. The solution is analytic on the real
interval before $\rho$, so its Taylor radius cannot be smaller than $\rho$.
Blow-up prevents a larger radius. Thus the radius is exactly $\rho$.

We next prove continuation at every other point of the convergence circle.
Fix $0<r<\rho$ and $\theta\notin2\pi\mathbb Z$. For $j=0,1,2$, every
coefficient of $H^{(j)}$ is positive. In particular its constant and linear
terms have different arguments at $re^{i\theta}$. Strict triangle
inequality gives
\[
|H^{(j)}(re^{i\theta})|<H^{(j)}(r).
\]
By continuity and the strict gaps, there is an $\eps\in(0,r)$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{his:o5:strict}
|H^{(j)}(re^{i\theta})|\le H^{(j)}(r-\eps),\qquad 0\le j\le2.
\end{equation}
Let $d_j=H^{(j)}(z_0)$ for $z_0=re^{i\theta}$. The differential equation
implies the local derivative recurrence
\[
d_{n+3}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom nk d_kd_{n-k}.
\]
The corresponding real derivatives at $r-\eps$ are positive and obey the
same recurrence. Induction from \eqref{his:o5:strict} bounds every $|d_n|$ by that
real derivative. Therefore the Taylor series at $z_0$ converges at least
in
\begin{equation}\label{his:o5:disk}
|z-z_0|<\rho-r+\eps.
\end{equation}
The majorant is $H(r-\eps+w)$; its Taylor series has radius at least
$\rho-r+\eps$, because this disk is contained in $|z|<\rho$ after translation.
It follows that \eqref{his:o5:disk} continues $H$ through $\rho e^{i\theta}$.

On compact arcs separated from $\theta=0$, continuity makes the strict
gaps uniform. A finite collection of these disks supplies continuation
through an annulus near those arcs. The continuations agree on overlaps:
the disks have centers in the original disk, and any nonempty intersection
contains a point on the segment between the centers, hence a point in the
original disk. The identity theorem then glues the local solutions. Section~\ref{his:o5:psisurface} supplies the
remaining punctured sector at $\rho$, with angular half-width greater
than $\pi/2$. Shrink collar neighborhoods that meet this sector so their
overlaps meet the original disk, and glue by the identity theorem.
Compactness of the remaining boundary arc then gives a common outer
radius increment. Together these give a
$\Delta$-domain: for some $\eta>0$ and $0<\vartheta<\pi/2$, $H$ is analytic in
\begin{equation}\label{his:o5:delta}
\{z:|z|<\rho+\eta,\ z\ne\rho,
\ |\arg(z-\rho)|>\vartheta\}.
\end{equation}
A smaller domain may always be taken. This explicit continuation argument
is what permits coefficient transfer; the real blow-up equivalent alone
would not suffice.

\section{A convergent local psi series}\label{his:o5:psisurface}
\subsection{Rescaling and the characteristic exponents}
Put $t=\rho-z$, $\sigma=-\log t$ and $F=t^3H/60$. On the positive real
approach, \eqref{his:o5:profile} gives $F\to1$. Its first two derivatives are
\[
F_\sigma=\frac{t^4y'-3t^3y}{60},\qquad
F_{\sigma\sigma}=\frac{t^5y''-7t^4y'+9t^3y}{60},
\]
and both tend to zero. For $F=1+w$ the exact equation is
\begin{equation}\label{his:o5:autonomous}
w'''+12w''+47w'-60w=60w^2,
\end{equation}
where primes now mean $\sigma$ derivatives. Its characteristic roots are
$1,-\alpha,-\overline\alpha$. The positive root is the displacement of
the blow-up location; the two nonreal roots are stable.

It is useful to put
\begin{equation}\label{his:o5:indicial}
Q(b)=(3-b)(4-b)(5-b),\qquad
D(b)=Q(b)-120=-(b+1)(b-\alpha)(b-\overline\alpha).
\end{equation}
We construct the stable surface rather than treating a formal local
expansion as if convergence were automatic.

\subsection{Coefficient recursion and Catalan majorization}
Let $a_{00}=0$, $a_{10}=a_{01}=1$. For $N=k+\ell\ge2$, define
\begin{equation}\label{his:o5:acoef}
a_{k\ell}=\frac{60}{D(\beta_{k\ell})}
\sum_{p=0}^{k}\sum_{q=0}^{\ell}
 a_{pq}a_{k-p,\ell-q}.
\end{equation}
Terms at the two endpoints vanish. Every term on the right thus has
smaller total degree, making the recursion well-defined.
There is no resonance: the real parts of
$\beta_{k\ell}+1$, $\beta_{k\ell}-\alpha$ and
$\beta_{k\ell}-\overline\alpha$ give
\begin{equation}\label{his:o5:Dbound}
|D(\beta_{k\ell})|\ge
\left(\frac{13N}{2}+1\right)
\left(\frac{13(N-1)}2\right)^2\ge\frac{1183}{2}.
\end{equation}
Write $A_N=\sum_{k+\ell=N}|a_{k\ell}|$ and $M=120/1183$.
Then $A_1=2$ and
\[
A_N\le M\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}A_iA_{N-i}.
\]
The scalar majorant $A(r)=2r+MA(r)^2$ has solution
\[
A(r)=\frac{1-\sqrt{1-8Mr}}{2M}
=\sum_{N\ge1}2^NM^{N-1}\operatorname{Cat}_{N-1}r^N.
\]
It is analytic for $|r|<1/(8M)=1183/960$.
Consequently $U(q_1,q_2)=\sum a_{k\ell}q_1^kq_2^\ell$ converges absolutely
whenever $\max(|q_1|,|q_2|)<1183/960$. Derivatives converge normally on
smaller bidisks.

Define $E=\alpha q_1\partial_{q_1}+\overline\alpha q_2\partial_{q_2}$.
The coefficient recursion verifies, as an analytic identity,
\[
[Q(E)-120]U=60U^2.
\]
It follows that
\begin{equation}\label{his:o5:theta}
\Theta(q_1,q_2)=(U,-EU,E^2U)
\end{equation}
is an invariant analytic surface for the first-order version of
\eqref{his:o5:autonomous}, with intrinsic dynamics
$q_1'=-\alpha q_1$, $q_2'=-\overline\alpha q_2$.
Its tangent vectors at zero are the independent stable eigenvectors
$(1,-\alpha,\alpha^2)$ and $(1,-\overline\alpha,\overline\alpha^2)$.

For reference, the first nonlinear coefficients are
\begin{equation}\label{his:o5:a2}

a_{11}=-\frac17,\qquad a_{02}=\overline{a_{20}}.
\end{equation}
In general $a_{\ell k}=\overline{a_{k\ell}}$ by the conjugate-symmetric
recursion. All these constants lie in $\mathbb Q(i\sqrt{71})$.

\subsection{The actual real orbit lies on this surface}
Adjoin the unstable eigenvector $e_u=(1,1,1)$. The map
\[
(q_1,q_2,p)\longmapsto\Theta(q_1,q_2)+pe_u
\]
has invertible derivative at zero, and hence is an analytic coordinate
chart near zero. Invariance of $p=0$ forces the transverse equation to have
the form
\begin{equation}\label{his:o5:transverse}
p'=p\,a(q_1,q_2,p),\qquad a(0,0,0)=1.
\end{equation}
The factorization follows by analyticity from the vanishing of the
transverse component on $p=0$.

Choose the chart respecting conjugation. For a real state,
$q_2=\overline{q_1}$ and $p$ is real, and the value of $a$ is real.
The actual state $(w,w',w'')$ tends to zero by \eqref{his:o5:profile}, so it stays
in this chart for every sufficiently large $\sigma$. On a sufficiently
late tail $a\ge1/2$. If $p$ were nonzero there, \eqref{his:o5:transverse} would
make $|p|$ grow at least exponentially at rate $1/2$, contradicting
$p\to0$. Therefore $p=0$ on the whole late tail.
The exact stable dynamics gives a constant $c\in\C$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{his:o5:local-c}
H(z)=60t^{-3}\{1+U(ct^\alpha,\overline c\,t^{\overline\alpha})\}.
\end{equation}
The normalization in Theorem~\ref{his:o5:main} is $C=c\rho^\alpha$, using the
real logarithm of the positive $\rho$.

For each fixed $\phi<\pi$, both complex powers tend uniformly to zero as
$t\to0$ in $|\arg t|\le\phi$:
$|t^\alpha|=|t|^{13/2}e^{-(\sqrt{71}/2)\arg t}$, and the conjugate power
has the opposite angular factor. Thus \eqref{his:o5:local-c} converges normally
in a sufficiently small slit sector and is holomorphic there on the fixed
logarithm branch. It agrees with $H$ along a real interval and hence by
analytic continuation. This proves the local continuation claimed in
\eqref{his:o5:delta}. No formal-series convergence assumption is left over.

\subsection{The oscillatory amplitude cannot vanish}
If $c=0$, the stable coordinates vanish, and \eqref{his:o5:local-c} is the exact
pole $H(z)=60(\rho-z)^{-3}$. The identity theorem extends this equality to
the whole Taylor disk. The initial data $H(0)=H'(0)=1$ would then require
$\rho^3=60$ and $\rho^4=180$. Dividing gives $\rho=3$, contradicting
$\rho^3=60$. Hence $c\ne0$ and $C\ne0$.
This argument proves nonvanishing without a numerical fit or a
numerically bounded amplitude.

\section{Transfer and every fixed coefficient order}\label{his:o5:transfer}
\subsection{Exact transfer of a sector}
For any complex $b$, generalized binomial coefficients give
\begin{equation}\label{his:o5:binom}
[z^n](1-z/\rho)^{b-3}
=\rho^{-n}\frac{(3-b)_n}{n!}
=\rho^{-n}\frac{\Gamma(n+3-b)}{\Gamma(3-b)\Gamma(n+1)},
\end{equation}
with the rising-factorial interpretation at exceptional integers.
This is an exact coefficient identity, not an asymptotic approximation.
Multiplication by $60\rho^{-3}n!$ and division by
$30\Gamma(n+3)\rho^{-n-3}$ gives exactly $2G_n(b)$.

Truncate the convergent series $U$ at total degree $J$. In each closed
subsector of the $\Delta$-domain, its tail is
$O(|1-z/\rho|^{13(J+1)/2})$. After multiplication by the pole factor,
the error in $H$ is
\[
O\!\left(|1-z/\rho|^{13(J+1)/2-3}\right).
\]
The big-$O$ transfer theorem for $\Delta$-analytic functions
[FO, Theorem 1, pp.~220--221] (see also
\cite[Chapter VI, Theorem VI.3]{fs2009}) gives coefficient error
$O(\rho^{-n}n^{2-13(J+1)/2})$ in the ordinary coefficients of $H$.
This big-$O$ theorem permits every real remainder exponent, including
nonnegative integers; the integer exception in an individual-power
asymptotic equivalent does not restrict this error estimate.
Multiplying by $n!$ and normalizing gives \eqref{his:o5:allsectors}.
This completes the proof of Theorem~\ref{his:o5:main}.

\subsection{Analytic sectors cancel exactly}
For $k=\ell\ge1$, $\beta_{kk}=13k$ is a positive integer and the
corresponding local contribution is a multiple of
$(1-z/\rho)^{13k-3}$, a polynomial. Its coefficients vanish exactly when
$n>13k-3$. Accordingly, in any fixed sector truncation all diagonal
terms disappear for sufficiently large $n$.
One must not evaluate a quotient of singular gamma factors separately;
\eqref{his:o5:binom} or continuation in $b$ specifies the answer unambiguously.
Off-diagonal exponents have nonzero imaginary part, so their gamma ratios
have no such qualification.

In particular, the degree-two expansion is
\begin{equation}\label{his:o5:degree2}
\frac{h_n}{30(n+2)!\rho^{-n-3}}
=1+4\Rea\{C G_n(\alpha)+a_{20}C^2G_n(2\alpha)\}
+O(n^{-39/2}).
\end{equation}
The missing $a_{11}|C|^2$ term is a genuine analytic-sector cancellation,
not a zero coefficient in the local solution. Nonlinear sectors do not
produce logarithmic factors: the recursion \eqref{his:o5:acoef} has no resonant
denominator.
\begin{writenote}[Added 5 October 2026, batch~103]
\eqref{his:o5:degree2} is the case $d=2$ of \eqref{his:eq:count-theorem}; the cancellation of the diagonal is \eqref{his:eq:diagonal}.
\end{writenote}


\subsection{Expanding the gamma ratios}
Let $B_j(x)$ denote the Bernoulli polynomials with
$te^{xt}/(e^t-1)=\sum_{j\ge0}B_j(x)t^j/j!$. Set
\begin{equation}\label{his:o5:ell}
\ell_r(b)=\frac{(-1)^{r+1}}{r(r+1)}
\{B_{r+1}(3-b)-B_{r+1}(3)\},\qquad r\ge1,
\end{equation}
and define $d_0(b)=1$ and
\begin{equation}\label{his:o5:dj}
jd_j(b)=\sum_{r=1}^{j}r\ell_r(b)d_{j-r}(b).
\end{equation}
For any fixed $b$ that is not an exceptional integer, Stirling's expansion
gives, for every fixed integer $K\ge1$,
\begin{equation}\label{his:o5:gammaasym}
G_n(b)=\frac{n^{-b}}{\Gamma(3-b)}
\left\{\sum_{j=0}^{K-1}\frac{d_j(b)}{n^j}+O(n^{-K})\right\}.
\end{equation}
The first two nonconstant polynomials are
\[
d_1(b)=\frac{b(b-5)}2,\qquad
d_2(b)=\frac{b(b+1)(3b^2-29b+74)}{24}.
\]
Equations~\eqref{his:o5:acoef}, \eqref{his:o5:ell} and \eqref{his:o5:dj} are an explicit
algorithm for every fixed coefficient order.
\begin{writenote}[Added 5 October 2026, batch~103]
The Bernoulli-polynomial recursion \eqref{his:o5:ell}--\eqref{his:o5:dj} is an explicit form not printed in Part~I; $d_1$ is the coefficient in \eqref{his:eq:gamma-first}. The intake checked $d_2$ numerically: at $b=2.3+1.1i$ the residual of \eqref{his:o5:gammaasym} with $K=3$, multiplied by $n^3$, is $21.06$, $21.10$, $21.10$ at $n=10^3,10^4,10^5$.
\end{writenote}


More precisely, to obtain a relative error $O(n^{-R})$ for any prescribed
real $R>0$, choose $J$ with $13(J+1)/2\ge R$. Keep the off-diagonal sectors
of degree at most $J$ with real exponent below $R$. In each such sector
keep the integer corrections with $\Rea\beta_{k\ell}+j<R$, expanding
far enough that its omitted tail has order at most $n^{-R}$.
Only finitely many sector and integer orders are involved. The procedure
determines all constants exactly in terms of $\rho$, $C$, gamma values,
and the rational algebraic coefficients $a_{k\ell}$.

\subsection{What log periodicity means here}
Write $C/\Gamma(3-\alpha)=A e^{i\varphi}$, where $A>0$ by nonvanishing
of $C$ and absence of zeros of the gamma function. Then
\[
4\Rea\!\left\{\frac{C}{\Gamma(3-\alpha)}n^{-\alpha}\right\}
=4A n^{-13/2}\cos\!\left(\varphi-\frac{\sqrt{71}}2\log n\right).
\]
Its log-period is $4\pi/\sqrt{71}$.
Thus the envelope has order $n^{-13/2}$, although the oscillation has
zeros and no pointwise lower bound of that order is asserted.
Using just this leading oscillation in \eqref{his:o5:first} changes its error to
$O(n^{-15/2})$. The exact gamma ratio in \eqref{his:o5:first} retains all integer
corrections in that sector and is needed for the sharper $O(n^{-13})$
statement.

The local bivariate expansion is convergent. The coefficient expansions
just described are Poincar\'e expansions at arbitrary fixed finite order.
Neither convergence of the resulting large-$n$ series nor summation of
infinitely many transferred sectors at fixed $n$ is claimed.

\section{Inverting the growth law}\label{his:o5:invert}
\begin{writenote}[Added 5 October 2026, batch~103]

\end{writenote}

\subsection{The smooth leading inverse}
Define
\[
M(x)=30\Gamma(x+3)\rho^{-x-3},\qquad
D_x=\frac{\dd}{\dd x}\log M(x)=\psi(x+3)-\log\rho,
\]
where $\psi=\Gamma'/\Gamma$. Since $D_x\sim\log x$, $M$ is strictly
increasing eventually. Let $q(X)$ be the inverse on this increasing tail.
The exact constant $\rho$ is used in all inversion assertions.
An exploratory numerical estimate in its place adds an error that these
formulas do not bound.

The mean value theorem applied to \eqref{his:o5:first} gives
\begin{equation}\label{his:o5:inversebasic}
q(h_n)=n+O\!\left(\frac{n^{-13/2}}{\log n}\right).
\end{equation}
Keeping the first correction explicitly sharpens this to
\begin{equation}\label{his:o5:inversecorrection}
q(h_n)=n+\frac{4\Rea\{C G_n(\alpha)\}}{D_n}
+O\!\left(\frac{n^{-13}}{\log n}\right).
\end{equation}
To justify it, take logarithms of \eqref{his:o5:first}; the squared correction is
$O(n^{-13})$. Taylor expansion of $\log M$ about $n$, with
$D_n\asymp\log n$ and $(\log M)''(n)=O(1/n)$, leaves a smaller remainder.
For numerical inversion the smooth leading model can be solved directly
with log-gamma and a monotone scalar method.

\subsection{Corrected smooth inverses at every fixed order}
For a fixed $J\ge1$, let
\[
S_J(x)=1+2\!\sum_{\substack{1\le k+\ell\le J\\k\ne\ell}}
 a_{k\ell}C^k\overline C^\ell
 \frac{\Gamma(x+3-\beta_{k\ell})}
 {\Gamma(3-\beta_{k\ell})\Gamma(x+3)}.
\]
For large real $x$, conjugation makes $S_J(x)$ real,
$S_J(x)=1+O(x^{-13/2})>0$, and $S_J'(x)=O(x^{-15/2})$.
Thus $M_J(x)=M(x)S_J(x)$ is eventually increasing.
Let $q_J$ denote its large inverse. Equation~\eqref{his:o5:allsectors} gives
\begin{equation}\label{his:o5:inverseJ}
q_J(h_n)=n+O\!\left(\frac{n^{-13(J+1)/2}}{\log n}\right).
\end{equation}
These are inverses of explicitly defined smooth models, not a purported
unique interpolation of the discrete sequence.
They provide inversion to any prescribed fixed algebraic order after
choosing $J$ sufficiently large.

\subsection{Explicit Lambert W approximations}
Put
\begin{equation}\label{his:o5:Wdefinitions}
L=\log(X\rho/30),\quad
w=W_0\!\left(\frac{L}{e\rho}\right),\quad
N_0=\frac Lw,\quad d=1+w,\quad
 a=\frac12\log(2\pi N_0).
\end{equation}
All assertions concern $X\to\infty$, so $L,w,N_0$ are positive.
The principal real Lambert branch solves $we^w=L/(e\rho)$; hence
$N_0\log(N_0/(e\rho))=L$ and $d=\log(N_0/\rho)$.
Writing $N=q(X)+2$, the exact inverse equation is
\[
\log\Gamma(N+1)-N\log\rho=L.
\]
Stirling's formula gives
\begin{equation}\label{his:o5:stirling}
\log\Gamma(N+1)-N\log\rho
=N\log\!\frac{N}{e\rho}
+\frac12\log(2\pi N)+\frac1{12N}+O(N^{-3}).
\end{equation}
Expansion around $N_0$ yields
\begin{align}
q(X)&=N_0-2-\frac ad+O\!\left(\frac1{N_0d}\right),\label{his:o5:Wone}\\
q(X)&=N_0-2-\frac ad
-\frac{a^2/(2d^2)-a/(2d)+1/12}{N_0d}
+O\!\left(\frac1{N_0^2d}\right).\label{his:o5:Wtwo}
\end{align}
Here $a/d$ stays bounded. The uncorrected $N_0-2$ alone has an order-one
shift; it is not an error-$o(1)$ inverse.
At $X=h_n$, the error in \eqref{his:o5:inversebasic} can be added to the displayed
Lambert errors and is smaller than either of them.

For completeness, the Lambert expansion also has an all-fixed-order
construction. Put $u=N_0^{-1}$ and seek
$\delta=N-N_0=\sum_{j\ge0}\delta_j u^j$ formally. Take $\delta_0=-a/d$.
Using the Bernoulli numbers $B_{2k}=B_{2k}(0)$, define the formal residual
\begin{align*}
\mathcal F(\delta,u)={}&d\delta+a
+\sum_{r\ge2}\frac{(-1)^r\delta^r u^{r-1}}{r(r-1)}
+\frac12\sum_{r\ge1}\frac{(-1)^{r+1}\delta^r u^r}{r}\\
&+\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{B_{2k}}{2k(2k-1)}
 u^{2k-1}(1+u\delta)^{-(2k-1)}.
\end{align*}
For $j\ge1$, let $\delta_j$ be $-1/d$ times the coefficient of $u^j$
in $\mathcal F(\sum_{r<j}\delta_ru^r,u)$. Only finitely many terms are
needed for each coefficient. This gives
$\delta_1=-[\delta_0^2/2+\delta_0/2+1/12]/d$, agreeing with
\eqref{his:o5:Wtwo}. At every fixed $K\ge0$, Stirling's finite-order remainder
and $d\asymp\log N_0$ justify
\begin{equation}\label{his:o5:Wall}
q(X)=N_0-2+\sum_{j=0}^{K}\frac{\delta_j}{N_0^j}
+O\!\left(\frac1{N_0^{K+1}d}\right).
\end{equation}
For example, the residual after the chosen cancellation is
$O(N_0^{-K-1})$, and division by the derivative $\asymp d$ gives the
stated error. Boundedness of $\delta_0$ and the recursive coefficients
at each fixed order makes this estimate uniform as $X\to\infty$.
This is again an asymptotic expansion, not a convergence assertion.

\subsection{Integer thresholds and the rounding boundary}
Let $T(X)=\min\{n\ge0:h_n\ge X\}$. The sequence is nondecreasing and
strictly increasing eventually. This follows directly from
\eqref{his:o5:rec}: after the initial terms, increasing the binomial upper index
and using the preceding monotonic inequalities compares $h_{n+4}$ with
$h_{n+3}$, with an additional positive endpoint. The asymptotic law also
gives $h_{n+1}/h_n\sim(n+3)/\rho$.

A formula with an error $o(1)$ need not determine an integer threshold by
rounding when $X$ lies very close to a jump. To state the exact limitation,
choose $K_0$ and $n_0$ such that
\[
|\log(h_n/M(n))|\le K_0 n^{-13/2}\qquad(n\ge n_0).
\]
Their existence follows from \eqref{his:o5:first}; no numerical values for them
are certified here. For sufficiently large $X$, define $q_+(X)$ and
$q_-(X)$ by the increasing-tail equations
\[
\log M(q_+)+K_0q_+^{-13/2}=\log X,\qquad
\log M(q_-)-K_0q_-^{-13/2}=\log X.
\]
Then
\begin{equation}\label{his:o5:rounding}
\lceil q_+(X)\rceil\le T(X)\le\lceil q_-(X)\rceil,
\qquad
q_-(X)-q_+(X)=O\!\left(\frac{q(X)^{-13/2}}{\log q(X)}\right).
\end{equation}
Indeed every integer $n<q_+$ has its upper envelope below $X$, while
every integer $n\ge q_-$ has its lower envelope at least $X$.
Thus $T(X)=q(X)+O(1)$. The two ceilings agree except in a shrinking
neighborhood of an integer boundary. Near a boundary, use exact
recurrence values or certified envelopes; neither a fitted $\rho$ nor
an arbitrarily rounded Lambert truncation is a rigorous decision rule.
The same envelope argument applies to each corrected model $M_J$ with
exponent $13(J+1)/2$ and its own eventual constants.

\section{Scope and further questions}\label{his:o5:sec:scope}
The proof settles the specified third-order initial-value problem and
therefore the $m=2$ instance of the cited conjecture. It does not prove
Conjecture 1 for $m\ge3$, the even-order B-tree conjecture, or a global
priority claim over all existing literature. The source paper identifies
the conjecture; the OEIS identifies the recurrence; neither alone
establishes novelty of every argument in this report.

Several concrete continuations remain.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Certified constants.} Combine interval integration of the
real rescaled system with an explicit analytic tail bound to enclose
$\rho$ and $C$, then derive numerical error constants and usable threshold
cutoffs. The present nonvanishing proof does not quantify $|C|$.
\item \textbf{Higher orders.} For $H^{(m+1)}=H^2$, determine when the real
positive orbit approaches the pole equilibrium and whether all remaining
modes have the stability and nonresonance properties needed here.
A higher-dimensional system need not inherit the planar Lyapunov
mechanism of \eqref{his:o5:XY}.
\item \textbf{Other initial data.} The positive real dynamics and the
analytic stable surface apply more broadly. Characterize the dependence
of the movable singularity and stable amplitudes on positive initial
conditions, including the exceptional exact-pole family.
\item \textbf{Global continuation.} Describe singularities beyond the
first circle and the multivalued continuation generated by the complex
powers. The local slit-sector construction does not classify the whole
Riemann surface.
\item \textbf{Effective asymptotics.} Optimize finite truncations for
numerical work and obtain certified uniform remainders. Convergence of
the local psi series alone does not justify an infinite coefficient
expansion at fixed index.
\end{enumerate}

\section{The exact model and main result}\label{his:o7:sec:model}
\subsection{Recurrence and counting convention}
Let
\begin{equation}\label{his:o7:rec}
h_0=h_1=h_2=h_3=1,\qquad
h_{n+4}=\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk h_kh_{n-k}\quad(n\ge0),
\end{equation}
and put
\begin{equation}\label{his:o7:ode}
H(z)=\sum_{n\ge0}h_n\frac{z^n}{n!},\qquad
H^{(4)}=H^2,\qquad H^{(j)}(0)=1\quad(0\le j\le3).
\end{equation}
Local complex analytic existence for this polynomial differential
system gives a positive Taylor radius. Coefficient comparison makes
\eqref{his:o7:rec} and \eqref{his:o7:ode} equivalent. The first values are
\[
1,1,1,1,1,2,4,8,16,34,82,226,678,2108,6892,\ldots.
\]
This is OEIS A336009~\cite{oeisr4}. The exact companion regenerates
$h_0,\ldots,h_{500}$ and checks the 31 terms of the retrieved OEIS
prefix. The longer recurrence computation is not described as a
published 501-term OEIS table.

In Burghart and Wagner~\cite[Section 4, equation (4)]{bw2026}, the
reduced $(2m+1)$-historic-tree generating function satisfies
$H^{(m+1)}=H^2$. Removing the initial $m$ vertices identifies the
reduced object with $n$ internal vertices with a B-tree shape history
of length $n+m$. Thus $h_n$ here counts reduced 7-historic trees with
$n$ internal vertices, equivalently order-seven B-tree histories of
length $n+3$. A history is a sequence of successive shapes; it is
neither an insertion permutation nor merely a final shape.
The interpretation uses the paper's bijection and initial-vertex removal,
rather than just the OEIS description.

\subsection{Main theorem}
Write
\begin{equation}\label{his:o7:alpha}
\alpha=\frac{11+i\sqrt{159}}2,\quad
\beta_{k\ell m}=12k+\alpha\ell+\ba m,\quad
G_n(b)=\frac{\Gamma(n+4-b)}{\Gamma(4-b)\Gamma(n+4)}.
\end{equation}
For a fixed real integer $b\ge4$, the associated local power is a
polynomial. Its contribution is interpreted as zero for all sufficiently
large $n$, rather than evaluating a gamma function at its poles.

\begin{theorem}\label{his:o7:main}
There are a positive real number $\rho$ and a nonzero complex number $C$
with the following properties.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item The number $\rho$ is the positive-real blow-up time, the Taylor
radius of $H$, and the only singularity on $|z|=\rho$. Moreover,
\begin{equation}\label{his:o7:bounds}
16800^{1/6}\le\rho\le840^{1/4}.
\end{equation}
\item Set $c_0=-1/18052070400$ and $A=c_0\rho^{12}$.
There is a convergent three-variable power series
\[
U(q_0,q_1,q_2)=\sum_{k+\ell+m\ge1}a_{k\ell m}q_0^kq_1^\ell q_2^m,
\qquad a_{100}=a_{010}=a_{001}=1,
\]
for which, on the branch real at $z<\rho$,
\begin{equation}\label{his:o7:local-main}
H(z)=840(\rho-z)^{-4}
\left[1+U\left(A(1-z/\rho)^{12},
C(1-z/\rho)^\alpha,\bC(1-z/\rho)^{\ba}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
This identity holds in a slit neighborhood of $\rho$.
\item For every fixed integer $J\ge0$, with $\nu=(k,\ell,m)$,
\begin{equation}\label{his:o7:allsectors}
\frac{h_n}{140\Gamma(n+4)\rho^{-n-4}}
=1+6\!\sum_{1\le|\nu|\le J}
 a_\nu A^k C^\ell\bC^{m}G_n(\beta_\nu)
 +O\!\left(n^{-11(J+1)/2}\right).
\end{equation}
The constant in the remainder may depend on $J$. In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{his:o7:first}
h_n=140(n+3)!\rho^{-n-4}
\left[1+12\Rea\{C G_n(\alpha)\}+O(n^{-11})\right].
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

The leading equivalent is the $m=3$ case of Conjecture 1 in
\cite[p.~10]{bw2026}, since $7!/(3!)^2=140$. The pole coefficient $840$
and the coefficient multiplier $140$ differ by $\Gamma(4)=6$.
The leading correction has a genuinely nonzero log-periodic envelope,
but its real oscillation can approach zero. In particular, a uniform
two-sided bound on the correction is not claimed.

The local expansion converges. The coefficient and inverse expansions
below are asserted to every fixed finite order, not as convergent
large-$n$ series. No fitted decimal value enters the proof.

\section{Positive real blow up and its profile}\label{his:o7:real}
\subsection{Exact comparison bounds}
For the real solution $y(x)=H(x)$, the first-order system is
\[
(y,y',y'',y''')'=(y',y'',y''',y^2).
\]
It is cooperative on the nonnegative orthant. All four coordinates
start at one and remain positive and increasing on their maximal
positive interval. Exact comparison solutions are
\[
p_R(x)=840(R-x)^{-4},
\]
with initial coordinates
\begin{equation}\label{his:o7:pole-data}
\left(\frac{840}{R^4},\frac{3360}{R^5},
\frac{16800}{R^6},\frac{100800}{R^7}\right).
\end{equation}
Let $R_-=16800^{1/6}$ and $R_+=840^{1/4}$. At $R_-$ these coordinates
are $(R_-^2/20,R_-/5,1,6/R_-)$, all at least one because
$5<R_-<6$. At $R_+$ they are
$(1,4/R_+,20/R_+^2,120/R_+^3)$, all at most one.
Cooperative comparison gives
\[
p_{R_+}(x)\le y(x)\le p_{R_-}(x)
\]
on the respective common intervals, including the corresponding
bounds on the first three derivatives.
If $y$ ended before $R_-$, the upper comparison would keep all state
coordinates bounded and permit continuation of the polynomial system.
If it survived beyond $R_+$, the lower comparison would contradict
finite values as $x\uparrow R_+$. Its finite maximal endpoint
$\rho$ therefore satisfies \eqref{his:o7:bounds}.

If $y$ stayed bounded as $x\uparrow\rho$, then $y^{(4)}=y^2$
would be bounded. Repeated integration would bound the other state

\begin{equation}\label{his:o7:vertical}
y(x)\longrightarrow+\infty\qquad(x\uparrow\rho).
\end{equation}

\subsection{The published real theorem and its hypotheses}
The substantive real-profile input is Astashova's
Theorem 3~\cite[p.~5]{astashova2013}. In the notation needed here, it concerns
\[
y^{(r)}=p(x,y,y',\ldots,y^{(r-1)})|y|^k\operatorname{sgn}y,
\qquad r\in\{3,4\},\quad k>1.
\]
The function $p$ must be positive and continuous, Lipschitz in its
state variables, and have a positive limit $p_0$ in the regime
$x\uparrow x^*$ and all its state arguments tending to $+\infty$.
For a positive solution with vertical asymptote $x=x^*$, the theorem
then gives a power profile with exponent $r/(k-1)$ and leading
constant
\[
\left(\frac{\gamma(\gamma+1)\cdots(\gamma+r-1)}{p_0}
\right)^{1/(k-1)},\qquad \gamma=\frac{r}{k-1}.
\]
Here $r=4$, $k=2$, and $p\equiv1$: positivity and continuity hold
everywhere, its state Lipschitz constant is zero, and its limiting
value is identically $p_0=1$. The solution is positive and has the
finite vertical asymptote \eqref{his:o7:vertical}. Every hypothesis is
therefore satisfied. The extra quantitative conditions labelled
(A) and (B) elsewhere in the cited paper are not additional
hypotheses of its Theorem 3; constant $p$ would satisfy them anyway.
The state-limit condition is a condition on the coefficient function,
so there is no need to presume derivative asymptotics in order to
apply the theorem here.

Consequently,
\begin{equation}\label{his:o7:profile}
y(x)\sim840(\rho-x)^{-4}.
\end{equation}
We do not differentiate this equivalent. Instead, the ODE gives
$y^{(4)}\sim705600(\rho-x)^{-8}$.
If $f(x)\sim K(\rho-x)^{-p}$ with $p>1$, elementary upper and lower
integral comparisons give
\[
\int_{x_0}^x f(u)\,\dd u
\sim\frac{K}{p-1}(\rho-x)^{-p+1}.
\]
The divergent power integral makes the finite integration constant
negligible. Three applications yield
\begin{equation}\label{his:o7:derivatives}
y'''\sim100800(\rho-x)^{-7},\quad
 y''\sim16800(\rho-x)^{-6},\quad
 y'\sim3360(\rho-x)^{-5}.
\end{equation}
Thus every normalized state coordinate needed below has a proved
limit. Astashova's result supplies this real input only; it does not
supply our complex continuation or coefficient transfer.

\section{The convergence circle and other boundary points}\label{his:o7:circle}
All $h_n$ are strictly positive by \eqref{his:o7:rec}. By Pringsheim's theorem,
a finite Taylor radius is a positive-real singularity. Real analytic
existence before $\rho$ and blow-up at $\rho$ imply that the Taylor
radius is exactly $\rho$.

Fix $0<r<\rho$ and $\theta\not\equiv0\pmod{2\pi}$. Each of
$H,H',H'',H'''$ has positive constant and linear Taylor coefficients
and nonnegative remaining coefficients. The constant and linear terms
alone force strict triangle inequality at $re^{i\theta}$, so
\[
|H^{(j)}(re^{i\theta})|<H^{(j)}(r)\qquad(0\le j\le3).
\]
Choose a common $\eps\in(0,r)$ small enough that
\begin{equation}\label{his:o7:strict}
|H^{(j)}(re^{i\theta})|\le H^{(j)}(r-\eps)
\qquad(0\le j\le3).
\end{equation}
For ordinary Taylor coefficients $a_n$ at any regular center,
\eqref{his:o7:ode} gives
\begin{equation}\label{his:o7:center-rec}
a_{n+4}=\frac{\sum_{k=0}^n a_k a_{n-k}}
{(n+1)(n+2)(n+3)(n+4)}.
\end{equation}
This recurrence preserves absolute coefficientwise majorization.
The Taylor expansion at $z_0=re^{i\theta}$ is therefore dominated
by that of $H(r-\eps+w)$. The latter has radius exactly
$\rho-r+\eps$: the original disk gives analyticity at least that
far, and the positive-axis endpoint is singular.
The resulting disk centered at $z_0$ contains $\rho e^{i\theta}$
in its interior. This proves analytic continuation across every
other point of $|z|=\rho$, rather than just boundedness on a ray.

These continuations glue. Intersecting comparison disks have a
connected intersection, and a point of their intersection can be
chosen on the segment joining their centers, which lies in the
original disk. Their two analytic functions agree there, hence
throughout the intersection. A compactness argument supplies an
outer collar away from a small neighborhood of $\rho$. A slit
sector at $\rho$, constructed next, will complete the domain
needed for coefficient extraction.

\section{A convergent three variable stable surface}\label{his:o7:stable}
\subsection{Normalization and characteristic exponents}
Put
\[
t=\rho-z,\qquad \sigma=-\log t,\qquad F=\frac{t^4H}{840},
\qquad w=F-1.
\]
A dot denotes differentiation in $\sigma$ in this section.
Since $\dd/\dd z=t^{-1}\dd/\dd\sigma$, the ODE becomes
\[
(\partial_\sigma+4)(\partial_\sigma+5)
(\partial_\sigma+6)(\partial_\sigma+7)F=840F^2,

Along the real solution, \eqref{his:o7:profile} gives $w\to0$.

&t^6y''-9t^5y'+16t^4y,\\

Substitution of \eqref{his:o7:profile}--\eqref{his:o7:derivatives} makes all three

\begin{equation}\label{his:o7:indicial}

\end{equation}

\subsection{Coefficient construction and convergence}
For $\nu=(k,\ell,m)\in\N_0^3$ let $|\nu|=k+\ell+m$ and
$\beta_\nu=12k+\alpha\ell+\ba m$.
Set $a_0=0$ and $a_{100}=a_{010}=a_{001}=1$. For $|\nu|\ge2$ define
\begin{equation}\label{his:o7:psi-rec}
a_\nu=\frac{840}{D(\beta_\nu)}
\sum_{\mu+\eta=\nu}a_\mu a_\eta.
\end{equation}
The endpoints with a zero multi-index contribute nothing.
We verify both nonresonance and convergence, rather than treating
this as only a formal recursion.

For $N=|\nu|\ge2$ we have $\Rea\beta_\nu\ge11N/2$, and thus
\[
|\beta_\nu+1|\ge\frac{11N}2+1,\qquad
|\beta_\nu-\alpha|,\ |\beta_\nu-\ba|
\ge\frac{11(N-1)}2.
\]
The equation $\Rea\beta_\nu=12$ would imply
$24k+11(\ell+m)=24$, whose only nonnegative integral solution
is $(k,\ell,m)=(1,0,0)$ of degree one. Since these real parts are
half-integers, $|\beta_\nu-12|\ge1/2$ for $N\ge2$. Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{his:o7:denom}
|D(\beta_\nu)|\ge
\left(\frac{11N}2+1\right)\frac12
\left(\frac{11(N-1)}2\right)^2\ge\frac{363}{2}.
\end{equation}
Every nonlinear denominator is nonzero and uniformly bounded away
from zero.

If $A_N=\sum_{|\nu|=N}|a_\nu|$, then $A_1=3$ and
\[

The scalar majorant $B(r)=3r+MB(r)^2$ is analytic for

\begin{equation}\label{his:o7:series}
U(q)=\sum_{|\nu|\ge1}a_\nu q^\nu

converges absolutely in a genuine tridisk; all its differentiated

bound \eqref{his:o7:denom} suffices for this purpose.

\subsection{Why the actual solution belongs to the surface}

\[
E=12q_0\partial_{q_0}+\alpha q_1\partial_{q_1}
+\ba q_2\partial_{q_2}.
\]
The convergent recursion verifies $[Q(E)-1680]U=840U^2$.
It follows that
\[
\Theta(q)=(U,-EU,E^2U,-E^3U)
\]
is an analytic invariant surface of \eqref{his:o7:normalized}, with exact
restricted dynamics
\begin{equation}\label{his:o7:linear-flow}
\dot q_0=-12q_0,\qquad\dot q_1=-\alpha q_1,\qquad
\dot q_2=-\ba q_2.
\end{equation}
Its tangent vectors are the three distinct stable eigenvectors
$(1,-r,r^2,-r^3)$, for $r=12,\alpha,\ba$.
Adding the unstable eigenvector $e_u=(1,1,1,1)$ makes
$(q,p)\mapsto\Theta(q)+p e_u$ an analytic local coordinate chart.
Invariance of $p=0$ forces the transverse equation to have the form
\[
\dot p=p\,a(q,p),\qquad a(0,0)=1,
\]
with $a$ analytic. Conjugation symmetry makes $p$ and $a$ real

\left[1+U(c_0t^{12},ct^\alpha,\overline c\,t^{\ba})\right].
\end{equation}
This is membership in an explicitly convergent surface, with an
explicitly linear restricted flow. An unstated analytic linearization
or formal stable-manifold assertion is not needed.

For $k\ge2$, all factors of $D(12k)$ are positive and increasing,

\frac{840}{D(12k)}\le\frac7{955}.

B(r)=r+MB(r)^2
=\frac{2r}{1+\sqrt{1-4Mr}},\qquad M=\frac7{955}.

This new one-variable bound is much larger than the earlier generic
tridisk bound; it is essential for reaching $z=0$ below.

\section{Coefficient expansions to every fixed order}\label{his:o7:transfer}
\subsection{Exact transfer and polynomial sectors}
Substitute $t=\rho(1-z/\rho)$ into \eqref{his:o7:local-t}.
The pole term has the exact EGF coefficient
\begin{equation}\label{his:o7:leading-transfer}
n![z^n]\,840(\rho-z)^{-4}
=840\rho^{-n-4}n!\binom{n+3}{3}
=140(n+3)!\rho^{-n-4}.
\end{equation}
For a nonreal exponent $\beta=\beta_\nu$, generalized binomial
extraction yields the relative contribution
\begin{equation}\label{his:o7:gamma-term}
6a_\nu A^k C^\ell\bC^m
\frac{\Gamma(n+4-\beta)}{\Gamma(4-\beta)\Gamma(n+4)}.
\end{equation}
This explains the factor six in \eqref{his:o7:allsectors}.

If $\ell=m$ and $|\nu|\ge1$, then
$\beta_\nu=12k+11\ell$ is an integer at least 11.
The power $(1-z/\rho)^{\beta_\nu-4}$ is a polynomial, whose
coefficients are exactly zero for $n>\beta_\nu-4$.
If $\ell\ne m$, the exponent is nonreal and the ordinary gamma
quotient applies. This identifies every analytic integer sector
rather than treating its formal gamma poles as numerical terms.
For a fixed truncation only finitely many such polynomials occur,
so they all vanish for sufficiently large $n$.

\subsection{Remainder transfer}
Absolute convergence of $U$ implies that truncation at degree $J$
has a uniform local remainder $O(|t|^{11(J+1)/2})$ inside the
fixed sector. After multiplication by $t^{-4}$, the absolute
remainder in $H$ is $O(|t|^{s-4})$, where $s=11(J+1)/2$.
The big-$O$ transfer theorem of Flajolet and
Odlyzko~[FO, Theorem 1, pp.~220--221], applied in the
$\Delta$-domain already proved, gives the ordinary coefficient bound
\[
O\bigl(\rho^{-n}n^{3-s}\bigr).
\]
The theorem permits real exponents, including nonnegative integers;
there is no gap when $s-4$ is integral.
Multiplication by $n!$ and division by \eqref{his:o7:leading-transfer}
give the relative $O(n^{-s})$ bound in \eqref{his:o7:allsectors}.
This completes the proof of Theorem~\ref{his:o7:main}.

\subsection{First oscillations and arbitrary algebraic precision}
At degree one the real term is eventually zero, and the conjugate
pair gives \eqref{his:o7:first}. For its gamma ratio,
\begin{equation}\label{his:o7:gamma-stirling}
G_n(\alpha)=\frac{n^{-\alpha}}{\Gamma(4-\alpha)}
\left[1+\frac{\alpha(\alpha-7)}{2n}+O(n^{-2})\right].
\end{equation}
The frequency in $\log n$ is $\sqrt{159}/2$ and the relative
envelope has order $n^{-11/2}$, because $C\ne0$ and
$\Gamma(4-\alpha)$ is finite and nonzero.
The exact gamma ratio retains all integer inverse-power corrections
from this first sector.

At the next useful precision, the pure second harmonics have real
exponent 11; the diagonal sector is polynomial, and the mixed
real/complex modes have real exponent $35/2$. Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{his:o7:two-harmonics}
\frac{h_n}{140(n+3)!\rho^{-n-4}}
=1+12\Rea\{CG_n(\alpha)+a_{020}C^2G_n(2\alpha)\}
+O(n^{-33/2}).
\end{equation}
Degree order and real-exponent order are different. Formula
\eqref{his:o7:allsectors} is safe because it may retain terms smaller than
its stated error. Alternatively, for any fixed $B>0$, retain the
finitely many nonpolynomial terms with $\Rea\beta_\nu<B$.
First take a sufficiently long degree truncation, then discard the
finitely many remaining terms with real exponent at least $B$.
The relative error is $O(n^{-B})$.

Finite-order Stirling expansion of each retained gamma ratio now
provides every prescribed fixed algebraic order. All exponents and
coefficients are determined by \eqref{his:o7:psi-rec}, $A$ and $C$.
No logarithmic multipliers are forced by a nonlinear resonance,
because every denominator in \eqref{his:o7:psi-rec} is nonzero.
The nonreal powers themselves generate the oscillations.
Convergence of this large-index expansion has not been proved or used.

\subsection{Inverse approximation at the sequence values}

The inverse mean-value theorem then gives
\begin{equation}\label{his:o7:inverse-error}
q_J(h_n)=n+O\!\left(\frac{n^{-11(J+1)/2}}{\log n}\right).
\end{equation}
These are approximations at the values of the discrete sequence;
they do not assert a canonical continuous inverse of that sequence.

\subsection{Two explicit Lambert approximations}
For the leading model $M_0$, set
\begin{equation}\label{his:o7:lambert-start}
L=\log\frac{X\rho}{140},\quad
w=W_0\!\left(\frac{L}{e\rho}\right),\quad
N_0=\frac Lw,\quad d=1+w,\quad
 a=\frac12\log(2\pi N_0).
\end{equation}
These quantities are real and positive where appropriate for
sufficiently large $X$. The change of variable $N=x+3$ turns
$\log M_0(x)=\log X$ into
\begin{equation}\label{his:o7:inverse-gamma}
\log\Gamma(N+1)-N\log\rho=L.
\end{equation}
The leading term $N\log(N/(e\rho))$ takes the value $L$ at
$N=N_0$ and has derivative $d$ there.
Taylor expansion of Stirling's formula gives
\begin{equation}\label{his:o7:lambert-one}
q_0(X)=N_0-3-\frac ad+O\!\left(\frac1{N_0d}\right),
\end{equation}
and the next correction is
\begin{equation}\label{his:o7:lambert-two}
q_0(X)=N_0-3-\frac ad
-\frac{a^2/(2d^2)-a/(2d)+1/12}{N_0d}
+O\!\left(\frac1{N_0^2d}\right).
\end{equation}
The signs, the shift by $-3$, and the factor $\rho/140$ in $L$
all follow from the exact normalization in \eqref{his:o7:inverse-gamma}.

\subsection{An all fixed order recursion}
Write $\eps=1/N_0$ and $N=N_0+s$. Expanding
\eqref{his:o7:inverse-gamma} around $N_0$ gives the formal equation
\begin{align}\label{his:o7:lambert-rec}
0={}&ds+a+
\sum_{k\ge2}\frac{(-1)^k\eps^{k-1}s^k}{k(k-1)}
+\frac12\log(1+\eps s)\notag\\
&+\sum_{r\ge1}
\frac{B_{2r}\eps^{2r-1}}
{2r(2r-1)(1+\eps s)^{2r-1}},
\end{align}
where $B_{2r}$ denotes a Bernoulli number, unrelated to the model
factor $B_J$. Set $P_0=-a/d$. For $j\ge1$, substitute
$s=\sum_{i<j}P_i\eps^i$ into the remaining expression in
\eqref{his:o7:lambert-rec}; $P_j$ is minus its coefficient of $\eps^j$
divided by $d$. In particular,
\[
P_1=-\frac1d\left(\frac{a^2}{2d^2}-\frac a{2d}+\frac1{12}\right).
\]
For fixed $K$, the resulting approximation has error
\begin{equation}\label{his:o7:lambert-error}
q_0(X)=N_0-3+\sum_{j=0}^KP_jN_0^{-j}
+O\!\left(\frac{N_0^{-K-1}}d\right).
\end{equation}
Indeed, $P_0=O(1)$ and every fixed $P_j$ with $j\ge1$ is
$O(1/d)$. A sufficiently long finite Stirling expansion leaves a
residual $O(N_0^{-K-1})$, while the derivative is $d+o(1)$.
This justifies each fixed order of the formal recursion and does
not assert its convergence.

At sufficiently high precision the complex correction in
$\log B_J$ matters. Lambert corrections to $q_0$ alone therefore
do not deliver arbitrary coefficient accuracy in \eqref{his:o7:inverse-error};
use $q_J$ with sufficiently large $J$ for that purpose.

\subsection{Integer thresholds and rounding}
Define $T(X)=\min\{n:h_n\ge X\}$. The leading equivalent implies
$h_n/h_{n-1}\sim(n+3)/\rho$, so the sequence is eventually
strictly increasing. Fix $J$ and put $s_J=11(J+1)/2$.
For some $K>0$ and all sufficiently large $n$,
\[
|\log h_n-\log M_J(n)|\le K n^{-s_J}.
\]
Define $q_{J,+}$ and $q_{J,-}$ on their large-$X$ tails by
\begin{align*}
\log M_J(q_{J,+})+Kq_{J,+}^{-s_J}&=\log X,\\
\log M_J(q_{J,-})-Kq_{J,-}^{-s_J}&=\log X.
\end{align*}
Both functions being inverted are eventually increasing. An index
below $\lceil q_{J,+}\rceil$ has its upper bound below $\log X$;
the index $\lceil q_{J,-}\rceil$ has its lower bound at least
$\log X$. Therefore, for all sufficiently large $X$,
\begin{equation}\label{his:o7:rounding}
\lceil q_{J,+}(X)\rceil\le T(X)\le\lceil q_{J,-}(X)\rceil,
\qquad
q_{J,-}-q_{J,+}
=O\!\left(\frac{q_J^{-s_J}}{\log q_J}\right).
\end{equation}
Large $X$ excludes the finite initial segment. The bracket often
resolves rounding, but values arbitrarily close to an integer
boundary preclude an unconditional exact-rounding promise from
an unquantified finite asymptotic formula. Numerical values of
$K$ and a valid starting threshold are not supplied here.

\section{Non D finiteness and non P recursiveness}\label{his:o7:monodromy}
This section adds a consequence of the convergent expansion, not
of numerical fitting or a single nonreal leading exponent.
It applies both to the order-seven problem proved here and to the
order-five companion~(Part~II). We use subscripts $2$ and $3$
to distinguish their generating functions and radii.

\begin{theorem}\label{his:o7:notdfinite}
For each $m\in\{2,3\}$, the sectorial germs obtained from $H_m$
by successive nonnegative windings about $\rho_m$ are linearly
independent in every finite initial collection. Representatives are
identified after shrinking their common sector. In particular,
$H_2$ and $H_3$ are not D-finite over $\C(z)$, and the exact
integer sequences A333497 and A336009 are not P-recursive over
$\C[n]$.
\end{theorem}

A function is D-finite if it satisfies a nonzero homogeneous linear
differential equation with rational-function coefficients. A sequence
is P-recursive if, eventually, it satisfies a nonzero linear
recurrence with polynomial coefficients in its index. The proof
will reduce a putative finite-order differential equation to a
finite-dimensional solution space at an ordinary point. No regular
singularity assumption is made.

\subsection{The two local inputs}

h^{(2)}_0=h^{(2)}_1=h^{(2)}_2=1,\qquad

For the rest of this section write $\alpha=\alpha_m$,
$\kappa=60$ or $840$, and $p=3$ or $4$ as appropriate.
Both expansions have the form $\kappa t^{-p}(1+U)$.
Their nonzero relative exponents have positive half-integral real
parts, with only finitely many monomials below any fixed real level.
Every differentiated series converges on a smaller polydisk.
The nonvanishing of the complex amplitude is essential in both
cases. In order seven it is the stronger exclusion of the entire
real-mode family, rather than just the pure pole, that provides it.

\subsection{Any fixed finite number of windings}
Put $w=\log t$. For an input with exponent $\gamma$ of positive
real part,
\[
|ce^{\gamma w}|=|c|\exp\{\Rea\gamma\,\Rea w
-\Ima\gamma\,\Ima w\}.
\]
On any bounded interval of $\Ima w$, all inputs to $U$ tend
uniformly to zero as $\Rea w\to-\infty$. Hence, for every
integer $N\ge0$ and fixed $0<\eps<\pi$, there is $r_N>0$
such that the expansion is holomorphic and locally absolutely
convergent on
\[
\Rea w<\log r_N,\qquad -\eps<\Ima w<2\pi N+\eps.
\]
It agrees with the original germ on the real branch by the local
theorem and the identity theorem. A vertical segment from
$w=\log r$ to $w=\log r+2\pi i j$, $r<r_N$, implements $j$
windings. The transformation $z=\rho-t$ preserves their orientation.
The integer pole factor has trivial monodromy; a relative power
$t^\beta$ acquires the multiplier $e^{2\pi i j\beta}$.

Thus branches $H_0,\ldots,H_N$ are defined on one common sector
$0<|t|<r_N$, $|\arg t|<\eps$ by replacing $w$ by
$w+2\pi i j$. The radius may decrease with $N$. There is no
assertion that a fixed-radius circle supports arbitrarily many
windings, and none is needed below.

\subsection{Uniqueness after grouping equal exponents}
We need an elementary uniqueness principle that allows different
monomials to have the same complex exponent.
Suppose
\[
F(t)=\sum_\nu b_\nu t^{\beta_\nu},\qquad 0<t\le t_0,
\]
where the real parts of the exponents lie in $\tfrac12\N_0$, there are only
finitely many terms below any fixed real level, and
$\sum_\nu|b_\nu|t_0^{\Rea\beta_\nu}<\infty$.
If $F$ vanishes for all sufficiently small positive $t$, then every
coefficient obtained by grouping equal complex exponents is zero.

To prove this, suppose a nonzero grouped coefficient exists and
choose the smallest real level $\lambda$ at which one occurs.
There are finitely many distinct imaginary parts
$\tau_1,\ldots,\tau_s$ there. The tail at larger real levels is
$O(t^{\lambda+1/2})$: its absolute value is at most
\[
t^{\lambda+1/2}t_0^{-\lambda-1/2}
\sum_\nu |b_\nu|t_0^{\Rea\beta_\nu}.
\]
Divide by $t^\lambda$ and set $x=-\log t$. The finite exponential
sum $T(x)=\sum_{a=1}^s B_ae^{-i\tau_ax}$ then satisfies
$T(x)=O(e^{-x/2})$. But
\[
\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac1R\int_X^{X+R}|T(x)|^2\,\dd x
=\sum_{a=1}^s|B_a|^2,
\]
since distinct-frequency cross terms have zero limiting mean.
Decay of $T$ makes the same mean zero, a contradiction.

This principle applies to a finite linear combination of the
branches. For $P(X)=\sum_{j=0}^N\xi_jX^j$, the extra coefficient
multiplier obeys
\[
|P(e^{2\pi i\beta})|\le
\sum_{j=0}^N|\xi_j|e^{-2\pi j\Ima\beta}.
\]
At sufficiently small $t_0>0$, each corresponding series is
absolutely convergent on its fixed sheet. This verifies the
required absolute summability.
Grouping is necessary: in order seven, for instance,
$12\cdot11=12\alpha+12\ba=132$, so distinct multivariate
monomials can share an exponent.

\subsection{Infinitely many distinct nonzero characters}
Restrict to the pure positive-complex axis:
\[
V(q)=U(q,0)\quad(m=2),\qquad
V(q)=U(0,q,0)\quad(m=3).
\]
Writing $V(q)=\sum_{k\ge1}v_kq^k$, we have $v_1=1$ and
\begin{equation}\label{his:o7:pure-axis}
D(\alpha q\partial_q)V=\kappa V^2.
\end{equation}
Restriction is legitimate because every polynomial in the Euler
operator preserves the coordinate axes. If $V$ were a polynomial
of degree $K\ge1$, the left side would have degree at most $K$
and the right side exactly $2K$, with nonzero top coefficient.
Therefore infinitely many $v_k$ are nonzero. It is unnecessary
to claim that every $v_k$ is nonzero.

The exponent $k\alpha$ belongs to its pure-complex monomial alone.
For $m=2$, the equality $a\alpha+b\ba=k\alpha$ implies
$a-b=k$ by imaginary parts, then $2\Rea\alpha\,b=0$ by real
parts, so $b=0$ and $a=k$.
For $m=3$, the equality $12a+b\alpha+c\ba=k\alpha$ gives
$b-c=k$ and then $12a+11c=0$. Nonnegativity again forces the
pure monomial. Thus exponent collisions elsewhere cannot cancel
any of these sectors.

Now suppose $\sum_{j=0}^N\xi_jH_j=0$ on the common sector
and put $P(X)=\sum_{j=0}^N\xi_jX^j$.
Divide by the common factor $\kappa t^{-p}$ and expand:
\[
P(1)+\sum_{\nu\ne0}a_\nu c^\nu
 P(e^{2\pi i\beta_\nu})t^{\beta_\nu}=0,
\]
where $c^\nu$ denotes the appropriate amplitude product.
Grouped uniqueness and the pure-exponent property give
\[
v_kc^kP(e^{2\pi ik\alpha})=0\qquad(k\ge1).
\]
Since $c\ne0$, $P$ has roots $e^{2\pi ik\alpha}$ for infinitely
many $k$. Their moduli $e^{-2\pi k\Ima\alpha}$ are strictly
decreasing, hence these roots are distinct. Therefore $P=0$
and all $\xi_j=0$. A relation holding initially as germs at a
point of the sector extends to the whole sector by the identity
theorem. This proves the asserted branch independence.

\subsection{The differential equation contradiction}
Suppose $H$ satisfied a nonzero homogeneous rational linear ODE
of order $d$. Order zero is impossible since $H\ne0$, so $d\ge1$.
Normalize its leading coefficient. A sufficiently small punctured
disk about $\rho$ contains no poles of the normalized coefficients;
$\rho$ itself may be ordinary, regular singular or irregular singular.
Choose the preceding construction with $N=d$ and shrink $r_d$
to lie in that disk. All winding paths consist of ordinary points
of the hypothetical linear equation, so continuation preserves
its identity. The $d+1$ branches all satisfy it on their common
sector.

At any ordinary point $z_*$ of that sector, its analytic solution
germs form a $d$-dimensional complex vector space: the map to
$(H(z_*),\ldots,H^{(d-1)}(z_*))\in\C^d$ is a linear bijection
by analytic initial-value existence and uniqueness.
Those $d+1$ germs must be linearly dependent with constant complex
coefficients, contradicting what was just proved. This establishes
non-D-finiteness without local classification of a singular point.

\subsection{From a polynomial recurrence to a linear ODE}
To transfer the conclusion to the integer sequence directly,
suppose $\sum_{j=0}^sp_j(n)h_{n+j}=0$ for all $n\ge N$,
where the polynomials $p_j$ are not all zero.
Set $f_n=h_n/n!$. Dividing by $n!$ gives
\[
\sum_{j=0}^sq_j(n)f_{n+j}=0,\qquad
q_j(n)=p_j(n)\prod_{a=1}^j(n+a).
\]
These polynomials are not all zero. Let
$F(z)=\sum_{n\ge0}f_nz^n=H(z)$ and $\vartheta=z\dd/\dd z$.
The polynomial-coefficient differential operator
\[
L=\sum_{j=0}^s z^{s-j}q_j(\vartheta-j)
\]
satisfies $LF=R(z)$ for a polynomial $R$, because its coefficient
at $z^{n+s}$ is the displayed recurrence for every $n\ge0$.
It is a nonzero operator: on $z^M$ its summands have distinct
powers $z^{M+s-j}$, and a nonzero $q_j(M-j)$ cannot vanish for
all sufficiently large integers $M$.
A sufficiently high derivative annihilates $R$, producing a
nonzero homogeneous differential operator annihilating $H$.
The composition is still nonzero because its leading derivative
coefficient remains nonzero. This contradicts non-D-finiteness
and proves non-P-recursiveness. No factorial-product closure
result is needed.
\begin{writenote}[Added 5 October 2026, batch~103]
A precision on the step ``its coefficient at $z^{n+s}$ is the displayed recurrence for every $n\ge0$'': for each $n\ge0$ that coefficient equals the left side of the recurrence at $n$, which vanishes for $n\ge N$; the coefficients of $z^k$ with $k<s$ and those with $n<N$ are finitely many and form the polynomial $R$.
\end{writenote}

\section{Scope and further research}\label{his:o7:sec:scope}
This proves the specified fourth-order initial-value problem and
the $m=3$ instance of the B-tree history conjecture. The real-profile
step depends on the stated published theorem. The construction does
not silently extend the elementary third-order argument from the
order-five companion to fourth order, nor does Astashova's stated
third- and fourth-order theorem settle the higher-order family.
A full literature-priority claim is outside the source checks made
here.

Several concrete questions remain.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Certified constants and effective errors.}
Use interval integration of a rescaled system and explicit analytic
tail bounds to enclose $\rho$ and $C$, quantify a positive lower
bound for $|C|$, and produce useful numerical remainder constants
and threshold cutoffs. The present symbolic nonvanishing proof
is qualitative in $|C|$.
\item \textbf{Higher derivative orders.}
For $H^{(m+1)}=H^2$, first establish that the particular positive
initial orbit tends to the pole equilibrium; then analyze its
stable spectrum and possible nonlinear resonances. The general
real-profile problem cannot be resolved merely by reusing the
fourth-order theorem. Higher-dimensional Emden--Fowler systems
can exhibit different normalized dynamics.
\item \textbf{Dependence on initial data.}
Describe the movable singularity and stable amplitudes for other
positive initial conditions, including the real-mode family and
its exceptional cases. The energy integral isolates one amplitude
but does not by itself determine the two real components of $C$.
\item \textbf{Global continuation.}
Locate singularities beyond the first circle and describe the
multivalued continuation and its sheets. A local slit expansion
and a $\Delta$-domain do not classify the whole Riemann surface.
\item \textbf{Effective asymptotic truncation.}
Optimize finite truncations for numerical use while retaining
rigorous error bounds. Convergence of the local psi series does
not justify an infinite coefficient expansion at fixed index.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Three remarks of the write}\label{his:o59:sec:remarks}
\begin{remark}[\textsf{[write]} The two cones coincide]\label{his:o59:rem:same-cone}
For every $v\in\R^d$, $V(v)\le2$ if and only if $s^-(v)\le2$, where $V$ is Part~IV's cyclic count \eqref{his:o59:cone} and $s^-$ is Part~I's linear count \eqref{his:eq:cone}. Hence $K=\mathcal C_3$.
\end{remark}
\begin{proof}
Delete the zero coordinates of $v$; let $\eps_1,\ldots,\eps_k\in\{\pm1\}$ be the remaining signs in order. If $k\le1$, both counts are zero. Otherwise $s^-(v)$ counts the indices $i<k$ with $\eps_i\ne\eps_{i+1}$, and $V(v)$ counts these together with the closing comparison of $\eps_k$ and $\eps_1$. So $s^-(v)\le V(v)\le s^-(v)+1$. Moreover $V(v)$ is even: going once around the cycle returns to the starting sign, so the sign changes an even number of times. If $V(v)\le2$, then $s^-(v)\le V(v)\le2$. If $s^-(v)\le2$, then $V(v)\le3$, and evenness gives $V(v)\le2$.
\end{proof}

\section{The statement being contradicted}\label{his:o59:sec:statement}
\subsection{Model, indexing, and the family}
For an integer $d\ge2$, define integers $h_n$ by
\begin{equation}\label{his:o59:recurrence}
h_0=\cdots=h_{d-1}=1,\qquad
h_{n+d}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom nk h_kh_{n-k}\quad(n\ge0).
\end{equation}
Their exponential generating function is the local analytic solution
\begin{equation}\label{his:o59:ivp}
H(z)=\sum_{n\ge0}h_n\frac{z^n}{n!},\qquad
H^{(d)}=H^2,\qquad H^{(j)}(0)=1\quad(0\le j<d).
\end{equation}
Local analytic existence follows by writing the polynomial first-order
system for the derivative jet. Comparing Taylor coefficients proves
\eqref{his:o59:recurrence}, so these two definitions identify the same function.
In particular, every coefficient is positive.

Burghart and Wagner's odd-order B-tree model has $d=m+1$ and B-tree
order $2m+1=2d-1$~\cite[Section 4, equation (4)]{bw2026}.
Here $h_n$ counts reduced $(2m+1)$-historic trees with $n$ vertices,
equivalently B-tree shape histories of length $n+m$. A history counts
successive shapes, rather than insertion permutations or just final
shapes. Their Conjecture 1 asserts, for every integer $m\ge1$, that
some $\rho_m>0$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{his:o59:conjecture}
h_n\sim\frac{(2m+1)!}{(m!)^2}(n+m)!\rho_m^{-n-m-1}.
\end{equation}
The parameter conversion is essential: derivative order $30$ means
B-tree order $59$, not B-tree order $30$.

For comparison, the $d=3$ member of \eqref{his:o59:recurrence} is
OEIS A333497, and the $d=4$ member is OEIS A336009~\cite{oeis,oeisr4}.
They belong to B-tree orders $5$ and $7$, respectively. The present
counterexample belongs to the same differential-equation family at
$d=30$. No OEIS identifier for this order-$59$ member is asserted.

Set
\begin{equation}\label{his:o59:Adef}
A=A_d=\rise d d=\prod_{a=d}^{2d-1}a
=\frac{(2d-1)!}{(d-1)!},
\end{equation}
where $\rise a j=a(a+1)\cdots(a+j-1)$ and $\rise a0=1$.
In the notation $d=m+1$, \eqref{his:o59:conjecture} is
\begin{equation}\label{his:o59:conjecture-d}
h_n\sim\frac{A}{(d-1)!}(n+d-1)!\rho^{-n-d}.
\end{equation}
This is precisely the coefficient asymptotic of the candidate pole
$p_\rho(z)=A(\rho-z)^{-d}$ after multiplication by $n!$.

\subsection{Main theorem and proof architecture}
\begin{theorem}\label{his:o59:main}
Let $d=30$, $A=(30)_{30}=59!/29!$, and let $H$ be the solution of
\eqref{his:o59:ivp}. There is no finite positive $\rho$ such that $H$ exists
on $[0,\rho)$ and
\begin{equation}\label{his:o59:forbidden-pole}
H(x)\sim A(\rho-x)^{-30}\qquad(x\uparrow\rho).
\end{equation}
Consequently there is no $\rho>0$ for which \eqref{his:o59:conjecture-d}
holds. The $m=29$ instance, and hence the universal statement of
Burghart--Wagner Conjecture 1, is false.
\end{theorem}
\begin{writenote}[Added 5 October 2026, batch~103]
This is the case $r=30$ of Part~I's Theorem~\ref{his:thm:counterexample}, which covers every $r\ge30$ (dictionary in Section~\ref{his:o59:sec:dictionary}); the scalar form \eqref{his:o59:forbidden-pole} is Part~I's remark at the end of Section~\ref{his:sec:obstruction}. The proof below is a second route (Section~\ref{his:o59:sec:map}).
\end{writenote}


The conclusion concerns a specified initial-value solution. An extra
unstable eigenpair at a candidate pole is insufficient by itself: a
special orbit might still approach its stable manifold. The proof here
rules that out by the following chain.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
\item For every candidate $\rho$, subtract the exact pole jet from
the all-ones solution. Convexity of the initial logarithmic pole jet
puts this difference in the closed cone $K=\{V\le2\}$.
\item A directed cyclic linear system preserves $K$. Positive Fowler
rescaling preserves it too, so the entire relevant normalized orbit
lies in $K$ and never vanishes.
\item At $d=30$, the complete spectrum has a positive real root and
one unstable conjugate pair. All stable modes have at least four
cyclic sign changes. A separate flow-and-limit argument proves the
stronger statement $E^s\cap K=\{0\}$ for the whole stable subspace.
\item A nonzero orbit in $K$ cannot approach the equilibrium along
a stable manifold tangent to $E^s$. This contradicts the derivative
limits forced by the candidate pole, and in particular by the
coefficient conjecture.
\end{enumerate}
All finite arithmetic needed for step 3 is exposed in
Section~\ref{his:o59:sec:certificate}. The analytic arguments, including the
cone and the actual-orbit exclusion, are proved in the text; finite
computer checks do not replace them.

\section{Positive existence and the coefficient-to-pole bridge}
\subsection{The positive real solution is nonvacuous}
The coordinates $H,H',\ldots,H^{(d-1)}$ stay positive throughout their
positive real interval of existence. Indeed, $H^{(d)}=H^2\ge0$;
successive integration from the positive initial values proves the
claim and shows that each state coordinate is increasing.

For context, the solution does have a finite positive maximal endpoint.
This fact is not needed to infer the contradiction from the coefficient
conjecture, but it locates the obstruction at a genuine blow-up problem.
\begin{proposition}\label{his:o59:blowup}
Put $r_j=[A(d)_j]^{1/(d+j)}$ for $0\le j<d$. The positive real maximal
existence time $\rho_*$ of \eqref{his:o59:ivp} satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{his:o59:rho-bounds}
0<\min_{0\le j<d}r_j\ \le\ \rho_*\ \le\
\max_{0\le j<d}r_j<\infty.
\end{equation}
Every state coordinate diverges as $x\uparrow\rho_*$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For every $T>0$, $p_T(x)=A(T-x)^{-d}$ is an exact positive solution,
and $p_T^{(j)}(0)=A(d)_jT^{-d-j}$. The derivative-jet system is
cooperative in the positive orthant: its off-diagonal derivatives are
nonnegative. Componentwise comparison follows, for example, by the
first-contact argument with a strictly positive perturbation and then
letting the perturbation tend to zero.

At $T=\min_jr_j$, the pole's initial jet dominates the all-ones jet,
so the solution stays below that pole on the common interval. It cannot
cease to exist at an earlier time: all its coordinates would then be
bounded by the finite pole coordinates, allowing continuation. This
proves the lower bound. At $T=\max_jr_j$, comparison gives the reverse
inequality; existence beyond $T$ would contradict divergence of the
lower pole bound at $T$. This proves the upper bound.

If $H$ were bounded at the finite maximal endpoint, then $H^{(d)}=H^2$
and repeated integration would bound every state coordinate, allowing
continuation. Thus $H\to\infty$. Boundedness of any higher state
derivative on this finite interval would in turn bound $H$ by repeated
integration. Monotonicity therefore makes every state derivative
diverge.
\end{proof}
\begin{writenote}[Added 5 October 2026, batch~103]
Proposition~\ref{his:o59:blowup} is new to the repository for general $d$: Part~I's Lemma~\ref{his:lem:allblowup} proves only that $\rho_*$ is finite. At $d=3$ it is Part~I's \eqref{his:eq:rho-bounds} ($\min$ at $j=1$, $\max$ at $j=0$), at $d=4$ Part~III's \eqref{his:o7:bounds} ($j=2$, $j=0$), and at $d=30$ it gives $41.0820\ldots\le\rho_*\le43.6364\ldots$ ($\min$ at $j=12$, $\max$ at $j=0$; intake computation).
\end{writenote}


\subsection{An Abelian implication with the exact normalization}
\begin{lemma}\label{his:o59:abelian}
If \eqref{his:o59:conjecture-d} holds for a positive $\rho$, then the analytic
solution exists on $[0,\rho)$, and, for every fixed $j\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{his:o59:derivative-limits}
H^{(j)}(x)\sim A(d)_j(\rho-x)^{-d-j}
\qquad(x\uparrow\rho).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Let $a_n=h_n/n!$ and let $b_n=[z^n]p_\rho(z)$. The binomial series gives
\begin{equation}\label{his:o59:pole-coeff}
b_n=\frac{A}{(d-1)!}\frac{(n+d-1)!}{n!}\rho^{-n-d}>0.
\end{equation}
The conjecture says exactly $a_n/b_n\to1$. Since $b_n$ has radius
$\rho$, so does $\sum a_nz^n$. Inside this radius the differentiated
and multiplied series satisfy \eqref{his:o59:ivp}; uniqueness identifies them
with the initial-value solution.

Fix $\eps>0$. Beyond some index $N$,
$(1-\eps)b_n\le a_n\le(1+\eps)b_n$. Sum after multiplying by $x^n$,
where $0<x<\rho$. The finitely many omitted terms remain bounded as
$x\uparrow\rho$, whereas $p_\rho(x)\to\infty$. Division by
$p_\rho(x)$, followed by $\eps\downarrow0$, proves $H/p_\rho\to1$.
For each fixed derivative order $j$, the ratio of the corresponding
coefficients is still
\[
\frac{[z^n]H^{(j)}}{[z^n]p_\rho^{(j)}}
=\frac{((n+j)!/n!)a_{n+j}}{((n+j)!/n!)b_{n+j}}\longrightarrow1.
\]
The same tail argument proves \eqref{his:o59:derivative-limits}.
\end{proof}
This is a necessary Abelian implication. It uses neither differentiation
of an asymptotic equivalent nor a complex-domain singularity-analysis
converse. It also supplies the entire common interval needed below;
no independent claim about the Taylor radius is being assumed.

\section{A closed cyclic-sign cone}
For a real vector $v=(v_0,\ldots,v_{d-1})$, delete all zero coordinates
and read the remaining signs cyclically in their original order.
Let $V(v)$ be the number of changes between opposite signs, including
the last-to-first comparison; set $V(0)=0$. Define
\begin{equation}\label{his:o59:cone}
K=\{v\in\R^d:V(v)\le2\}.
\end{equation}
The count is always even. Positive diagonal maps and nonzero scalar
multiplication preserve it. The word cone here means invariance under
positive scalar multiplication; convexity is neither asserted nor used.

\begin{lemma}\label{his:o59:closed}
$K$ is closed. More generally, every sublevel set $\{V\le2r\}$ is closed.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
If a vector has more than $2r$ cyclic changes, there are $2r+2$
cyclically ordered nonzero entries with alternating signs. These signs
persist in a neighborhood of the vector. The complement of the stated
sublevel set is therefore open. For $K$, four alternating nonzero
entries suffice. Zero coordinates elsewhere cause no difficulty.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Directed-cycle invariance]\label{his:o59:invariance}
Suppose a continuous real linear system, on a finite time interval,
has the form
\begin{equation}\label{his:o59:cyclic-system}
v_j'=a_j(t)v_j+b_j(t)v_{j+1},\qquad b_j(t)\ge0,
\end{equation}
where indices are modulo $d$. Its forward evolution cannot increase
$V$ and in particular preserves $K$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
On a compact interval, forward Euler approximation with sufficiently
small positive steps has updates
\[
\widetilde v_j=\alpha_jv_j+\beta_jv_{j+1},
\qquad \alpha_j>0,\quad\beta_j\ge0.
\]
Interpolate the old coordinates linearly around a circle. Division by
$\alpha_j+\beta_j>0$ makes the new coordinate a sample on the arc from
old coordinate $j$ to coordinate $j+1$. The sampling points occur in
cyclic order. A linear arc contributes a sign change exactly when its
nonzero endpoint signs are opposite; zero endpoints or zero segments
add no change after zeros are deleted. Consequently the interpolated
circle has no more sign alternations than the original cyclic list,
and sampling it in cyclic order cannot increase their number.

Each Euler step therefore preserves every sublevel $\{V\le2r\}$.
Euler products converge to the exact solution for continuous linear
coefficients on the compact interval. Closedness, from
Lemma~\ref{his:o59:closed}, passes the property to that limit. Applying this
to successive compact intervals proves the assertion wherever the
system exists.
\end{proof}
Negative diagonal coefficients and large positive off-diagonal
coefficients are allowed. The special cyclic-neighbor structure is
important: no claim about arbitrary positive matrices is being used.
This weak invariance statement has been proved directly, without
requiring a stronger strict zero-number theorem.

\subsection{Why the specified initial condition belongs to the cone}
Fix any $\rho>0$, and put
\begin{equation}\label{his:o59:difference}
p(x)=A(\rho-x)^{-d},\qquad
w_j(x)=H^{(j)}(x)-p^{(j)}(x),\quad 0\le j<d.
\end{equation}
Since $(d)_d=A$, $p^{(d)}=p^2$. Its initial jet is
\[
b_j=p^{(j)}(0)=A(d)_j\rho^{-d-j}.
\]
The logarithms have strictly increasing successive differences:
\begin{equation}\label{his:o59:convexity}
\log b_{j+1}-\log b_j=\log(d+j)-\log\rho.
\end{equation}
Thus the strict sublevel set $\{j:b_j<1\}$ is an interval of consecutive
indices, possibly empty or all indices. The positive entries of
$w(0)=(1-b_j)_j$ form one block; its negative entries are in the cyclic
complement. Equalities give zero coordinates, which are deleted. Hence
\begin{equation}\label{his:o59:initial-cone}
w(0)\in K\qquad\text{for every }\rho>0.
\end{equation}

On the common positive interval the difference solves
\begin{equation}\label{his:o59:difference-flow}
w_j'=w_{j+1}\quad(j<d-1),\qquad
w_{d-1}'=(H+p)w_0.
\end{equation}
Both $H$ and $p$ are positive there, so Lemma~\ref{his:o59:invariance} gives
$w(x)\in K$ throughout that interval. This is an initial-data-specific
constraint, valid simultaneously for every possible pole location.

For $d=30$, $w$ never vanishes as a vector. If its full jet vanished
at an interior point, uniqueness for the polynomial differential
system would give $H=p$ back to zero. But having $b_0=b_1=b_2=1$
would require both $d/\rho=1$ and $(d+1)/\rho=1$, which is impossible.
Equivalently, the linear evolution in \eqref{his:o59:difference-flow} is
invertible and its initial vector is nonzero.

\section{Fowler coordinates and complete spectral ordering}\label{his:o59:sec:fowler}
From now on fix $d=30$ and $A=(30)_{30}$. Suppose for contradiction
that \eqref{his:o59:forbidden-pole} holds. Set
\begin{equation}\label{his:o59:fowler}
t=\rho-x,\qquad \tau=-\log t,\qquad u_j(\tau)=t^{d+j}w_j(x).
\end{equation}
The integration argument \eqref{his:o59:integration-limits} implies
$u(\tau)\to0$ as $\tau\to\infty$. Positive coordinatewise rescaling
and the preceding section give
\begin{equation}\label{his:o59:orbit-properties}
u(\tau)\in K\setminus\{0\}\quad\text{for all }\tau\ge-\log\rho.
\end{equation}
Because $\dd x/\dd\tau=t$, direct differentiation gives the autonomous
polynomial system
\begin{align}
u_j'&=-(d+j)u_j+u_{j+1} &&(0\le j<d-1),\label{his:o59:fowler-system}\\
u_{d-1}'&=-(2d-1)u_{d-1}+2Au_0+u_0^2.\notag
\end{align}
Write this as $u'=Bu+e_{d-1}u_0^2$. The off-diagonal entries of $B$
are $1$ along the forward chain and $2A$ on the closing edge, so
$e^{\tau B}$ preserves $K$ for $\tau\ge0$.

Eliminating coordinates of an eigenvector yields
\begin{equation}\label{his:o59:characteristic}
P(\lambda)=\prod_{a=30}^{59}(\lambda+a)-2A,
\end{equation}
and, for a root $\lambda$, an eigenvector normalized by $v_0=1$ is
\begin{equation}\label{his:o59:eigenvector}
v_j(\lambda)=\prod_{\ell=0}^{j-1}(\lambda+30+\ell),
\quad 0\le j<30.
\end{equation}
The translation root $\lambda=1$ is retained. There is also a real
root $\lambda=-90$. Both will be included in the full splitting.

\subsection{The upper modulus curve}
Write $Q(\lambda)=\prod_{a=30}^{59}(\lambda+a)$. We first show
\begin{equation}\label{his:o59:real-modulus}
|Q(\sigma)|<2A\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad -90<\sigma<1.
\end{equation}
On $[-59,-30]$, every factor has modulus at most $29$, and thus the
product is at most $29^{30}<A$. On $[-90,-59]$ all factors are
nonpositive and their product of magnitudes strictly decreases from
$2A$ to zero. On $[-30,1]$ all factors are nonnegative and this product
strictly increases from zero to $2A$. Outside $[-90,1]$ it exceeds
$2A$. The endpoint identities follow from
$\prod_{a=30}^{59}(a+1)=2A$ and
$\prod_{a=30}^{59}(90-a)=2A$.

For each $-90<\sigma<1$, the modulus
$|Q(\sigma+i\omega)|$ increases strictly from below $2A$ to infinity
as $\omega>0$ increases. There is therefore a unique $\omega(\sigma)>0$
such that
\begin{equation}\label{his:o59:modulus-curve}
\prod_{a=30}^{59}\sqrt{(\sigma+a)^2+\omega(\sigma)^2}=2A.
\end{equation}
It is smooth by the implicit function theorem, and tends to zero at
both endpoints. Outside the closed strip, each modulus factor can
only increase when an imaginary part is added, so there are no missing
roots there. At an endpoint only $\omega=0$ is possible.

Along the curve define its continuously summed phase by
\[
\theta(\sigma)=\sum_{a=30}^{59}\arg(\sigma+a+i\omega(\sigma)),
\]
with each argument in $(0,\pi)$. Put
\[
S_0=\sum_{a=30}^{59}\frac1{(\sigma+a)^2+\omega^2},\qquad
S_1=\sum_{a=30}^{59}\frac{\sigma+a}{(\sigma+a)^2+\omega^2}.
\]
Implicit differentiation of the logarithmic modulus gives
\begin{equation}\label{his:o59:phase-monotonicity}
\omega'=-\frac{S_1}{\omega S_0},\qquad
\theta'=-\omega S_0-\frac{S_1^2}{\omega S_0}<0.
\end{equation}
As $\sigma$ runs from $-90$ to $1$, the phase decreases continuously
from $30\pi$ to zero.

\begin{proposition}\label{his:o59:spectrum-order}
There are exactly fourteen upper-half-plane roots
$\lambda_k=\sigma_k+i\omega_k$ of $P$, characterized by
$\theta(\sigma_k)=2\pi k$ for $1\le k\le14$. They satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{his:o59:ordering}
1>\sigma_1>\sigma_2>\cdots>\sigma_{14}>-90.
\end{equation}
Together with their conjugates and the real roots $1,-90$, these are
all thirty roots. Every root is simple.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
On the modulus curve, $Q=2A$ exactly when its total phase is an integer
multiple of $2\pi$. Strict decrease proves existence, uniqueness and
ordering for the fourteen interior multiples. The real-axis discussion
leaves only $1$ and $-90$ as real roots. This already gives thirty
roots. For an upper root,
\[
\frac{Q'(\lambda)}{Q(\lambda)}
=\sum_{a=30}^{59}\frac1{\lambda+a}=S_1-i\omega S_0\ne0.
\]
The conjugate case follows. At each real endpoint all reciprocal
factors have the same nonzero sign, so $Q'$ is nonzero there as well.
\end{proof}
Thus no Jordan blocks, hidden real roots or ties between distinct
conjugate-pair real parts remain to be dealt with later.

\section{The compact exact phase certificate}\label{his:o59:sec:certificate}
The only finite arithmetic required by the proof determines where the
imaginary axis meets the upper modulus curve. Define
\begin{equation}\label{his:o59:Fdef}
F(y)=\prod_{a=30}^{59}\left(1+\frac{iy}{a}\right),\qquad
\Theta(y)=\sum_{a=30}^{59}\arctan\frac ya\quad(y\ge0).
\end{equation}
There is a unique $\omega_*>0$ with $|F(\omega_*)|=2$; it equals
$\omega(0)$ from \eqref{his:o59:modulus-curve}. We will certify
\begin{equation}\label{his:o59:phase-target}
2\pi<\Theta(\omega_*)<4\pi.
\end{equation}

Take $q=911/100$, and compute exact integers
\begin{equation}\label{his:o59:gaussian-product}
R+iI=\prod_{a=30}^{59}(100a+911i),\qquad S=100^{30}A.
\end{equation}
Then $F(q)=(R+iI)/S$. The following seven strict inequalities use only
integers or rational numbers:
\begin{align}
R&>0, & I&>0, & R^2+I^2&<4S^2,\label{his:o59:cert-one}\\
6&<\sum_{a=30}^{59}\left(\frac qa-\frac{q^3}{3a^3}\right),
&&\sum_{a=30}^{59}\frac qa<7,\label{his:o59:cert-two}\\
\prod_{a=30}^{59}(a^2+100)&>4A^2,
&&\sum_{a=30}^{59}\frac{10}{a}<8.\label{his:o59:cert-three}
\end{align}
They are independently reproducible by the short program in
Section~\ref{his:o59:app:certificate} and the fuller companion checker.
Neither transcendental-function evaluation nor floating-point
arithmetic is involved in these comparisons. As optional exact rational
enclosures for orientation, the same computations give
\[
1.994<R/S<1.995,\qquad 0.0049<I/S<0.0050.
\]
The finite decimals here denote exact rationals, not floating-point
error estimates; the seven inequalities above remain the certificate.

For $x>0$, integration of
$1-x^2<1/(1+x^2)<1$ gives
\[
x-\frac{x^3}{3}<\arctan x<x.
\]
Thus \eqref{his:o59:cert-two} implies $6<\Theta(q)<7$.
Using $3<\pi<22/7$, this interval lies within
$(3\pi/2,5\pi/2)$. Since $I>0$, its sine is positive. The only
possibility in this interval is therefore $\Theta(q)>2\pi$.
The modulus inequality \eqref{his:o59:cert-one}, and strict increase of
$|F(y)|$, give $q<\omega_*$. The first inequality in
\eqref{his:o59:cert-three} is $|F(10)|^2>4$, so $\omega_*<10$.
Finally,
\begin{equation}\label{his:o59:certificate-chain}
2\pi<\Theta(q)<\Theta(\omega_*)<\Theta(10)<8<4\pi.
\end{equation}
This proves \eqref{his:o59:phase-target} with strict margins.

\begin{corollary}\label{his:o59:spectral-count}
$B$ is hyperbolic. Its unstable eigenvalues are exactly
$1,\lambda_1,\overline{\lambda_1}$; its stable eigenvalues are
$-90$ and $\lambda_k,\overline{\lambda_k}$ for $2\le k\le14$.
Its real stable subspace has dimension $27$ and its real unstable
subspace has dimension $3$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
At $\sigma=0$, the modulus-curve phase is
$\theta(0)=\Theta(\omega_*)\in(2\pi,4\pi)$.
By strict decrease of $\theta$, the root with phase $2\pi$ has
positive real part, whereas every root with phase $2\pi k$, $k\ge2$,
has negative real part. No root lies on the imaginary axis. Include
the two real roots and use Proposition~\ref{his:o59:spectrum-order}.
\end{proof}

For orientation, an independently computed rational Routh table for
$P(\lambda)/(\lambda-1)$ has first-column signs
\[
\underbrace{+\ \cdots\ +}_{28\ \text{entries}}\quad-\quad+.
\]
This second finite check agrees with the spectral conclusion. The
proof above does not require Routh's theorem or a large determinant
table. A numerical root approximation is not an input to the proof.

\section{The entire stable subspace misses the cone}
The ordering just proved has a precise sign interpretation.
For an upper root $\lambda_k$, set
\[
\delta_j=\arg(\lambda_k+30+j)\in(0,\pi),\qquad
s_0=0,\quad s_j=\sum_{\ell<j}\delta_\ell.
\]
Append $v_{30}=2Av_0$ to the eigenvector \eqref{his:o59:eigenvector}. Its
unwrapped phase is $s_{30}=2\pi k$, so the appended coordinate
closes the cyclic sign comparison with a positive multiple of $v_0$.

\begin{lemma}\label{his:o59:eigen-sign}
For a generic phase $\phi$, meaning that no displayed coordinate
vanishes, every vector $\Rea(c v(\lambda_k))$ with $\arg c=\phi$
and $c\ne0$ has exactly $2k$ cyclic sign changes. An eigenvector for
$-90$ has $30$ cyclic sign changes.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The real coordinates have signs of
$\cos(\phi+s_j)$, since their amplitude factors are positive.
The consecutive phase arcs have lengths $\delta_j<\pi$ and together
cover an interval of length $2\pi k$. Each arc contains at most one
zero of cosine, and the full interval contains exactly $2k$ zeros.
At a generic phase none is an endpoint. Every zero crossing changes
the sampled sign across that arc, and every sampled sign change
requires a crossing. This gives exactly $2k$ cyclic changes, including
the closing arc to $v_{30}=2Av_0$.
For $-90$, all factors $-90+30+j$ are negative, so the coordinates
alternate signs. Since $d=30$ is even, the closing edge also changes
sign, giving thirty changes.
\end{proof}
Only generic phases will be needed. No convention that assigns signs
to zero coordinates is introduced.

\begin{proposition}\label{his:o59:stable-exclusion}
For the full real stable subspace $E^s$ of $B$,
\begin{equation}\label{his:o59:intersection}
E^s\cap K=\{0\}.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Suppose $0\ne z\in E^s\cap K$. The linear flow $e^{\tau B}z$
remains in $K$ by Lemma~\ref{his:o59:invariance}. Decompose $z$ into its
components in the simple real eigenspace and simple conjugate-pair
real eigenspaces. Among its nonzero components select the one with
largest real part. Proposition~\ref{his:o59:spectrum-order} makes this
component unique up to its conjugate pair.

If it is the pair indexed by $k\ge2$, there is a $c\ne0$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{his:o59:leading-linear}
e^{-\sigma_k\tau}e^{\tau B}z
=\Rea\!\left(c e^{i\omega_k\tau}v(\lambda_k)\right)+o(1)
\qquad(\tau\to\infty).
\end{equation}
All other components decay relatively, by the strict real-part
ordering. Choose a generic phase and a sequence $\tau_n\to\infty$
on which $\arg c+\omega_k\tau_n$ is that phase modulo $2\pi$.
Such a sequence is an arithmetic progression of spacing
$2\pi/\omega_k$; no density assumption on discrete rotations is needed.
The left side of \eqref{his:o59:leading-linear} belongs to $K$, because its
rescaling factor is positive. Its limit is a nonzero generic vector
of that pair, with $2k\ge4$ changes by Lemma~\ref{his:o59:eigen-sign}.
This contradicts closedness of $K$.

If the leading component is the real mode $-90$, the same positive
rescaling yields a nonzero real eigenvector with thirty changes,
again contradicting closedness. These cases exhaust the stable
subspace and prove \eqref{his:o59:intersection}.
\end{proof}
This proposition supplies an indispensable step. Counting signs in
individual eigenvectors alone would not control arbitrary linear
combinations. Here invariance of the cone, strict spectral ordering,
and a leading-mode limit establish the statement for the entire
$27$-dimensional stable subspace.

\section{Excluding the actual nonlinear orbit}\label{his:o59:sec:orbit}
The vector field \eqref{his:o59:fowler-system} is polynomial, with quadratic
remainder at its equilibrium $0$. Corollary~\ref{his:o59:spectral-count}
makes that equilibrium hyperbolic. The ordinary local stable-manifold
theorem gives a $C^1$ graph
\begin{equation}\label{his:o59:stable-graph}
W^s_{\mathrm{loc}}=
\{s+h(s):s\in E^s\text{ near }0\},\qquad
h(s)\in E^u,\quad h(0)=0,\quad Dh(0)=0,
\end{equation}
and every trajectory tending to $0$ eventually lies in this graph.
For an explicit reference, apply Lanford's Theorem 7.3.1(1),(4)
to the local time-one map~\cite[printed p.~74, PDF p.~76]{lanford1997}.
Its derivative is $e^B$, its stable and unstable spaces are $E^s,E^u$,
and no multiplier has modulus one. Choose the spectral cutoff between
the largest stable multiplier modulus and $1$. In the notation of that
theorem, $\rho_-<1<\rho_+$, which is the ordinary hyperbolic stable
case. Local ODE existence supplies this map on a neighborhood of the
origin; global completeness is unnecessary. A convergent trajectory's
sufficiently late future stays in the required neighborhood.

\begin{lemma}\label{his:o59:tangent-cone}
The graph \eqref{his:o59:stable-graph} has no sufficiently small nonzero point
in $K$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Otherwise there are $z_n=s_n+h(s_n)\in K\setminus\{0\}$ tending to
zero. Pass to a subsequence such that $z_n/\norm{z_n}$ converges to
a unit vector $v$. It lies in $K$ by positive-scaling invariance and
closedness. Since $h(s)=o(\norm{s})$ and the fixed projection onto
$E^s$ along $E^u$ is bounded, $\norm{s_n}\le C\norm{z_n}$.
Consequently $h(s_n)/\norm{z_n}\to0$, so $v\in E^s$.
This contradicts Proposition~\ref{his:o59:stable-exclusion}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Completion of Theorem~\ref{his:o59:main}]
The assumed scalar pole asymptotic implies \eqref{his:o59:integration-limits},
and hence $u(\tau)\to0$. The actual all-ones orbit has
$u(\tau)\in K\setminus\{0\}$ by \eqref{his:o59:orbit-properties}.
For all sufficiently large $\tau$, the stable-manifold theorem puts
it in \eqref{his:o59:stable-graph}, contradicting Lemma~\ref{his:o59:tangent-cone}.
Thus the scalar asymptotic is impossible. If the published coefficient
asymptotic held, Lemma~\ref{his:o59:abelian} would imply that same scalar
asymptotic (and all normalized derivative limits directly).
It therefore fails for $d=30$, which is $m=29$ and B-tree order $59$.
\end{proof}

The argument needs no asymptotic leading-eigenmode theorem for a
nonlinear trajectory. In particular, it makes no nonresonance
assumption and no assumption that the slowest stable coefficient is
nonzero. Approaching a stronger stable submanifold, higher-order
cancellation, or unusually fast decay cannot evade the tangent-cone
contradiction. Neither a genericity argument nor a dimension count
has been substituted for the all-ones initial condition.

\section{What is established, and what remains open}\label{his:o59:sec:open}
\subsection{Logical scope}
The counterexample settles the universal formulation by one member of
the natural family. It does not invalidate every smaller-order case.
The exact seven-inequality certificate concerns $d=30$ only; this report
does not claim a complete stability classification of integer orders.
In particular, it does not use or require a separate finite computation
through $d=150$, and it does not claim here that $30$ is the first
order with an extra unstable pair. The eigenvalue $1$ is always
present; any discussion of a later ``loss of stability'' must specify
that it concerns additional modes beyond this translation direction.

No replacement for \eqref{his:o59:conjecture-d} has been proved for order $59$.
The theorem excludes convergence to the pure-pole equilibrium for this
orbit; it does not identify an alternative limiting object. Oscillatory
transients, numerical periodic-looking plots and a local Hopf
calculation would not, on their own, prove a periodic attractor or its
basin for the specified solution. The existence of non-power solutions
for other exponents or other initial conditions also does not supply
that missing conclusion.

The sources used here establish the combinatorial model, the exact
published conjecture, and the local stable-manifold theorem. The
finite certificate, cone argument and orbit exclusion are presented
explicitly in this report. The companion's independent implementations
and negative tests are mathematical and reproducibility checks, not
formal proof-assistant verification or external peer review. No global
literature-novelty determination is asserted.

\subsection{Further work directed by the obstruction}
The most useful next questions concern the actual high-order behavior,
rather than refining a disproved pure-pole formula.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
\item \textbf{Identify the real normalized orbit.}
For $U_j=(\rho_*-x)^{d+j}H^{(j)}$, the uncentered Fowler equations are
$U_j'=U_{j+1}-(d+j)U_j$ and
$U_{d-1}'=U_0^2-(2d-1)U_{d-1}$.
One needs rigorous bounds and a description of the actual orbit's
limit set. If a periodic profile is proposed, prove its existence,
stability and attraction of this initial condition. The present
exclusion proves none of these three properties.
\item \textbf{Recover coefficient asymptotics.}
A real blow-up description alone would not justify coefficient
transfer. Establish suitable complex continuation, control all
singularities of smallest modulus, and prove uniform error bounds.
A genuinely log-periodic singular profile would require transferring
its harmonics with their individual gamma factors; an assumed
profile cannot be used as a theorem.
\item \textbf{Build valid inverse and threshold estimates.}
After a replacement coefficient law is proved, derive the inverse
counting scale and any phase-dependent corrections, together with
error control strong enough for integer threshold brackets.
Inversion of the false pure-pole model at order $59$ is not justified.
\item \textbf{Resolve the transition across orders.}
Exact spectral certificates for successive integer $d$ can locate
additional crossings; continuous parameter families can distinguish
spectral crossings from genuine nonlinear bifurcations. Then determine
which initial data approach which limiting profiles. Stability at a
smaller order, by itself, proves neither the all-ones pole behavior
nor the coefficient conjecture at that order.
\end{enumerate}
These are research targets, not consequences silently appended to the
counterexample.

\section{A short standalone exact certificate (Report~117, Appendix~A)}\label{his:o59:app:certificate}
The following standard-library program regenerates all seven
inequalities. Its integer pair update is multiplication by
$100a+911i$; the simultaneous assignment uses the old $R,I$ in both
right-hand expressions. Every comparison is exact. Failure guards
are ordinary conditionals, so \texttt{python -O} cannot remove them.
\begin{verbatim}
from fractions import Fraction as F
from math import prod

d = 30
A = prod(range(d, 2*d))
q = F(911, 100)
R, I = 1, 0
for a in range(d, 2*d):
    R, I = 100*a*R - 911*I, 911*R + 100*a*I
S = 100**d * A
lower = sum(q/a - (q/a)**3/3 for a in range(d, 2*d))
upper = sum(q/a for a in range(d, 2*d))
checks = {
    "real positive": R > 0,
    "imaginary positive": I > 0,
    "modulus at q below 2": R*R + I*I < 4*S*S,
    "lower phase bound exceeds 6": lower > 6,
    "upper phase bound below 7": upper < 7,
    "modulus at 10 exceeds 2":
        prod(a*a + 100 for a in range(d, 2*d)) > 4*A*A,
    "phase bound at 10 below 8":
        sum(F(10, a) for a in range(d, 2*d)) < 8,
}
for name, ok in checks.items():
    if not ok:
        raise RuntimeError(name)
print("PASS: all seven exact inequalities")
\end{verbatim}
This short replay illustrates the complete critical arithmetic;
\texttt{checks/check.py} adds independent reconstructions, frozen exact
fixtures, identity checks and strict integrity validation. The
analytical inference from these seven comparisons to the spectrum
is Section~\ref{his:o59:sec:certificate}, not an unstated property of the program.

\section{A proof dependency checklist (Report~117, Appendix~B)}\label{his:o59:sec:checklist}
For a line-by-line audit, the essential dependencies are:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
\item \textbf{Published normalization:}
$d=m+1$, B-tree order $2d-1$, the all-ones jet, and the factorial and
radius powers in \eqref{his:o59:conjecture-d}.
\item \textbf{The Abelian bridge:}
positive coefficient tails imply the real limits of all needed
derivatives; no singularity-analysis converse is used.
\item \textbf{Closedness at zeros:}
$K$ uses deleted-zero cyclic variation, and every prohibited sign
pattern is witnessed by nonzero alternating coordinates.
\item \textbf{Forward invariance:}
Euler steps sample consecutive cyclic arcs and pass to the continuous
flow through the closed cone.
\item \textbf{The actual initial jet:}
logarithmic convexity holds for every candidate $\rho$, positivity
makes the closing coupling nonnegative, and uniqueness excludes the
exact-pole exception.
\item \textbf{Complete spectral control:}
the modulus curve gives all roots, simplicity and strict real-part
ordering; seven strict rational/integer inequalities put exactly one
conjugate pair beyond the translation direction in the unstable space.
\item \textbf{Whole-space exclusion:}
linear-flow invariance and a phase-chosen limit control all stable
linear combinations, not just individual eigenvectors.
\item \textbf{Nonlinear exclusion:}
hyperbolicity gives the local stable graph; its tangency and
$E^s\cap K=\{0\}$ prohibit a nonzero normalized orbit in $K$ from
converging to the equilibrium.
\end{enumerate}
The supplementary blow-up enclosure and the independent Routh table
are useful checks and context, but neither is required for this
logical chain from the conjecture to a contradiction.

The detailed third-order proof dependencies can be checked in the following order.
\begin{enumerate}
 \item Verify the change of variables \eqref{his:eq:coordinates} and the exact cancellation in \eqref{his:eq:Vdot}. Compactness must allow the harmless boundary $p=0$, while keeping $q$ away from zero.
 \item Reconstruct the real solution by \eqref{his:eq:reconstruction}. Finite $\rho$ and the constant $60$ follow without a numerical approximation.
 \item Check the nonresonant Poincar\'e hypotheses for \eqref{his:eq:jacobian}; a smooth or formal linearization would not suffice for the claimed convergence.
 \item Check the signs in \eqref{his:eq:S} and \eqref{his:eq:w}, then the exact time identity \eqref{his:eq:time-exact}. The coefficient denominators in $S$ and $B$ have real parts growing with total degree.
 \item Apply analytic implicit inversion at $(\mathcal R,\eta)=(1,0)$, where the derivative is $1$. Nonzero orbit constants and \eqref{his:eq:linearcoeff} prove $C\ne0$.
 \item Separate the proof of the radius from the proof of uniqueness on the circle. Strict positivity for each of $H,H',H''$ gives the common margin in \eqref{his:eq:strict} and the dominating disk \eqref{his:eq:disk}.
 \item Transfer finitely many terms only. Remove analytic diagonal monomials, retain exact Gamma ratios, and expand one extra grade to justify the noninteger-tail remainder.
 \item Invert the logarithmic expansion using monotonicity and a ceiling window. Neither fitted constants nor a root extremely near an integer justify dropping that window.
\end{enumerate}

For the fourth-order result, additionally check the monotone-density derivative upgrade, the three nonresonant exponents, the conserved energy, and the backward-Metzler exclusion of a pure real mode. For the uniform obstruction, verify the finite rational phase bounds and the infinite-range inequalities, the whole-subspace sign-variation theorem, and the generalized stable-manifold hypotheses. The last step must use the slow/fast spectral gap; ordinary hyperbolic stability alone would not cover every high order.
