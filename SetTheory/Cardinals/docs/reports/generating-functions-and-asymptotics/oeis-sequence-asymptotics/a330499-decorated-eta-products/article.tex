\begin{abstract}
Let $L(z)=-\log(1-z)$, $\rho=1-e^{-1}$ and $M=e-1$. This report prints two manuscripts on the compositions $\sum_{k\ge1}\log(1+L(z)^k)$ (OEIS A330499) and $\sum_{k\ge1}k^{-1}\log(1+L(z)^k)$ (OEIS A330498), and on the same compositions with any analytic decoration $B$ in place of $L$ that has nonnegative coefficients, a root $B(\rho)=1$ inside its disk, difference span one and positive variance. Part~I proves, by the Dedekind eta transformation and a uniform stationary-phase estimate on the coefficient circle, that $a_n\rho^n/n!=\pi^2/(12(e-1))+\sum_{j<N}n^{-3/4-j/2}H_j(\sqrt n)+O(n^{-3/4-N/2})$ for every $N$, with explicit almost periodic $H_j$; this identifies the decimal $0.478656\ldots$ recorded in A330499. It shows that the error changes sign at the scale $n^{-3/4}$, that root-of-unity cusps exclude a standard Delta-domain at $\rho$, and gives two-ceiling inverse envelopes at every order. Part~II proves, by a signed divisor Voronoi identity, a Schwartz-space renewal interpolation and a uniform Hankel estimate, that $n\rho^na(n)/n!=\log2+\sum_{j<J}n^{-1/4-j/2}H_j(\sqrt n)+O(n^{-1/4-J/2})$, with $P_0,\dots,P_3$ explicit; in particular $a(n)\sim\log2\,(n-1)!\rho^{-n}$, which proves Kot\v{e}\v{s}ovec's conjecture $c=\log2$ in A330498. It also gives signed subsequences at scale $n^{-1/4}$, eventual positivity, increase and strict log-convexity, and inverse envelopes. The write records that the two leading oscillations have the same frequencies, damping and phase and differ only in their amplitudes, and places the inverses relative to the repository's transseries volume. Effective constants, other spans, vanishing variance, error laws and higher-order structure remain open. The report is unrefereed, and nothing in it is formalized.
\end{abstract}

\emph{One framework, two kernels.} Both Parts study a composition
$\sum_{k\ge1}w_k\log(1+B(z)^k)$ with an analytic decoration $B$ that has
nonnegative coefficients, a positive root $B(\rho)=1$ inside its disk of
analyticity, difference span one and positive variance; both specialize to
$B=L=-\log(1-z)$, where $\rho=1-e^{-1}$, $M=e-1$ and the size law has mean and
variance $\mu=v=M$. Part~I takes $w_k=1$: the arithmetic weight is
$c_m=\sigma(\odd(m))/m>0$, the exact transformation is the Dedekind eta
identity, the coefficients have a pole-sized main term $\pi^2/(12\mu)$ after
multiplication by $\rho^n$, and the first correction is
$n^{-3/4}H_0(\sqrt n)$; for $B=L$ this is OEIS A330499. Part~II takes
$w_k=1/k$: the weight is the signed $h(m)/m$, the transformation is a signed
divisor Voronoi identity, $n\rho^n[z^n]$ tends to $\log2$, and the first
correction is $n^{-1/4}H_0(\sqrt n)$; for $B=L$ this is OEIS A330498, and the
$\log2$ is the constant that the entry states as a conjecture. Part~II
continues Part~I explicitly (its Section~\ref{dep:hl:sec:sources}, ``Relation
to Report185''), and Part~I excludes A330498 from every theorem
(Section~\ref{dep:eta:sec:sources}). Neither theorem specializes the other.

\begin{writenote}[7 October 2026; the write's observation]
The two leading oscillatory functions have the same frequencies, damping and
phase, and differ only in their amplitudes. For a decoration $B$ with mean
$\mu$ and variance $v$, Part~I's \eqref{dep:eta:eq:H0general} has, with
$\alpha_m=2\pi^2m$, the frequency $2\sqrt{\alpha_m/\mu}=2\sqrt{2\pi^2m/\mu}$,
which is twice Part~II's $\beta_m$ of \eqref{dep:hl:eq:generaldef}, and the
damping $e^{-\alpha_md}=e^{-\pi^2mv/\mu^2}$ of Part~II's
\eqref{dep:hl:eq:generalH}; and $-\cos(x+\pi/4)=\sin(x-\pi/4)$ for every real
$x$, since both equal $(\sin x-\cos x)/\sqrt2$. So
\begin{align*}
 H_0^{\mathrm I}(u)&=\sum_{m\ge1}c_m\frac{(2\pi^2m/\mu)^{1/4}}{\sqrt\pi}e^{-\pi^2mv/\mu^2}\sin(2\beta_mu-\tfrac\pi4),\\
 H_{B,0}^{\mathrm{II}}(u)&=\sum_{m\ge1}d_o(m)\Bigl(\frac{8\mu}m\Bigr)^{1/4}e^{-\pi^2mv/\mu^2}\sin(2\beta_mu-\tfrac\pi4),
\end{align*}
with the arithmetic weights $c_m=\sigma(\odd(m))/m$ and $d_o(m)=\tau(\odd(m))$
and the powers $m^{1/4}$ and $m^{-1/4}$. For $B=L$ both have damping
$r^m$, $r=e^{-\pi^2/M}$, which is why the two first-harmonic certificates
(Lemma~\ref{dep:eta:lem:tail} and \eqref{dep:hl:eq:tail}) start from the same
bound $r<1/300$. Neither manuscript states this comparison; it does not make
either theorem a consequence of the other, because the exact transformations
and the main terms differ.
\end{writenote}

\section*{The inverses and the transseries volume}\phantomsection\label{dep:sec:inverses}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{The inverses and the transseries volume}
The repository's transseries volume \cite{tsvol} has a Part~0 apparatus for
inverting rapidly growing functions. Its symbols are subscripted ``vol''
here ($\kappa_\vol$, $d_\vol$, $L_\vol$, $\Phi_\vol$, $\varrho_\vol$).
Statement by statement:
\begin{itemize}
\item Part~I's Lambert initialization \eqref{dep:eta:eq:lambert}, the root of
$x_0\log(x_0/(e\rho))=y$, and Part~II's \eqref{dep:hl:eq:Lambert}, the same
equation with $Y$ for $y$: \emph{instances} of the factorial core
\file{p0:prop:factorial-core} with $\kappa_\vol=1$, $d_\vol=-(1+\log\rho)$ and
target $L_\vol=y$ (resp.\ $Y$). That proposition gives
$v_\vol=W_0(y/(e\rho))$ and $X_\vol=e\rho\,e^{v_\vol}=y/v_\vol$, which is
$x_0$; the side condition $\kappa_\vol\log X_\vol+d_\vol=v_\vol>0$ holds for
$y>0$. The $O(1)$ distance to the true root stated in Part~II is Part~II's own
estimate.
\item Part~I's Lagrange reversion \eqref{dep:eta:eq:reversion}: as a formal
series in $\varepsilon$, an \emph{instance} of the perturbation-operator
series \file{p0:eq:operator-series} of \file{p0:prop:operator-form}, with the
core $\Phi_\vol=\mathcal F_*$ of \eqref{dep:eta:eq:core} and the perturbation
$\varrho_\vol=E_J$: Part~I's $D_y=(1/\mathcal F_*'(x))D_x$ is the volume's
$\mathscr D_0$ and $X'(y)=1/\Phi_\vol'(X)$. That the series may be evaluated
at $\varepsilon=1$ for large $y$ is Part~I's own argument; the write did not
compare it with the volume's remainder-transport theorem.
\item Part~I's one-step correction \eqref{dep:eta:eq:lambertcorrect}, its
first oscillatory shift \eqref{dep:eta:eq:shift} (Proposition~\ref{dep:eta:prop:shift}),
and Part~II's first inverse correction \eqref{dep:hl:eq:inversecorr}: each is
the first-order term $-\varrho_\vol(X)/\Phi_\vol'(X)$ of the same series, about
the cores $x\log(x/(e\rho))$, $\mathcal F_*$ and
$\log\Gamma(b)-b\log\rho+\log\log2$, with error bounds proved by the Parts
themselves. \emph{Analogues}, not instances of a theorem of the volume.
\item Part~I's Theorem~\ref{dep:eta:thm:inverse} and Part~II's
Theorem~\ref{dep:hl:thm:inverse}: two-ceiling brackets proved directly at the
integers from a pointwise error bound for $\log a_n$, without an admissible
interpolation of the sequence; \emph{not} instances of
\file{p0:thm:staircase}. When the two ceilings agree they determine the
threshold, the same conclusion as item~(2) of that theorem, and both Parts'
warnings that a smooth root cannot be rounded by one ceiling at every jump
describe the failure modes of its items~(2) and~(5).
\end{itemize}
Neither Part claims novelty for the inversion mechanics.

\part{Decorated logarithmic eta products and OEIS A330499: uniform oscillatory asymptotics and threshold inverses}\label{dep:eta:part}
\partsource{Bundle Report~185, archive \file{Decorated_Eta_Oscillatory_Asymptotics_and_Inverses_Source.zip}, delivered as \file{Report185.tex} (21 pages), the base of the merge. Delivered title block ``Report185 / Decorated logarithmic eta products and OEIS A330499 / Uniform oscillatory asymptotics and threshold inverses / 3 October 2026''. \begin{partabstract}
We prove an all-fixed-orders coefficient theorem for the composition
$F_B(z)=\sum_{k\ge1}\log(1+B(z)^k)$, where $B$ is analytic with nonnegative
coefficients and its occupied sizes have difference span one. If
$B(\rho)=1$ strictly inside the disk of analyticity, the leading coefficient
is $\pi^2/(12\mu)$ after multiplication by $\rho^n$, where $\mu$ is the
mean of the decorating size law. The first correction is
$n^{-3/4}H_0(\sqrt n)$, followed by half-power orders; each $H_j$ is an
explicit entire trigonometric series, bounded and uniformly almost periodic
on the real axis. Its mode damping is determined by the variance divided
by the squared mean. A pole-subtracted boundary integral and a uniform
stationary-phase estimate justify summing the actual remainders, even
though accumulating root-of-unity cusps prevent standard Delta-domain
transfer. For $B(z)=-\log(1-z)$, we identify the exact leading constant
of A330499, give its first three oscillatory terms, prove signed
subsequences that exclude a pure $1/n$ refinement, and derive
rounding-aware inverse envelopes to every fixed order. Exact finite
checks and optional floating-point diagnostics accompany the proof.
\end{partabstract}

\paragraph{Scope.}
The reusable contribution is the decorated-product coefficient theorem,
including its uniform remainder. The eta transformation, stationary
phase, labelled supernecklaces and inverse reversion are classical
inputs. The bounded source comparison in Section~\ref{dep:eta:sec:sources} is
not a worldwide priority determination. All orders and the decoration
are fixed before $n\to\infty$. The constants in the asymptotic remainder
are existence constants; this report does not supply a certified
large-$n$ cutoff or a certified numerical inverse algorithm.

\section{Main results and their meaning}\label{dep:eta:sec:main}
Let
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:model}
 L(z)=-\log(1-z),\qquad
 F(z)=\sum_{k\ge1}\log(1+L(z)^k)
     =\sum_{n\ge0}a_n\frac{z^n}{n!}.
\end{equation}
All logarithms in the defining series are the power-series logarithms
at the origin. The sequence $(a_n)_{n\ge0}$ is OEIS A330499~\cite{oeisA330499}.
Its first values are
\[
 0,\ 1,\ 2,\ 13,\ 71,\ 558,\ 5344,\ 60926,\ 766898,\ 10759096.
\]
The OEIS record inspected on 3 October 2026 gives the leading equivalent
$n!c(1-e^{-1})^{-n}$ with the displayed decimal $c=0.478656\ldots$.
Define
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:logconstants}
 \rho=1-e^{-1},\qquad M=e-1,\qquad C=\frac{\pi^2}{12M}
       =0.478656655620749637061291495659\ldots.
\end{equation}
The decimal is a numerical evaluation of the exact expression, not an
interval certificate.

\begin{theorem}[A330499 at every fixed order]\label{dep:eta:thm:A}
There are real entire functions $H_j$, $j\ge0$, bounded on the real axis,
such that for every fixed integer $N\ge1$,
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:Aall}
 a_n=n!\rho^{-n}\left\{C+
       \sum_{j=0}^{N-1}n^{-3/4-j/2}H_j(\sqrt n)
       +\OO_N(n^{-3/4-N/2})\right\}.
\end{equation}
Each $H_j$ and each of its derivatives is given by an absolutely
convergent trigonometric series. The first three functions are explicit
in Section~\ref{dep:eta:sec:three}; a finite recipe for every further function
is given in Theorem~\ref{dep:eta:thm:general}. In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:threeintro}
 \frac{a_n\rho^n}{n!}=C+n^{-3/4}H_0(\sqrt n)
        +n^{-5/4}H_1(\sqrt n)+n^{-7/4}H_2(\sqrt n)
        +\OO(n^{-9/4}).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

The oscillation in \eqref{dep:eta:eq:Aall} is a power-scale effect, not an
exponentially small correction. In particular, there is no constant
$c_1$ for which $a_n\rho^n/n!=C+c_1/n+\OO(n^{-2})$.
Section~\ref{dep:eta:sec:sign} proves this from an exact rational first-harmonic
tail estimate and explicit signed subsequences. A leading equivalent
by itself does not reveal this obstruction.

The proof is most useful in a broader setting. For a nonnegative
analytic decoration $B$, the numbers $b_j\rho^j$ at $B(\rho)=1$ define
a probability law. If its mean and variance are $\mu$ and $v$, the
$m$th oscillation has damping
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:damping}
 \exp\left(-\frac{\pi^2mv}{\mu^2}\right).
\end{equation}
This identifies a structural smoothing parameter. A small variance
relative to the squared mean makes many modes relevant; it does not
justify taking a zero-variance limit of a theorem whose constants depend
on the decoration.

\paragraph{Why the proof uses the coefficient boundary.}
The modular eta identity is exact, but the coefficient boundary
approaches its singular point tangentially. The local real-axis
expansion loses the oscillatory information. Moreover, singular
preimages of odd-order roots of unity accumulate at $\rho$ from
nearly vertical directions. Section~\ref{dep:eta:sec:cusps} proves that these
are actual nonremovable cusps. The standard Delta-domain hypothesis
for singularity analysis is therefore unavailable. We instead subtract
the dominant pole, use dominated boundary coefficient extraction, and
control every dual eta mode uniformly before summing its error.

\section{Exact coefficients and labelled interpretation}\label{dep:eta:sec:exact}
For an integer $m\ge1$, let $\odd(m)$ be its largest odd divisor,
and let $\sigma(m)=\sum_{d\mid m}d$. Put
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:cm}
 c_m=\frac{\sigma(\odd(m))}{m}.
\end{equation}
The distinction between $c_m$ and the leading constant $C$ will be used
throughout.

\begin{lemma}[The positive logarithmic product coefficients]\label{dep:eta:lem:cm}
For $|q|<1$,
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:F0}
 F_0(q):=\sum_{k\ge1}\log(1+q^k)=\sum_{m\ge1}c_mq^m.
\end{equation}
In particular, $0<c_m\le1+\log m$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The defining logarithmic series converges locally normally. Writing
$P(q)=\prod_{k\ge1}(1-q^k)$ and using its analytic logarithm gives
\[
 F_0(q)=\log P(q^2)-\log P(q),\qquad
 \log P(q)=-\sum_{m\ge1}\sigma_{-1}(m)q^m,
\]
where $\sigma_{-1}(m)=\sum_{d\mid m}d^{-1}=\sigma(m)/m$.
Consequently the coefficient is
\[
 \sigma_{-1}(m)-\mathbf1_{2\mid m}\sigma_{-1}(m/2).
\]
Writing $m=2^\nu u$ with $u$ odd shows that this difference equals
$\sigma(u)/m$. Positivity follows, while
$c_m\le\sigma_{-1}(m)\le\sum_{j=1}^m1/j\le1+\log m$.
Alternatively, expanding the individual logarithms gives the exact
identity $c_m=\sum_{j\mid m}(-1)^{j+1}/j$.
\end{proof}

Unsigned Stirling numbers of the first kind are denoted by
$\stir nk$. They satisfy
\[
 \frac{L(z)^k}{k!}=\sum_{n\ge k}\stir nk\frac{z^n}{n!},
 \qquad
 \stir{n+1}k=n\stir nk+\stir n{k-1}.
\]
Thus \eqref{dep:eta:eq:F0} yields the finite exact transform
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:stirling}
 a_n=\sum_{k=1}^n\stir nk\,(k-1)!\,\sigma(\odd(k)),\qquad a_0=0.
\end{equation}
This is an integer formula with positive weights. It is also an
independent way to evaluate the coefficients without summing truncated
analytic products numerically.

\begin{proposition}[Marked cycles of cycles and strict growth]\label{dep:eta:prop:counts}
The number $a_n$ counts labelled cycles of cycles with the following
additional choice: if there are $k$ inner cycles, choose an odd divisor
$d$ of $k$ and one of $d$ colors. Furthermore,
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:monotone}
 a_{n+1}>n a_n\quad(n\ge1),
\end{equation}
and $(a_n)_{n\ge0}$ is strictly increasing.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
There are $\stir nk$ ways to decompose $n$ distinct labels into $k$
nonempty cycles. These $k$ distinguishable cycles can be arranged into
an outer directed cycle in $(k-1)!$ ways. The additional finite choice
has $\sum_{d\mid k,\ d\text{ odd}}d=\sigma(\odd(k))$ possibilities.
This gives \eqref{dep:eta:eq:stirling}. The underlying unmarked labelled
cycle-of-cycles construction is classical supernecklace
combinatorics~\cite{supernecklace,ac}; the divisor-colored version here
is the direct interpretation of the displayed weight.

Write $w_k=(k-1)!\sigma(\odd(k))>0$. Applying the Stirling recurrence
and taking $\stir nk=0$ outside $0\le k\le n$ gives
\[
 a_{n+1}=n a_n+\sum_{j=0}^n\stir nj w_{j+1}>n a_n.
\]
The last sum is positive for $n\ge1$. Together with $a_0=0$ and
$a_1=1$, this proves the assertion.
\end{proof}

Unmarked supernecklaces have generating function $-\log(1-L(z))$
and a different leading scale $n!\rho^{-n}/n$. The finite marking in
Proposition~\ref{dep:eta:prop:counts} is not asymptotically innocuous: the
logarithmic eta product has a pole-sized leading term and oscillatory
power corrections.

\section{The general analytic decoration theorem}\label{dep:eta:sec:general}
Assume that
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:Bassump}
 B(z)=\sum_{j\ge1}b_jz^j,\qquad b_j\ge0,
 \qquad B\text{ holomorphic for }|z|<R,
 \qquad 0<\rho<R,\quad B(\rho)=1.
\end{equation}
The support is required to have \emph{difference span one}:
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:span}
 \gcd\{j-k:b_jb_k>0\}=1.
\end{equation}
Here the gcd of the differences is the positive generator of the
subgroup they span. In particular the support is not a singleton.
This hypothesis is stronger than $\gcd\{j:b_j>0\}=1$.
For example, $(z+z^3)/2$ satisfies the latter condition, but
$B(-1)=-1$ gives another boundary contact.

Define the positive-integer-valued variable $K$ by
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:law}
 \Pr\{K=j\}=b_j\rho^j,
 \qquad \mu=\EE K,\quad v=\Var K>0.
\end{equation}
Since $\rho<R$, this law has a moment generating function analytic
near zero. Denote its cumulants by $\kappa_r$, so
$\kappa_1=\mu$, $\kappa_2=v$. Let
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:modes}
 F_B(z)=F_0(B(z)),\quad \alpha_m=2\pi^2m,
 \quad d=\frac{v}{2\mu^2},\quad
 q_1=\frac{\kappa_3}{6\mu^2}-\frac{v^2}{4\mu^3}.
\end{equation}
The dual-mode parameter $\alpha_m$ is unrelated to the sequence value $a_n$.

On a neighborhood of real $\theta=0$, use the logarithm branch with
$-\log B(\rho)=0$, and define the removable germ
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:W}
 W(\theta)=\frac1{-\log B(\rho e^{\ii\theta})}
                  -\frac{\ii}{\mu\theta},
 \qquad E_\alpha(\theta)=e^{-\alpha(W(\theta)-d)}.
\end{equation}
The first terms are $W(\theta)=d+\ii q_1\theta+\OO(\theta^2)$.
Let
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:Y}
 Y(w)=\left(\frac{w+\sqrt{w^2+4}}2\right)^2,
 \qquad Y(0)=1.
\end{equation}
The square root is the analytic branch positive at zero. The identity
$Y+Y^{-1}=2+w^2$ is exact.

\begin{theorem}[Uniform transfer at every fixed order]\label{dep:eta:thm:general}
Under \eqref{dep:eta:eq:Bassump}--\eqref{dep:eta:eq:span}, for every fixed integer
$N\ge1$,
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:general}
 \rho^n[z^n]F_B(z)=\frac{\pi^2}{12\mu}
  +\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}n^{-3/4-j/2}H_j(\sqrt n)
  +\OO_{B,N}(n^{-3/4-N/2}).
\end{equation}
For $\alpha>0$, form the finite expression
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:Qrecipe}
\begin{split}
 Q_j(\alpha)={}&\sum_{r+k=j}
 \frac{(-\ii)^r}{4^rr!k!}
 \left(\frac\alpha\mu\right)^{k/2}
 \left(\frac\mu\alpha\right)^{r/2}E_\alpha^{(k)}(0)\\[-2pt]
 &\hspace{25mm}\cdot
 \left.\frac{\mathrm d^{2r}}{\mathrm dw^{2r}}
                \{Y(w)^kY'(w)\}\right|_{w=0}.
\end{split}
\end{equation}
There is a real function $P_j$ with $Q_j=(-\ii)^jP_j$;
$P_j(\alpha)$ is $\alpha^{-j/2}$ times a polynomial in $\alpha$
of degree at most $2j$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:Hrecipe}
\begin{split}
 H_j(u)=-\sum_{m\ge1}&c_m e^{-\alpha_md}
          \frac{(\alpha_m/\mu)^{1/4}}{\sqrt\pi}P_j(\alpha_m)\\
 &\cdot\cos\left(2u\sqrt{\alpha_m/\mu}+\frac\pi4+\frac{j\pi}2\right).
\end{split}
\end{equation}
Every such series, and each fixed derivative in $u$, converges
absolutely on the real axis and locally uniformly on the complex
plane. The functions $H_j$ are real entire and uniformly almost
periodic on the real axis; they and all their derivatives are bounded
there.
\end{theorem}

The adjective ``entire'' does not mean bounded on the complex plane.
Uniform almost periodicity means uniform approximation on the real
axis by finite trigonometric sums; it follows immediately from the
absolute convergence asserted in the theorem.

The first terms of the finite recipe are particularly simple:
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:P01}
 P_0(\alpha)=1,\qquad
 P_1(\alpha)=\frac{q_1\alpha^{3/2}}{\sqrt\mu}
                      +\frac{3\sqrt\mu}{16\sqrt\alpha}.
\end{equation}
Thus
\begin{align}
 H_0(u)&=-\sum_{m\ge1}c_me^{-\alpha_md}
      \frac{(\alpha_m/\mu)^{1/4}}{\sqrt\pi}
      \cos\left(2u\sqrt{\alpha_m/\mu}+\frac\pi4\right),\label{dep:eta:eq:H0general}\\
 H_1(u)&=\sum_{m\ge1}c_me^{-\alpha_md}
      \frac{(\alpha_m/\mu)^{1/4}}{\sqrt\pi}P_1(\alpha_m)
      \sin\left(2u\sqrt{\alpha_m/\mu}+\frac\pi4\right).\label{dep:eta:eq:H1general}
\end{align}
In view of $\alpha_md=\pi^2mv/\mu^2$, these formulas give
\eqref{dep:eta:eq:damping} without fitting an empirical decay constant.

\section{The exact eta identity and boundary extraction}\label{dep:eta:sec:boundary}

\subsection{The transformed product}
The classical modular transformation of the Dedekind eta function
implies, for positive real $t$,
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:Peta}
 P(e^{-t})=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{t}}
    \exp\left(\frac t{24}-\frac{\pi^2}{6t}\right)
    P(e^{-4\pi^2/t}).
\end{equation}
The logarithm and square-root branches extend analytically from
$t>0$ to $\Rea t>0$. See DLMF 23.18~\cite{eta} and the explicit
product identity (15) of Ardehali--Rosengren~\cite{ardehali}.
Subtracting the logarithm of \eqref{dep:eta:eq:Peta} from the same identity
with $t$ replaced by $2t$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:etaexact}
 F_0(e^{-t})=\frac{\pi^2}{12t}-\frac{\log2}{2}+\frac t{24}
                 -\sum_{m\ge1}c_m e^{-2\pi^2m/t},\qquad \Rea t>0.
\end{equation}
Indeed, with $Q=e^{-2\pi^2/t}$, the remaining product logarithm is
$\log P(Q)-\log P(Q^2)=-F_0(Q)$, and $|Q|<1$ because
$\Rea(1/t)>0$. The identity holds first on the positive real axis and
then on the entire right half-plane by holomorphic continuation. This
also fixes the logarithmic branch without a possible hidden multiple
of $2\pi\ii$.

On the coefficient circle $z=\rho e^{\ii\theta}$, the cumulant identity
$\log B(\rho e^{\ii\theta})=\log\EE e^{\ii\theta K}$ gives
\begin{align}
 t(\theta):=-\log B(\rho e^{\ii\theta})
  &=-\ii\mu\theta+\frac v2\theta^2
           +\frac{\ii\kappa_3}{6}\theta^3+\OO(\theta^4),\label{dep:eta:eq:texpand}\\
 \frac1{t(\theta)}
  &=\frac{\ii}{\mu\theta}+d+\ii q_1\theta+\OO(\theta^2).
 \label{dep:eta:eq:invtexpand}
\end{align}
In particular $W$ in \eqref{dep:eta:eq:W} is analytic. Choose a sufficiently
small $\delta>0$ so that it is holomorphic on a neighborhood of
$[-\delta,\delta]$ and
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:Wpositive}
 \Rea W(\theta)\ge d/2\quad (|\theta|\le\delta,\ \theta\in\mathbb R).
\end{equation}
This positive real part provides the summable mode damping on the
actual boundary.

\subsection{Why span one removes the other boundary contacts}
By the triangle inequality, $|B(\rho e^{\ii\theta})|\le1$.
Equality is possible exactly when all the unit complex numbers
$e^{\ii j\theta}$ for occupied sizes $j$ are identical. Equivalently,
$e^{\ii(j-k)\theta}=1$ for every pair of occupied sizes. The span-one
hypothesis is therefore exactly what implies
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:strictcircle}
 |B(\rho e^{\ii\theta})|<1\quad
         (\theta\not\equiv0\pmod{2\pi}).
\end{equation}
On any compact arc avoiding zero, the upper bound is strictly below
one. Since $B$ is analytic beyond the circle, the defining
$F_0(B(z))$ series extends holomorphically through that arc. Hence its
boundary values are smooth there, with all derivatives bounded on
compact arcs.

\subsection{Subtracting the pole before taking boundary values}
Set $x=z/\rho$. A direct Taylor expansion at $x=1$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:poleexpand}
 \frac1{-\log B(\rho x)}
 =\frac1{\mu(1-x)}+\frac{v-\mu}{2\mu^2}+\OO(1-x).
\end{equation}
For $|x|<1$,
$\Rea(1/(1-x))\ge1/2$. The constant contribution to the lower
bound in \eqref{dep:eta:eq:poleexpand} is
$1/(2\mu)+(v-\mu)/(2\mu^2)=d$. Thus, after shrinking the local
neighborhood,
\[
 \Rea\frac1{-\log B(\rho x)}\ge d/2
       \qquad (|x|<1,\ x\text{ near }1).
\]
The dual-mode sum in \eqref{dep:eta:eq:etaexact} is consequently bounded there
by $\sum_m c_m e^{-\alpha_md/2}<\infty$.

It follows that
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:G}
 G(x)=F_B(\rho x)-\frac{\pi^2}{12\mu(1-x)}
\end{equation}
is bounded on the unit disk. Near $x=1$, this follows from
\eqref{dep:eta:eq:poleexpand}, the bounded mode sum and the analytic term
$t/24$; away from $1$, it follows from \eqref{dep:eta:eq:strictcircle} and
compactness. Boundary values exist and are smooth away from $1$,
and are bounded near it. Dominated convergence on radial circles
therefore gives Fourier coefficient extraction from those boundary
values. No assumption of analytic continuation across $1$ is made.

Choose a real even $C^\infty$ cutoff $\chi$, supported in
$(-\delta,\delta)$ and equal to one on a smaller neighborhood of
zero. The analytic pole-subtracted part of $\pi^2/(12t)$ and the
remaining elementary terms in \eqref{dep:eta:eq:etaexact}, multiplied by
$\chi$, are smooth functions of $\theta$. All terms outside the
cutoff are smooth as well. Repeated integration by parts makes their
$n$th Fourier coefficients $\OO_L(n^{-L})$ for every fixed $L$.
We have reduced the coefficient problem to
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:reduction}
 \rho^n[z^n]F_B(z)-\frac{\pi^2}{12\mu}
 =-\sum_{m\ge1}c_mJ_{\alpha_m}(n)+\OO_L(n^{-L}),
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:J}
 J_\alpha(n)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\delta}^{\delta}
   \chi(\theta)e^{-\ii(n\theta+\alpha/(\mu\theta))}
                     e^{-\alpha W(\theta)}\dd\theta.
\end{equation}
The value at the single point $\theta=0$ is irrelevant. The exchange
of the sum with the integral is justified by the summable majorant
$c_me^{-\alpha_md/2}$ from \eqref{dep:eta:eq:Wpositive}.

\section{Uniform stationary phase and the summed remainder}\label{dep:eta:sec:uniform}
The following estimate is the quantitative step needed for the
infinite mode sum. Its uniformity is stronger than a fixed-$m$
stationary-phase expansion.

\begin{lemma}[Uniform single-mode estimate]\label{dep:eta:lem:uniform}
Fix $\alpha_0>0$ and an integer $N\ge1$. There are positive constants
$\eta,c,C_N$ and a nonnegative integer $M_N$, depending on the fixed
decoration, $N$ and $\alpha_0$, such that for all sufficiently large $n$ and
$\alpha_0\le\alpha\le\eta n$,
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:Jasym}
\begin{split}
 J_\alpha(n)={}&n^{-3/4}e^{-\alpha d}
      \frac{(\alpha/\mu)^{1/4}}{\sqrt\pi}
      \Rea\left\{e^{-\ii(2\sqrt{\alpha n/\mu}+\pi/4)}
                  \sum_{j=0}^{N-1}n^{-j/2}Q_j(\alpha)\right\}\\
 &+R_{N,\alpha}(n),
\end{split}
\end{equation}
with $Q_j$ as in \eqref{dep:eta:eq:Qrecipe} and
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:uniformerror}
 |R_{N,\alpha}(n)|\le
 C_Nn^{-3/4-N/2}(1+\alpha)^{M_N}e^{-c\alpha}.
\end{equation}
The constants do not depend on $n$ or $\alpha$ in this range.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Put
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:scaling}
 s=\sqrt{\frac\alpha{\mu n}},\qquad
 \lambda=\sqrt{\frac{\alpha n}{\mu}},\qquad \theta=sy.
\end{equation}
Then
\[
 n\theta+\frac\alpha{\mu\theta}
       =\lambda\phi(y),\qquad \phi(y)=y+y^{-1}.
\]
The phase has exactly two real stationary points, $y=1$ and $y=-1$,
and $\phi''(1)=2$, $\phi''(-1)=-2$. Choose $\eta$ small enough
that $s\le s_0$ and fixed neighborhoods of these two points lie in
$\chi(sy)=1$ for all allowed $s$. Use a fixed partition into those
two neighborhoods and the nonstationary complement.

\paragraph{Uniform amplitude bounds.}
On either fixed stationary neighborhood, every fixed-order derivative
in $y$ of $e^{-\alpha W(sy)}$ is bounded by
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:ampbounds}
 C_j(1+\alpha)^{D_j}e^{-c\alpha},
\end{equation}
uniformly for $0<s\le s_0$. This follows from the chain rule, bounded
derivatives of $W$, and \eqref{dep:eta:eq:Wpositive}. Factors of $s$ are
bounded by fixed constants. The same estimate holds after an analytic
coordinate change with bounded derivatives on the chosen compact
neighborhood. Constants may change from line to line.

\paragraph{An explicit stationary-phase remainder bound.}
Near $y=1$, set $w=(y-1)/\sqrt y$, so $y=Y(w)$ and
$\phi(y)=2+w^2$. For a compactly supported smooth amplitude $f$, the
Gaussian integral gives
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:gaussian}
 \int_{\mathbb R}e^{-\ii\lambda w^2}f(w)\dd w
 =e^{-\ii\pi/4}\sqrt{\frac\pi\lambda}
    \sum_{r=0}^{N-1}\frac{(-\ii)^r}{4^rr!\lambda^r}f^{(2r)}(0)
   +\mathcal R_N.
\end{equation}
For completeness, Fourier inversion expresses the left side as the
Gaussian factor times the integral of
$\widehat f(\xi)e^{\ii\xi^2/(4\lambda)}$. Taylor expansion of the
last exponential through degree $N-1$, using its real-axis remainder
bound, gives
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:gaussianbound}
 |\mathcal R_N|\le C_N\lambda^{-N-1/2}
             \int_{\mathbb R}|\xi|^{2N}|\widehat f(\xi)|\dd\xi.
\end{equation}
The weighted Fourier norm is bounded by a fixed combination of the
$L^1$ norms of $f$ and its derivatives through order $2N+2$: use the
$L^1$ bound for $|\xi|\le1$ and integrate by parts $2N+2$ times
for $|\xi|>1$. This proves a remainder bound uniform over our
amplitudes, by \eqref{dep:eta:eq:ampbounds}. One may justify the Gaussian
Fourier interchange first with a factor $e^{-\varepsilon w^2}$ and
then let $\varepsilon\downarrow0$.

The actual amplitude here is a fixed cutoff times
\[
 e^{-\alpha d}E_\alpha(sY(w))Y'(w).
\]
At zero the cutoff is identically one. In the term indexed by $r$
in \eqref{dep:eta:eq:gaussian}, Taylor-expand $E_\alpha(sY(w))$ in $s$
through degree $N-r-1$. The retained terms are
\[
 \frac{s^k}{k!}E_\alpha^{(k)}(0)Y(w)^kY'(w),\qquad r+k<N.
\]
Real Taylor remainder estimates, also after the finitely many needed
$w$-derivatives, give a bound polynomial in $\alpha$ times
$e^{-c\alpha}s^{N-r}$. The damping remains valid on the real
Taylor segments $0\le\xi\le sY(w)$ because of
\eqref{dep:eta:eq:Wpositive}. Multiplying by
$s\lambda^{-r-1/2}$ gives exactly
$n^{-3/4-N/2}$ times a fixed power of $\alpha$.
The remainder in \eqref{dep:eta:eq:gaussianbound}, after the outside factor
$s$, has the same required form.

For the retained terms,
\[
 s\lambda^{-1/2}=(\alpha/\mu)^{1/4}n^{-3/4},\qquad
 s^k\lambda^{-r}
 =n^{-(r+k)/2}(\alpha/\mu)^{k/2}(\mu/\alpha)^{r/2}.
\]
These identities give precisely \eqref{dep:eta:eq:Qrecipe}. Since the
coefficients of $B$ are real, $W(-\theta)=\overline{W(\theta)}$.
The negative stationary point supplies the complex conjugate of the
positive one. The factor $2$ from adding them combines with
$1/(2\pi)$ and the Gaussian factor $\sqrt\pi$ to give
$1/\sqrt\pi$ in \eqref{dep:eta:eq:Jasym}.

\paragraph{The nonstationary complement.}
There are three ranges, each controlled by ordinary integrations by
parts with the phase derivative.
\begin{enumerate}
\item On a fixed compact set avoiding $0,1,-1$, $|\phi'|$ is bounded
below. After $L$ integrations the contribution, including the outside
factor $s$, is bounded by
$C_Ls\lambda^{-L}(1+\alpha)^{D_L}e^{-c\alpha}$.
\item On $0<|y|<1/2$, substitute $u=1/y$. The phase is again
$u+u^{-1}$ and the Jacobian is $u^{-2}$. The transformed amplitude and
every needed derivative have integrable tails as $|u|\to\infty$.
Boundary terms there vanish; at the other endpoint the partition
cutoff handles the boundary. The same bound follows.
\item On $|y|>2$, $\phi'$ is bounded away from zero and derivatives
of $1/\phi'$ decay integrably. For $k\ge1$,
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:outerL1}
 \int\left|\frac{\mathrm d^k}{\mathrm dy^k}
       \{\chi(sy)e^{-\alpha W(sy)}\}\right|\dd y
 \le C_ks^{k-1}(1+\alpha)^{D_k}e^{-c\alpha}.
\end{equation}
This follows by changing variables back to $\theta$ and the chain
rule. Since $s\le s_0$, the bounds are uniform. Terms with no
derivative on the amplitude instead have an integrably decaying
derivative of $1/\phi'$. Thus $L\ge1$ integrations give the same
$C_Ls\lambda^{-L}$ bound. The outer smooth cutoff removes endpoint
terms at the support boundary.
\end{enumerate}
Take $L=N+1$. The power of $n$ in $s\lambda^{-L}$ is
$n^{-1-N/2}$, smaller than the target $n^{-3/4-N/2}$; powers of
$\alpha$ are absorbed in $(1+\alpha)^{M_N}$, since
$\alpha\ge\alpha_0$. This proves the lemma.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Summing actual mode errors}
Apply Lemma~\ref{dep:eta:lem:uniform} to $\alpha_m=2\pi^2m$ with
$\alpha_m\le\eta n$. The sum of the actual remainders is bounded by
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:sumerror}
 C_Nn^{-3/4-N/2}
   \sum_{m\ge1}c_m(1+\alpha_m)^{M_N}e^{-c\alpha_m}
     =\OO_{B,N}(n^{-3/4-N/2}).
\end{equation}
For $\alpha_m>\eta n$, the integral itself satisfies
\[
 |J_{\alpha_m}(n)|\le C e^{-\alpha_md/2}.
\]
The entire large-mode tail is exponentially small in $n$. Each
retained coefficient series also has an exponentially small tail
there: its coefficient is an exponential times a fixed power of
$\alpha_m$. Therefore the partial sums of the retained terms may be
extended to all $m\ge1$. Finally, choose the smooth-circle remainder
order $L$ in \eqref{dep:eta:eq:reduction} large enough. This proves
\eqref{dep:eta:eq:general} with a true $n$-independent remainder constant.

\subsection{Reality and convergence of the coefficient recipe}
The symmetry $W(-\theta)=\overline{W(\theta)}$ implies that
$E_\alpha^{(k)}(0)$ is $(-\ii)^k$ times a real polynomial in
$\alpha$ of degree at most $k$. The derivatives involving $Y$ in
\eqref{dep:eta:eq:Qrecipe} are real. Thus $Q_j=(-\ii)^jP_j$. The factor
$\alpha^{(k-r)/2}=\alpha^{-j/2}\alpha^k$ shows that $P_j$ is
$\alpha^{-j/2}$ times a polynomial of degree at most $2j$.
Only finitely many cumulants enter at any fixed order.

The real part in \eqref{dep:eta:eq:Jasym} is
$P_j(\alpha)\cos(2u\sqrt{\alpha/\mu}+\pi/4+j\pi/2)$,
which gives \eqref{dep:eta:eq:Hrecipe} including its signs. Derivatives of
any fixed order in $u$ add only powers of $\sqrt{\alpha_m}$.
For complex $u$ in a compact set, the trigonometric factors have
absolute value at most a fixed multiple of
$\exp(2|\Ima u|\sqrt{\alpha_m/\mu})$. Exponential decay linear in
$m$ dominates this square-root growth and every polynomial.
Consequently all asserted convergences, analyticity and real-axis
boundedness follow. This completes the proof of
Theorem~\ref{dep:eta:thm:general}.

\section{Explicit terms for the logarithmic decoration}\label{dep:eta:sec:three}
For $B(z)=L(z)$, the solution $B(\rho)=1$ is
$\rho=1-e^{-1}<1$, strictly within its analytic disk. Its positive
coefficients occupy all sizes, so the theorem applies.
Using the logarithmic moment generating function
$\log L(\rho e^s)$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:cumulants}
 \mu=v=M,\qquad
 \kappa_3=M^3+M,\qquad \kappa_4=M^4+6M^3+M.
\end{equation}
For instance $\mu=\rho/(1-\rho)=M$; the remaining identities follow
by successively applying $z\,\mathrm d/\mathrm dz$ to
$\log L(z)$ and evaluating at $z=\rho$. In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:dqlog}
 d=\frac1{2M},\qquad q_1=\frac M6-\frac1{12M}.
\end{equation}
Define
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:AKphi}
 A_m=\frac{2\pi^2m}{M},\qquad
 K_m=c_me^{-A_m/2}\frac{A_m^{1/4}}{\sqrt\pi},\qquad
 \Phi_m(u)=2u\sqrt{A_m}+\frac\pi4,
 \qquad U=\frac{2M^2-1}{12}.
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{align}
 H_0(u)&=-\sum_{m\ge1}K_m\cos\Phi_m(u),\label{dep:eta:eq:H0log}\\
 H_1(u)&=\sum_{m\ge1}K_m
     \frac{(8M^2-4)A_m^2+9}{48\sqrt{A_m}}\sin\Phi_m(u).
 \label{dep:eta:eq:H1log}
\end{align}
These expressions are absolutely convergent, and the tail decays
rapidly for this particular decoration.

\subsection{The third oscillatory coefficient with its sign}
For a general decoration, write
\[
 W(\theta)=d+\ii q_1\theta+r_2\theta^2+\OO(\theta^3).
\]
Reciprocal-series expansion of \eqref{dep:eta:eq:texpand} through the next
term yields
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:r2}
 r_2=-\frac{v^3}{8\mu^4}
            +\frac{v\kappa_3}{6\mu^3}
            -\frac{\kappa_4}{24\mu^2}.
\end{equation}
The three terms with $r+k=2$ in \eqref{dep:eta:eq:Qrecipe} give
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:Q2general}
 Q_2(\alpha)=-\frac{\alpha^3q_1^2}{2\mu}
          -\frac{\alpha^2r_2}{\mu}
          -\frac{15\alpha q_1}{16}
          +\frac{15\mu}{512\alpha}.
\end{equation}
To check the constants directly,
\[
 Y'(0)=1,\qquad (Y Y')''(0)=15/4,\qquad Y^{(5)}(0)=-15/16,
\]
while $E_\alpha'(0)=-\ii\alpha q_1$ and
$E_\alpha''(0)=-2\alpha r_2-\alpha^2q_1^2$.
Note that $Q_2=-P_2$, rather than $Q_2=P_2$.

For $B=L$, equations \eqref{dep:eta:eq:cumulants} and \eqref{dep:eta:eq:r2} simplify
to $r_2=-M^2/24-M/12$. To avoid confusing the scaled variable with
the universal recipe, denote $Q_2(MA)$ by $R_2(A)$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:R2}
 R_2(A)=-\frac{A^3U^2}{2}
          +A^2\left(\frac{M^3}{24}+\frac{M^2}{12}\right)
          -\frac{15AU}{16}+\frac{15}{512A},
\end{equation}
and the third coefficient is
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:H2log}
 H_2(u)=-\sum_{m\ge1}K_mR_2(A_m)\cos\Phi_m(u).
\end{equation}
Equations \eqref{dep:eta:eq:H0log}, \eqref{dep:eta:eq:H1log}, \eqref{dep:eta:eq:H2log},
and Theorem~\ref{dep:eta:thm:general} prove Theorem~\ref{dep:eta:thm:A}.

\subsection{An independent Laguerre route for finite diagnostics}
Put $w=1-z/\rho$. Direct series inversion gives
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:localjet}
 \frac1{-\log L(z)}
   =\frac1{Mw}-\frac M6w+\frac{M^2}{24}w^2
                         -\frac{7M^3}{180}w^3+\OO(w^4).
\end{equation}
Thus, for a fixed dual parameter $\alpha$, the analytic amplitude
multiplying $\exp(-A/w)$, $A=\alpha/M$, begins as
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:expjet}
 e^{-\alpha/[-\log L(z)]}
 =e^{-A/w}\left\{1+\frac{\alpha M}{6}w
       +M^2\left(\frac{\alpha^2}{72}-\frac\alpha{24}\right)w^2
       +\OO_\alpha(w^3)\right\}.
\end{equation}
The associated-Laguerre generating function implies the exact identity
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:laguerre}
 [x^n](1-x)^j e^{-A/(1-x)}=e^{-A}L_n^{(-j-1)}(A).
\end{equation}
It follows simply by multiplying
$\sum_{n\ge0}L_n^{(\nu)}(A)x^n=(1-x)^{-\nu-1}
\exp(-Ax/(1-x))$ by $e^{-A}$ and taking $\nu=-j-1$.
Finite jets in \eqref{dep:eta:eq:expjet} therefore give independently
computable Laguerre approximants.

The fixed-positive-argument Laguerre expansions in
DLMF 18.15(iv)~\cite{laguerre} agree with the first terms above.
Their stated compact-argument uniformity does not cover a dual
parameter tending to infinity. Accordingly, this is a cross-check and
numerical route; it is not used in place of the uniform summation
argument of Section~\ref{dep:eta:sec:uniform}.

\section{A rational tail bound and genuine sign changes}\label{dep:eta:sec:sign}
For the logarithmic decoration, put
\[
 r=e^{-\pi^2/M},\qquad
 K_1=e^{-\pi^2/M}\frac{(2\pi^2/M)^{1/4}}{\sqrt\pi}>0.
\]
Numerically $r=0.003202319565807\ldots$ and
$K_1=0.0033262016024504409820\ldots$. We next prove the bound needed
for sign changes using only rational inequalities, without relying on
those decimals.

\begin{lemma}[First-harmonic dominance]\label{dep:eta:lem:tail}
For $K_m$ defined in \eqref{dep:eta:eq:AKphi},
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:tail}
 \frac{\sum_{m\ge2}K_m}{K_1}
 <\frac{359101}{26730899}<\frac1{50}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
An elementary upper bound for $e$ is
\[
 e<\sum_{k=0}^6\frac1{k!}
           +\frac{1/7!}{1-1/8}
     =\frac{31967}{11760}<\frac{87}{32}.
\]
The geometric bound is valid since successive terms after $1/7!$
have ratios at most $1/8$. Hence $M<55/32$. Archimedes' classical
bound $\pi>223/71>157/50$ gives
\[
 \frac{\pi^2}{M}>\frac{(157/50)^2}{55/32}
     =\frac{197192}{34375}>\frac{573}{100}.
\]
The finite positive rational sum
$\sum_{k=0}^{20}(573/100)^k/k!$ is greater than $300$.
Therefore $e^{\pi^2/M}>300$, and $r<1/300$.

By Lemma~\ref{dep:eta:lem:cm},
\[
 \frac{K_m}{K_1}=c_m m^{1/4}r^{m-1}
        \le(1+\log m)m^{1/4}r^{m-1}
        \le m^2r^{m-1}\quad(m\ge2).
\]
For the last inequality, $1+\log m\le m$ and $m^{1/4}\le m$
suffice. Summing $\sum_{m\ge1}m^2r^{m-1}=(1+r)/(1-r)^3$ and
using $r<1/300$ yields
\[
 \sum_{m\ge2}\frac{K_m}{K_1}
 <\frac{1+1/300}{(1-1/300)^3}-1
 =\frac{359101}{26730899}<\frac1{50}.
\]
All the final comparisons are rational and are replayed by the exact
verification program.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Signed subsequences]\label{dep:eta:thm:sign}
For A330499,
\begin{align}
 \liminf_{n\to\infty}n^{3/4}
       \left(\frac{a_n\rho^n}{n!}-C\right)&\le-\frac{49K_1}{50},\label{dep:eta:eq:liminf}\\
 \limsup_{n\to\infty}n^{3/4}
       \left(\frac{a_n\rho^n}{n!}-C\right)&\ge\frac{49K_1}{50}.
 \label{dep:eta:eq:limsup}
\end{align}
In particular the normalized error is not $o(n^{-3/4})$ and admits
no pure integer-power refinement beginning with $C+c_1/n$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Set $A_1=2\pi^2/M$. For all sufficiently large integers $j$, choose
$n_j$ to be a nearest integer to
\[
 \left(\frac{2\pi j-\pi/4}{2\sqrt{A_1}}\right)^2.
\]
Changing this square by at most $1/2$ changes its square root by
$\OO(1/j)$. Hence $\cos\Phi_1(\sqrt{n_j})\to1$. Equation
\eqref{dep:eta:eq:H0log} and Lemma~\ref{dep:eta:lem:tail} imply
$\limsup_j H_0(\sqrt{n_j})\le-49K_1/50$.
Choosing instead a nearest integer to
$(((2j+1)\pi-\pi/4)/(2\sqrt{A_1}))^2$ makes the first cosine tend
to $-1$, giving the opposite bound. These are increasing unbounded
subsequences after discarding finitely many initial values.
Finally, Theorem~\ref{dep:eta:thm:A} with $N=1$ gives
\[
 n^{3/4}\left(\frac{a_n\rho^n}{n!}-C\right)
          =H_0(\sqrt n)+\OO(n^{-1/2}).
\]
This proves both comparisons. A refinement $C+c_1/n+\OO(n^{-2})$
would make this expression tend to zero, a contradiction.
\end{proof}
The strict tail inequality actually permits strict improvements of
\eqref{dep:eta:eq:liminf}--\eqref{dep:eta:eq:limsup}. No exact value of either extreme
is claimed. Their determination would require controlling the joint
phase behavior, not just the dominant harmonic.

\section{Actual root of unity cusps and the Delta obstruction}\label{dep:eta:sec:cusps}
The failure of a usual analytic-continuation hypothesis should not be
inferred just from the appearance of an infinite product. The
following argument proves genuine singularities directly.

\begin{proposition}[Odd-order cusps]\label{dep:eta:prop:cusps}
Let $\zeta$ be a primitive root of unity of odd order $h$. With the
analytic logarithmic branch of $F_0$ in the unit disk,
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:cusp}
 \lim_{t\downarrow0}tF_0(\zeta e^{-t})=\frac{\pi^2}{12h^2}.
\end{equation}
In particular each such root is a nonremovable singularity.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Absolute convergence for $|q|<1$ permits the Lambert representation
\[
 \log P(q)=-\sum_{r\ge1}\frac{q^r}{r(1-q^r)}.
\]
At $q=\zeta e^{-t}$ the terms $r=h\ell$ contribute, after
multiplication by $t$,
\[
 -\sum_{\ell\ge1}\frac{t}{h\ell(e^{h\ell t}-1)}
       \longrightarrow -\frac{\pi^2}{6h^2}.
\]
Dominated convergence is justified by
$t/(e^{h\ell t}-1)\le1/(h\ell)$.
For $r$ not divisible by $h$, the finitely many nontrivial root
arguments have radial segments bounded away from $1$. Thus
$|1-\zeta^re^{-rt}|\ge c_h>0$, uniformly in $r,t$. The absolute
contribution of these terms is at most
\[
 c_h^{-1}\sum_{r\ge1}\frac{e^{-rt}}r=\OO_h(\log(1/t)),
\]
which disappears after multiplication by $t$.
Since $h$ is odd, $\zeta^2$ still has order $h$. Applying the same
limit to $q^2=\zeta^2e^{-2t}$ yields
$t\log P(q^2)\to-\pi^2/(12h^2)$.
Subtracting $t\log P(q)$ proves \eqref{dep:eta:eq:cusp}.
The order $h$ is fixed before taking the limit; no uniform-in-$h$
estimate has been used.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[No standard Delta-domain at the positive point]\label{dep:eta:cor:delta}
For any decoration in Theorem~\ref{dep:eta:thm:general}, $F_B$ has no
holomorphic continuation to a standard Delta-neighborhood of $\rho$
that excludes only an outward wedge of half-angle less than $\pi/2$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Since $B'(\rho)=\mu/\rho>0$, let $g$ be its local holomorphic
inverse near $B(\rho)=1$. For odd $h\to\infty$, put
$\zeta_h=e^{2\pi\ii/h}$ and $z_h=g(\zeta_h)$. Taylor expansion gives
\[
 z_h=\rho+\frac\rho\mu(\zeta_h-1)+\OO(|\zeta_h-1|^2),
 \quad z_h\to\rho,\quad \arg(z_h-\rho)\to\pi/2.
\]
The conjugate sequence has limiting argument $-\pi/2$.
The local inverse image of the disk $|q|<1$ is connected near
$\rho$ and meets the original coefficient disk. Along the radial
curves $g(\zeta_he^{-t})$, Proposition~\ref{dep:eta:prop:cusps} gives a
divergence at $z_h$. This is the same analytic branch as $F_B$ by
continuation from their common interior region.

A standard Delta-neighborhood consists locally of points sufficiently
close to $\rho$, except for an outward wedge
$|\arg(z-\rho)|\le\phi$ with some $\phi<\pi/2$.
For all sufficiently large $h$, $z_h$ lies strictly inside the
remaining neighborhood. The nearby radial curves also lie there and
connect to the interior branch through the local inverse image of
$|q|<1$. A holomorphic continuation to that whole neighborhood would
therefore have to be regular at $z_h$, contradicting the divergence.
\end{proof}

This establishes the obstruction needed to select the proof method.
It does not classify all root-of-unity cusps or all possible analytic
continuation domains. The boundary proof is independently valid and
does not use the obstruction as a substitute for an error estimate.

\section{Smooth inverses and pointwise integer envelopes}\label{dep:eta:sec:inverse}
The coefficient theorem yields arbitrarily accurate fixed-order
\emph{smooth charts}. The discrete threshold remains a staircase, so
its statement must retain rounding explicitly. Throughout this
section the sequence is A330499 and the constants are
\eqref{dep:eta:eq:logconstants}.

\subsection{Every fixed order with two ceilings}
Define, for $Y>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:threshold}
 \mathcal N(Y)=\min\{n\ge1:a_n\ge Y\}.
\end{equation}
Proposition~\ref{dep:eta:prop:counts} proves strict monotonicity, and
Theorem~\ref{dep:eta:thm:A} proves unboundedness, so this is well-defined.
For a fixed integer $J\ge0$, put
\begin{align}
 S_J(x)&=C+\sum_{j=0}^Jx^{-3/4-j/2}H_j(\sqrt x),\label{dep:eta:eq:SJ}\\
 \mathcal L_J(x)&=\log\Gamma(x+1)-x\log\rho+\log S_J(x),\label{dep:eta:eq:LJ}\\
 p_J&=\frac34+\frac{J+1}{2}.\label{dep:eta:eq:pJ}
\end{align}
These functions are defined for all sufficiently large real $x$,
since $S_J(x)=C+\OO(x^{-3/4})>0$.

\begin{theorem}[Pointwise threshold envelopes]\label{dep:eta:thm:inverse}
For each fixed $J\ge0$, there are $D_J>0$ and a starting point
$x_J$ such that the charts
$\mathcal L_J(x)\pm D_Jx^{-p_J}$ are strictly increasing on
$x\ge x_J$, and their large roots defined by
\begin{align}
 \mathcal L_J(x_+)+D_Jx_+^{-p_J}&=\log Y,\label{dep:eta:eq:xplus}\\
 \mathcal L_J(x_-)-D_Jx_-^{-p_J}&=\log Y\label{dep:eta:eq:xminus}
\end{align}
satisfy, for every sufficiently large $Y$,
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:ceil}
 \lceil x_+\rceil\le\mathcal N(Y)\le\lceil x_-\rceil.
\end{equation}
Moreover $x_+\le x_-$ and
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:gap}
 x_--x_+=\OO_J\left(\frac{x_+^{-p_J}}{\log x_+}\right).
\end{equation}
The constants and starting point are existence statements, not
numerically certified values.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Theorem~\ref{dep:eta:thm:A} with $N=J+1$, followed by the logarithm on a
fixed neighborhood of the positive constant $C$, gives
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:logbackerror}
 |\log a_n-\mathcal L_J(n)|\le D_Jn^{-p_J}
\end{equation}
for all sufficiently large integers $n$, after choosing $D_J$.
Boundedness of the derivatives of $H_j$ gives
$S_J'(x)=\OO_J(x^{-5/4})$. The standard digamma expansion implies
\[
 \mathcal L_J'(x)
 =\psi(x+1)-\log\rho+\frac{S_J'(x)}{S_J(x)}
 =\log(x/\rho)+\OO(1/x)+\OO_J(x^{-5/4}).
\]
Both perturbed charts therefore have positive derivative for large
$x$ and tend to infinity. Their roots exist uniquely there, and the
upper chart reaches a given height first, so $x_+\le x_-$.

If an integer $n<x_+$ is sufficiently large, its upper chart is
less than $\log Y$, and \eqref{dep:eta:eq:logbackerror} forces $a_n<Y$.
The finitely many smaller integers also satisfy this once $Y$ is
large enough. Hence $\mathcal N(Y)\ge\lceil x_+\rceil$.
For $n=\lceil x_-\rceil$, the lower chart is at least $\log Y$,
so $a_n\ge Y$ and the upper bound follows. This reasoning remains
valid if either root is an integer, or if $Y$ is arbitrarily close to
a sequence value.

Subtracting the root equations and using the lower derivative bound
$c\log x$ for $\mathcal L_J$ gives \eqref{dep:eta:eq:gap} by the mean value
theorem. The roots are asymptotically equivalent, as follows already
from their common leading factorial scale.
\end{proof}

For large $Y$ the two ceilings are equal or adjacent, since the real
root gap tends to zero. They cannot uniformly be collapsed to one
ceiling of the central chart. Arbitrarily small changes in $Y$ near
a jump can change $\mathcal N(Y)$, however accurate the smooth chart
may be.

\subsection{The first oscillatory displacement of the exact core}
Let $x_*=x_*(Y)$ be the unique sufficiently large root of the
\emph{exact factorial core}
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:core}
 \mathcal F_*(x)=\log\Gamma(x+1)-x\log\rho+\log C=\log Y.
\end{equation}
Its derivative is
$\mathcal F_*'(x)=\psi(x+1)-\log\rho\sim\log(x/\rho)$.

\begin{proposition}[First oscillatory inverse shift]\label{dep:eta:prop:shift}
Set
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:shift}
 x_{\mathrm{osc}}=x_*-
 \frac{x_*^{-3/4}H_0(\sqrt{x_*})}
      {C\{\psi(x_*+1)-\log\rho\}}.
\end{equation}
The central smooth root of $\mathcal L_0(x)=\log Y$ differs from
$x_{\mathrm{osc}}$ by
$\OO(x_*^{-3/2}/\log x_*)$. In particular there is a fixed $K>0$
such that the actual integer threshold satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:firstceil}
 \left\lceil x_{\mathrm{osc}}-
            \frac{Kx_*^{-5/4}}{\log x_*}\right\rceil
 \le\mathcal N(Y)\le
 \left\lceil x_{\mathrm{osc}}+
            \frac{Kx_*^{-5/4}}{\log x_*}\right\rceil
\end{equation}
for all sufficiently large $Y$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Write $E_0(x)=\log(S_0(x)/C)$. Then
\[
 E_0(x)=C^{-1}x^{-3/4}H_0(\sqrt x)+\OO(x^{-3/2}),
 \qquad E_0'(x)=\OO(x^{-5/4}).
\]
The central root of $\mathcal F_*+E_0$ differs from $x_*$ by
$\OO(x_*^{-3/4}/\log x_*)$. Taylor expansion over that interval
shows that its first displacement is
$-E_0(x_*)/\mathcal F_*'(x_*)$, with an error bounded by
$\OO(x_*^{-2}/(\log x_*)^2)$ from varying $E_0$ and smaller
core-curvature terms. Replacing $E_0(x_*)$ by its first displayed
term leaves the stated $\OO(x_*^{-3/2}/\log x_*)$ error.
The two roots of Theorem~\ref{dep:eta:thm:inverse} for $J=0$ are within
$\OO(x_*^{-5/4}/\log x_*)$ of the central root. Enlarge a single
constant $K$ to contain all these errors and take ceilings.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Lambert initialization and the accuracy it does not supply}
Write $y=\log Y$ and use the positive real branch of Lambert $W$.
The solution of $x_0\log(x_0/(e\rho))=y$ is
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:lambert}
 x_0=\frac{y}{W(y/(e\rho))}.
\end{equation}
Stirling's expansion gives
\[
 \mathcal F_*(x)=x\log\frac{x}{e\rho}
        +\frac12\log(2\pi x)+\log C+\frac1{12x}+\OO(x^{-3}).
\]
Since the omitted half-logarithmic term is $\OO(\log x_0)$ and the
core derivative is of order $\log x_0$, the exact core root first
differs from $x_0$ by $\OO(1)$. One Taylor correction yields
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:lambertcorrect}
 x_*=x_0-
       \frac{\frac12\log(2\pi x_0)+\log C}{\log(x_0/\rho)}
        +\OO\left(\frac1{x_0\log x_0}\right).
\end{equation}
Indeed the remaining Stirling term, derivative variation and quadratic
core term are all $\OO(1/x_0)$ in the equation, hence
$\OO(1/(x_0\log x_0))$ in the root.

This Lambert expression is a useful seed for solving
\eqref{dep:eta:eq:core}. Its displayed error is smaller than the leading
oscillatory displacement, but larger than the next half-power inverse
corrections. Therefore replacing $x_*$ in an arbitrarily high-order
claim by this finite seed without propagating its error would be
incorrect. The root in \eqref{dep:eta:eq:shift} is the exact factorial-core
root, not just \eqref{dep:eta:eq:lambertcorrect}.

\subsection{Generating higher smooth inverse terms}
For any fixed $J$, put $E_J(x)=\log(S_J(x)/C)$, so that
$\mathcal L_J=\mathcal F_*+E_J$. Let $X(y)$ be the large local
inverse of $\mathcal F_*$ and write $D_y=(1/\mathcal F_*'(x))D_x$
when acting on a function of $x=X(y)$. Ordinary Lagrange reversion
of the perturbation parameter $\varepsilon$ in
$\mathcal F_*(x)+\varepsilon E_J(x)=y$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{dep:eta:eq:reversion}
 x=X(y)+\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{(-\varepsilon)^k}{k!}
     D_y^{k-1}\left\{X'(y)E_J(X(y))^k\right\}.
\end{equation}
This is a local analytic identity in $\varepsilon$ near zero and a
constructive finite-order reversion rule. For sufficiently large
$y$, one can set $\varepsilon=1$: in a fixed complex neighborhood
of the real root the perturbation and its first derivative tend to
zero, whereas the core derivative has size $\log X(y)$, so the
analytic implicit solution exists on a parameter disk containing
$|\varepsilon|\le1$. Equivalently, finite Taylor reversion with a
remainder follows by repeated substitution and the same derivative
bounds.

Only finitely many derivatives and products are needed for any
requested fixed precision. Increasing $J$ reduces the coefficient
backward error; reversion then converts it into a smooth root error
of order $x^{-p_J}/\log x$. Finally, Theorem~\ref{dep:eta:thm:inverse}
converts that information to a pointwise integer statement. These are
classical inverse-chart and backward-error operations; the
model-specific input is the oscillatory coefficient law. They do not
create an unambiguous real inverse of the discrete staircase.

\section{Further questions and present limits}\label{dep:eta:sec:further}
The theorem gives every fixed order for a fixed decoration. Several
natural extensions require additional work.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Effective error constants and cutoffs.}
The proof tracks polynomial-times-exponential bounds but does not
optimize explicit cutoff radii, amplitude norms or stationary-phase
constants. An interval implementation of these choices could turn
Theorem~\ref{dep:eta:thm:inverse} into a certified inverse algorithm. The
current finite diagnostics do not supply those missing constants.
\item \textbf{Other support spans.}
If the difference span exceeds one, multiple unit-modulus boundary
contacts occur. Their phases and cusp types must be combined. Merely
replacing the span-one condition by gcd of the occupied sizes is
invalid, as $(z+z^3)/2$ demonstrates.
\item \textbf{Vanishing variance.}
As $v/\mu^2$ decreases, the number of appreciable harmonics grows.
A uniform crossover theorem toward monomial decorations would need
new two-parameter estimates. It is not a consequence of keeping
$B$ fixed in Theorem~\ref{dep:eta:thm:general}.
\item \textbf{Joint phases and limiting error laws.}
The frequencies are proportional to $\sqrt m$. Their arithmetic
relations may permit sharper extreme-value or distribution results
for $H_0(\sqrt n)$. The signed subsequences here need only the first
harmonic and establish neither an empirical limit law nor the exact
limsup and liminf.
\item \textbf{Growing orders and exponentially improved expansions.}
The coefficient recipe is finite at every fixed order. It does not
control coefficient growth as the requested order tends to infinity,
optimal truncation, or a complete exponentially improved transseries.
\item \textbf{Weighted kernels.}
The harmonic weighting in A330498 leads to a different transformation
problem. Its neighboring sequence number is not evidence that the
same theorem applies. Other products or weights should be treated
from their own exact kernels.
\end{enumerate}

These six questions, with the limits stated after Theorem~\ref{dep:eta:thm:sign} and Corollary~\ref{dep:eta:cor:delta}, are gathered with Part~II's in Section~\ref{dep:sec:further}. Item~6 is answered for the weight $1/k$ by Part~II; other weights remain open.
\end{writenote}

\paragraph{Conclusion.}
For A330499, the exact leading constant is only the first part of the
answer. Analytic decoration damps the eta modes by a variance ratio
but leaves a real $n^{-3/4}$ oscillation, followed by explicitly
computable half-power orders. A uniform boundary proof makes that
infinite oscillatory expansion rigorous and gives inverse envelopes
that remain valid at the jumps of the integer threshold.

\section{Main result and the conjectural constant}\label{dep:hl:sec:main}
Let
\begin{equation}\label{dep:hl:eq:def}
 L(z)=-\log(1-z),\qquad
 F(z)=\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{\log(1+L(z)^k)}{k}
     =\sum_{n\ge0}a(n)\frac{z^n}{n!}.
\end{equation}
All logarithms in this definition are power-series logarithms at zero.
This is OEIS A330498. The inspected text export, revision 13 dated
17 December 2019, gives the equivalent
$a(n)\sim c(n-1)!(1-e^{-1})^{-n}$ and labels $c=\log 2$
conjectural~\cite{oeisA330498}. This is a statement about that inspected record,
not a claim that every later proof or related paper has been excluded.

 d_o(m)=\tau(\odd(m)),
\end{equation}
where $\tau$ is the divisor-counting function and $\odd(m)$ is the odd
part of $m$. Define
\begin{equation}\label{dep:hl:eq:mode}
 K_m=d_o(m)\left(\frac{8M}{m}\right)^{1/4}r^m,
 \qquad \Phi_m(u)=2\beta_m u-\frac\pi4.
\end{equation}
\begin{theorem}\label{dep:hl:thm:main}
For every fixed integer $J\ge1$,
\begin{equation}\label{dep:hl:eq:main}
 \frac{n\rho^n a(n)}{n!}
 =\log 2+\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}n^{-1/4-j/2}H_j(\sqrt n)
       +\OO_J(n^{-1/4-J/2}),
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}\label{dep:hl:eq:H}
 H_j(u)=\sum_{m\ge1}K_mP_j(\beta_m)
              \sin\left(\Phi_m(u)+\frac{j\pi}{2}\right).
\end{equation}
The $P_j$ are real Laurent polynomials explicitly determined by the
finite recipe in Section~\ref{dep:hl:sec:recipe}. Each $H_j$ is entire, uniformly
almost periodic on $\RR$, and bounded there with every derivative.
In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{dep:hl:eq:equiv}
 a(n)\sim \log 2\,(n-1)!\rho^{-n}.
\end{equation}
The normalized leading error has positive and negative subsequences of
nonzero size at scale $n^{-1/4}$.
\end{theorem}
Thus a constant-coefficient $1/n$ refinement of the leading equivalent
cannot describe this sequence. The error is algebraic and oscillatory.
The proof does not infer coefficient errors from radial Mellin residues
or assume a Delta-domain for $F$.

\section{Exact arithmetic and the signed divisor weight}\label{dep:hl:sec:exact}
Expanding the two logarithms formally in \eqref{dep:hl:eq:def} gives
\begin{equation}\label{dep:hl:eq:h}
 F(z)=\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{h(m)}mL(z)^m,\qquad
 h(m)=\sum_{d\mid m}(-1)^{d-1}
     =\tau(m)-2\mathbf1_{2\mid m}\tau(m/2).
\end{equation}
Indeed the coefficient of $L^m$ is
$\sum_{kd=m}(-1)^{d-1}/(kd)=h(m)/m$.
If $m=2^v u$ with $u$ odd, then
\begin{equation}\label{dep:hl:eq:odd}
 h(m)=(1-v)\tau(u).
\end{equation}
Using $L(z)^m=m!\sum_{n\ge m}\stir nm z^n/n!$ yields the integer formula
\begin{equation}\label{dep:hl:eq:stirling}
 a(0)=0,\qquad
 a(n)=\sum_{m=1}^n\stir nm(m-1)!h(m)\quad(n\ge1).
\end{equation}
The Stirling numbers here are unsigned first-kind numbers. The weight
$h$ is signed; a positive-mark interpretation is not being asserted.
The eventual positivity below follows from asymptotics, not from
termwise positivity in \eqref{dep:hl:eq:stirling}.

The independent recurrence
\[
 q_{n,k}=(n-1)q_{n-1,k}+(k-1)q_{n-1,k-1},\qquad q_{1,1}=1,
\]
with all other out-of-range entries zero, computes
$q_{n,k}=\stir nk(k-1)!$. It supplies exact numerators for the portable
diagnostics in Section~\ref{dep:hl:sec:checks} without floating recurrence
truncation.

\section{A fixed analytic decoration theorem}\label{dep:hl:sec:general}
Let $B(z)=\sum_{k\ge1}b_kz^k$, with $b_k\ge0$, be analytic in $|z|<R$.
Suppose $B(\rho)=1$ for $0<\rho<R$. Impose the difference-span condition
\begin{equation}\label{dep:hl:eq:span}
 \gcd\{k-\ell:b_kb_\ell>0\}=1
\end{equation}
and positive variance for the law
\begin{equation}\label{dep:hl:eq:law}
 \Pr(K=k)=b_k\rho^k,\qquad
 \mu=\EE K,\quad v=\Var K>0.
\end{equation}
Let $\kappa_j$ denote its cumulants. These exist to all orders since
$\rho<R$. The support of $K$ lies in the positive integers. Define
\begin{equation}\label{dep:hl:eq:generaldef}
 F_B(z)=\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{h(m)}m B(z)^m,
 \qquad U_n=\rho^n[z^n]F_B(z),
 \qquad \beta_m=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi^2m}{\mu}}.
\end{equation}
The notation $\beta_m$ agrees with \eqref{dep:hl:eq:constants} when $B=L$.
\begin{theorem}\label{dep:hl:thm:general}
For every fixed decoration $B$ as above and every fixed $J\ge1$,
\begin{equation}\label{dep:hl:eq:general}
 nU_n=\log 2+\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}n^{-1/4-j/2}H_{B,j}(\sqrt n)
                    +\OO_{B,J}(n^{-1/4-J/2}).
\end{equation}
Its leading function is
\begin{equation}\label{dep:hl:eq:generalH}
 H_{B,0}(u)=\sum_{m\ge1}d_o(m)\left(\frac{8\mu}{m}\right)^{1/4}
 e^{-\pi^2mv/\mu^2}\sin(2\beta_mu-\pi/4).
\end{equation}
At order $j$, the summand is multiplied by a real Laurent polynomial
$P_{B,j}(\beta_m)$ and its phase is advanced by $j\pi/2$.
In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{dep:hl:eq:generalP1}
 P_{B,1}(b)=\frac{2\mu\kappa_3-3v^2}{12\mu^2}b^3
              -\frac{v}{2\mu}b-\frac1{16b}.
\end{equation}
All these functions are entire, uniformly almost periodic on the real
line, and bounded with all real derivatives.
\end{theorem}
There is no asserted uniformity as $B$ varies, $v$ tends to zero, the
support span changes, or $J$ grows. The difference span is important:
$\gcd\{k:b_k>0\}=1$ alone does not exclude other points where
$|B(\rho e^{\ii\theta})|=1$.

\section{Smooth renewal interpolation}\label{dep:hl:sec:schwartz}
Let $S_k$ be a sum of $k$ independent copies of $K$. Coefficient
extraction gives the exact finite sum
\begin{equation}\label{dep:hl:eq:renewal}
 U_n=\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{h(k)}k\Pr(S_k=n).
\end{equation}
It is finite because $K\ge1$. Set
$\phi(\theta)=B(\rho e^{\ii\theta})$ and choose its local logarithm
$\psi(\theta)=\log\phi(\theta)$ near zero, so
\[
 \psi(\theta)=\sum_{r\ge1}\kappa_r(\ii\theta)^r/r!.
\]
Choose an even smooth cutoff $\eta$, supported in a sufficiently small
symmetric interval $(-\delta,\delta)$ and equal to one near zero.
Choose $\chi\in C_c^\infty((c,C))$ for positive $c,C$, equal to one
on a neighborhood of $1/\mu$. Define
\begin{equation}\label{dep:hl:eq:fn}
 f_n(x)=\frac{\chi(x/n)}{2\pi x}
    \int_\RR\eta(\theta)e^{x\psi(\theta)-\ii n\theta}\dd\theta.
\end{equation}
It is real and smooth, and has compact support away from zero.

\begin{lemma}\label{dep:hl:lem:interpolation}
For every $A>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{dep:hl:eq:interpolation}
 U_n=\sum_{k\ge1}h(k)f_n(k)+\OO_A(n^{-A}).
\end{equation}
Put $s=n^{-1/2}$, $x=n/\mu+\sqrt n\,y$, and
$g_s(y)=n^{3/2}f_n(n/\mu+\sqrt n\,y)$.
The family $g_s$ extends smoothly through $s=0$ in the Schwartz space
$\mathcal S(\RR)$. In particular, for every fixed $N,p,q$,
\begin{equation}\label{dep:hl:eq:schwartz}
 \sup_y(1+|y|)^p\left|\partial_y^q
 \left(g_s(y)-\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}s^jg_j(y)\right)\right|
 \le C_{N,p,q}|s|^N,
 \qquad g_j=\frac{\partial_s^jg_s|_{s=0}}{j!}.
\end{equation}
The leading density is
\begin{equation}\label{dep:hl:eq:g0}
 g_0(y)=\frac{\mu^{3/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi v}}
                   e^{-\mu^3y^2/(2v)}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
We first establish the smooth family, including continuity in its
seminorms. Let
\[
 t=1/\mu+sy,\qquad a(t)=\chi(t)/t,\qquad
 Q(\theta)=\frac{\psi(\theta)-\ii\mu\theta}{\theta^2},
 \quad Q(0)=-v/2.
\]
Extend $a$ smoothly by zero off its positive compact support. Changing
variables $\theta=su$ in \eqref{dep:hl:eq:fn} gives, for $s>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{dep:hl:eq:safeintegral}
 g_s(y)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_\RR a(1/\mu+sy)\eta(su)
 e^{\ii\mu yu}\exp\{(1/\mu+sy)u^2Q(su)\}\dd u.
\end{equation}
This formula also defines the family for negative $s$ and at zero; it
has removed the apparent negative powers of $s$. The function $Q$ is
analytic at zero. Shrink $\delta$ until $\Repart Q\le-c_0<0$ on the
support of $\eta$. On the support of $a$, $t$ stays in a fixed compact
subset of $(0,\infty)$. The last exponential is therefore bounded by
$e^{-c_1u^2}$, uniformly in $s,y$.

For any fixed number of $s$ and $y$ derivatives, the differentiated
non-Fourier amplitude is bounded by a polynomial in $|y|,|u|$ times a
uniform Gaussian. Before $s$ differentiation, its $y$ dependence
occurs through the bounded variable $t$ and through factors of $s$.
Each fixed $s$ derivative introduces at most one additional unabsorbed
factor of $y$. Thus the degree in $y$ is bounded independently of how
many later $u$ derivatives are taken. Derivatives of $\eta(su)$ are
supported where $|u|\asymp |s|^{-1}$ and are additionally Gaussian-small.
Arbitrarily many integrations by parts against $e^{\ii\mu yu}$ now
give uniform bounds for every weighted $y$ derivative, also after any
fixed number of $s$ derivatives. Boundary terms vanish by the cutoff
and Gaussian decay.

These are not merely bounded-seminorm statements. On each compact
$y$ interval, dominated convergence gives continuity of every
$s,y$ derivative at $s=0$. Uniform bounds with a strictly stronger
weight control the complementary $y$ tails. Combining the two regions
proves continuity in every Schwartz seminorm. The same argument applies
to all derivatives; Taylor's theorem in each seminorm gives
\eqref{dep:hl:eq:schwartz}. Substituting $s=0$ in
\eqref{dep:hl:eq:safeintegral} gives \eqref{dep:hl:eq:g0}. The cutoffs are constant
near their central values and disappear from every $g_j$.

We next compare integer values with \eqref{dep:hl:eq:renewal}. Exponential
moments exist near zero. Away from a fixed neighborhood of
$k/n=1/\mu$, Chernoff bounds are exponential uniformly in the relevant
ratios. For very small $k/n$, use a fixed positive exponential tilt;
for very large $k/n$, use a fixed negative tilt; on the remaining
compact ratio sets use strict convexity. For $k>n$ the probabilities
are zero. Inside the central neighborhood, \eqref{dep:hl:eq:span} gives
$|\phi(\theta)|\le q<1$ on the compact complement of the arc where
$\eta=1$. Also $k\asymp n$, so the omitted Fourier arcs cost
$\OO(q^{c_2n})$. Multiplication by $|h(k)|\le\tau(k)\le k$ and
summation over polynomially many indices are harmless. In real-variable
cutoff transition regions, $|y|$ is a fixed positive multiple of
$\sqrt n$; the proved Schwartz bounds make $f_n$ superpolynomially
small there. This proves \eqref{dep:hl:eq:interpolation}.
\end{proof}
\begin{remark}
The interpolation error is only claimed to be superpolynomial. A smooth
compact cutoff is not analytic; no contour shift of that cutoff and no
exponential global interpolation remainder have been assumed.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Normalization and the first density correction}
\begin{lemma}\label{dep:hl:lem:mass}
For every $A>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{dep:hl:eq:mass}
 \int_0^\infty f_n(x)\dd x=\frac1n+\OO_A(n^{-A}).
\end{equation}
Consequently $\int g_0=1$ and $\int g_j=0$ for $j\ge1$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Poisson summation applied to the uniformly Schwartz family
$g_s$, with mesh $n^{-1/2}$, makes the difference between the integral
and integer sum of $f_n$ smaller than every power of $n^{-1}$.
The unweighted version of Lemma~\ref{dep:hl:lem:interpolation} identifies
that sum with $\rho^n[z^n](-\log(1-B(z)))$ to the same accuracy.
Positivity and the span hypothesis imply that $B(z)=1$ has no other
solution in $|z|\le\rho$. The zero at $\rho$ is simple since
$B'(\rho)>0$. Hence
$(1-B(z))/(1-z/\rho)$ is analytic and nonzero on a slightly larger
disk, and has an analytic logarithm there. Its coefficients are
exponentially small after multiplication by $\rho^n$. The remaining
term $-\log(1-z/\rho)$ has coefficient $1/n$ under this normalization.
The assertions about $g_j$ follow by integrating
\eqref{dep:hl:eq:schwartz} to arbitrary order.
\end{proof}
Direct differentiation at $s=0$ also gives
\begin{equation}\label{dep:hl:eq:g1}
 \frac{g_1(y)}{g_0(y)}=
 \left(-\frac{3\mu}{2}+\frac{\kappa_3\mu^2}{2v^2}\right)y
 +\left(\frac{\mu^4}{2v}-\frac{\kappa_3\mu^5}{6v^3}\right)y^3.
\end{equation}
It will reproduce the universal first correction \eqref{dep:hl:eq:generalP1}.

\section{The exact signed Voronoi identity}\label{dep:hl:sec:voronoi}
For smooth $f$ of compact support in $(0,\infty)$, the classical divisor
Voronoi formula states
\begin{align}\label{dep:hl:eq:classical}
 \sum_{k\ge1}\tau(k)f(k)
 &=\int_0^\infty f(x)(\log x+2\gamma)\dd x\notag\\
 &\quad+\sum_{m\ge1}\tau(m)\int_0^\infty
 f(x)\mathcal K(4\pi\sqrt{mx})\dd x,\\
 \mathcal K(q)&=4K_0(q)-2\pi Y_0(q).\notag
\end{align}
The usual boundary term $f(0)/4$ vanishes. We use precisely the
compact-support version quoted as Theorem 1.1, equation (8), by
Egger--Steiner and credited there to Hejhal~\cite{voronoi}.
Absolute convergence here follows by integrations by parts for the
oscillatory kernel, with the $K_0$ part exponentially decreasing.

Apply \eqref{dep:hl:eq:classical} to $f(x)$ and to $2f(2x)$ and subtract.
The main integrals differ by $\log 2\int f$. In the dual part, use
the common scale $4\pi\sqrt{mx/2}$. Its coefficient is
\[
 \mathbf1_{2\mid m}\tau(m/2)-\tau(m)=-d_o(m).
\]
Thus the exact signed identity is
\begin{equation}\label{dep:hl:eq:signed}
 \sum_{k\ge1}h(k)f(k)=\log 2\int_0^\infty f(x)\dd x
 -\sum_{m\ge1}d_o(m)\int_0^\infty
                 f(x)\mathcal K(4\pi\sqrt{mx/2})\dd x.
\end{equation}
In particular the oscillatory contribution has sign \emph{positive}
$2\pi Y_0$. No estimate for the unsmoothed divisor problem is used.

Apply \eqref{dep:hl:eq:signed} to $f_n$. Lemmas~\ref{dep:hl:lem:interpolation}
and \ref{dep:hl:lem:mass} supply the main term $\log 2$ in $nU_n$.
The support has $cn\le x\le Cn$. The standard large-positive-argument
bound for $K_0$~\cite{dlmfK} and $d_o(m)\le m$ make its total
contribution $\OO(e^{-c_3\sqrt n})$, after adjusting the positive
constant $c_3$. The remaining expression is
\begin{equation}\label{dep:hl:eq:oscintegral}
 \sum_{m\ge1}d_o(m)\int_\RR g_s(y)\,2\pi Y_0
 \left(\frac{2\beta_m}{s}\sqrt{1+\mu sy}\right)\dd y,
 \qquad s=n^{-1/2}.
\end{equation}
The all-harmonic justification of its expansion is the next step.

\section{A uniform all harmonic remainder}\label{dep:hl:sec:uniform}
Define the exact normalized Hankel amplitude for $q>0$ by
\[
 \mathcal A(q)=\sqrt{\frac{\pi q}{2}}\,
      e^{-\ii(q-\pi/4)}H_0^{(1)}(q).
\]
The standard coefficients satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{dep:hl:eq:ak}
 a_0=1,\qquad a_k=-\frac{(2k-1)^2}{8k}a_{k-1}\quad(k\ge1).
\end{equation}
For every fixed $K,r\ge0$ (with $K\ge1$),
\begin{equation}\label{dep:hl:eq:symbol}
 \partial_q^r\left(\mathcal A(q)-\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\ii^ka_kq^{-k}\right)
                 =\OO_{K,r}(q^{-K-r}).
\end{equation}
The extra $q^{-r}$ is essential. It follows from the complex Hankel
remainder bounds in DLMF 10.17.13--15~\cite{dlmfH}, uniformly in a
small cone around the positive axis, by Cauchy's derivative estimates
on disks of radius proportional to $q$. Merely differentiating a
formal real-axis asymptotic expression would not justify this step.

For $\beta=\beta_m$, write
\begin{align}\label{dep:hl:eq:Jidentity}
 &\int_\RR g_s(y)\,2\pi Y_0
       \left(\frac{2\beta}{s}\sqrt{1+\mu sy}\right)\dd y\notag\\
 &\hspace{6mm}=2\sqrt\pi\,\beta^{-1/2}s^{1/2}
       \Impart\left\{e^{\ii(2\beta/s-\pi/4)}J_\beta(s)\right\},\\
 J_\beta(s)&=\int_\RR g_s(y)(1+\mu sy)^{-1/4}
 e^{\ii\Psi_{\beta,s}(y)}
 \mathcal A\left(\frac{2\beta}{s}\sqrt{1+\mu sy}\right)\dd y,\label{dep:hl:eq:J}\\
 \Psi_{\beta,s}(y)&=\frac{2\beta\mu y}{\sqrt{1+\mu sy}+1}.
 \label{dep:hl:eq:phase}
\end{align}
Every amplitude is understood only on the support of $g_s$ and is
smoothly zero-extended with its cutoff. There
$1+\mu sy$ stays in a fixed positive compact interval. The rationalized
phase \eqref{dep:hl:eq:phase} has no singularity at $s=0$, and
\begin{equation}\label{dep:hl:eq:gradient}
 \partial_y\Psi_{\beta,s}(y)=\frac{\beta\mu}{\sqrt{1+\mu sy}}
                              \asymp\beta.
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}\label{dep:hl:lem:uniform}
For every fixed $N,L\ge0$ there is a constant independent of
$\beta\ge\beta_1$ and sufficiently small $s\ge0$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{dep:hl:eq:derivativebound}
 |\partial_s^N J_\beta(s)|\le C_{N,L}(1+\beta)^{-L}.
\end{equation}
Consequently, for every fixed $J,L$,
\begin{equation}\label{dep:hl:eq:uniformTaylor}
 J_\beta(s)=\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}C_j(\beta)s^j+R_{J,\beta}(s),\qquad
 |R_{J,\beta}(s)|\le C_{J,L}s^J(1+\beta)^{-L}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The seminorm bounds in \eqref{dep:hl:eq:schwartz} permit arbitrary fixed
$s,y$ derivatives of the renewal amplitude. For the composed Hankel
symbol, use the reciprocal argument
\[
 p=\frac{s}{2\beta\sqrt{1+\mu sy}}.
\]
Its fixed derivatives are bounded by $\beta^{-1}$ times polynomials in
$|y|$ on the support. The symbol bounds \eqref{dep:hl:eq:symbol}, taken to
sufficiently high order, give a smooth extension of the composition to
$s=0$, with uniform differentiated bounds. This addresses the apparent
singularity of the original argument $q\asymp\beta/s$.

Each $s$ derivative of $e^{\ii\Psi}$ contributes at most one additional
factor of $\beta$ times a polynomial in $y$; more explicitly,
$|\partial_s^j\Psi|\le C_j\beta|y|^{j+1}$. After $N$ derivatives,
factor out at most $\beta^N$. What remains is a uniformly Schwartz
amplitude, including after every fixed number of $y$ derivatives.
Now integrate by parts $R$ times using
\[
 e^{\ii\Psi}=\frac{1}{\ii\partial_y\Psi}
                         \partial_y(e^{\ii\Psi}).
\]
The reciprocal gradient is
$\sqrt{1+\mu sy}/(\beta\mu)$. It and its required $y$ derivatives
supply $\beta^{-1}$ per integration; the other factors are bounded
powers of $1+\mu sy$ and powers of $s$. All boundary terms vanish.
The resulting absolute integral is at most
$C_{N,R}\beta^{N-R}$. Choose $R>N+L$ to obtain
\eqref{dep:hl:eq:derivativebound}. The smooth extension and seminorm
continuity give the same bound at $s=0$. Taylor's theorem with its
integral remainder proves \eqref{dep:hl:eq:uniformTaylor}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Summing the actual remainders}
The prefactor in \eqref{dep:hl:eq:Jidentity} is
$2\sqrt\pi\,\beta_m^{-1/2}s^{1/2}$. Since $d_o(m)\le m$ and
$\beta_m\asymp\sqrt m$, the sum of remainder bounds is at most
\[
 C s^{J+1/2}\sum_{m\ge1}m^{3/4-L/2}=\OO(s^{J+1/2})
 \quad(L>4).
\]
This is $\OO(n^{-1/4-J/2})$. It bounds the actual remainder over
\emph{all} harmonics. There is no step that sums only fixed-mode
asymptotic error statements whose constants could grow with $m$.

The coefficients $C_j$ are finite Gaussian Fourier moments, so
\begin{equation}\label{dep:hl:eq:Cj}
 C_j(\beta)=\ii^j e^{-v\beta^2/(2\mu)}P_{B,j}(\beta)
\end{equation}
with real Laurent polynomials $P_{B,j}$. For $j=0$, the Gaussian
transform in \eqref{dep:hl:eq:g0} is $e^{-v\beta^2/(2\mu)}$.
Multiplying it by $2\sqrt\pi\,\beta_m^{-1/2}$ gives
\[
 \left(\frac{8\mu}{m}\right)^{1/4}e^{-\pi^2mv/\mu^2},
\]
which verifies all constants in \eqref{dep:hl:eq:generalH}. The higher
coefficients follow from the recipe below, and \eqref{dep:hl:eq:g1} gives
\eqref{dep:hl:eq:generalP1}. This proves Theorem~\ref{dep:hl:thm:general}.
For each fixed $j$, the exponential damping in $m$ dominates every
polynomial factor, every real derivative, and
$e^{C\sqrt m|\Impart u|}$ on compact complex $u$ sets. Thus the mode
series is entire and its derivatives converge uniformly on $\RR$.
Uniform approximation by finite trigonometric sums proves uniform
almost periodicity.

\section{A finite recipe for every fixed order}\label{dep:hl:sec:recipe}
This section makes \eqref{dep:hl:eq:Cj} constructive and connects the proof
to the exact symbolic programs. All series in $s$ are truncated at the
finite order needed; no convergence as $J\to\infty$ is asserted.
Let $\sigma^2=v/\mu^3$, with $g_0$ as in \eqref{dep:hl:eq:g0}, and define
\begin{equation}\label{dep:hl:eq:Ej}
 E_j(y,u)=\frac{\kappa_{j+2}(\ii u)^{j+2}}{\mu(j+2)!}
             +\frac{y\kappa_{j+1}(\ii u)^{j+1}}{(j+1)!}.
\end{equation}
Expand
\[
 (1+\mu sy)^{-1}\exp\left(\sum_{j\ge1}E_j(y,u)s^j\right).
\]
In the coefficient of each $s^j$, replace $(\ii u)^r$ by
$\mu^{-r}g_0^{(r)}(y)/g_0(y)$. The result is
$G_j(y)=g_j(y)/g_0(y)$. This is exactly Gaussian differentiation
under the Fourier integral in \eqref{dep:hl:eq:safeintegral}.

Next set
\begin{align}
 R(s,y)&=\sum_{r\ge1}2\beta\binom{1/2}{r+1}
                         \mu^{r+1}y^{r+1}s^r,\label{dep:hl:eq:Rrecipe}\\
 T(s,y)&=\left(\sum_{j\ge0}G_j(y)s^j\right)(1+\mu sy)^{-1/4}
                 e^{\ii R(s,y)}\notag\\
 &\hspace{14mm}\cdot\sum_{k\ge0}a_k
       \left(\frac{\ii s}{2\beta}\right)^k(1+\mu sy)^{-k/2}.
 \label{dep:hl:eq:Trecipe}
\end{align}
The coefficients are
\begin{align}
 C_j(\beta)&=\int_\RR g_0(y)e^{\ii\mu\beta y}[s^j]T(s,y)\dd y,
 \label{dep:hl:eq:Crecipe}\\
 P_{B,j}(\beta)&=\ii^{-j}e^{v\beta^2/(2\mu)}C_j(\beta).
 \label{dep:hl:eq:Precipe}
\end{align}
Only finitely many cumulants, powers and Gaussian moments are needed
at any fixed order. The parity $G_j(-y)=(-1)^jG_j(y)$ and the phase
and symbol parities make $P_{B,j}$ real. These formulas explain the
successive half-powers in the coefficient expansion.

\subsection{Specialization to the logarithmic decoration}
For $B=L$, the cumulant generating function is
\begin{equation}\label{dep:hl:eq:cumgen}
 \log B(\rho e^t)=\log\left(1-\log(1-M(e^t-1))\right).
\end{equation}
In particular,
\begin{align*}
 \kappa_1&=\kappa_2=M,\\
 \kappa_3&=M(M^2+1),\\
 \kappa_4&=M(M^3+6M^2+1),\\
 \kappa_5&=M(8M^4+10M^3+25M^2+1).
\end{align*}
Put $z=My$. The base measure becomes the standard Gaussian
$\gamma(z)\dd z=(2\pi)^{-1/2}e^{-z^2/2}\dd z$.
The Gaussian-derivative replacement is
$(\ii u)^r\mapsto(-1)^r\operatorname{He}_r(z)$ in these scaled
coordinates, where $\operatorname{He}_r$ is the probabilists' Hermite
polynomial. The final normalized Fourier moment is
$z^r\mapsto\ii^r\operatorname{He}_r(\beta)$.
The script \texttt{derive\_coefficients.py} implements these exact
rules and produces \eqref{dep:hl:eq:p0}--\eqref{dep:hl:eq:p3}.

A second script, \texttt{verify\_coefficients.py}, uses actual
symbolic Gaussian derivatives rather than a Hermite routine, and the
integration-by-parts moment recurrence
\begin{equation}\label{dep:hl:eq:momentrec}
 m_0=1,\quad m_1=\ii b,\quad
 m_{r+1}=\ii b\,m_r+r\,m_{r-1}.
\end{equation}
It reconstructs $G_0,\ldots,G_3$ and the first four kernel coefficients
before comparing with the saved polynomials. For transparency, write
$D_r=\gamma^{(r)}/\gamma$ and $c_j=\kappa_j/M$. The third density
coefficient is
\begin{align}\label{dep:hl:eq:G3}
 G_3={}&-z^3+\frac{z^3D_2}{2}-\frac{z^3D_4}{8}
       +\frac{z^3D_6}{48}+\frac{c_5D_5}{120}\notag\\
 &+\left(\frac{c_3^2}{72}+\frac{c_4}{48}\right)zD_6
 +\left(\frac{c_3c_4}{144}+\frac{c_3z^2}{48}\right)D_7
 +\frac{c_3^2zD_8}{144}+\frac{c_3^3D_9}{1296}.
\end{align}
The cubic kernel coefficient, after multiplying the geometric,
phase and Hankel factors, is
\begin{align}\label{dep:hl:eq:kernel3}
 Q_3={}&-\frac{25z^3}{256}+\frac{5b^2z^5}{128}
          +\frac{45z}{2048b^2}\notag\\
 &+\ii\left(\frac{b^3z^6}{384}-\frac{75bz^4}{512}
             -\frac{75z^2}{2048b}+\frac{75}{8192b^3}\right).
\end{align}
If $Q_1,Q_2$ denote the lower kernel coefficients, applying
\eqref{dep:hl:eq:momentrec} to $G_3+G_2Q_1+G_1Q_2+Q_3$ and dividing by
$\ii^3$ gives \eqref{dep:hl:eq:p3}. The checks use exact symbolic identities,
not a numerical fit.

\section{Nonvanishing and signed subsequences}\label{dep:hl:sec:sign}
For the logarithmic decoration the first harmonic dominates. From
$d_o(m)\le m$ and $m^{-1/4}\le1$,
\begin{equation}\label{dep:hl:eq:tail}
 \sum_{m\ge2}\frac{K_m}{K_1}
 \le\sum_{m\ge2}mr^{m-1}
 =\frac1{(1-r)^2}-1<\frac1{100}.
\end{equation}
Here is an exact rational certificate for the last step. The elementary
bounds $e<87/32$ and $\pi>157/50$ imply
\[
 \frac{\pi^2}{e-1}>\frac{(157/50)^2}{55/32}>\frac{573}{100}.
\]
The positive Taylor sum
$\sum_{k=0}^{20}(573/100)^k/k!>300$ proves $r<1/300$.
Then $1/(1-r)^2-1<599/89401<1/100$. The checker verifies these
rational inequalities and an exponential-series upper bound on $e$;
it takes the elementary bound $\pi>157/50$ as an input.

Choose $u_\ell\to\infty$ with
$\Phi_1(u_\ell)=\pi/2+2\pi\ell$, and let $n_\ell$ be a nearest
integer to $u_\ell^2$. Then
$\sqrt{n_\ell}-u_\ell=\OO(1/u_\ell)$. Bounded derivatives and
\eqref{dep:hl:eq:tail} give
$H_0(\sqrt{n_\ell})\ge0.99K_1+o(1)$.
Choosing phase $3\pi/2+2\pi\ell$ instead gives
$H_0(\sqrt{n_\ell})\le-0.99K_1+o(1)$. By \eqref{dep:hl:eq:main},
\[
 n^{1/4}\left(\frac{n\rho^na(n)}{n!}-\log 2\right)
\]
has positive and negative subsequences bounded away from zero. In
particular, the error is not $o(n^{-1/4})$ and cannot be
$\OO(n^{-1})$. These conclusions do not identify an exact limsup or
liminf, a limiting distribution, or independence of the harmonic
phases.

\section{Eventual shape and smooth inverse envelopes}\label{dep:hl:sec:inverse}
\begin{corollary}\label{dep:hl:cor:shape}
The sequence $a(n)$ is eventually positive, strictly increasing, and
strictly log-convex. More precisely,
\begin{equation}\label{dep:hl:eq:ratio}
 \frac{a(n+1)}{a(n)}\sim\frac n\rho,\qquad
 \log\frac{a(n-1)a(n+1)}{a(n)^2}=\frac1n+\OO(n^{-5/4}).
\end{equation}
No explicit onset is asserted.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Positivity and the ratio follow immediately from \eqref{dep:hl:eq:equiv}.
For fixed $J\ge1$, define the eventually positive smooth function
\begin{equation}\label{dep:hl:eq:AJ}
 A_J(t)=\Gamma(t)\rho^{-t}
  \left[\log 2+\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}t^{-1/4-j/2}H_j(\sqrt t)\right].
\end{equation}
With $J=2$, \eqref{dep:hl:eq:main} implies
$\log a(n)=\log A_2(n)+\OO(n^{-5/4})$.
Bounded derivatives of the $H_j$ and the usual digamma estimates give
$(\log A_2)''(t)=1/t+\OO(t^{-5/4})$.
For any twice differentiable $f$,
\[
 f(n+1)-2f(n)+f(n-1)=\int_{-1}^1(1-|u|)f''(n+u)\dd u.
\]
Apply this identity to $\log A_2$ and bound the three discrete
$\OO(n^{-5/4})$ errors separately. This proves the second assertion in
\eqref{dep:hl:eq:ratio}, eventually positive. No discrete error term has
been differentiated.
\end{proof}

For fixed $J$, logarithmic differentiation of \eqref{dep:hl:eq:AJ} gives
\begin{equation}\label{dep:hl:eq:logder}
 (\log A_J)'(t)=\log(t/\rho)+\OO(t^{-1})+\OO_J(t^{-3/4})>0
\end{equation}
for sufficiently large $t$. Also, with $\alpha=1/4+J/2$,
\begin{equation}\label{dep:hl:eq:relative}
 a(n)=A_J(n)(1+\OO_J(n^{-\alpha})).
\end{equation}
Fix an integer $n_0$ beyond the eventual positivity and monotonicity
onset, and define
\begin{equation}\label{dep:hl:eq:N}
 N(x)=\min\{n\ge n_0:a(n)\ge x\}.
\end{equation}
The number $n_0$ is fixed, not allowed to vary with $x$.
Let $t_J(x)$ be the unique sufficiently large solution of $A_J(t)=x$.

\begin{theorem}\label{dep:hl:thm:inverse}
There is a constant $C_J$ such that, for every sufficiently large $x$,
\begin{equation}\label{dep:hl:eq:ceilings}
 \left\lceil t_J-C_J\frac{t_J^{-\alpha}}{\log t_J}\right\rceil
 \le N(x)\le
 \left\lceil t_J+C_J\frac{t_J^{-\alpha}}{\log t_J}\right\rceil,
 \qquad \alpha=\frac14+\frac J2.
\end{equation}
The displacement shrinks to zero. The constants and onset are
existential. This is not an unconditional single-ceiling rule at jumps.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
From \eqref{dep:hl:eq:relative},
$|\log a(n)-\log A_J(n)|\le Cn^{-\alpha}$ for large integers.
Put $t=t_J$ and $\delta=C't^{-\alpha}/\log t$.
For $C'$ sufficiently large, \eqref{dep:hl:eq:logder} makes the decrease of
$\log A_J$ from $t$ to any nearby integer below $t-\delta$ dominate
this pointwise error. Those integers have $a(n)<x$. Similarly the
integer $\lceil t+\delta\rceil$ has $a(n)\ge x$. The derivative
bounds are uniform throughout a fixed neighborhood of $t$, and
enlarging $C'$ handles variation across one lattice spacing.
Eventual monotonicity handles earlier integers. This proves both
ceilings, even when $x$ lies arbitrarily close to a jump.
\end{proof}
Any prescribed finite prefix has a finite maximum. Once $x$ exceeds
that maximum, it cannot change the threshold; thus \eqref{dep:hl:eq:N}
agrees eventually with the full-sequence convention if that convention
is used. No positivity or monotonicity of the prefix is assumed.

\subsection{A useful first inverse correction}
Let $b=b(x)$ solve
$\Gamma(b)\rho^{-b}\log 2=x$ on its increasing large branch, and put
$D(b)=\psi_0(b)-\log\rho$, where $\psi_0$ is the digamma function.
For every fixed $J\ge1$,
\begin{equation}\label{dep:hl:eq:inversecorr}
 t_J(x)=b-\frac{b^{-1/4}H_0(\sqrt b)}{\log 2\,D(b)}
               +\OO\left(\frac{b^{-1/2}}{\log b}\right).
\end{equation}
This follows by logarithmic Taylor expansion and \eqref{dep:hl:eq:logder}.
The square in $\log(1+\varepsilon)$ contributes at order
$b^{-1/2}/\log b$; retaining only the next oscillatory $H_1$ term
would miss it. The integer inverse still requires \eqref{dep:hl:eq:ceilings}.
A Lambert-$W$ starting approximation from Stirling's formula is
\begin{equation}\label{dep:hl:eq:Lambert}
 Y=\log\frac{x}{\sqrt{2\pi}\log2},\qquad
 b=\frac{Y}{W(Y/(e\rho))}+\OO(1),
\end{equation}
where the positive real branch is used for large $x$.
Ordinary monotone scalar solving of \eqref{dep:hl:eq:AJ} preserves all the
displayed oscillations. No new inversion principle is claimed.

\begin{enumerate}
\item\label{dep:q:effective} \emph{Effective constants, cutoffs and onsets}
(Part~I, item~1; Part~II, ``Claims left outside scope'' and ``Further
calculations''). All remainder constants and starting indices in
Theorems~\ref{dep:eta:thm:A}, \ref{dep:eta:thm:general},

what is missing is an explicit choice of the cutoffs, explicit stationary-phase

own phases and cusps; the example $(z+z^3)/2$ shows that the condition
$\gcd\{j:b_j>0\}=1$ does not suffice. Open for both kernels.
\item\label{dep:q:variance} \emph{Vanishing variance} (Part~I, item~3;
Part~II, ``Claims left outside scope'' and ``Further calculations''). As
$v/\mu^2\to0$ the number of appreciable harmonics grows, since the damping is
$e^{-\pi^2mv/\mu^2}$; a crossover theorem uniform in a family of decorations
approaching a monomial needs new two-parameter estimates. Neither fixed-$B$
theorem gives it.
\item\label{dep:q:phases} \emph{Joint phases, extremes and limiting error laws}
(Part~I, item~4 and the remark after Theorem~\ref{dep:eta:thm:sign}; Part~II,
Section~\ref{dep:hl:sec:sign} and ``Claims left outside scope''). The
frequencies are proportional to $\sqrt m$; the signed subsequences use only
the first harmonic and determine neither the exact $\limsup$ and $\liminf$ of
the scaled errors nor a limiting distribution of $H_0(\sqrt n)$, nor whether
the harmonic phases behave independently. Open.
\item\label{dep:q:orders} \emph{Growing orders, optimal truncation and
exponentially improved expansions} (Part~I, item~5; Part~II, Scope and ``Claims
left outside scope''). The recipes are finite at every fixed order and say
nothing about the growth of the $H_j$ or $P_j$ as $j\to\infty$, optimal
