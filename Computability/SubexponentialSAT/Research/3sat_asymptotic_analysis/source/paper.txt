                                               1. Introduction
   For every fixed k ⩾ 3, k-SAT is the canonical NP-complete problem [Coo71], and its exponential-
time complexity underlies the Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH) and the Strong Exponential
Time Hypothesis (SETH) [IP01, IPZ01]. In this paper, we refute ETH by giving, for every fixed
k ⩾ 2, a deterministic algorithm that counts the satisfying assignments of a k-CNF in subexponential
time and polynomial space.

1.1. Related work. We first discuss earlier SAT and counting algorithms and the complexity
hypotheses contradicted by Theorem 1.1. We then compare our approach with earlier algebraic
algorithms and identify the existing constructions used in our proof, whose roles are developed in
the roadmap in Section 2.
1.1.1. SAT algorithms and the exponential-time hypotheses. Schöning’s local-search algorithm and
PPSZ are landmarks in the development of exponential-time algorithms for k-SAT [Sch99, PPSZ05].
Schöning’s randomized algorithm starts from a random assignment and repeatedly flips a uniformly
chosen variable in an unsatisfied clause. Its bounded-error decision time is
                                       (n + m)O(1) (2 − 2/k)n
for each fixed k ⩾ 3 [Sch99, Section 2]. In particular, this gives (n + m)O(1) (4/3)n time for 3-SAT.
The PPSZ algorithm [PPSZ05] uses a different mechanism: bounded resolution derives additional
clauses, after which variables are assigned in a random order, taking forced values when available and
guessing otherwise. For k = 3, 4, its original analysis gave stronger bounds for uniquely satisfiable
formulas than for general ones. The unique-SAT guarantees were later extended to general 3-SAT
and 4-SAT; for 3-SAT this gave a randomized O(1.308n ) bound [Her14]. Hansen, Kaplan, Zamir, and
Zwick improved the PPSZ bound for every fixed k ⩾ 3 by biasing the algorithm’s guesses [HKZZ19].
Scheder obtained an improvement for the same range of k by sharpening the analysis of the original
PPSZ algorithm [Sch24, Theorem 1.5]. For general 3-SAT, a preprint by Jiang and Cai gives a
randomized O∗ (1.307031578n ) bound [JC26, Corollary 1.2], where O∗ suppresses factors polynomial
in the input size. Liu’s deterministic algorithm combines branching with derandomized local search
and solves 3-SAT in O(1.32793n ) time [Liu18, Theorem 21].
   Randomized local search has also produced effective heuristics. The WalkSAT heuristic chooses an
unsatisfied clause and flips one of its variables, combining random choices with greedy moves [SKC94].
Experiments on formulas from circuit synthesis and diagnosis demonstrated the practical value of
this approach [SKC94, pp. 340–342, Tables 4–5].
4                                                OPENAI


   Among uniformly random k-CNFs, instances at the satisfiability threshold were conjectured
to be the hardest for deciding satisfiability [VW21, Section 1]; for fixed k ⩾ 3 and lower clause
densities m/n ⩽ 2k /k C , where C is a universal constant, polynomial-time almost-uniform sampling
of satisfying assignments is known with high probability over the formula [CLWYY25, Theorem 1.2].
For a uniformly random formula with polynomially many clauses, conditioned on satisfiability, Vyas
and Williams [VW21, Theorem 3] give a PPZ-based algorithm that finds a solution in 2n(1−Ω(log k/k))
time with high probability over both the formula and the algorithm’s random choices.
   The Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH) asserted that 3-SAT requires 2Ω(n) deterministic time
in the worst case, and hence so does k-SAT for every fixed k ⩾ 3 [IP01, Theorem 1]. The Strong
Exponential Time Hypothesis (SETH) made the sharper prediction that k-SAT requires 2n(1−ok (1))
time as k grows. Precisely, for every ε ∈ (0, 1), there is a fixed k for which no deterministic algorithm
solves k-SAT in 2(1−ε)n (n + m)O(1) time. The running-time bounds for Schöning’s algorithm and
PPSZ helped motivate this prediction: their improvements over exhaustive search diminish as k
grows [IP01, Open Problems, item 2].
   The Gap Exponential Time Hypothesis (Gap-ETH) strengthens ETH by postulating constants
δ, γ > 0 for which no O(2γn )-time algorithm can distinguish a satisfiable 3-CNF from one in which
every assignment violates at least a δ fraction of the clauses, even when m = O(n) [Din16, MR17].
The randomized version rules out an algorithm with the same running time that distinguishes
the two cases with probability at least 2/3 on every promised input [CCK+ 20, Conjecture 2.5].
It has been used to prove hardness of approximating Label Cover [Din16] and dense constraint
satisfaction problems [MR17]. It also rules out nontrivial fixed-parameter approximations for Clique
and Dominating Set. For Clique, the excluded ratios are o(OPT), where OPT is the maximum
clique size [CCK+ 20, Corollaries 5.3 and 5.6].
   The Nondeterministic Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis (NSETH) asserts that, for every ε > 0,
some fixed k admits no nondeterministic 2(1−ε)n (n + m)O(1) -time algorithm for deciding whether
a k-CNF is unsatisfiable, or equivalently whether a k-DNF is a tautology [CGI+ 16]. NSETH has
been used to establish barriers to deterministic fine-grained reductions from SAT to problems such
as 3SUM and All-Pairs Shortest Paths [CGI+ 16], and to delineate SETH-based lower bounds for
approximating directed graph diameter [Li21].
   Theorem 1.1 refutes ETH, SETH, Gap-ETH, and NSETH. Indeed, testing whether the exact count
is positive or zero decides both satisfiability and unsatisfiability in deterministic 2o(n) (n+m)Ok (1) time
for every fixed k; deciding satisfiability also solves the Gap-ETH promise problem. The deterministic
algorithm also refutes the bounded-error randomized versions of ETH [DHM+ 14, Section 1] and
SETH [CDL+ 16, Definition 2.1], and of Gap-ETH. It does not conflict with unconditional exponential
lower bounds for restricted proof systems such as regular resolution [BI13]. In 2016, Ryan Williams
refuted the Merlin–Arthur version of SETH (MA-SETH) by giving a protocol that certifies k-CNF
unsatisfiability in 2n/2 poly(n, m) time [Wil16, Theorem 3.3].


1.1.2. Exact counting and counting complexity. In Valiant’s framework, #SAT and #3-SAT are
#P-complete under parsimonious reductions [Val79, Section 3, Facts 1–2]. For fixed k ⩾ 3, previous
exact-counting algorithms achieve polyk (n, m)2n−Ω(n/k) time, with a Las Vegas expected-time
bound in [IMP12, Lemma 3.5] and a deterministic bound in [CW21, Theorem 1.6]. For #2-SAT,
Wahlström’s algorithm counts satisfying assignments in O∗ (1.2377n ) time [Wah08]. For #3-SAT,
Kutzkov uses complementary branching to count satisfying assignments in O∗ (1.637n ) time [Kut26].
Both algorithms are deterministic.
   The counting Exponential Time Hypothesis (#ETH) asserted that exactly counting the satisfying
assignments of a 3-CNF requires 2Ω(n) deterministic time [DHM+ 14, Section 1]. Under #ETH,
Curticapean [Cur18] rules out deterministic 2o(v) -time algorithms for counting perfect matchings
even in unweighted bipartite graphs with v vertices and O(v) edges. The perfect-matching algorithm

in Corollary 1.3 applies in particular to these graphs. Our algorithm (Theorem 1.1) solves #3-SAT
deterministically in 2o(n) poly(n, m) time and therefore refutes #ETH.
1.1.3. Implications for fine-grained complexity. ETH and SETH became standard assumptions for
parameterized and fine-grained complexity [IPZ01, VW19]. ETH-based reductions yielded lower
bounds for the dependence on solution size in Clique [CHKX06], and on treewidth in constraint
satisfaction and partitioned subgraph isomorphism [Mar10]. SETH supported sharp exponential
bounds for Hitting Set and Set Splitting [CDL+ 16], and for Independent Set and Dominating Set
parameterized by treewidth [LMS18]. It also gave bounds on possible speedups of pseudopolynomial
algorithms for Subset Sum and Bicriteria Path [ABHS19].
   For problems in P, SETH was used to rule out polynomial-factor speedups over quadratic running
times for edit distance [BI18], longest common subsequence and dynamic time warping [BK15],
Fréchet distance [Bri14], and regular-expression matching [BI16]. Other applications concerned
approximating graph diameter [RV13] and maintaining reachability counts or the size of the largest
strongly connected component under graph updates [AW14]. Together these results made SETH a
common basis for explaining the apparent optimality of algorithms across quite different problems.
   Our refutation of ETH and SETH removes one basis for the hardness claims above. Some
of the same lower bounds follow from weaker hypotheses that may still hold. For example, the
lower bounds for longest common subsequence and regular-expression matching follow from the
Orthogonal Vectors Conjecture (OVC) [BK15, BI16]. OVC asserts that for every fixed ε > 0, no
O(N 2−ε poly(d))-time algorithm can decide whether two sets of N Boolean vectors of dimension
d contain an orthogonal pair, one vector from each set. The reduction from SAT to Orthogonal
Vectors shows that SETH implies OVC [Wil05, VW19]. The lower bounds for Fréchet distance,
graph diameter approximation, and the treewidth dependence of Independent Set and Dominating
Set also follow from the hypothesis that unrestricted CNF-SAT admits no 2(1−δ)n poly(n, m)-time
algorithm for any constant δ > 0 [Bri14, RV13, LMS18]. Theorem 1.1 gives a running-time guarantee
for each fixed clause width k, while this hypothesis allows the width to grow with n.
   To obtain a faster algorithm for a target problem from our SAT algorithm, we need a reduction in
the other direction, from that problem to fixed-width SAT, that controls the number of variables.
Set Splitting with sets of size at most k has such a reduction [CDL+ 16, Observation 3.6]: use one
Boolean variable per element to specify its side of the split. For each set, one clause requires at least
one of its variables to be true, and a second requires at least one to be false. The resulting k-CNF
has one variable per universe element, so for fixed k Theorem 1.1 gives subexponential time in the
universe size, up to a polynomial input-size factor.
1.1.4. Prior work on algebraic algorithms for combinatorial problems. Our algorithm is algebraic,
based on polynomial interpolation of carefully chosen auxiliary variables. Below we discuss prior work
on using algebraic methods to design algorithm for combinatorial problems. For broader background,
see the surveys of Koutis and Williams [KW16] on algebraic fingerprints, Williams [Wil14] on the
polynomial method in algorithm design, and Husfeldt [Hus11] on algorithmic inclusion–exclusion.
   Williams’s split-and-list algorithm [Wil05] partitions the variables of a 2-CSP into three groups,
enumerates partial assignments, and combines them using fast matrix multiplication. Koivisto [Koi06]
gives a direct sum–product formulation. The algorithm of Sections 3–6 likewise enumerates as-
signments within groups. Fixing the shared measurements ⃗y makes their contributions multiply
independently. Scott and Sorkin [SS09, Section 6] extend split-and-list to polynomial-valued weights
and arbitrary rings. Our summation routine similarly uses truncated polynomial rings to retain
several coefficients in one computation.
   Björklund [Bjö16, Sections 2–3] uses inclusion–exclusion and polynomial interpolation to reduce
permanent and directed Hamiltonian-cycle counting to a weighted sum over smaller instances. He
adds the weights of identical instances in a table, then solves each distinct instance with nonzero
total weight once. Björklund, Kaski, and Williams [BKW19] use low-degree curves and generalized
6                                              OPENAI


Kakeya sets to recover polynomial values from evaluations on a small structured set, yielding faster
algorithms for fermionants, including the permanent of integer matrices whose entries are bounded
in absolute value by a fixed constant. Our polynomial replacements serve a similar purpose: they
connect the desired count to structured evaluations from which it can be reconstructed. Here the
useful structure is separation into groups after fixing a few shared measurements.
   Bhargava, Ghosh, Kumar, and Mohapatra [BGKM23, Sections 3.1–3.2] evaluate multivariate
polynomials over finite fields along low-degree curves, using Hasse derivatives and Hermite interpola-
tion when values alone do not suffice. For a univariate restriction g, the order-j Hasse derivative at
t is the coefficient of z j in g(t + z). We use the same recovery principle: compute several Taylor
coefficients at each point admitting fast summation, then recover the original count by Hermite
interpolation.
   Woodruff and Yekhanin [WY07] use values and first derivatives along a line to recover a target
value in private information retrieval. Kopparty, Saraf, and Yekhanin [KSY14] use higher-order
Hasse derivatives for local correction of multiplicity codes, extending the line-based recovery of
Reed–Muller codes. In our algorithm, the matrix construction supplies low-degree replacement forms
that let us compute the Taylor coefficients at the interpolation points by fast summation after fixing
a few shared measurements.
1.1.5. Antecedents of the matrix construction. Our counting algorithm evaluates sums of products
of polynomials over Boolean assignments. The polynomials share variables, so these sums do not
factor into independent smaller sums. We introduce linear substitutions, depending polynomially on
an interpolation parameter, that make such a factorization possible after fixing a few integer linear
measurements. An auxiliary square rational matrix M0 underlies these substitutions: a bound on
rank(I − M0 ) limits the number of measurements, while restrictions on the nonzero entries of M0
keep the substitution degrees small. Section 2.7 explains this connection.
   Related interpolation methods have been used to obtain low-rank approximations to structured
matrices. In [AW17, Section 3], a low-degree polynomial matches (−1)j on an interval of integer
inner products j, using the interpolation lemma of [AW15, Lemma 3.1]. This gives low-rank
matrices agreeing with Walsh–Hadamard matrices in most entries, a result in the study of matrix
rigidity [Val77]. Related interpolation gives rigidity bounds for Kronecker powers in [AGP23,
Theorem 6.1]. Dvir and Liu [DL20, Claim 2.20 and Lemmas 3.3–3.4] use a spectral approach: sparse
changes to a function invariant under permutations of its coordinates make many Fourier coefficients
vanish, reducing the rank of its group-circulant matrix.
   Our construction also interpolates (−1)j on a short interval, now to obtain a polynomial in
commuting permutation matrices. The same polynomial gives both the rank bound for I − M0
and the restrictions on the nonzero entries of M0 . We prove these properties and their quantitative
bounds directly in Section 4.
1.1.6. Sparsification and parity tests. We use the counting-preserving version [DHM+ 14, Appendix A]
of the sparsification lemma [IPZ01], with quantitative bounds from [CIP06, Section 6] and [Cal09,
Lemma 5.14 and equation (5.4)]. This partitions the satisfying assignments among sparse formulas
on the same Boolean variables. Appendix A records the disjoint branching argument and the bounds
needed when its parameter varies with the input size.
   The initial parity tests are the constraints of a Tanner code [Tan81, Section II], applied to the
binary vector recording which clauses an assignment violates. We use the edge-based expander-code
construction of [SS96, Sections IV and VI]: each graph edge carries one bit, and each vertex requires
its incident bits to belong to a fixed binary linear code. The local code’s minimum distance, together
with graph expansion, forces every nonzero vector satisfying all these constraints to have large
support. This construction and distance argument are standard. Lemma 6.1 supplies the parameter
choices needed here, including the bounds on the number and support size of the tests, and constructs
the graph and local code by deterministic exhaustive searches. The graph is the bipartite double

cover of a Cayley multigraph. The shifts defining that multigraph nearly balance every nonconstant
parity on the base group: this is the small-bias condition [NN93, AR94], stated explicitly in (6.2).
Fixing unused edge bits to zero gives a code on exactly m coordinates. Additional parity tests
exclude the remaining nonzero violation vectors. We choose these tests by the averaging principle
underlying the method of conditional expectations [Rag88], using exact counts to evaluate each
candidate. Section 6 gives the constructions and quantitative bounds needed here.

   We first count satisfying assignments of a 3-CNF in which each variable occurs in at most three
clauses. The product of its clause polynomials has degree at most 3n, so we can use interpolation
to recover the exact count from sums over small groups of variables. Section 2.8 then uses disjoint
sparsification and variable copying to count the satisfying assignments of an arbitrary k-CNF in
(n + m)Ok (1) 2o(n) time for every fixed k ⩾ 2. Taking k = 3 already refutes ETH. Section 2.9 explains
how counting on the original variables gives the exponent Ok (ρ(n)) of Proposition 6.4.

                      4. Constructing low-degree replacement forms
   The counting routine needs one replacement form for each pair (f, v) where factor f reads variable
v. For the application in Section 5, f is a polynomial equation and its scope is the union of the
variable sets of its monomials. This section uses only those scopes. We construct the rows P⃗f,v (T ) and
measurement matrices Ct,g in Definition 3.1, together with the partitions St,g and factor assignments

H grows linearly with R, so a small R/Q reduces the number of Taylor coefficients needed at each
point. Choosing the rows independently at T = 0, 1, . . . , Q and interpolating would generally give
degree Q. We instead construct their values at all these points together.

then place each factor–variable pair in a copy of that array. Several positions can contain the same
Boolean variable xv ; their values are always equal, and they do not become independent variables in
the counting sum.
