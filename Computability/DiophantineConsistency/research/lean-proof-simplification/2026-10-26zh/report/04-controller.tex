\textbf{Mathematical proof.} Let \(p\) be prime with \(p\mid C\), and let \(\phi:\Z/C\to\Z/p\) be the reduction homomorphism. Suppose \(\phi(b)\ne0\). At least one of \(\phi(t)\) and \(\phi(t-b)\) is nonzero: otherwise subtracting gives \(\phi(b)=0\). The existing Boolean-choice theorem therefore supplies \(e\in\{0,1\}\) such that, for \(r=t-eb\), \(\phi(r)\ne0\).

Set \(a=r.\mathrm{val}\). The standard cast identity identifies \((a:\Z/p)\) with \(\phi(r)\). By \((a:\Z/p)=0\iff p\mid a\), we obtain \(p\nmid a\). If \(a=0\), then \(p\mid a\), a contradiction; hence \(0<a\). The canonical representative also satisfies \(a<C\), while its cast in \(\Z/C\) is exactly \(r\). Finally, primality gives \(\gcd(a,p^n)=1\) for every \(n\), including \(n=0\). Every witness and endpoint in the old theorem is retained.
