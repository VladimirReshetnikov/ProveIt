\section{A rejected 215-state experiment and a reusable new lemma}
The latest archives include a separate scratch-biased short-transfer experiment. Its 6,152-case physical certificate is not a consistency calibration. Our audit finds a stronger problem than the stated open cofinality question: its literal program does not implement its advertised scheduler.

The original return was \texttt{CC3} at address \texttt{10011}. Replacing that leaf by the pair \texttt{(CI6,CC3)} moves the continue instruction to \texttt{100111}. A structured continue counts right ancestors; the newly introduced right ancestor changes its target. Two independently implemented control-flow resolvers agree over all 4,733 source occurrences. At ordinal 4697, this \texttt{CC3} returns to the selector at ordinal 23, \emph{not} the scheduler at ordinal 0. A direct semantic repair at that address would require \texttt{CC4}, whose physical realization is a separate obligation.

After a normal AX or MP, the selector, cache, and arithmetic scratches are zero. The extra \texttt{CI6} makes scratch $X=1$. The wrong return therefore enters the short-transfer branch, clears the active top, and executes its cache-copy restoration phase with this preexisting scratch unit. That phase creates $H=1$. The subsequent NIP test reaches the halt instruction, independently of the stack and other retained natural data. If the incoming top is $t$, the source reaches CH after $2t+15$ nonhalting primitives; the retained $t=42$ witness has 99 such steps, followed by CH itself. We do not claim a measured whole-blank binary halting time. The archived startup enters an AX suffix, whose totality and physical realization give the mathematical connection to this unavoidable false halt.

The portable rejection audit retains the exact source and table, two control-flow reconstructions, the complete local witness, and its provenance. This illustrates why a full arbitrary-natural primitive certificate can be correct while the overall program is wrong. The rejected bytes are not included as a successful second consistency machine.

\subsection{Adjacent successful candidates for the intended biased schedule}
A useful new encoding lemma survives independently of those rejected bytes. Consider the \emph{intended} stateful schedule with S tagged 0, MP tagged 1, AX tagged at least 2, and bias 0 after S versus bias 1 after MP or AX. At a positive residual $r$, it reads $r-1+b$, where $b$ is the bias; a fresh candidate is read without bias. Consequently, if $n=P(a,y)$, its fresh word starts with S and continues at $y-1$ when $a=0,y>0$; starts with MP and continues at $f(y)$ when $a=1,f(y)>0$; or starts with AX and continues at $f(f(y))$ when $a\ge2$ and that residual is positive. Zero residual terminates the word. Each positive recursive argument is smaller than $n$.

Assume $r>0$ is a fresh code for a suffix that halts from \emph{every} natural work tuple. For any $v\ge1$, set
\[
u=P(1,r),\qquad y=P(v,u),\qquad n=P(2,y).
\]
The elementary identities
\[
n+1=P(1,y+1),\quad y+1=P(v-1,u+1),\quad u+1=P(0,r+1)
\]
show that candidate $n$ executes $\mathrm{AX}(0;1,v)$ followed by the universal suffix $W(r)$, while candidate $n+1$ executes $\mathrm{MP}(v-1)$, then S, then the same suffix. Both halt from every initial tuple, either earlier or in that suffix. As $v$ grows, these adjacent successful pairs are cofinal. An increasing enumeration with jumps of at most two cannot skip both members of any such pair above its starting point.

This is a conditional mathematical theorem, not a calibration of the rejected source. To use it in a repaired machine still requires a literal parser correspondence, a stateful strong wire compiler, and a soundness invariant. The attached note states these premises explicitly. No repaired 214- or 215-state machine is asserted on the strength of this lemma alone.
